Introduction

Behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) is marked by profound impairments in empathy, perspective-taking, and social regulation, yet the neural mechanisms linking these behavioral traits to brain function remain poorly understood. Traditional task-based fMRI often isolates specific cognitive components but may fail to capture the dynamic, internally driven processes that underlie real-world social cognition. Naturalistic paradigms offer a complementary approach by engaging distributed socio-emotional networks in a manner closer to everyday experience.

Inter-subject correlation (ISC) of fMRI responses during naturalistic viewing quantifies the extent to which individuals process complex stimuli in a shared versus idiosyncratic way. Prior work shows that ISC is sensitive to social cognition and theory of mind and is reduced in neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative conditions[1]. However, it remains unclear whether reduced ISC in bvFTD reflects uniform neural disruption or heightened trait-dependent variability linked to socio-emotional functioning.

Here, we examined neural synchrony during viewing of a short animated film previously shown to robustly engage empathy and theory-of-mind networks[2]. We tested whether (i) ISC differs between bvFTD and healthy controls in fronto-limbic regions, and (ii) individual differences in empathic concern and perspective-taking modulate ISC within bvFTD. We hypothesized that the amygdala would show both reduced synchrony and behavioral sensitivity, whereas inferior frontal regions would show trait-dependent effects without a global group difference.

Methods

16 individuals with behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia and 28 age-matched healthy controls each completed a single fMRI scan while passively viewing a short animated film composed of emotionally and socially salient scenes previously validated to engage empathy and theory-of-mind networks. Participants also completed the Interpersonal Reactivity Index, including empathic concern (IRI-EC) and perspective-taking (IRI-PT) subscales.

Functional MRI data were preprocessed using standard procedures including motion correction, spatial normalization, nuisance regression, and temporal filtering. Subject-specific regions of interest were defined using AssemblyNet, a functional parcellation framework that generates individualized cortical and subcortical ROIs based on each participant’s functional data. Subject-level AssemblyNet ROIs were subsequently warped to common space to enable group-level comparison while preserving individual-specific functional boundaries.

Regional time series were extracted for all AssemblyNet ROIs, including left amygdala and inferior frontal gyrus subdivisions. Inter-subject correlation was computed using a leave-one-out approach, yielding subject-level ISC z-scores for each ROI across the full stimulus and relevant stimulus segments. Analyses were repeated across multiple signal representations (raw BOLD, percent signal change, and confound-regressed variants).

Group differences in ISC were assessed using Welch’s t-tests with permutation-based family-wise error correction across ROIs. Within-group behavioral associations were tested using linear regression models relating ISC to IRI-EC and IRI-PT scores separately for bvFTD and controls.

Results

Group-level analyses revealed robust reductions in inter-subject neural synchrony in bvFTD relative to controls within the left amygdala. Across signal representations and stimulus segments, amygdala ISC was significantly lower in bvFTD (Cohen’s d ≈ −1.0 to −1.3, FDR-corrected q < 0.02), indicating marked divergence in emotional salience processing during naturalistic viewing. No significant group differences were observed in the left inferior frontal gyrus. But perspective-taking selectively predicted ISC in the left orbital frontal gyrus (β ≈ 0.40–0.45, p < 0.01), implicating higher-order social–cognitive control regions. These relationships were absent or nonsignificant in healthy controls, who showed weak and anatomically diffuse ISC–behavior associations.

Thus, the amygdala exhibited both a global group deficit and within-bvFTD behavioral modulation, whereas inferior frontal regions showed behavioral sensitivity without a main group difference, indicating distinct mechanisms of vulnerability and trait-dependent variability within socio-emotional networks.

Conclusion

Naturalistic neural synchrony reveals how socioemotional traits shape brain function in behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia. Reduced amygdala synchrony indicates a shared disruption of emotional salience processing, while strong trait-dependent modulation of amygdala and inferior frontal synchrony demonstrates that individual differences in empathy and perspective-taking remain neurally meaningful in disease. The dissociation between regions showing global group differences versus selective behavioral sensitivity suggests multiple pathways through which socioemotional dysfunction emerges in bvFTD.

These findings highlight inter-subject correlation during naturalistic viewing as a sensitive, low-burden approach for linking clinical traits to neural mechanisms in neurodegenerative disease and underscore the value of individualized functional parcellation for capturing behaviorally relevant neural variability.
