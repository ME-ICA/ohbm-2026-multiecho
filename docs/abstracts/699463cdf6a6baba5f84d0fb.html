Introduction

Amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulation is a defining feature of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and one of its earliest detectable pathological events. Aβ-targeting positron emission tomography (PET) has transformed AD research and clinical care by enabling in vivo detection of Aβ plaques. In clinical contexts, Aβ-PET is central to etiologic diagnosis, treatment selection, and identifying trial-eligible participants1. With the recent approval of disease-modifying therapies for AD2 requiring confirmation of Aβ pathology before treatment, scalable and standardized imaging tools are urgently needed to support equitable access to accurate diagnosis and treatment. However, visual reads (VR), the clinical standard for evaluating Aβ pathology on Aβ-PET, can be subjective and can vary with reader expertise, image quality, and tracer type, leading to inconsistencies across and within centers3. Quantitative analysis, such as centiloid (CL)4, offers objective assessments but depends on tracer, reference region, and software, and may yield ambiguous results in borderline or artifact-prone cases. These pipelines often require MRI or CT and remain time-consuming and technically demanding. Even when applied correctly, discrepancies between visual and quantitative reads persist5, highlighting the need for standardized, scalable, and reliable tools. Deep learning offers a promising path forward. Here, using multi-tracer Aβ-PET scans, we present Auto-rADiology, an interpretable deep-learning framework that automates visual reads and CL quantification to improve standardization, efficiency, and confidence in Aβ-PET implementation.

Methods

Aβ-PET scans were aggregated from IDEAS (Imaging Dementia-Evidence for Amyloid Scanning, n=10,346), a population clinical sample with VR performed by one local radiologist per scan, and ADNI (Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, n=1,157), a research sample with VR performed via expert consensus (Fig.1A). IDEAS includes Aβ-PET scans with three radiotracers (florbetapir, florbetaben, flutametamol), whereas ADNI includes scans with two tracers (florbetapir, florbetaben). In IDEAS, scans were warped to stereotaxic PET space, harmonized to a uniform voxel size and resolution, intensity normalized to cerebellum and smoothed to 10mm3 (Fig.1B top). In ADNI, scans were linearly transformed to standard orientation, harmonized to a uniform voxel size, and smoothed to 6mm3 (Fig.1B bottom). CL values were derived via the MRI-free pipeline for IDEAS and MRI-coregistered processing for ADNI. 

Two 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) models were developed (Fig.1C): a VR model classifying scans as Aβ-positive or Aβ-negative, and a CL model predicting continuous CL values. Both models used the same architecture consisting of four convolutional blocks (each with two 3D convolution layers and a max-pooling layer), followed by a global average pooling layer, a dropout layer, and a fully connected output layer. Before input to the model, smoothed 3D Aβ-PET images were reoriented, resized, intensity-normalized, and scaled to a standard volume of 1×128×128×128 to ensure consistent model input across tracers, preprocessing pipelines, and image dimensions. Models were trained on 80% of IDEAS Aβ-PET scans, and tested on (i) the remaining 20% and (ii) separately on the external ADNI dataset. The VR model used cross-entropy loss, and the CL model used mean squared error. Model interpretability was assessed with Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) to ensure decisions were driven by appropriate regions.

To assess the model’s additive value beyond human visual reads and quantitative CL, we compared model reads (mV), human reads (V) and quantitative cut-offs (Q; dichotomized at CL ≥ 24) across the full range of centiloid values, with a special focus on the ambiguous centiloid range (CL 10-40). Effects of demographics on agreement were also evaluated.

Results

The VR model achieved 89% agreement with human reads in IDEAS (AUC=0.94), with similar performance in the external ADNI cohort (agreement=89%, AUC=0.96) (Fig.2A,B). These values match the agreement typically observed among expert readers3 and between expert reads and CL-based dichotomies5. The CL model reached a mean absolute error of 3.7 CL (R²=0.99) in IDEAS, but systematically underestimated CL in ADNI (MAE=18, R²=0.78) (Fig.2C,D). Model attention for VR focused on mediofrontal and posterior regions, matching areas that experts rely on when determining scan positivity (Fig.2A,B right). For CL, attention was distributed across the brain, with the strongest signals in lateral and ventromedial regions, which correspond to regions used for CL extraction (Fig.2C,D right). In IDEAS, 84.1% scans showed agreement across V, mV, and Q. mV aligned with V and Q better than V and Q aligned with each other (Fig.2E). Agreement decreased near the CL threshold (CL=10-40), though the greatest agreement within this window was between mV and V (Fig.2F,G). Disagreement did not relate to age, sex, tracer, race, ethnicity or diagnosis.

Conclusion

Auto-rADiology exceeded clinical-level agreement with CL despite being trained only on VR. It also showed strong potential to aid decision-making around centiloid cutoffs, where human visual read is typically challenging and agreement between human reads and CL-based thresholds is less consistent. This framework demonstrates a scalable, interpretable approach to standardizing Aβ-PET evaluation in the era of therapeutic implementation. Out-of-sample generalization was good but can be improved through fine-tuning, especially for CL. In the future, we intend to develop a more transferable system that can operate directly on raw images without preprocessing, and we plan to incorporate additional datasets into training.
