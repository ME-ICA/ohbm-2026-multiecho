Introduction

Identifying robust EEG effects can require larger samples than a single laboratory can practically deliver, motivating a need for multi-site collaboration. Unlike in fMRI, where travelling-participant datasets have helped quantify cross-site and cross-system variability and inform multi-site study design, there is a scarcity of such work for EEG. Such resources are important because they allow within-subject variability to be separated from site- and system-related factors, providing an empirical basis for benchmarking preprocessing, quality control, and harmonisation methods. To address this gap, we introduce a multi-site travelling-participant EEG dataset designed to quantify variability in canonical EEG signatures across laboratories and acquisition setups, and to provide a reusable benchmark corpus for reproducibility-focused methods development.

Methods

The study protocol was preregistered (https://osf.io/zgcp2; currently embargoed). Participants completed a standardised battery of tasks selected to elicit well-characterised event-related responses and spectral features: (i) face perception targeting the N170, (ii) visual search targeting the N2pc, (iii) active oddball targeting the P3b, (iv) passive auditory stimulation eliciting the auditory steady-state response (ASSR), and (v) eyes-open/eyes-closed resting-state recordings. Task implementations were built in PsychoPy and the full experiment code is openly available (GitHub: https://github.com/dominikwelke/eeg-multisite-study/tree/v1.0-prereg). The ERP paradigms are taken from [1] (reimplemented in PsychoPy), the ASSR paradigm from [2], and the resting task from [3].

Data were collected across three UK laboratories (University of Leeds; University of Sheffield; Manchester Metropolitan University). A minimum of 27 participants (a priori powered) were recruited across sites. Each participant completed four sessions: two sessions at their home site and one session at each of the two other sites, enabling estimation of within-person repeatability and travel/site effects. EEG was recorded using three systems (two gel-based 64-channel montages; one water-based 16-channel montage), alongside vertical and horizontal EOG and ECG. To support modelling of variance sources, we additionally captured an extensive set of laboratory descriptors and participant state/trait covariates. The dataset is being curated in EEG-BIDS with rich metadata to support interoperability, automated QC, and reuse.

Results

We report estimates of within-subject reliability and cross-site/cross-system variability for canonical EEG signatures, including ERP subcomponents (N170, N2pc, P3b), ASSR signal-to-noise metrics, and resting-state time–frequency features. These analyses yield benchmark summaries for evaluating preprocessing pipelines, quality control, and harmonisation strategies across heterogeneous EEG acquisitions.

Conclusion

This travelling-participant EEG dataset is intended as a community validation resource for reproducibility and harmonisation research in EEG, complementing analogous efforts in fMRI. By combining repeated within-person measurements, cross-site travel, heterogeneous hardware, and rich metadata in EEG-BIDS, the dataset can be used to support principled modelling of where EEG variability arises and deliver a benchmark corpus to support experimental design and methods development following public release.
