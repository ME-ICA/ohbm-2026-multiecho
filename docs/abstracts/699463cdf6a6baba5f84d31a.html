Introduction

Vascular dysfunction and impaired brain clearance can individually contribute to brain aging. Peripheral vascular abnormalities — from large arteries to the microvasculature — may influence brain structure and function through reduced perfusion, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and oxidative stress.1 In parallel, the brain clearance system (BCS), which includes the glymphatic system and the choroid plexus (CP), plays a critical role in cerebrospinal fluid circulation, metabolic waste removal, and neuroimmune regulation to maintain cerebral homeostasis.2,3 Although both systems have been studied independently, how peripheral vascular health may influence brain aging through these distinct clearance pathways is poorly understood. This study aimed to examine whether BCS measures mediate the associations between peripheral vascular indicators and two MRI markers of brain aging: gray matter brain age gap (BAG) and white matter hyperintensity (WMH) burden.

Methods

This cross-sectional study used brain imaging and clinical data from 26,628 middle-aged and older individuals without major disorders in the UK Biobank (Figure 1). Peripheral vascular indicators encompassed carotid intima–media thickness (IMT), estimated glomerular filtration rate derived from creatinine (eGFRcrea) or cystatin C (eGFRcys), serum urate, urine microalbumin, resting pulse rate, arterial stiffness index, reflection index, and waveform peak-to-peak time. 

Brain clearance measures included the diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index as a glymphatic marker, and bilateral CP volume derived from T1-weighted MRI which was then normalized to total intracranial volume to reduce inter-participant variability. 

Brain aging outcomes were BAG and WMH. Firstly, we evaluated brain biological age using gray matter structure which included cortical volume, surface area, thickness, and subcortical volumes by partial least squares regression. BAG was calculated as the difference between estimated brain age and chronological age. A positive BAG indicates that the participant's brain is older than their chronological age, suggesting the presence of accelerated aging; conversely, a negative BAG indicates delayed aging. Secondly, the total WMH volume was estimated from T1-weighted MRI and T2-weighted FLAIR data, which was then normalized to total intracranial volume and log-transformed to make the data normally distributed. 

The covariates included demographics (age, sex, ethnicity, and handedness), socioeconomic status (years of education and Townsend deprivation index), lifestyle factors (alcohol consumption frequency, smoking frequency, and sleeplessness), clinical information (body mass index, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, history of diabetes, history of hyperlipidemia, history of hypertension) and imaging sites.

Mediation analyses were conducted to assess whether BCS measures mediated the associations between peripheral vascular indicators and BAG or WMH. P values were adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR), and statistical significance was set at two-tailed P < 0.05.  

Results

Among 26,628 normal-aging participants [age 64.0 (58.0–69.0) years; 53.16% females], mediation analyses revealed two distinct brain clearance pathways linking peripheral vascular health to brain aging (Figure 2A and 2B). Reduced DTI-ALPS mediated the effects of lower eGFRcys on BAG (β = −0.0033, P = 0.014; mediation proportion = 21.75%) and WMH (β = −0.0034, P = 0.011; 19.45%), as well as higher IMT on BAG (β = 0.0036, P = 0.004; 9.37%) and WMH (β = 0.0037, P = 0.002; 14.22%). These findings suggested the mediation role of the glymphatic system in the relationship of brain aging with renal filtration dysfunction and carotid atherosclerosis. 

In contrast, enlarged CP volume mediated the associations between elevated urate and BAG (β = 0.0072, P = 0.046; 61.20%) or WMH (β = 0.0028, P = 0.041; 14.92%), and between higher pulse rate and BAG (β = 0.0057, P = 0.046; 14.70%) or WMH (β = 0.0021, P = 0.042; 6.73%). These findings indicated that the CP alteration plays a mediation role in linking brain aging with microvascular oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation.

Taken together, the mediation patterns highlight that peripheral vascular burden may exacerbate brain aging through two distinct but convergent clearance pathways.

Conclusion

In this large population-based cohort, peripheral vascular health was linked to brain aging partly through the glymphatic system and the choroid plexus. Reduced glymphatic function mediated the effects of renal filtration dysfunction and carotid atherosclerosis on gray matter and white matter aging, while increased CP volume mediated the effects of inflammatory vascular stress. These findings provide mechanistic insight into how systemic vascular burden may influence brain aging through distinct brain clearance pathways, underscoring the potential value of incorporating brain clearance function assessment into clinical assessment to provide risk evaluation and guide more targeted neuroprotective care.
