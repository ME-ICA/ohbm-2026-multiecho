Introduction

The first three years of life are marked by rapid brain development in infants. Neurodevelopmental changes during these early years can be measured using both periodic and aperiodic components of EEG data (Wilkinson et al, 2024). The concept of brain age models, which is the estimate of chronological age from neuroimaging-derived features, originated in the context of aging research and neurodegenerative disorders, where accelerated brain aging has been found to be associated with pathological disorders. Predicting chronological age using neuroimaging enables the quantification of deviations from typical neurodevelopmental patterns and holds promise as a potential diagnostic tool for the early identification of developmental delays and neurodevelopmental disorders. In recent years, several infant brain age models have been developed and validated to evaluate their accuracy in predicting infants’ chronological age using structural MRI, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), and functional MRI. Although these models have achieved promising results in brain age prediction, MRI data in infants and toddlers often face challenges of low usability due to high susceptibility to motion artifacts. In contrast, electroencephalography (EEG) presents a more practical solution that is substantially less sensitive to motion, providing a non-invasive and cost-effective means to capture neural dynamics with high temporal resolution. In addition, existing infant EEG brain age model studies remain limited to samples from relatively homogenous populations, often with a predominant racial or ethnic group, and there has been limited cross-cultural validation of brain age prediction models in early development. We aim to use EEG collected in Singapore to validate a published infant brain age model that was originally trained using 938 EEG recordings (59 – 1150 age in days) from a large cohort in the U.S. (An et al, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, 2025). 

Methods

Singapore is a multi-ethnic country with its citizen population comprising of 76% Chinese, 16% Malays, and 8% Indians. EEG data from Singapore used in this validation study were collected across 3 cohorts (GUSTO, MAMS and FABS) from 3-36 months of age (n=613).  In the GUSTO cohort, a subset of participants underwent EEG recording at 18-months of age. EEGs were acquired in a dimly lit, electrically shielded room using 128-channel Geodesic Sensor Nets connected to a DC-coupled amplifier (Net Amp 300, Electrical Geodesics, Inc.) with a sampling rate of 250Hz. Resting-state EEGs were recorded continuously for 3-minutes while the child was seated on the caregiver’s lap and watched bubbles being blown in the room. For the MAMS-CO and FABS cohorts, resting-state EEGs were acquired in a dimly lit, electrically shielded room using 128-channel Geodesic Sensor Nets connected to a DC-coupled amplifier (Net Amp 400, Electrical Geodesics, Inc.) sampled at 1000Hz (3-, 6-, 12-, 18-, and 36-months). Recordings lasted 3-5 minutes while the child was seated on the caregiver’s lap and watched a screensaver displayed on the monitor. We first harmonized the pre-processing and processing of resting-state EEG with the research group who published the brain age model. Pre-processing of the infant EEG data was completed using the Batch Electroencephalography Automated Processing Platform (BEAPP; Levin et al., Front. Neurosci., 2018) integrated with Harvard Automated Preprocessing Pipeline for EEG (HAPPE; Gabard-Durnam et al., Front. Neurosci., 2018). HAPPE quality metrics were then used to determine usability of EEG data for further analyses and a total of 399 infant EEG (79 – 1123 age in days) recordings from the Singapore cohort were retained after pre-processing (Wilkinson et al., Nat. Commun., 2024). Briefly, Power Spectral Density (PSD) was computed for each electrode using multitaper spectral analysis, then averaged across electrodes within each region of interest: frontal, central, temporal, and posterior. The resulting PSD was parameterized into aperiodic and periodic components using SpecParam version 1.0.0. Next, we harmonised the EEG features extracted for the age prediction model, including aperiodic offset and slope, power within canonical frequency bands, as well as kurtosis and skewness of the power distribution for each region (see Figure 1). Given that the pilot testing was promising, we then utilize the EEG recordings to validate the original brain age model. To measure the shifts in broad alpha and broad beta bands with respect to age, principal component analysis (PCA) was also performed for each frequency band to identify intrinsic functions (see Figure 1). A total of 76 extracted features were then applied into the winning multilayer perceptron (MLP) brain-age model, as described in the original study by An et al. (2025) (see Figure 2A, 2B & 2C). The U.S.-trained infant EEG brain-age model was first validated using an independent Singapore cohort, after which prediction accuracy was assessed for the model retrained on combined cohorts (see Figure 2A & B). Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²) and mean absolute error (MAE).

Results

Preliminary analyses showed comparable developmental trajectories of EEG measures extracted across both cohorts (see Figure 1). We first examine cross-cultural validity with the original brain age model The model accurately predicted chronological age in the Singapore sample, demonstrating cross-cultural generalizability (R² = 0.68; mean absolute error (MAE) = 109.6 days). However, prediction accuracy was higher for within-cohort U.S. data (R2 of 0.83 and MAE of 91.7 days) than for cross-cohort application to the Singapore sample (see Figure 2C). We further retrained the model using a combined U.S. and Singapore dataset to examine whether incorporating a more culturally diverse training sample would further enhance brain-age prediction performance. When trained on the combined U.S. and Singapore cohorts, the infant EEG brain-age model demonstrated more accurate brain age predictions in both samples. For the U.S. cohort, prediction accuracy improved, with a MAE of 89.4 days and R² of 0.83 (see Figure 2C). In the Singapore cohort, the combined model achieved a MAE of 86.3 days and an R² of 0.76 (see Figure 2C). In addition, t-tests showed significant reduction in prediction error for both U.S. samples and Singapore samples in younger age bins below one year of age only (see Figure 2E). Specifically, prediction errors were significantly lower in the 0–140 day (mean difference = 20.2 days, p<0.01) and 140–165 day bins (mean difference = 5.4 days, p< 0.01) for the U.S. cohort. For the Singapore cohort, prediction errors were significantly reduced in the 0–140 day (mean difference = 24.9 days, p<0.01) and 165–250 day bins (mean difference = 9.8 days, p<0.01) (See Figure 2E). 

Conclusion

This study is the first to validate an infant EEG brain-age model originally trained on a U.S. cohort using infant EEG data from a culturally distinct Singapore cohort. Results indicate that the U.S.-trained model captures a shared, biologically meaningful EEG developmental indices that generalizes across populations, although cohort-specific variability reduces the proportion of age-related variance explained under cross-cultural application. Importantly, improved brain age prediction with brain age model trained with combined cohorts suggests that increasing sample size and diversity during early developmental periods enhances model sensitivity to age-related EEG features and brain age prediction. Future studies should examine whether deviations in brain-age estimates are associated with neurodevelopmental delays or disorders.
