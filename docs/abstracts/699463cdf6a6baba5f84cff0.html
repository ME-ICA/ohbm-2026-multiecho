Introduction

The human cerebral cortex is a unique biological space that hosts complex processes unfolding at multiple scales. To study these processes, an abundance of imaging technologies have evolved over many decades to produce large-scale, continuous mappings of structural, functional and molecular features of the cortex (e.g. magnetic resonance imaging, positron emission tomography, magnetoencephalography)1. In parallel, a rich universe of techniques for cellular and molecular biology have supplied us with fine-scale, highly specific and reliable measurements in sparsely sampled tissue specimens (e.g. immunohistochemistry, whole-genome sequencing, intracranial electrophysiology). To represent cortical processes integratively across scales, spatial interpolation proves necessary for bridging sparse and continuous data. The absence of a field consensus for realistic interpolation of features over the cortical surface2,3,4 prompts a comprehensive comparison of existing frameworks from interdisciplinary literature5. Here, we benchmark the performance of relevant deterministic (Inverse Distance Weighting, K-Nearest Neighbours, and Radial Basis Function) and spatially-informed (Spatially Weighted Regression, Kriging, and Regression-Kriging) strategies using a combined simulation and data-driven approach.

Methods

We implement and test six spatial interpolation approaches that follow deterministic (Inverse Distance Weighting, K-Nearest Neighbours, Radial Basis Function) or spatially-informed (Spatially Weighted Regression, Kriging, Regression-Kriging) frameworks (Fig 1a), adapted from publicly available interdisciplinary Python libraries for spatial statistics. Sparse samples are taken from a continuous simulated or empirical “ground truth” map, then interpolated to the entire mesh based on known distance relationships between mesh vertices. To produce spatially autocorrelated simulated data, we instantiate scalar maps over the fsLR-4k canonical sphere from Gaussian Random Fields with effective range of the spatial covariance model set to 25, 50, 75, and 100-mm. Next, we sparsely sample 2, 5, 10, 25, and 50% of the simulated maps using point sets evenly distributed over the mesh as approximated by a Fibonacci lattice (Fig 1b). To examine the relevance of different methods for interpolating empirical data to cortical geometry, we selectively test them with feature maps of multiscale neurobiology with different global spatial autocorrelation (measured by Moran’s I statistic) downloaded using the Neuromaps interface1 (Fig 2a). Empirical data are sparsely sampled at the same percentages using the Desikan-Killiany atlas as an anatomical prior that specifies the center of mass for randomly chosen normally distributed point sets (Fig 2b). For deterministic methods, we optimize spatial weights and kernel functions by choosing the inverse power between 2 and 10 and the number of nearest neighbours between 2 and 20 that yield the highest ranked performance. For methods which involve regression, such as Spatially Weighted Regression and Regression-Kriging, we generate five regression covariates for simulated ground truth data by introducing white noise, global and local blurring, rotation, or a numerical threshold. For empirical ground truth data, we chose the top five empirical features that were most correlated to the ground truth as regression covariates. Interpolation performance is systematically recorded by comparing interpolated and actual values at unsampled vertices, using a rank score ranging from 0 to 1 that balances multiple metrics of accuracy (larger Pearson correlation coefficient, Spearman correlation coefficient, structural similarity index, and r2 values raise rank; larger root mean square error, Jensen-Shannon dissimilarity, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance values lower rank). We track computational efficiency by timing interpolation function runs.

Results

With simulated data on a spherical mesh, Kriging and Radial Basis Function interpolation recover the ground truth with the best performance across values of spatial autocorrelation and sampling sparsity (Fig 1c). The run times of all deterministic interpolation functions, as well as Kriging and Regression-Kriging, are stably under two minutes without parallelization, whereas Spatial Weighted Regression is remarkably expensive due to iterative calculations of spatial weights for regression at each mesh vertex (Fig 1c). Spatially-informed methods that include a Kriging step (Kriging and Regression-Kriging) rely on an adequate model for the spatial covariance structure of the data. In our data, the experimental variograms of sparsely sampled data provide less stable estimates of covariance across the spatial domain at sparser sampling percentages, although this is not correlated to the performance of Kriging interpolation (Fig 1d). With empirical data on a cortical mesh, the Gaussian variogram model does not accurately capture underlying spatial covariance structures from experimental variograms (Fig 2c). Correspondingly, deterministic methods largely outperform spatially-informed methods across features and sampling sparsity (Fig 2d). Fitting experimental variograms to a theoretical Gaussian variogram model with empirical cortical data is more error-prone than doing so with a spherical mesh. The root mean square error of the variogram fit is negatively correlated to the performance of interpolation methods that include a Kriging step (Fig 2e).

Conclusion

In this work, we survey deterministic and spatially-informed interpolation methods from interdisciplinary literature and benchmark their performance across simulation and data-driven experimental cases. Our data highlights that Radial Basis Function interpolation can be optimized to provide a reliable and efficient reconstruction of continuous cortical maps from sparse samples. However, when an adequate model for the underlying spatial covariance structure is available, the spatially-informed Kriging approach most accurately reconstructs continuous data. Altogether, with the growth of open-data initiatives around the globe, our work provides foundational methodology for data integration while illustrating the relevance of spatial modelling for multiscale neuroscience.
