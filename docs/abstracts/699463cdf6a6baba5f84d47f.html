Introduction

The experience of stressful events represents a major risk factor for mental disorders. Exposure to acute stress elicits an endocrine response via the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, stimulating the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and, subsequently, cortisol. This circuit is regulated by the brain, reflected by major changes in neuronal activation and network interactions as commonly assessed with BOLD fMRI. 1 These studies highlight the relevance of the default mode network (DMN) including the anterior cingulate cortex in the processing of stress, in particular implicating them in individual stress responsiveness. 2,3 However, research addressing the underlying metabolic demands during stress and their susceptibility to individual endocrine stress responsiveness is sparse. 3 This is partly because cerebral glucose metabolism is most reliably assessed with PET, which previously required separate scanning sessions for the different task conditions (e.g., stress vs. control and rest). However, functional PET (fPET), 4 using a bolus + constant infusion for radioligand administration, allows for evaluation of multiple task conditions within a single measurement. Using simultaneous fPET/fMRI, we examined the effects of stress exposure and individual responsiveness on the metabolic demands of the brain and how it is further linked to functional connectivity (FC) across networks.

Methods

56 healthy study participants (28 female, mean age of 23.9±4.9) underwent a simultaneous PET/MRI measurement, conducted on a Siemens Biograph mMR scanner. During each session, participants underwent a controlled stress exposure using a modified version of the Montreal Imaging Stress Test (MIST). This requires subjects to solve simple mathematical problems while being confronted with external evaluation, (negative) feedback and manipulated time limits. The control condition consisted of similar exercises, however, without any feedback or time restrictions. The experimental design included approximately one hour of acclimatization in the scanner to maintain normal levels of ACTH, while acquiring (among others) structural MRI data for data preprocessing (MPRAGE, TE/TR = 4.2/2200ms). This was followed by 56min of fPET imaging with the radioligand [18F]Fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG, bolus + constant infusion protocol, 5.1 MBq/kg body weight). Simultaneously with fPET, BOLD fMRI was acquired (TE/TR = 30/2000ms) during continuous performance of the control and stress blocks (8min each, separated by 9 min of rest). Six venous blood samples were obtained and analyzed for ACTH levels to determine the endocrine stress response (before fPET, before stress, 4x after stress). The maximum relative difference before vs. after stress was used to classify participants into groups of (non-)responders by application of a Gaussian mixture model. fPET data were reconstructed to dynamic frames of 30s and preprocessed in SPM12, including motion correction, spatial normalization via the structural MRI and Gaussian smoothing (8mm FWHM). The task-specific changes in glucose metabolism were separated from baseline radiotracer uptake by application of a general linear model and percent signal change values were extracted as implemented in the fPET toolbox (https://github.com/NeuroimagingLabsMUV/fPET-toolbox). fMRI data were preprocessed in SPM12 using slice time correction, motion correction, spatial normalization and Gaussian smoothing (8mm FWHM). Potentially confounding signals were removed by regression against motion parameters and signals from white matter and cerebrospinal fluid, motion scrubbing (framewise displacement > 0.5 mm) as well as bandpass filtering (0.01 < f < 0.15Hz). One sample t-tests were used to identify brain regions with significant stimulation-induced changes in glucose metabolism as compared to baseline metabolism. Repeated measures ANOVA was applied to assess differences in glucose metabolism between stress and control (main effect condition) and differences between responders and non-responders during stress processing (interaction effect group*condition). In addition to a whole-brain analysis, the anterior cingulate cortex was used as an a priori region of interest based on previous literature. Subsequently, task-specific FC was computed voxel-wise, with seeds determined during the fPET analysis, again followed by repeated measures ANOVA. Analyses of glucose metabolism and FC were corrected for multiple comparisons at p<0.05 FWE cluster level following p<0.001 uncorrected voxel level.

Results

A stress response was observed in 34 subjects (60%), exceeding an increase in ACTH levels of more than 23% as determined by Gaussian mixture modeling. During task performance, glucose metabolism increased in the visual frontoparietal control (FPN), dorsal attention networks (DAN) and DMN but decreased for regions of the DAN and DMN (Figure 1A-B). Direct comparison between these conditions showed that acute stress induced increased glucose metabolism in various regions of the default mode (posterior (PCC) and anterior cingulate cortices (ACC), angular gyrus, thalamus), salience (anterior insula) and visual networks (main effect, Figure 1C). This difference between conditions was significantly higher for stress responders than non-responders in the rostral ACC (interaction effect, Figure 1D, with small-volume correction). Based on the results of the fPET analyses, we selected the activated regions of the PCC and ACC (see Figure 1C-D, green circles) as seeds for the subsequent FC analyses, again comparing the different task conditions. This showed decreased FC during stress within the DMN (see Figure 1E-F) for both seed regions, and increased FC with the FPN as well as DAN, specifically for the PCC seed region (see Figure 1E).

Conclusion

The analysis of cerebral metabolic dynamics with fPET revealed that regions of the DMN were highly engaged during stress, complementing previous studies 2 by providing a metabolic viewpoint. This was also true for the subcortical representation of the DMN with the thalamus, further highlighting the involvement of subcortical brain areas as important hubs for supporting DMN function. In contrast to previously reported results during cognitive processing, stress exposure did not only increase the magnitude of metabolic demands in regions that were already active in the control condition, but also changed the overall localization of activation by recruitment of new brain regions (thalamus, PCC). This suggests that even if the participants complete the exact same task of solving arithmetic problems, the additional exposure to psychosocial stress fundamentally alters the neuronal response. Our findings further underline the role of the DMN, and the midline core structures in particular, as key regions also on a metabolic level, which suggests that this network’s involvement in stress processing may relate to individual stress resilience. Finally, the analysis of FC revealed that these stress-induced increases in metabolic demands are driven by increased network integration of the DMN with other higher-order networks such as the FPN and DAN. Together with previous work, 5 these findings emphasize the distinct role of the DMN in the integration of neurometabolic and neurovascular changes during cognitive stimulation and stress exposure.
