Introduction

Attention is a fundamental cognitive ability that allows the brain to prioritize meaningful stimuli over distractions. Despite its inherently dynamic nature, most fMRI studies have relied on static functional connectivity (sFC), which characterizes correlations in brain activity averaged over the entire scan duration1. Such static measures may mask critical temporal fluctuations that are intrinsic to attentional processes. While sFC has been shown to predict an individual’s general attention ability across multiple tasks2, the contribution of temporal variability in functional connectivity to general attention remains less explored. This study investigates whether the temporal variability of functional connectivity can predict general attention and generalize across tasks, and whether such dynamic features provide complementary predictive value beyond static connectivity. 

Methods

Participants and tasks 

A total of 127 right-handed adults participated in the fMRI study2. Data from 47 were excluded due to excessive head motion, insufficient volumes after censoring, outlier task performance, or poor image quality, leaving 80 participants with complete behavioural and imaging data for analysis. The fMRI data were acquired while participants performed three attention demanding tasks - the gradual onset continuous performance task (gradCPT), multiple object tracking (MOT), and visual short-term memory (VSTM) as well as during two task-neutral states (resting-state and movie-watching). General attention was defined as the mean of z-scored performance across three tasks2. 

Static and dynamic functional connectivity 

Static functional connectivity (sFC) was computed as Pearson’s correlation between regional time series over the entire scan. Dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) was estimated using a sliding-window approach with a tapered window (width = 60 TR, step = 1 TR)3. Within each window, pairwise Pearson correlations were computed to generate time-resolved connectivity matrices. To quantify temporal variability, we calculated the variance of correlation coefficients across windows for each edge. The resulting matrices of sFC strength and dFC variability served as input features for predictive modeling. 

Brain-based prediction of general attention 

Connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM) was used to predict general attention from edgewise dFC variability, with cross-task prediction assessing model generalizability2, 4. Models were trained and evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation, and this procedure was repeated 1000 times with subjects randomly assigned to folds We also compared predictive power of dFC with sFC, and also tested whether combined sFC+dFC can provide improved prediction accuracy. Lastly, we implemented CPMs with computational lesions in large-scale brain networks, using a canonical 10-network parcellation. We iteratively removed all connections involving each network to assess their relative and network-specific contributions. 

Results

Prediction performance of the dFC-based general attention CPM 

The dFC-based models successfully predicted general attention within and across different tasks (Fig.1). Within-task predictions were significant for gradCPT (r = 0.42, p < 0.001), MOT (r = 0.27, p = 0.014) and VSTM (r = 0.26, p = 0.021). In cross-task predictions, models trained on one attention task also significantly predicted attention scores when tested on dFC from the other tasks. Additionally, when using task-neutral states to predict task-specific performance, a gradCPT-based model predicted gradCPT from resting-state dFC (r = 0.40, p < 0.001). 

Network contributions revealed by computational lesion CPMs  

In the dFC-based models, prediction accuracy decreased most when visual and default-mode networks were lesioned (Fig.2), indicating that these networks are particularly critical for attention prediction. This pattern differed from the sFC-based models2, in which salience and cerebellar networks were most critical, suggesting that  partly distinct large-scale networks contribute to the predictive performance of static and dynamic connectivity. 

Complementary predictive value of temporal variability in FC 

Combined sFC and dFC predicted all three attention tasks more accurately than models based on either measure alone (Blue bars in Fig.3). Although sFC-based models generally predicted task performance better than dFC-based models, adding dFC features to sFC consistently improved prediction accuracy, indicating that static and dynamic connectivity provide complementary information about individual differences in attention. 

Conclusion

Our results suggest that moment-to-moment fluctuations in brain connectivity may provide complementary and generalizable neural markers of attention, beyond those identified through static connectivity analyses alone. These findings indicate that static and dynamic connectivity capture partially distinct aspects of attentional processing. Integrating stable and time-varying features of brain networks may therefore offer a more comprehensive framework for characterizing individual differences in attention and for developing brain-based markers of attentional dysfunction.
