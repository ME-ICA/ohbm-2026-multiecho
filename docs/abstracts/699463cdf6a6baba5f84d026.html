Introduction

Childhood trauma (CT) is a significant risk factor for psychopathology and is linked to higher symptom severity, comorbidity, and poorer treatment outcomes across psychiatric disorders. CT is assumed to contribute to psychopathology by altering typical brain development and has been associated with lasting alterations in brain regions and networks frequently implicated in the pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders. Nonetheless, evidence for a direct link between CT-related neural alterations and psychopathology remains inconsistent.

This may partly be a result of the predominance of group-based analytical approaches used to analyze such data, which are oftentimes inadequate to capture heterogeneity and comorbidity in psychiatric populations and may therefore obscure clinically meaningful individual differences.1 Thus, shifting the focus from the group-level toward individualized assessments of neurobiological CT correlates may offer a more precise framework for understanding its impact on mental health.

A recent study by Holz and colleagues2 addressed this by applying voxel-wise normative modeling3 to identify a stable signature of adversity-related structural brain alterations in early adulthood. The authors further showed that individual deviations from this signature were predictive of anxiety symptoms. However, these findings were observed within a predominantly subclinical range (in a cohort with a low prevalence of psychiatric diagnoses), leaving open whether the signature generalizes to clinical populations.

In this pre-registered study, we aimed to replicate this adversity signature in heterogeneous clinical populations consisting of patients with a current psychiatric disorder and healthy controls, focusing specifically on CT, and examining potentially distinct effects of different trauma types. Further, we investigated whether, and if so how, individual structural deviations from the adversity signature are associated with transdiagnostic symptomatology.

Methods

Our samples were drawn from three heterogeneous clinical cohorts (MIND-Set, BeCOME, STRATIFY/ESTRA) comprised of psychiatric patients and unaffected controls, and two largely healthy cohorts (HBS, IMAGEN; N total = 1974, N patients = 457, N controls = 1517; age range = 18-79 years). Psychiatric patients in MIND-Set met the diagnostic criteria for at least one of the following disorder clusters: mood disorder, anxiety disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder. BeCOME included patients with mood and anxiety disorders. Patients in both cohorts displayed high levels of psychiatric comorbidity (73% MIND-Set, 48% BeCOME). STRATIFY/ESTRA comprised patients with major depressive disorder, alcohol use disorder, psychosis, or anorexia nervosa. STRATIFY/ESTRA and IMAGEN data were collected at overlapping sites using largely consistent acquisition procedures and were therefore combined for the analyses.

CT was assessed using the 28-item version of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) which measures five types of trauma before the age of eighteen: emotional neglect, emotional abuse, physical neglect, physical abuse, sexual abuse. In MIND-Set, the NEMESIS was used to assess the presence and frequency of the same trauma types, except for physical neglect, before the age of sixteen. In all cohorts, T1-weighted anatomical brain images were acquired using a 3T MRI scanner. In the following analyses, we used trauma scores to predict the Jacobian determinants (JDs) of the deformation fields derived from a nonlinear registration, which reflect volumetric expansions and contractions relative to the standard space.

Specifically, we estimated voxel-wise normative models of trauma-related volumetric alterations for (1) the CTQ sum score (‘aggregate model’) and (2) four trauma subscale scores (emotional neglect, emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse; ‘subscale model’), using age, sex, intracranial volume, and dummy-coded scanning site as covariates, and employing a random train-test split of the data (Figure 1). We quantified individual patterns of structural brain deviations by extracting normative probability maps (NPMs) that reflect voxel-wise deviations from the normative range for each individual. To disentangle the contribution of each CT variable to the predicted adversity pattern, we calculated structure coefficients (i.e. the Pearson correlation between each predictor variable and the predicted JD value at each voxel). Transdiagnostic symptom domains were derived by conducting exploratory factor analysis across an extensive set of questionnaires assessing psychiatric symptomatology and functioning separately in the MIND-Set, BeCOME, and combined IMAGEN/STRATIFY/ESTRA cohorts.

To investigate associations between transdiagnostic symptomatology and brain deviations, as predicted by emotional neglect, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, and physical abuse, we performed sparse canonical correlation analysis (sCCA). Specifically, we linked the unthresholded NPMs (z-scores) to individual factor loadings for each of the obtained symptom domains, using random splits of the data into training and test sets. Stability selection was employed to identify the most stable brain features across all splits of the data (i.e. features selected in more than 80% of the resampled datasets). We then calculated the squared cross-loadings of the brain values onto the symptom factor loadings, reflecting the proportion of variance in each symptom domain explained by the brain deviations, as well as the squared cross-loadings of the symptom factor loadings onto the stable brain features. Statistical significance of the mean test canonical correlation was assessed using permutation tests. To derive unbiased estimates of generalizability, the analyses were performed separately in each cohort and aggregated across two split-half resamples of the data. 

Results

We observed a pattern of morphometric alterations that was consistent with the adversity signature previously described by Holz et al. (2023), as quantified by the voxel-wise correlation between the true and the predicted values of the JDs in the normative models (Figure 2a). The pattern was stable across both the aggregate and subscale models and comprised brain regions previously described by Holz et al. (2023), including the hippocampus, amygdala, basal ganglia, ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). Overall, the model explained up to 67% of neuroanatomical variance in JD scores.

When comparing the contribution of the aggregate trauma score (CTQ sum score) in our clinical population with the findings obtained by Holz and colleagues,2 as quantified by the structure coefficients, we identified a shared pattern of morphometric contractions encompassing divisions of the vmPFC, subcallosal cortex, insular cortex, cerebellum and basal ganglia nuclei (Figure 2b). Additional trauma-related contractions were observed in the pre- and subgenual cingulate cortex, frontal pole, amygdala, and hippocampus, whereas Holz and colleagues2 reported more widespread expansions in these areas. Both models identified volumetric expansions in the insular cortex; however, we localized these more anteriorly as opposed to more posteriorly in the original model.

Next, we evaluated trauma-related volumetric alterations predicted by emotional neglect, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, and physical abuse separately. The overall pattern of contractions and expansions was consistent across trauma types and closely aligned with the alterations predicted by the aggregated trauma score. The observed trauma pattern was relatively consistent across patients and controls and across cohorts, though volumetric expansions appeared more pronounced in clinical cohorts (MIND-Set and BeCOME). 

In MIND-Set and IMAGEN/STRATIFY/ESTRA, we derived three comparable symptom domains capturing negative valence, social functioning, and arousal/inhibition. In BeCOME, analyses revealed three factors reflecting negative valence, arousal, and somatic anxiety. Significant associations between the unthresholded brain deviation maps and factor loadings in the unseen test data were identified in the MIND-Set and the IMAGEN/STRATIFY/ESTRA cohorts (rtest= 0.30 and r=0.20, respectively, both p < 0.05 under permutation), but not in BeCOME (r = -0.05, n.s.). We obtained the highest squared cross-loadings (i.e. explained variance) in the social domain in MIND-Set, in the arousal domain in BeCOME, and in the negative valence domain in the combined IMAGEN/STRATIFY/ESTRA cohort. The symptom loadings across all three domains explained up to 11% of variance in the stable brain features in MIND-Set and BeCOME, and 5% in the IMAGEN/STRATIFY/ESTRA cohort. Consistently observed brain features included the precentral gyrus, superior frontal gyrus, anterior cingulate cortex, frontal pole, and the cerebellum. 

Conclusion

This study replicates and extends a neural signature of early life adversity to a heterogeneous adult clinical population. Using voxel-wise normative modeling, we estimated models based on aggregate and subscale trauma scores, both of which reproduced the adversity profile reported by Holz et al. (2023). 

Investigating the neurobiological alterations predicted by CT specifically, we revealed both shared and distinct alterations relative to the original model. The identified CT pattern was broadly consistent across patients, controls, and cohorts. The similarities observed between psychiatric patients and healthy controls suggest that this trauma signature may reflect vulnerability to CT that is largely independent of psychopathology and may interact with other protective, compensatory, or vulnerability factors to shape mental health outcomes. 

Notably, this trauma signature was consistent for the aggregate trauma score and the trauma subtypes (emotional neglect, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical abuse), supporting the view that different trauma types may converge on shared neurobiological pathways. This suggests that the overall severity or chronicity of trauma may be a stronger predictor of neurobiological outcomes than the specific nature of the trauma per se.4 Given the high co-occurrence of CT in our sample, (51% reporting exposure to at least two trauma types), the observed trauma signature may therefore reflect the cumulative impact of multiple stressors.

We further showed that individual deviations from the adversity signature were associated with transdiagnostic symptom domains in a clinical cohort (MIND-Set, ~70% patients) and a predominantly healthy cohort (IMAGEN/STRATIFY/ESTRA, ~7% patients). Although the CCA was not significant overall in BeCOME, the model still explained non-negligible variance in the symptom domains. 

Taken together, our results demonstrate the value of normative modeling for capturing heterogeneity in adversity, neurobiology, and psychopathology. Conventional group-level models that assume uniform brain alterations may oversimplify the neurobiological consequences of trauma and contribute to inconsistent findings in the literature.1 Accounting for individual variability may, therefore, facilitate identification of more consistent brain-behavior relationships in clinical populations. 

More importantly, our findings highlight the value of conceptualizing psychopathology in terms of transdiagnostic symptom domains to better understand functional similarities and differences within and across diagnostic boundaries. While we cannot infer causality, our results align with the view that CT and its associated neurobiological alterations constitute a universal risk factor for psychopathology, with effects that transcend diagnostic boundaries and may extend to individuals without a formal diagnosis. These findings underscore the broad relevance of CT-related neurobiological patterns across psychiatric conditions. Transdiagnostic risk factors such as CT are increasingly recognized as meaningful targets for treatment that may be integrated into clinical care, for example through trauma-focused approaches.
