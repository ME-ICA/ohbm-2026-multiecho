Introduction

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) provides rich spatiotemporal data essential for understanding human cognition and pathology.1 However, the application of deep learning in neuroimaging is often limited by the scarcity of large, labeled clinical datasets.2 Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a promising solution to this data bottleneck by leveraging large-scale unlabelled datasets to learn generalizable brain representations. A particularly powerful SSL approach is the masked autoencoder (MAE), which learns by masking portions of the input data and training a model to reconstruct the missing signal, thereby inferring complex latent structures from partial observations.3

However, standard MAE approaches adapted from vision transformers typically employ random masking (e.g., masking 50% of patches uniformly). We argue that this strategy is suboptimal for fMRI. Unlike natural images, where information is more uniformly distributed, fMRI data are often sparse and noisy, with signal-to-noise ratio varying substantially between cortical tissue and background. Random masking is therefore “biologically agnostic”, as models often waste capacity reconstructing static or non-informative voxels rather than learning complex neural dynamics.

To address this, we propose neurobiologically-aware token selection (NATS), a novel masking framework that departs from random selection by using intrinsic fMRI signal properties to guide the learning process. By calculating a saliency score based on temporal BOLD fluctuations, NATS prioritizes masking in biologically active regions. We validate this approach on the Acute to Chronic Pain Signatures (A2CPS) dataset, predicting subjective pain scores evoked by cuff pressure.4 We hypothesize that by forcing the model to reconstruct highly dynamic, salient regions, NATS facilitates the learning of more robust functional biomarkers compared to standard random masking.

Methods

Figure 1 provides an overview of our proposed framework, which uses a 4D transformer-based masked autoencoder to learn generalizable representations from volumetric fMRI data. The architecture comprises a standard vision transformer encoder with 6 layers, augmented with 4D sinusoidal positional embeddings to preserve the spatiotemporal geometry of the brain, and a lightweight decoder with 3 layers that reconstructs the input from the latent representations.

Standard masking strategies treat all tokens equally, selecting patches uniformly at random, which is suboptimal for fMRI where information is sparsely distributed. NATS instead employs a neurobiologically grounded, saliency-guided strategy. We first compute a voxel-wise saliency map (S_BOLD) based on the magnitude of temporal BOLD signal fluctuations where we formally compute the first-order temporal derivative of the BOLD signal (i.e., frame-to-frame differences) at each voxel. These voxel-wise maps are downsampled via average pooling to generate patch-level saliency probabilities. NATS diverges from standard “top-k” approaches, which could skew the training distribution by only sampling highly active or salient regions. Instead, we implement a stochastic weighted sampling strategy using a multinomial distribution derived from the saliency map. This ensures that high-variance functional areas are masked more frequently, while the model still encounters a diverse range of cortical activity. We also enforce a “tube masking” strategy where if a spatial patch is selected for masking, it is masked across the entire temporal window. This prevents the model from leaking information from adjacent timeframes, forcing it to learn long-range temporal dependencies to reconstruct the missing signal.

The model was pretrained on resting-state fMRI data from 6,000 subjects from the UK Biobank (UKB) dataset. We utilized a masking ratio of 50% creating a challenging self-supervised objective. The reconstruction target was the per-patch normalized voxel values, minimizing the mean squared error only on the masked patches. We evaluated the model on the Acute to Chronic Pain Signatures (A2CPS) dataset (N=790 with the following train/valid/test splits: 552/119/119) for the downstream task of predicting subjective pain ratings (0-10 scale) evoked by cuff pressure. To respect the ordinal nature of pain ratings, we replaced the standard regression head with an ordinal regression head that learns K-1 thresholds. We employed a partial fine-tuning strategy to bridge the domain gap where the initial ViT encoder layers were frozen to retain robust low-level features learnt from the UKB dataset, while the final three transformer blocks were fine-tuned with the ordinal regression model to learn A2CPS specific representations for predicting pain ratings. We utilized differential learning rates, applying a lower learning rate (1e-5) to the pre-trained encoder layers and a higher rate (5e-4) to the randomly initialized prediction head, ensuring stable adaptation without catastrophic forgetting. We compared this NATS-based model against a baseline pretrained with standard random tube masking. To assess the neurobiological plausibility of the learned features, we utilized integrated gradients (IG) [5] to visualize voxel-wise contributions to the pain prediction task, comparing the resulting attribution maps against established neurologic pain signatures.

Results

The NATS-pretrained model demonstrated superior generalization capabilities on the A2CPS test set (N=119) compared to the random masking baseline. NATS achieved a 20% reduction in test mean absolute error (NATS: 2.34 vs Baseline: 2.90) and a 3-fold increase in Spearman’s rank correlation (NATS: 0.12 vs Baseline: 0.04). While the absolute rank correlation reflects the inherent difficulty of decoding subjective pain scores from fMRI data, the comparative performance indicates that the NATS model better captures the relationship between brain activity and pain ratings. These results suggest that saliency-guided masking learns representations that are more relevant to subjective cognitive states. Integrated gradients analysis showed distinct differences in how the models processed pain information. The NATS model's attribution maps highlighted a distributed network of regions consistent with established neurologic pain signatures. Specifically, the model placed high importance on the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), anterior insula, thalamus, and secondary somatosensory cortex (S2) when predicting high pain scores. In contrast, the random masking baseline produced diffuse attribution maps with non-specific activation in ventricular and white matter regions, suggesting a greater reliance on global signal artifacts or non-neural artifacts rather than pain-related neural processing.

Conclusion

We introduced NATS, a neurobiologically grounded training strategy for masked autoencoders in fMRI. Our findings demonstrate that incorporating BOLD signal saliency into the masking process significantly improves the learning of functional brain representations compared to random masking. The NATS framework successfully leverages the rich spatiotemporal dynamics of fMRI, leading to better generalization in downstream tasks such as pain prediction. Furthermore, partial fine-tuning of the transformer encoder allowed the model to effectively bridge the domain gap between large-scale resting-state pretraining and task-specific clinical prediction. The alignment of our interpretability results with established pain neurophysiology suggests that NATS encourages the model to learn biologically meaningful features, offering a promising avenue for developing robust, interpretable biomarkers for complex subjective states such as pain.
