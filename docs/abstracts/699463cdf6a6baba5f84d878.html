Introduction

The brain is a multilayered system in which structure, function, and metabolism are closely interrelated. Any attempt to model this complexity for basic research or translational purposes critically depends on the availability of deeply phenotyped, multimodal data. While large-scale multimodal initiatives such as the Human Connectome Project1 and the Human Brain Project2,3 have substantially advanced the characterization of brain structure and function, its complex dimension has remained largely underrepresented.

The 5M-Connectome Dataset (5M), developed within the EBRAINS 2.0 research infrastructure, was designed to meet this need by providing the community with a comprehensive, open-access multimodal neuroimaging resource across the adult lifespan. The dataset is acquired in a multicenter study across Italy, Austria, Denmark, and Germany, using identical PET–MR scanners to allow simultaneous PET, MRI, and EEG acquisitions under standardized protocols. Rather than maximizing per-modality or per-site image quality, the project sets a high bar: delivering state-of-the-art performance across all modalities simultaneously. Synchronous acquisition maintains temporal coherence among signals, preserves biological coupling, and enables reliable cross-modal fusion, all crucial for computational modeling and machine learning. Simultaneity thus becomes a scientific prerequisite, not a design choice, avoiding the confounds inherent to sequential or multi-session approaches, where fluctuations in physiology or cognitive state compromise comparability. By exploiting the maximal capabilities of hybrid scanning technology, the 5M delivers synchronized multimodal data unattainable with MR-only or with separate PET and EEG sessions. As a cornerstone of EBRAINS 2.0, it provides a next-generation foundation that integrates structure, function, metabolism, and electrophysiology placing inter-individual variability at the center of future computational and translational neuroscience.

Methods

The 5M-Connectome dataset is designed to acquire multimodal data from healthy volunteers (N = 204; age range: 21-79 years) across four European centers located in Italy, Denmark, Germany, and Austria. All sites are equipped with identical Siemens Biograph mMR hybrid scanner, allowing fully simultaneous PET–MR acquisitions under harmonized hardware and protocol conditions. 

Each participant undergoes a single-session imaging protocol (Figure 1) combining multiple MRI sequences with simultaneous [18F]FDG-PET and EEG acquisitions. Structural MRI comprises T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and FLAIR sequences are acquired at 1 mm isotropic resolution. Diffusion MRI is acquired using a multi-shell protocol with a maximum b-value of 2000 s/mm² and 2 mm isotropic voxel size. Resting-state functional MRI consists of a 20-minute EPI acquisition (TR = 1.5 s, 3 mm isotropic resolution) acquired simultaneously with high-density EEG using MR-compatible systems. Cerebral perfusion is assessed using multi-delay pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL). Metabolic imaging is performed using dynamic [¹⁸F]FDG PET following bolus injection, with a total acquisition time of approximately 80 minutes.

In addition to imaging and EEG data, extensive phenotypic information is collected for each participant. This includes demographic variables, clinical screening instruments, cognitive assessments, self-report questionnaires covering sleep quality and hormonal status, and blood-based measures. Together, these variables provide a broad characterization of individual differences relevant to brain structure, function, and metabolism. 

Results

Current acquisition status of the 5M-Connectome dataset includes 71 healthy subjects (34% of the target sample), with completion expected by the end of 2026 (total 204, age range 21–79 years). All data are organized according to the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) standard, including extensions for PET and EEG, to ensure interoperability and reproducibility. Preprocessed data, together with derived features and summary metrics, are being stored and made accessible through the Platform for Human Imaging (PHI; https://phidb.pnc.unipd.it/).

Preprocessing pipelines have been fully implemented for all imaging modalities using widely adopted, open-access, and state-of-the-art neuroimaging tools, including sMRIPrep, fMRIPrep, QSIPrep, QSIRecon, ASLPrep, and PETPrep. This processing framework provides harmonized outputs for structural MRI, diffusion MRI, functional MRI, ASL, EEG, and PET data.

The processed data will allow the extraction of multimodal features to describe the brain at multiple levels. These will include connectivity matrices derived from DWI-based tractography, functional connectivity from resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), and connectivity measures from EEG data. Additionally, a comprehensive set of regional properties will be extracted, including cerebral blood flow (CBF) and arterial transit times (ATT) from pCASL, tissue microstructural properties from DWI, local features from EEG (e.g., power across canonical frequency bands) and rs-fMRI (e.g., regional homogeneity and fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations) as well as PET-derived parameters reflecting glucose transport and metabolism. 

Conclusion

The 5M dataset is a multimodal neuroimaging resource offering a rich and deeply phenotyped dataset for the scientific community. By providing a harmonized collection of high-fidelity, intrinsically synchronized data across PET, MRI, and EEG modalities, the dataset will address critical challenges in multimodal integration. The unique multicenter approach ensures that the data are collected under consistent, standardized protocols, enhancing the robustness and comparability of the resulting findings.

The integration of 5M multiscale neuroimaging metrics in healthy individuals (spanning global network organization, microstructural properties, perfusion, metabolism, and local neural synchrony) plays a fundamental role in building next-generation brain atlases, normative reference models, and predictive frameworks. When we study healthy participants, in fact, we gain access to the true baseline architecture of the human brain: its natural variability, its developmental trajectories, and the boundaries within which physiology and function can be considered healthy. This information is essential because pathology can only be meaningfully identified by contrasting it with a well-characterized normative landscape.

Brain atlases and connectivity matrices built from healthy subjects provide the spatial and organizational scaffolding needed to interpret any new dataset. “Classical” atlases—structural templates, cortical parcellations, and average morphometry maps—were crucial steps, but neuroimaging also allows us to construct additional atlases enabling us to evaluate multidimensional information. 

In healthy populations, these atlases and maps reveal how regions communicate, how physiological resources support function, and how metabolic and vascular signals vary across the cortex and subcortex. The result is not merely a division of brain space but a comprehensive reference of how a typical human brain works across multiple biological scales. 

These 5M atlases and maps form the basis for normative modelling enhanced with additional biological measures, enabling a more accurate characterization of the expected variability in healthy individuals across age, sex, and other phenotypic dimensions. Normative models built on harmonized, atlas-aligned data are especially powerful because they allow for highly specific comparisons: a patient’s low CBF in a particular cortical region, for example, only gains meaning when evaluated relative to the normative CBF distribution for that exact region. Similarly, aberrant network topology, reduced metabolism, or atypical local dynamics can only be interpreted when a solid reference of age- and sex-matched healthy individuals is known. This makes the role of normative datasets indispensable: they define the quantitative boundaries between normality and pathology.

Having access to normative atlases and maps further enable predictive approaches. Because all data are acquired in a fully synchronized manner on the same scanner and with uniform protocols, the resulting dataset minimizes inter-scanner noise and removes biases typically introduced by heterogeneous acquisition schemes or multi-site variability. This coherence greatly enhances the stability of the extracted features and strengthens the reliability of downstream analyses. AI-based models trained on such harmonized healthy data can more accurately predict what a typical brain pattern should look like at a given age or cognitive level. This provides highly sensitive markers for early detection, risk stratification, and the identification of emerging pathological trajectories.

In summary, focusing on healthy subjects is not a limitation but a necessity: their brains define the reference space, the variability bounds, and the biological patterns against which pathology is recognized. Multiscale neuroimaging pipelines now allow us to create richly informative atlases and normative models that capture the full complexity of the typical human brain.
