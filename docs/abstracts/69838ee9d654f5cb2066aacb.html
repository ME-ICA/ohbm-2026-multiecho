Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a common neurodegenerative disorder, yet many patients experience insufficient tremor control due to medication side effects or waning efficacy. Magnetic resonance-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) thalamotomy targeting the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) / nucleus ventralis oralis posterior (VOp) has emerged as an noninvasive alternative for tremor-dominant PD patients who are refractory to medication or unsuitable for deep brain stimulation (DBS).1 Although MRgFUS achieves robust and durable tremor suppression in Essential Tremor (ET), clinical experience in PD indicates that 30–50% of patients experience tremor relapse within 1–6 months following initially successful treatment.2

Descriptive studies suggest that factors such as younger age and smaller lesion size may influence long-term efficacy, yet a mechanistic understanding of why some patients relapse while others maintain benefit is lacking.2 Although recent work has delineated favorable lesion locations within the thalamic region3, 4, it is increasingly recognized that therapeutic success depends less on a single anatomical target and more on modulating the distributed neural networks underlying tremor.

To date, however, network-level predictors of sustained benefit after MRgFUS in PD remain largely unknown. Such mechanistic insights are critical for refining patient-specific targeting strategies and moving beyond reliance on generalized historical coordinates toward connectome-informed, pathophysiology-based lesion planning.

Methods

Seventeen patients with tremor-dominant PD (mean age 60.1 years; 16 males) underwent unilateral VIM/VOp MRgFUS thalamotomy. Tremor severity (FTM-A/B and UPDRS-III tremor subscores) was assessed at baseline and at 6 months, and improvement was calculated as the relative change from baseline. Patients were classified as responders (>30% tremor reduction) or non-responders (<30%), consistent with established criteria.2

Lesions were manually segmented on 6-month post-treatment in their T1w images. Seed regions for connectivity analyses were derived from responder and non-responder lesion masks using thresholded overlaps of all responder overlaps and all non-responder overlaps (25% voxel agreement) for functional and structural connectivity.

Functional connectivity fingerprints were generated using two independent normative resting-state fMRI datasets of Healthy controls and PD patients: OASIS-3 (n=300) and the PPMI cohort (n=250). Both datasets were preprocessed using standard pipelines implemented in the CONN toolbox (RRID:SCR_009550)5, including motion correction, normalization to MNI space, smoothing, nuisance regression (CompCor, motion parameters, outlier scans), and temporal band-pass filtering. Seed-based connectivity maps were computed using Fisher-transformed correlation coefficients, and group differences were assessed using voxelwise random-effects GLMs with cluster-level FWE correction (p-FWEc <0.05).

Structural connectivity fingerprints were generated using diffusion MRI from the 17 patients and an independent structural connectome from the PPMI cohort (n=85). Preprocessing included susceptibility, eddy current, and motion correction, followed by fiber orientation estimation (BEDPOSTX) and probabilistic tractography. Streamlines intersecting each individual lesion mask were extracted, thresholded at 5% to remove spurious fibers, binarized, and weighted by clinical improvement. “Sweettrack” pathways associated with better outcomes were identified using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests testing for above 30% improvement (p<0.05) and validated with 5000-permutation testing. Only voxels with overlap in at least two patients were included. This method was applied in previous “sweetspot mapping” literature.4

Results

On average, tremor severity on the treated side improved by 39% at 6 months. Responders (n=10) demonstrated a 72% tremor score reduction, whereas non-responders (n=7) improved by only 11%.

Functional connectivity analyses revealed that responder lesions showed stronger connectivity to the precentral and postcentral gyri compared with non-responders (p-FWEc < 0.05). Responders additionally showed reduced connectivity to right cerebellar Crus I/II and lobule VI, and increased connectivity within the right lateral occipital cortex (Figure 1a).

Structural connectivity profiles associated with favorable outcomes overlapped with the previously reported VIM-VOp-Vc border sweetspot4. Effective outcome-related streamlines primarily projected to the primary motor cortex (M1) (Figure 1b).

Conclusion

This study provides first evidence for imaging-based markers that differentiate long-term effective from relapsing tremor outcomes after VIM/VOp MRgFUS thalamotomy in tremor-dominant PD. Structural and functional connectivity analyses converged on a common pattern: responder lesions showed stronger engagement of the precentral and postcentral gyri, consistent with effective modulation of the cerebello-thalamo-cortical motor network reported in previous work1. 

These findings highlight the importance of targeting motor-relevant thalamic circuitry with previously published targets and paves the way for individualized lesion placement guided by connectivity fingerprints using patients’ fMRI in order to improve durability of tremor control.
