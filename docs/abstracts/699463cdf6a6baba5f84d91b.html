Introduction

Our experiences of and responses to cognitive challenges, positive and negative emotions, and social interactions are core determinants and reflections of our mental health. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has become central tool for probing the neurobiology of these functional domains. However, a consistent discrepancy has emerged: fMRI-based brain features tend to show moderately strong associations with cognition but relatively weak associations with non-cognitive domains of mental health1. This observation raises a critical question: are prevailing analytic approaches and metrics failing to capture signal that is there, or does brain activity evoked by prevailing fMRI paradigms carry little non-cognitive mental health signal to begin with? Moreover, recent work has begun to show that subtypes derived from fMRI-versus behavior and self-report-based mental health data are largely misaligned2. The consequences of this asymmetry remain to be fully elucidated. 

Methods

Using the Human Connectome Project–Young Adult sample (HCP-YA; n=1,200), we combined hierarchical factor analysis (hFA) of 87 behavioral and symptom measures with tensor independent component analysis (tICA) of five fMRI paradigms (emotion, gambling, social cognition, working memory, movie-watching) to benchmark and dissect the divergence in cognitive versus non-cognitive mental health signal across task-evoked brain network states. tICA3, an unsupervised learning technique that detects brain activity aligned with task stimulation time courses, quantified subject loadings on task-evoked brain networks (model order = 20). For hFA, we used Schottner and colleagues' five-factor solution4 quantifying subject-level loadings on the following latent mental health domains: cognition, positive affect (well-being), negative affect (internalizing), processing speed, and substance use. Thus, we generated for each subject a brain activation profile (across tasks and networks) and mental health profile (across latent factors). Univariate and multivariate links between brain network activation and cognitive versus non-cognitive mental health were examined across tasks and networks (Fig. 1); reliability and inter-individual variation were compared within and across modalities. Subtyping solutions from brain activation data versus mental health data were compared. Replicability of findings was assessed using split-half samples stratified by family membership to avoid data leakage. 

Results

Only cognition displayed replicable, corrected (p<.0001) univariate links with brain activation across all tasks and brain networks (Fig. 1); multi-network/task random forest regression models only significantly predicted cognition (r=.15, p=.005, MSE=0.919) and processing speed (r=.11, p=.041, MSE=1.184). The cognitive versus non-cognitive signal divergence was not attributable to differences measurement reliability or variability, as non-cognitive mental health factors displayed comparably high test-retest reliability (ICC>0.9) and greater interindividual variability relative to the cognition factor. Instead, we identified a representation space mismatch between fMRI features and mental health features: subjects’ brain activations profiles were significantly less diverse than their mental health profiles (p<.001). Moreover, this representation space mismatch contributed to task fMRI– and mental health–derived subtypes that were not only mutually discordant (k=5: Χ²(16)=23.81, p=.094; k=10: Χ²(16)=76.91, p=.608) but asymmetrically informative, yielding modality-contingent group-level inferences (Fig. 2). For instance, a high cognition/low substance use subtype (Subtype 1) was defined by high emotion task activation and low gambling task activation in the mental health-based solution, but by low emotion task activation and high working memory task activation in the fMRI-based solution. 

Conclusion

Overall, findings indicate that current mainstay fMRI task batteries may not elicit brain activity with robust non-cognitive mental health relevance in normative cohorts. This suggests a fundamental limit on the potential of analytical advances to extract clinically meaningful signal from these data, and supports exploration of alternative fMRI task batteries for future large-scale brain imaging studies that may more effectively evoke interindividual variability in non-cognitive brain activity.
