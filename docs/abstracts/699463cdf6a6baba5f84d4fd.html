Introduction

Parent-child interpersonal interactions play an essential role in children's social, emotional, and cognitive development. These early experiences not only set a foundation for future social engagements but are also pivotal in fostering social adaptability, such as resilience. Resilience refers to the capacity to cope with adversity and maintain psychological well-being, making it a key indicator of healthy development. Previous research has demonstrated that parent-child interactions during the preschool years are significant predictors of children's developmental outcomes, including resilience and executive function. However, much of the existing research has relied on questionnaire-based measures or behavioral coding schemes, which may fail to capture the dynamic and reciprocal nature of these interactions. Recently, hyperscanning using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) has emerged as a promising method for examining the real-time neural coupling between interacting individuals. Building on this advancement, the present study utilizes fNIRS-based hyperscanning to assess parent-child interbrain neural synchrony (INS) and explores whether these in-task neural dynamics are associated with children' s long-term developmental outcomes. Furthermore, while prior work has predominantly emphasized the benefits of heightened parent-child INS, this study introduces parental distress (mental health) as a moderating variable to elucidate its role in shaping the nuanced relationship between interpersonal neural processes and children' s resilience.

Methods

The study involved 64 parent-child dyads (N = 128 individuals; 41 boys, 23 girls; 5 fathers, 59 mothers) and assessed their interbrain neural synchrony (INS) during a cooperative Etch-a-Sketch task, with a focus on hemodynamic activity in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and temporoparietal junction (TPJ) related regions. 

Before the formal task, parents and children were instructed to sit comfortably, keep their heads still to minimize motion artifacts of the fNIRS signal, and close their eyes for 6 minutes to collect their resting-state data. They then participated in a 5-minute online Etch-a-Sketch game (ESO) on pre-provided laptops, either cooperatively or individually, with the order of conditions counterbalanced. In the cooperative condition, participants used a keyboard to draw pictures to match the example image presented onscreen, each controlling separate keys: parents used the 'O' and 'M' keys to control the pen up and down to draw vertical lines, while children used the 'A' and 'D' keys to control the pen side to side to draw horizontal lines. Diagonal lines could be drawn using key combinations, but it was not disclosed to the parent and child. A "clear" button on the screen allowed them to empty their drawing using the mouse. Dyads received explicit instructions to avoid touching each other's keys 1. In the individual condition, each participant drew independently on separate computers, controlling all the keys to complete the task.

Hemodynamic signal changes were measured using a multichannel fNIRS system (LABNIRS, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) with multiple continuous wavelengths (780, 805, and 830 nm) at a sampling rate of 37.037Hz. Each participant's head is fitted with two patches containing 14 laser sources and 13 optical detectors, forming 39 channels covering the prefrontal cortex (PFC, including 22 channels) and right temporoparietal junction related (rTPJ and rSTG, including 17 channels). The distance between each source and detector is set at 3 cm using rigid paper spacers. The spatial position of the optode was established using a 3D magnetic space digitizer Patriot Digitizer (Polhemus Inc., Colchester, Vermont, US). 

The quantification of fNIRS data for changes in oxyhemoglobin (HbO), deoxyhemoglobin (HbR), and total hemoglobin (HbT) concentrations was performed using a built-in implementation of the modified Beer-Lambert Law, as provided by the proprietary LABNIRS system (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan). For data preprocessing, HbO signals from the cooperative and individual conditions were first extracted and baseline-corrected using the mean of the resting-state data. To ensure signal quality, the scalp coupling index (SCI) was computed across the entire signal Following quality control, a polynomial detrending method was applied to remove linear drift from the retained channel data. Additionally, a band-pass filter (0.01 - 0.5 Hz) was used to eliminate high- and low-frequency noise. Finally, the first 30 seconds and the last 60 seconds of each recording were removed due to excessive noise contamination (Fig. 1).

To further reduce the risk of Type I errors and enhance the reliability of the results, channel-wise data were merged into region-wise data.

Parental psychological distress experienced by parents over the past 30 days was measured using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10). Depressive symptoms over the past 2 weeks were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). Generalized anxiety symptoms experienced over the past 2 weeks were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7). Child resilience was measured using the Rasch-Validated Revision of Child and Youth Resilience Measure (CYRM-R) questionnaire, including both the child self-report version and the person most knowledgeable (PMK) version. It comprises 17 items that assess resilience across two subscales: personal resilience (PR) and caregiver/relational resilience (CR).

Results

Contrast analyses revealed several clusters showing greater parent-child INS during the cooperative Etch-a-Sketch task compared to the individual task in the 0.01-0.15Hz frequency band. Based on the contrast analyses, and with reference to previously reported frequency bands of interest, we selected the 0.016-0.04Hz band for the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and the 0.016-0.03Hz band for the temporoparietal junction area (TPJ and STG) for further analyses. Specifically, significantly higher INS during the cooperative task was observed in the right middle frontal gyrus (rMFG, BA46; t(59) = 2.41, p = 0.019, Cohen’s d = 0.31) and the right superior temporal gyrus (rSTG, BA8; t(53) = 2.73, p = 0.008, Cohen’s d = 0.37). Permutation tests further revealed that WTC values in the rMFG were significantly higher for true dyads than for randomized baselines (t(59) = 2.51, p = 0.015, d = 0.32). No significant difference was observed in the rSTG (t(59) = 1.53, p = 0.13, d = 0.19), possibly due to lower signal quality near the TPJ in children.

After controlling for demographic variables (child's gender and age), and family monthly income, parent-child INS in the left MFG (BA46) during cooperative ESO task was negatively correlated with child-reported personal resilience (r = -0.28, p = 0.037) and positively correlated with parent-reported relational resilience subscale (r = 0.31, p = 0.021). However, no significant association emerged between parent-child INS and the total resilience scores, regardless of whether they were reported by the child (p = 0.149) or the parent (p = 0.089). In addition, significant association was observed between parent–child INS in the left FPC (BA10) and child resilience, based on either child (p = 0.719) or parent reports (p = 0.619). Taken together, parent-child INS in the left MFG (BA46) was significantly associated with child resilience, with opposite directions for child- and parent-reported outcomes, whereas no other direct associations were found in regions such as the FPC.

Parental distress (including parent psychological distress, parent depression, and parent anxiety) was considered to play moderate roles between parent-child neural synchrony and child resilience. After controlling for demographic variables (child’s gender and age) and family monthly income, moderation analyses revealed significant interaction effects between parent-child INS in the left frontopolar prefrontal cortex (FPC) and parental distress, including psychological distress, depression, and anxiety (INS × parent psychological distress: β = -0.44, t = -3.63, p < 0.001, 95%CI = [-0.69,-0.20]; INS × parent depression: β = -0.33, t = -2.34, p = 0.023, 95%CI = [-0.61,-0.05]; INS × parent anxiety: β = -0.28, t = -2.20, p = 0.033, 95%CI = [-0.54,-0.02]). To further enhance the robustness of the results, parent psychological distress, depression, and anxiety scores were standardized and add together into a single variable named “parental distress” (M = 0.04, SD = 2.90). After controlling for the same covariates, moderation analyses revealed significant interaction effects between parent-child INS in the left FPC and parental distress (INS × parental distress: β = -0.37, t = -2.80, p < 0.007, 95%CI = [-0.63, -0.10]). Subsequent simple slope analyses the association between parent-child INS in the left FPC and self-reported child resilience was significant when parental distress was lower than -3.27 or higher than 2.29 (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

In the present study, we investigated whether parent-child interbrain neural synchrony (INS) during a cooperative ESO task is associated with child developmental outcomes (i.e., resilience). Moreover, we explored the moderate effects of parental distress on the relationship between INS and child resilience. Consistent with our hypotheses, our findings revealed higher INS in the right middle frontal gyrus (MFG, BA46) and the right superior temporal cortex (STG) during the cooperative ESO task compared to the individual task. Moreover, significant correlations emerged between parent-child INS and long-term developmental outcomes. Specifically, parent-child INS in the left MFG (BA46) was positively related to parent-reported relational resilience but negatively associated with child-reported personal resilience. 

Importantly, previous hyperscanning studies have demonstrated the great potential of parent-child INS in reflecting parent-child real-time communication and cooperation, as well as in predicting long-term developmental outcomes such as emotion regulation 2 and psychopathological risk 3. However, while most studies emphasize the positive effects of parent-child INS, few have addressed its potential negative consequences. Although a small number of studies have reported that certain contextual or parental characteristics may influence INS, or modulate its impact on child outcomes (Su et al., 2024), these effects remain underexplored and are rarely framed in terms of potential risks. This is somewhat counter-intuitive, given that behavioral research has long demonstrated that unhealthy dynamics can also emerge within parent-child relationships and may exert negative effects on children. The present study helps fill this gap by highlighting the double-edged nature of parent-child neural synchrony. Specifically, our findings consistently underscore the moderating role of parental distress between parent-child INS and child resilience, whether reported by children or parents. When parents are in low distress, higher INS with their child during cooperative tasks is associated with better child resilience. In contrast, when parents experience high levels of distress, depression, or anxiety, higher INS is linked to poorer child resilience.

These results point to the specific dynamics that unfold within parent-child interactions. Undoubtedly, interactions between parents and children play a central role in a child's social development. However, research often indicates that not all interactions benefit children. For example, a study on parent-child communication about traumatic events suggests that not all communication has a positive impact. The positive effects of interactions on child well-being often depend on parental traits4, such as parental sensitivity. Another study focusing on the relationship between maternal depression, parent-child relationships, and child resilience more directly points out that a mother's level of depression can interact with the parent-child relationship to jointly predict child resilience outcomes. 

In our study, parent-child INS in the FPC (BA10) appeared to be involved in this association. The FPC is known to support social cognitive processes, particularly those involved in inferring others’ emotions and mental states. In the context of high parental distress, parents may adopt maladaptive communicative strategies, such as excessive emotional monitoring or negative behavioral control, in order to manage or interpret the child' s internal states. At the same time, children may mirror these patterns by accommodating their parents' emotional cues and expectations. This reciprocal dynamic may lead to heightened parent-child INS in the FPC during cooperative tasks. Over time, such interaction patterns may hinder the development of children's adaptive social behaviors and reduce their access to external social support, ultimately undermining their resilience.
