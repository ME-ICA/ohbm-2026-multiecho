Introduction

Childhood trauma is a major public health issue and a strong predictor of long-term psychological difficulties.^1 Yet many trauma-exposed individuals maintain mental health, underscoring the importance of psychological resilience.^2 Resilience is viewed as a dynamic process supported by cognitive-emotional capacities, particularly the ability to regulate stressors rather than merely the absence of pathology.

Memory suppression—the intentional inhibition of unwanted memories—has been proposed as a key resilience-related mechanism.^3 It relies on prefrontal control over hippocampal and amygdala systems and is closely tied to the regulation of intrusive memories. Effective suppression can reduce the emotional impact of distressing recollections, and recent work shows that training this ability improves mental health. Neuroimaging studies implicate a frontal–hippocampal–amygdala network, including rIFG, rMFG, ACC, and parietal control regions.^4, 5

However, it remains unclear whether memory suppression contributes to resilience specifically among individuals with significant childhood trauma. Prior studies mainly examined clinical populations or basic mechanisms in healthy adults. To address this gap, we combined behavioral and neuroimaging approaches. A large-scale survey tested whether suppression mediates the association between childhood trauma and resilience. An fMRI Think/No-Think task then compared trauma-exposed high-resilience (HR), low-resilience (LR), and trauma-naïve control (HC) groups. We hypothesized that: (1) suppression mediates the trauma–resilience link; (2) HR individuals show better suppression than LR individuals; and (3) this advantage is supported by enhanced prefrontal-parietal inhibitory activity and connectivity.

Methods

Participants

Part One involved a large online survey to examine relationships among childhood trauma, resilience, and memory suppression in university students aged 18–35. Two survey waves (March and September 2021) yielded 1,820 and 1,578 valid responses; after exclusions, 2,914 participants remained. Inclusion criteria were: university student aged 18–35, no major neuropsychiatric disorders, informed consent, and valid questionnaire completion. Exclusions included invalid consent, random responding, non-student status, or duplicate entries.

In Part Two, 97 participants were randomly selected from Part One and assigned to three groups: a high-resilience trauma group (HR; n=30; top 27% CD-RISC scores with documented trauma), a low-resilience trauma group (LR; n=31; comparable trauma but bottom 27% resilience), and a trauma-naïve healthy control group (HC; n=30). Additional exclusions included MRI contraindications and structural abnormalities. All participants underwent fMRI during the Think/No-Think (TNT) task. The study was approved by the Peking University Health Science Center Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written consent.

Questionnaires

Demographic information and multiple psychological measures were collected via Questionnaire Star. Childhood trauma was assessed using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ). Resilience was measured using the CD-RISC-10, with high- and low-resilience groups defined using the top/bottom 27% of scores. Thought control ability was assessed using the TCAQ. Additional measures included BDI, BAI, SSRS, PSS, SWLS, CFI, and GSES.

Think/No-Think task

An adapted TNT paradigm was used to assess suppression of aversive memories. Seventy-two neutral–negative picture pairs from CAPS were divided into three lists and assigned to Think, No-Think, baseline, or unprimed conditions (counterbalanced across participants). Stimuli were presented using E-prime 2.0. The task included: (1) Learning phase, where participants learned picture pairs to ≥90% accuracy; (2) TNT phase, in which cues instructed participants to recall (Think) or suppress (No-Think) associated negative images, followed by intrusion reports; (3) Test phase, using a perceptual identification task to quantify suppression effects.

fMRI acquisition and preprocessing

Imaging was performed on a 3T GE EXCITE HD scanner with an 8-channel head coil. T1 images were acquired with standard parameters. Functional images were collected with gradient-echo EPI (TR=2000 ms, TE=30 ms, flip angle=90°, 43 slices, 240 volumes). Preprocessing was performed in SPM12 and DPABI, including slice-timing correction, realignment, co-registration, segmentation, DARTEL normalization, and 4 mm FWHM smoothing. Volumes with excessive motion (>3 mm / >3°) were excluded.

Brain activity analysis

A GLM modeled Think and No-Think events using the canonical HRF, including motion regressors and AR(1) correction. Individual contrast maps (Think vs. No-Think) were entered into random-effects analyses. Whole-brain results were thresholded at voxel-level pFWE < 0.05; ROI analyses used voxel-level p < 0.001 (uncorrected).

Functional connectivity analysis

gPPI analyses were conducted using the CONN toolbox. For each predefined ROI, the model included the seed time series, task regressors, and their interaction terms. Connectivity differences for No-Think vs. Think were captured using Fisher-transformed regression coefficients. Group comparisons used a voxel-level threshold of p < 0.001 (uncorrected) and cluster-level pFDR < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

All variables were standardized before analysis. Mediation models were conducted in R (lavaan, mediation), controlling for age and gender. Significance was assessed with 1,000 bias-corrected bootstrap samples.

Results

Memory suppression fully mediates the association between childhood trauma and resilience

Among 2,914 university students, 1,616 reported no childhood trauma and 1,298 met trauma criteria. The two groups differed significantly across demographic and psychological variables (all p < 0.01), with the Non-Trauma group showing higher resilience (CD-RISC-10) and better self-reported suppression ability (TCAQ). Across the full sample, resilience was negatively associated with trauma severity (r = -0.25, p < 0.01, Fig. a) and positively associated with suppression ability (r = 0.27, p < 0.01, Fig. b). Trauma severity and suppression ability were strongly correlated (r = -0.45, p < 0.01, Fig. c). Mediation analysis showed that memory suppression fully mediated the trauma–resilience relationship (indirect effect = -0.10, p < 0.01; direct effect nonsignificant, Fig. d), indicating that impaired suppression capacity accounts for reduced resilience in individuals with childhood trauma.

Subgroup analyses revealed that suppression partially mediated the trauma–resilience link in the Non-Trauma group, suggesting that even subthreshold adversity may influence resilience via suppression mechanisms. However, in the Trauma group, trauma severity was no longer associated with resilience, eliminating the mediation effect, hinting at a more complex or non-linear trauma–resilience relationship.

Enhanced memory suppression in the high-resilience group

Ninety-one fMRI participants were selected into high-resilience trauma (HR), low-resilience trauma (LR), and trauma-naïve control (HC) groups. Psychological measures differed significantly across groups (all p < 0.05). HR individuals resembled HC participants and outperformed LR individuals on most measures, validating group stratification.

During the Think/No-Think task, suppression success increased across repetitions (F = 47.67, p < 0.01), with a significant group × repetition interaction (F = 2.03, p = 0.01). The HR group demonstrated higher overall suppression rates than the LR group (p = 0.01), while both trauma-exposed groups were comparable to HC. No group differences were found in implicit memory accessibility, indicating that HR’s advantage was specific to intentional suppression.

Left IPS activation underlies successful suppression and resilience

Whole-brain analysis (No-Think > Think) revealed increased activation in pre-SMA, MFG, insula, and left intraparietal sulcus (IPS), and decreased activation in visual regions (pFWE < 0.05, Fig. e). Within trauma-exposed individuals, the HR group showed significantly greater left IPS activation during suppression compared with the LR group (pFWE < 0.05, Fig. f). Across trauma-exposed participants, left IPS activation correlated positively with suppression success (r = 0.27, p < 0.05, Fig. g). Mediation analysis showed that IPS activation significantly mediated the link between suppression performance and resilience (31.2% of total effect, p = 0.025, Fig. h), suggesting that enhanced IPS function supports resilience in trauma-exposed individuals.

Functional connectivity of the left IPS supports memory suppression

gPPI analysis using the left IPS as seed revealed increased connectivity with anterior cingulate and occipital fusiform regions (pFDR < 0.01), and decreased connectivity with the frontal pole, middle temporal gyrus, and posterior cingulate cortex during No-Think vs Think (pFDR < 0.05, Fig. i). This pattern reflects adaptive modulation of control and memory-related systems during suppression. No group differences in connectivity emerged.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that enhanced inhibitory control capacity, particularly through memory suppression mechanisms, serves as a critical protective factor for psychological resilience in individuals with childhood trauma. The left IPS emerges as a central neural hub coordinating this adaptive process, with its activation patterns and connectivity dynamics providing a neurobiological basis for successful trauma adaptation. These insights advance our understanding of resilience mechanisms and highlight potential targets for evidence-based interventions aimed at promoting mental health in trauma-exposed populations.
