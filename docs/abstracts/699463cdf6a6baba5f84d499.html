Introduction

Introduction: One of the primary goals of human brain mapping studies is understanding the functional and causal mechanisms of brain network dynamics. Recently, both experimental and computational studies have suggested that maintaining the appropriate balance between synaptic excitation and inhibition (E/I balance) is crucial for sustaining the normality and health of brain network dynamics. Disruption of E/I balance can result in network dysfunction in the brain (e.g., hyper/hypo synchrony, hyper/hypo excitation), and as a result, it can lead to cognitive and mental dysfunction, including neurodevelopmental diseases [1]. Therefore, clarifying the functional relationship between excitation-inhibition (E/I) balance and brain network dynamics is essential for understanding neural mechanisms in the human brain. Even though cutting-edge measurement techniques for neural activity have been developed, it remains challenging to understand the relationship between E/I balance changes and brain network dynamics and their underlying mechanisms based only on observed neural data. This is due to the inherent limitations of measuring E/I balance in the intact human brain. To address this, we recently proposed a data assimilation (DA) method to estimate both network structure and E/I balance using only observed multivariate electroencephalographic (EEG) signals (Fig. 1A) [2,3]. In this proposed method, the observed multivariate EEG signals are directly fitted to the network neural mass model that contains parameters regarding brain network connectivity and activation gain for each excitatory and inhibitory neuron population in the brain. This approach allows us not only to estimate the network structure but also the intercortical E/I state based on the estimated E/I gain parameters, in a data-driven manner. The numerical validity of this method has been confirmed; however, its neurophysiological validity remains insufficiently evaluated (Fig.1A).

Methods

Methods: To address this issue, we applied the proposed method to an open dataset of resting-state EEG (rsEEG) that was recorded before and after theta-burst stimulation (TBS) using transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS). The dataset included both pre- and post-rsEEG data for two TMS protocols: intermittent TBS (iTBS) and continuous TBS (cTBS). iTBS and cTBS are established approaches for non-invasively modulating cortical excitability and inhibitory effects, respectively. We utilized the EEG-DA method to compare estimates of E/I balance and network connectivity in pre- and post-resting state EEGs for each TBS protocol. This evaluation serves as a benchmark test of the neurophysiological validity of the DA method by assessing its ability to detect after-effects of cortical excitability induced by TBS. To quantify the intercortical E/I balance reflected in the observed EEG, we calculated the ratio of estimated E/I gain parameters in the NNM model, referred to as the model-based E/I ratio (mE/I ratio). A higher value indicates an excitatory-dominant state in the brain, while a lower value indicates an inhibitory-dominant state. In this evaluation, we expected that the estimated mE/I ratio obtained using our DA method would increase or decrease according to the type of TBS protocol, iTBS or cTBS, respectively.

Results

Results: Our results showed that the after-effects of the E/I state estimated by the DA method in three healthy participants aligned with the expected intercortical E/I effects depending on the type of TBS protocol (Fig. 1B). Moreover, we also estimated the network propagation of the TBS effect (Fig.1C). In this study, we evaluated after-stimulation effects on the mE/I ratio and network structures by applying our EEG-DA method to pre- and post-resting-state EEG for TBS stimulation. Fig. 1B and C showed the estimation results of the changes in the mE/I ratio and network pathway between pre- and post-stimulation obtained from three healthy participants. In the results in mE/I ratio estimations, the averaged value of mE/I ratio was decreased in cTBS condition, that in iTBS condition was increased (Fig. 1B). However, as shown in Fig.1C, we found almost no differences in after-stimulation effects in network pathways from stimulation point (F3) to other brain regions depending on TBS protocol. In summary, while we found the cortical E/I changes aligned with the expected effects depending on the type of TBS protocol, the routes of network propagation for the stimulation effect in the brain did not change depending on the TBS type. This indicates the possibility that our EEG-DA method can correctly detect stimulation-dependent changes in cortical E/I balance while robustly detecting brain network structures independent of the stimulation protocol.

Conclusion

Conclusions: These results are preliminary. Therefore, we should discuss the group-averaged results with a larger number of participants to conclude the generalization of the current results. However, the current result suggests the potential capability of our DA method for estimating E/I changes and brain network dynamics in parallel using only observed EEG signals.
