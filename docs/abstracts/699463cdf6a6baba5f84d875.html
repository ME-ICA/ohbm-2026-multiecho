Introduction

Individual characterisation of subjects based on their functional connectome (FC), also termed brain fingerprinting [2], has become a central topic in contemporary neuroscience research. While numerous studies focus on “adult” brain identifiability in both healthy [1] and clinical populations [3] using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), little is known about the existence and emergence of brain fingerprints in newborns across birth gestational age groups. In this study, we first used resting-state fMRI data of 464 subjects from the developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) to examine whether it is possible to identify neonatal subjects based on their FC profiles in both preterm and full-term infants. 

Following the analysis using the dHCP data, we then used a similar independent dataset to validate our pipeline and results. This includes resting-state fMRI data of 85 neonatal subjects, including both preterm and full-term infants [4].

Methods

We first used resting-state fMRI data of 464 newborns from the dHCP and analysed the static and dynamic functional fingerprints in four different birth gestational age (GA) groups. These are: extremely preterm (EP, <28 weeks GA), very preterm (VP, between 28 and 32 weeks GA), moderate preterm (MP, between 32- and 37-weeks GA), and full term (FT,  37 weeks GA).

 All subjects underwent MRI scanning at the Evelina Newborn Imaging Centre, St Thomas’ Hospital, London, UK. Structural, diffusion, and functional data were acquired using a 3 Tesla Philips Achieva system (Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) with a customized neonatal imaging system, including a 32-channel phased-array head coil (Rapid Biomedical, Rimpar, Germany).A total of 300 diffusion MRI volumes were acquired over 19 min and 20 s with b-values of 400 s/mm 2, 1000 s/mm2 and 2600 s/mm2 spherically distributed in 64, 88 and 128 directions respectively, with 20 b = 0 s/mm2 images. After reconstruction, image resolution was 1.5 mm isotropic.

For the static approach, we computed within-scan and across-scans FC comparisons to obtain an n × n non-symmetric matrix, the identifiability matrix A [1, 2], encoding the correlations between the subjects’ FCs (Figure 1). Here, ‘Iself’ is the average of the diagonal of A, containing the Pearson correlation values between FC of the same subjects (self-identifiability); ‘Iothers’ refers to the average correlation between FC of different subjects. ‘Idiff’ is defined as the percentage difference between Iself and Iothers, which quantifies the individual differentiability of the cohort.

For the dynamic approach, we present a connectome identification for one subject [5] in Figure 1. For the within-scan approach, once the exploration time scale (window length) is set, we compute dynamic FCs (dFCs) for all windows in both the test and retest fMRI datasets.  We explored the temporal characteristics of these fingerprints across different time scales by considering six window lengths,7.2, 30, 72, 144, 288, 360 seconds) with a fixed sliding window step of 7.2 seconds. Finally, we computed a matrix encoding the all-vs-all comparisons, which we denote as dynamic identifiability matrix. The dynamic differential identifiability dIdiff generalises the concept of Idiff to the case of a block diagonal matrix, where each block represents the self-similarity within the dFC frames of a specific subject. The off-diagonal blocks then encode between-dFC frames similarity across subjects (dynamic Iothers) instead.

To test the robustness and generalisability of our fingerprinting framework and the results produced by the analysis of the dHCP data we applied identical processing steps, static and dynamic FC estimation and identifiability quantification to the independent data set [4]. Exploring a second independent neonatal fMRI dataset allows us to evaluate whether patterns observed in the dHCP cohort are replicated in an external population and whether our pipeline is stable across datasets.

Results

After completing the first analysis, we found that within-scan brain fingerprinting [1] is very reliable in all birth gestational age groups. Additionally, extremely and very preterm newborns are on average more identifiable (approx 42% Idiff) compared to moderate preterm babies or full-term newborns (approx 35% Idiff). By considering longitudinal scans for a subset of 46 subjects in the neonatal period, we show that similarity between functional connectomes of the same subject at different time points is extremely low (approx 0.5% Idiff). This represents the fast-changing of the developing functional connectome during a critical time of brain growth and may also reflect the rapid acquisition of new skills to support adaptation to the extra-uterine environment, as hypothesised in a previous study.

Furthermore, by analysing dynamic fingerprinting at six different window lengths (i.e., 7.2, 36, 72, 144, 288, and 360 s), we found that very preterm newborns are the most identifiable age group. Interestingly, despite the best identification emerging at longer time scales, even at faster time scales (72 s), very few frames suffice to identify an individual from the others.

Finally, we further explored edgewise fingerprinting properties at a localised level through edgewise intraclass correlation coefficient matrices. We found that in all preterm age groups the activation is mainly concentrated within and between sensorimotor areas. In contrast, full-term newborns exhibited pronounced activation within and between higher-order regions, including the frontoparietal. This pattern aligns with models of neurodevelopment proposing an early maturation of sensory systems followed by the progressive emergence and integration of higher-order associative networks.

Conclusion

Overall, our approach which combines static and dynamic identifiability, demonstrates that within-scan identification in newborns is reliable across two independent datasets, particularly at longer window lengths. Among the groups, the very preterm newborns were the most identifiable ones. Additionally, through edgewise fingerprinting analysis, preterm infants showed that activation is mainly concentrated within and between sensory-motor areas, whereas full-term newborns exhibit pronounced activation within and between higher-order regions such as the frontoparietal and default mode networks. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that brain development follows sequence, from early maturing sensory systems to higher-order associative networks.
