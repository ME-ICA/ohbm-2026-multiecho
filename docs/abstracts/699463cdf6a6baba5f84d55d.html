Introduction

Coma is a critical medical condition that frequently leads to long-term cognitive and behavioral impairments, yet the structural and functional disruptions underlying these outcomes remain incompletely characterized. Resting-state functional connectivity (FC) has emerged as a useful tool for probing large-scale network alterations in coma, and graph-theoretical measures such as the Hub Disruption Index have revealed substantial reorganization of network architecture.1 However, traditional graph-theoretical approaches introduce conceptual and practical constraints. FC edges are typically derived from correlations between regional mean signals, discarding voxel-level variability, and sparse graph construction depends on thresholds used to identify statistically significant connections, which may vary across studies and can prevent inclusion of patients with severely disrupted connectivity.1 Moreover, correlations are defined using anatomically or functionally prescribed regions, without accommodating subject-specific variability. These limitations point to the need for analytic strategies that can (i) retain voxel-level information, (ii) use principled statistical criteria to define connectivity rather than arbitrary thresholds, and (iii) generate networks even in patients with severely reduced connectivity. Conceptually, these features align with approaches that reduce or eliminate reliance on fixed parcellations. To address these challenges, we developed a voxel-informed framework with probabilistic, variable graph topology that models inter-regional connectivity, enabling construction of subject-level networks with finer granularity than is achievable through regional averaging and threshold-based graph construction.

Methods

Twenty-five patients with coma (14 traumatic, 11 anoxic; 9 females) admitted to the University Hospital of Toulouse were recruited 2–30 days post-injury. Structural MRI and resting-state fMRI were acquired with minimal inter-scan delay. Twenty age- and sex-matched healthy controls were included as described by Sarton et al.² Resting-state fMRI data were acquired over ~11 minutes. Preprocessing followed Malagurski et al.¹ with minimal deformation to preserve voxel-wise structure. Residual voxel time series were decomposed into four scales using the maximal overlap discrete wavelet transform; analyses focused on scale 3 (0.048–0.096 Hz). We introduced a novel FC pipeline to capture subject-specific, voxel-level inter-regional interactions. (1) Within each region, voxels were clustered using hierarchical clustering with automatic selection of cluster number.³ (2) Cluster time series were obtained by averaging voxel signals.³ (3) For each region pair, all cluster-to-cluster correlations were computed, yielding a distribution of inter-regional correlations. A quantile-based threshold derived from surrogate data at α = 0.05 provided the fraction of correlations considered true positives.³ (4) These fractions defined a subject-specific 89×89 probabilistic adjacency matrix, where each edge is modeled as a Bernoulli trial⁴ with probability equal to the fraction of significant correlations. In this framework, graphs no longer have a fixed edge structure, but are represented as edges with a probability of existence, resulting in networks whose topology varies across realizations. The pipeline is illustrated in Figure 1.

To characterize the probabilistic structure of these networks, we extended the node-equivalence framework of Carboni et al.⁵ For each node, degree distributions were obtained by repeatedly simulating the presence of adjacent edges. Using bounded statistical distances (Total Variance, Hellinger, Jensen–Shannon) to compare degree distributions, nodes were assigned to equivalence classes with threshold τ, defined as the smallest rounded distance ensuring all nodes in a class are mutually within that distance.  Two nodes u and v are structurally equivalent under relation R if the distance between their degree distributions, rounded to x digits, does not exceed the class-specific threshold τ, and this threshold itself is within the global tolerance ϵ. 

This relation was implemented via a union–find algorithm applied to the pairwise distance matrix of node degree distributions. The resulting classes allowed us to compute the Correspondence Structural Pattern Score⁵ (CSPS) to compare the structure of pairs of graphs with varying topologies; values closer to 1 indicate greater similarity. One hundred realizations of each subject’s probabilistic graph were generated, and node degree distributions were compared using statistical distances to define equivalence classes (x = 2, ϵ = 0.19). CSPS distributions were computed within groups, and group differences were assessed using Z-tests.

MRI preprocessing was performed with in-house scripts using SPM12 (MATLAB). GM-weighting, regression, wavelet decomposition, and probabilistic-graph construction were implemented in R. Equivalence-class computation, CSPS calculation, and statistical testing were performed in Python.

Results

In the equivalence-class framework, node labels play a critical role: when a set of nodes is equivalent within one population but not within another, the resulting class structure changes accordingly. The method is therefore inherently sensitive to node labeling. Comparing CSPS distributions of degree-based equivalence classes between healthy controls and comatose patients revealed significant group differences across all distance metrics. Mean CSPS values differed for Total Variation (p=6.43×10-5), Jensen–Shannon (p=1.25×10-7), and Hellinger (p=2.3×10-16). CSPS distributions of probabilistic node degree by group are shown in Figure 2. These findings indicate that, within the probabilistic-graph framework and across distinct distance definitions, the structure of equivalence classes differs reliably between groups, indicating systematic alterations in the organization of FC networks in patients with coma.

Conclusion

We developed a new methodological framework for studying brain functional connectivity with graph models that overcomes the limitations described by Malagurski et al.1, enabling network construction even for subjects with severely disrupted functional connectomes. From a methodological standpoint, the framework advances FC modeling by retaining subject-specific, voxel-level information and leveraging it with statistical tools to construct whole-brain graphs with varying topologies. It further removes the ambiguity introduced by fixed graph-density thresholds by replacing them with statistically principled inference based on null models tailored to each pair of regions. Conceptually, it introduces a more realistic representation of network topology by modeling edges as probabilistic rather than fixed, and tracking their structure through statistical distances and equivalence classes. Our results show that probabilistic graph structure is consistently different in the comatose population compared to healthy controls, indicating the statistical robustness of the method. A limitation of this study is the reproducibility of these results across different parcellation schemes; future work should assess reliability under varying regional definitions. Because this framework demonstrates that whole-brain FC graphs can be derived directly from voxel-level information, it suggests a path toward future methods that do not rely on predefined parcellations and can instead operate on finer-grained or data-driven representations.
