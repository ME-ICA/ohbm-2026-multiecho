Introduction

Ketamine, an N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) antagonist, has long been used as an anesthetic and analgesic, and more recently at sub-anesthetic doses as a fast-acting treatment for treatment-resistant depression. Ketamine at low doses, has antidepressant and anxiolytic effects accompanied by reduced alpha-band power and increased pyramidal cell firing. At higher doses, ketamine induces anesthesia characterized by EEG slowing and reduced pyramidal firing rate.1,2 These distinct dose-dependent effects are associated with an antagonism of NMDAR expressed by various excitatory and inhibitory neuron types. 

At the cellular level, ketamine blocks NMDARs resulting in reduced excitatory neurotransmission at high doses. Paradoxically, low doses can increase pyramidal cell firing despite ketamine‘s excitatory receptor blockade. The low-dose effect is hypothesized to result from a preferential binding to NMDARs located on GABAergic interneurons impairing excito-inhibitory neurotransmission. This binding in turn leads to the disinhibition of pyramidal cells and therefore increased excitatory neurotransmission.3 Despite ketamine‘s clinical importance, its pharmacodynamics lack a unified computational framework that explains how ketamine’s receptor-level antagonism produces the diverse dose-dependent effects on brain dynamics. 

Methods

To disentangle these dose-dependent effects we employ a brain network model within The Virtual Brain (TVB) framework integrating human structural connectome data with a neural mass model with biologically interpretable parameters.4 Each brain region is represented as an idealized cortical column comprising two excitatory and one inhibitory subpopulation that are locally coupled. Ketamine’s mechanism of action is implemented by scaling the local coupling between the regional subpopulations. We first examine a uniform NMDAR antagonism across receptor sites by scaling all local coupling parameters linearly by the same factor according to the ketamine dose. We then implement a selective site-dependent antagonism in which specific local coupling parameters are modulated through sigmoidal transformations reflecting distinct NMDAR site affinities. The selective antagonism is modeled such that NMDARs on inhibitory interneurons are more affected at low doses, transitioning to greater impact on pyramidal cell receptors at higher doses.

Results

Only this site-dependent antagonism, but not the uniform one reproduces results in line with empirical changes. In this site-dependent antagonism, for low ketamine values, the pyramidal firing rate initially increases and the alpha-band power decreases. For larger ketamine values the firing rate and alpha-band power decreases while the theta-band power increases. We find that even though ketamine blocks an excitatory receptor the excitation-inhibition (E/I) ratio increases at low doses due to the site-dependent antagonism, ultimately leading to an initial increases in pyramidal firing, in line with experimental findings.1 When upping the simulated ketamine dosage further, a decrease of the E/I ratio and decreased firing rate follows. Our modeling results suggest that low-dose ketamine preferentially impairs excito-inhibitory neurotransmission while higher doses increasingly affect excito-excitatory coupling. In-depth bifurcation analyses reveal that these changes correspond to the emergence and disappearance of limit cycles in the neural mass model driven by alterations in local coupling. We find that the limit cycle that is responsible for the alpha oscillations of the system progressively diminishes by increasing ketamine leading to the decrease in alpha-band power.

Conclusion

Our model helps bridge the gap between ketamine's micro-scale mechanisms and its meso- to large-scale effects. To the best of our knowledge, our here-presented work is the first study to capture ketamine’s full-dose spectrum within one modeling framework. Overall, our results support that low-dose ketamine preferentially disrupts excito-inhibitory signaling by blocking NMDARs on interneurons, whereas anesthetic doses produce broader antagonism affecting excito-excitatory pathways as well. This whole-brain modeling framework offers a mechanistic account of ketamine’s dose-dependent neurophysiological signatures and provides a generalizable approach for investigating NMDAR-related pharmacodynamics.
