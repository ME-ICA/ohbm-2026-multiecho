Introduction


MR spectroscopic imaging (MRSI) provides localized metabolic information with high potential for clinical applications in tumors and neurodegenerative diseases. However, clinical adoption is hindered by the complexity of reconstructing high-resolution MRSI data, which typically requires exporting large raw files  to powerful external workstations and  offline processing using custom software. This requires substantial local development effort, complicating the distribution of the sequence and reconstruction, and hinders cross-site comparability.

To overcome this, we developed an automated online implementation that produces spectra and metabolic maps within minutes at the scanner console. We utilize the concentric ring trajectory (CRT)¹ with a fast online reconstruction in the Siemens ICE framework to obtain spectra, followed by (1) lipid and water removal (WALINET)² and (2) spectral fitting (a modified PHIVE network³) in the Siemens FIRE framework.

To demonstrate the robustness and reproducibility of our automated solution, we performed a multi-center validation study involving 20 volunteers across four international sites, comparing the online results directly to a standard offline reconstruction pipeline.

Methods

Study Design and Participants 
Five healthy volunteers were scanned at each of four international sites: Brisbane, London, Vienna, and UCSF, resulting in a total of 20 datasets. All participants provided written informed consent, and the procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Measurements were performed on Siemens 7 T Magnetom DotPlus (Vienna, Brisbane, UCSF) and 7 T Terra (London) systems.

Data Acquisition
A 3D CRT sequence was used with the following parameters: TR = 420 ms, TE = 1.3 ms, FoV = 220 × 220 × 120 mm³, voxel size = 3.4 × 3.4 × 3.4 mm³, and spectral bandwidth = 2778 Hz. The total scan time was 12 min 04 s.

Reconstruction Pipeline
The reconstruction pipeline integrated conventional image processing within the Siemens ICE framework and two neural networks within the FIRE framework (Figure 1).

1. Classical reconstruction (ICE): Preprocessing included coil compression, noise decorrelation, frequency drift and gradient delay correction, chemical-shift phase correction, k-space density correction (Hamming filter), and spatial Fourier transforms. This resulted in spectra that can be viewed on the console.

2. Deep Learning Methods (FIRE): The spectra were passed to Python-based reconstruction for B0 correction, lipid/water removal, spectral fitting and masking.

  • WALINET: A convolutional neural network used for nuisance signal removal (lipid and water). It was trained on data from 4 (of 5) volunteers at each site Brisbane, London, and Vienna (3×4=12 subjects total)

  • PHIVE: A physics-informed variational autoencoder used for spectral fitting, retrained using the remaining fifth volunteer from each of these sites.

  • Testing: All UCSF volunteers served as an independent test set unseen during training

The reconstruction produced full spectroscopic datasets, WALINET-processed spectra, iMUSICAL localiser images⁴, metabolite and Cramér–Rao lower bound (CRLB) maps, and their corresponding WALINET-processed versions—all exportable as DICOMs (Figure 1, bottom half).

Validation
Results were compared against an established offline BASH/MATLAB reconstruction pipeline using exported raw data (~40GB), which utilized LCModel-based fitting and L2-based lipid regularization.

Figure 1: CRT Sequence and reconstruction, including the online reconstruction workflow in ICE (grey) and FIRE (red). The lower half shows the output images opened at the Siemens scanner console. On the left are the metabolic maps and CRLBs, on the right side are the opened unprocessed (raw) and WALINET spectra for a selected voxel.

Results

Metabolite maps obtained from the online reconstruction were visually comparable to the offline L2-regularized reconstruction. Figure 2 (see attached image selection) demonstrates Glx maps for all 20 volunteers, including the unseen UCSF test data. Maps exhibited clear gray-white matter contrast across all sites. In specific cases (e.g., Vol 2U), the online WALINET processing effectively suppressed strong lipid artifacts that were present in the raw data. Further results (not shown) suggested a superior performance of the WALINET lipid suppression compared to offline L2 regularization, since it avoided artefacts present after L2 regularization.

The online reconstruction completed approximately 4 minutes 30 seconds after scan completion, while the offline pipeline required approximately 9 hours 42 minutes on a 40-core server (Intel Xeon Platinum 8280 @ 2.7 GHz, 503 GB RAM), mostly attributed to LCModel processing. In addition, data storage requirements are greatly reduced, as the processed results occupy only ~2.2 GiB compared to ~40 GiB for the raw data.
Average LCModel SNRs (averaged first over all brain voxels, then volunteers) for the offline reconstruction were 7.71±0.91, 6.78±1.04, 7.18±0.42, 7.24±1.18 for Brisbane, London, Vienna, and UCSF, respectively. Corresponding FWHMs were 14.7±1.7 Hz, 16.3±0.6 Hz, 15.9±2.6 Hz, 16.1±1.7 Hz.

Figure 2: Glx concentration maps from all four sites and all 20 volunteers. The UCSF datasets, used exclusively as independent test data, are outlined in green. Different volunteers were scanned at the different sites and are labeled accordingly (e.g., “Vol 1B,” “Vol 1L,” “Vol 1V,” and “Vol 1U” for Brisbane, London, Vienna, and UCSF, respectively). All Glx maps exhibit clear gray–white matter contrast and overall high quality, except for Vol 5L, Vol 3U, and Vol 4U, which show increased noise levels.

Conclusion

The proposed online reconstruction framework enables rapid, automated 3D CRT-MRSI reconstruction at 7 T. By integrating deep learning-based artifact removal (WALINET) and spectral quantification (PHIVE) directly on the scanner, we achieved results comparable to computationally expensive offline methods while reducing processing time from hours to minutes.

This new implementation allows for immediate quality control and re-measurement if necessary, addressing a major pain point in current procedures. In addition, the substantial reduction in data size and the standardization of the reconstruction pipeline makes multi-center studies feasible. 

The complete sequence and reconstruction framework is available through the Siemens teamplay platform and will be continuously updated with improved versions.
