Introduction

In decision making, sensory information and reward processing interact to shape learning with primary sensory and frontal cortices implicated in each, respectively. The Reward Positivity (RewP) is a sensitive EEG marker of reward,1 typically measured at frontocentral electrodes that has also been measured in MEG.2 The RewP is likely generated by a distributed cortical network whose individual nodes contribute heterogenous information.2 Given the broad set of contributors to this signal, using a univariate approach such as ERP/ERF analysis may not capture the full scope of this signal. Additionally, the RewP has been proposed as a potential biomarker of Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), though many of the studies exploring this idea rely on sample averages of the RewP across many participants. Multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) has shown increased sensitivity to neural signals and allowed for within-subject successful classification of perceptual stimuli based on evoked neural activity.3 Here we tested whether: 1) MVPA of MEG data can be used to temporally decode rewarding and non-rewarding feedback, 2) this representation is independent of the sensory properties of the reward cue, and 3) decoding is reliable at the level of individual participants. MVPA also has the potential to pick up on a wider breadth of variance in this reward-associated signal, allowing for exploration of the temporal dynamics of both perceptual and cognitive attributes of feedback. To further probe these dynamics, we used two reinforcement learning tasks, one with visual indicators of feedback and one with auditory indicators. This allows us to differentially decode the cognitive content of feedback from the sensory properties in two sensory modalities. Using these tasks, we are able to explore both unique variance within a sensory modality, as well as shared variance between modalities.

Methods

Task Description

Participants completed a reinforcement learning task with either visual or auditory indicators of feedback, with some participants completing both tasks. Task order was counterbalanced, with half of the participants completing the visual task first, and the other half completing the auditory task first. Each task required participants to learn, through trial and error, which of two buttons to press in response to four visually presented shapes. The four shapes for a given participant were randomly selected from twelve different shapes. For participants that completed both tasks, different shapes were used in each task. Each cue was probabilistically rewarding, with 70% of trials giving a reward for one button, and 30% of trials rewarding the other button. 

            Participants were shown a fixation point then presented with one of four shapes. The shape remained on the screen until the participant pressed either the left or right button. After a response was made, the participant received an indicator of reward or non-reward. In the visual task the screen turned either yellow or blue to indicate whether a participant earned a reward, while the auditory task used tones of 220 Hz and 440 Hz. The color/pitch-mapping of reward and non-reward alternated every run, and participants were informed of the current feedback-mapping at the start of each run. The more rewarding button remained constant for each shape across runs. 

            Each participant completed 2 training runs, one for each feedback-mapping consisting of 40 trials each. Participants then completed 8 test runs of the task, each consisting of 80 trials for 640 total trials. Each reward earned during the test runs represented $0.05 they would be paid at the end of the task, up to a maximum of $20.  

MEG Data Acquisition and Analysis

            MEG data were recorded at using a CTF 275 MEG composed of a whole-head array of 275 radial 1st order gradiometers. Synthetic 3rd gradient balancing was used to remove background noise on-line. Head localization was done at the beginning and end of each run. Brainsight was used for online coregistration of the HPI coils to the participant’s T1.

            MEG data were analyzed using MNE-Python. Each run was preprocessed separately. Basic preprocessing included filtering, downsampling, and ICA to remove artifacts. Data were then epoched around presentation of the cue. Epoched data were then baseline corrected using a pre-cue baseline then timeshifted to create new epochs around presentation of feedback. 

             At each timepoint, a logistic regression model was trained, then tested on held out data. Each model was cross-validated using a stratified 5-fold split. Within each feedback modality, MVPA was done for three models: perceptual feature (i.e. color/pitch), feedback, and feedback cross-decoded across perceptual feature. Additionally, the feedback from both tasks were combined into three additional models: task, feedback, and feedback cross-decoded across task. 

            Source estimation was done using dynamic statistical parametric mapping (dSPM). For each participant, source activity was estimated for each epoch. From these source estimates, we extracted time courses of activation for each vertex of each parcel of the HCP-MMP atlas (Glasser et al., 2016), yielding a matrix of epochs x vertex x time for each parcel. We then performed a searchlight MVPA by using each vertex within a parcel as a virtual sensor to compute accuracy for each parcel at each timepoint. The MVPA pipeline was identical to that used in sensor space. 

            A Bayesian t-test was used to calculate Bayes Factors (BF) for each timepoint in both sensor and source space.3 BFs reflect the likelihood that the data would be observed under the alternative hypothesis, compared to the null hypothesis. Here we used a threshold of BF=3, to report findings where above chance decoding accuracy is 3 times more likely than under the null hypothesis. 

Results

Perceptual Feature Decoding Model

            The first model of interest was a sanity check for perceptual feature.  In the visual task, the whole-head sensor-level color model showed a likely cluster of timepoints from ~70-400 ms. The pitch model in the auditory task showed a similar cluster of timepoints from ~100-350 ms.  The visual task source space searchlight showed accurate decoding of color within early visual areas (V1-V4; EVC) from ~70-400ms.  Color decoding was minimal in value-associated areas such as ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), with no clear temporal cluster.  Similarly, the auditory source space searchlight showed accurate decoding of pitch within auditory from ~100-350ms.  Pitch decoding was minimal in VMPFC with no clear temporal cluster. Unexpectedly, Auditory cortex showed accurate decoding of color (~100-220ms) in the visual task, while only V1 showed a small window of accurate pitch decoding (~190-210ms).   

Feedback Decoding Model

The visual sensor-level feedback model showed a likely cluster of timepoints from ~200-600ms.  The auditory feedback model showed a slightly earlier cluster of timepoints from ~180-500ms.   The visual source space searchlight showed accurate decoding of feedback in VMPFC, with a cluster appearing from ~200-650ms. EVC also showed accurate decoding from ~225-350ms.  The auditory source space searchlight showed accurate decoding of feedback in VmPFC from ~300-500ms. Auditory cortex showed earlier decoding of feedback from ~180-250ms, as well as decoding accuracy from ~300-500ms. Again, Auditory cortex showed accurate decoding of feedback in the visual task (200-350ms and 480-570ms) while EVC showed accurate decoding of feedback in the auditory task (300-425ms).

Feedback Cross-Decoding Model

            The visual sensor-level feedback cross-decoding model showed a late cluster of accurate feedback decoding (~450-600ms), despite the training and testing sets representing a flip in the sensory-cognitive mapping (i.e. the model is trained that blue is reward and tested on data where blue is non-reward).  The same is true of the auditory feedback cross-decoding model with accurate feedback decoding from ~250-500ms.  The visual source space searchlight showed accurate decoding in VMPFC from ~450-650ms. EVC, however showed minimal feedback cross-decoding accuracy.  The auditory source space searchlight showed accurate decoding in VMPFC from ~350-450ms. Unlike EVC in the visual task, Auditory cortex showed a small amount of feedback cross-decoding accuracy (450-480). 

Cross-Task Decoding

            The sensor-level feedback model including both tasks showed accurate decoding from ~200-600ms.  The source space searchlight revealed accurate decoding of feedback in VmPFC from ~200-325ms and ~400-750ms, peaking at 450ms.  EVC also decoded feedback from ~225-450ms, peaking at 400ms.  Auditory cortex accurately decoded feedback from ~200-375ms, peaking at 350ms.  

The sensor-level cross-decoding model (i.e. a model trained on visual or auditory feedback and tested on the other modality) showed a small cluster of accurate decoding from ~375-425ms. The source space searchlight revealed accurate cross-decoding in VmPFC from ~375-425ms, while V1 showed accurate decoding from ~375-400ms, and A1 could decode from ~275-325ms. 

Conclusion

In this study we report the novel use of a technique typically used to classify sensory or perceptual attributes to instead classify reward receipt, laying the groundwork for further use of MVPA in understanding cognition.  The findings here suggest that we can differentially decode both the perceptual features of feedback indicators and the cognitive content in a complex learning task. In two reinforcement learning tasks with visual or auditory indicators of feedback, these elements seem to be temporally staggered, with perceptual features decoding earlier than cognitive, though they share some temporal overlap as evidenced by the within-task cross-decoding models. Cross-decoding across tasks showed that this representation was independent of sensory modality, as we were able to decode visual indicators of feedback using a model trained on auditory indicators and vice versa. This decoding ability was present not only at the group level, but in each participant as well, though peak decoding accuracy and latency varied across individuals.

The source space decoding further revealed the temporal dynamics of feedback processing with primary sensory areas showing earlier preferential decoding of their respective sensory modality and value-associated frontal cortex showing later preferential decoding of feedback. Simultaneous processing of perceptual features and feedback in differing brain areas would explain the time windows during which whole-head sensor-level MVPA was unable to cross-decode.

Interestingly, primary sensory areas also showed an ability to decode feedback and, to a smaller extent, perceptual features in the task of the other modality.  This could be explained by any number of phenomena associated with reward and task-design, such as arousal.4 This may also support the growing body of work that suggests widespread cortical involvement in complex behaviors.5 Further investigation into potential explanatory phenomena is needed before any further claims can be made. 

The univariate signal of the RewP has been proposed as a biomarker of MDD,1 though the ability to detect this signal varies by the individual.   For a biomarker to be effective, we need reliable measures that can be observed in individual patients.  Our multivariate decoding of feedback processing is observable at this level yet still shows variability in decoding strength.  Future studies could explore demographic or phenotypic traits associated with this variability. 

In this experiment, we successfully used MVPA to decode the response to the cognitive content of feedback, not just the perceptual attributes at the level of individual participants. Source estimation suggests widespread ability to decode both perceptual features and cognitive content with functionally localized brain regions showing greater decoding ability for their respective functions. This method could allow for differential decoding of perceptual and cognitive attributes of reward, such as reward magnitude and identity, enabling us to explore how perceptual elements of reward guide decision-making.
