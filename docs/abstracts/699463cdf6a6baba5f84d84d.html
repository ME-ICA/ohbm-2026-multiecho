Introduction

Dyscalculia is a number- and math-specific learning disorder whose cognitive and neural mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Prior work has reported alterations in functional, effective, and structural connectivity in dyscalculia, yet these findings stem from heterogeneous samples and methods, making it difficult to understand how connectivity alterations relate to core numerical processing deficits in the disorder. However, one region that has been consistently implicated across neuroimaging studies of dyscalculia is the intraparietal sulcus (IPS), also referred to as the numerical parietal cortex (NPC). The NPC plays a central role in numerical representation and visuospatial working memory—two cognitive functions repeatedly linked to dyscalculia. 

Here, we investigate whether altered functional integration of this region may be specific to dyscalculia, by looking at its connectivity with the rest of the brain while it is actively engaged during a magnitude comparison task. Furthermore, we test if any such differences might reflect stable, trait-like features or task-phase–dependent (state) modulations. To further interpret such connectivity patterns, we extend the connectivity analyses with precision functional mapping, to quantify how much cortical territory within the NPC is allocated to distinct functional networks. With this, we combine continuous measures of functional integration with network-based estimates of cortical territory allocation, providing complementary perspectives on NPC connectivity alterations in dyscalculia.

Methods

Participants were 33 adolescents with a clinical diagnosis of dyscalculia and 33 age- and gender-matched neurotypical controls (15–23 years, 28 females per group). They completed ~40 min of fMRI during a non-symbolic magnitude comparison task, sequential viewing (6–9 s jitter) two sets of coins and indicating via button press which appeared larger at the second presentation. fMRI data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep to obtain surface-based timeseries in fsaverage5 space, followed by nuisance regression and temporal filtering to generate whole-brain correlation matrices. Gradients were computed by kernel-transforming and embedding these matrices using diffusion mapping, then aligned to the group average (Fig. 1A), all done with the BrainSpace toolbox1. Task-phase specificity was assessed using single-trial beta estimates from GLMsingle3 for stimuli 1 and 2, which were concatenated by type to compute phase-resolved correlation matrices and derive the corresponding beta gradients.

NPC-specific measures were derived from a mask based on the variance-explained map of a numerical population receptive field (nPRF) model fitted to stimuli 1 betas2. NPC dispersion was calculated in the 2D gradient space defined by the first two gradients (G1 × G2) as the mean Euclidean distance of all vertices to the mask centroid. Whole-brain dispersion along gradient 1 (principal sensory–association axis) was calculated as the standard deviation across all cortical vertices. Mixed ANOVAs with stimulus (within-subject) and group (between-subject) factors tested for task-phase (state) and group-level (trait) effects on NPC and whole-brain dispersions.

To complement the gradient-based analyses, individualized network maps were derived using precision functional mapping4, based on the infomap algorithm5. Group-average maps, generated with the same pipeline and labeled using the Cole–Anticevic atlas (Fig. 1C), served as a reference for individual label assignment. NPC network composition was quantified as the surface area (number of vertices) assigned to each network, providing a complementary, interpretable measure of how cortical territory is allocated across functional systems.

Results

To assess Dyscalculia specific differences in how the NPC is functionally integrated with the rest of the brain during a numerical task, we first examined NPC gradient dispersion derived from the whole timeseries. Individuals with dyscalculia showed significantly reduced NPC dispersion compared to controls (Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.003), indicating greater heterogeneity in connectivity  (less integration)  in this region (see Fig. 2A).

To test whether the trait-level differences in NPC organization were influenced by task state, we repeated the analysis using phase-specific beta-derived gradients and evaluated effects with a mixed ANOVA. This replicated the whole-timeseries findings, revealing a significant main effect of group on NPC dispersion (F(1,63) = 4.75, p = 0.033, η²ₚ = 0.07), with increased dispersion in dyscalculia, but no effect of stimulus phase (F(1,63) = 0.87, p = 0.355) and no group × stimulus interaction (F(1,63) = 0.57, p = 0.452). Thus, NPC dispersion differences were not modulated by task phase. In contrast, whole-brain dispersion along the principal (sensory–association) gradient showed no group effect (F(1,63) = 0.25, p = 0.615, η²ₚ = 0.004) but a robust stimulus-phase effect (F(1,63) = 41.30, p < 2 × 10⁻⁸, η²ₚ = 0.396), with greater compression during the decision phase than the perceptual phase (Fig. 1B, bottom). The interaction was not significant (F(1,63) = 3.26, p = 0.076), indicating that beta-derived gradients capture state-dependent reconfiguration of global connectivity, whereas dyscalculia-related differences are stable and region-specific.

To provide a complementary, network-based interpretation of the gradient dispersion effects, we related NPC dispersion to categorical network organization derived from precision functional mapping (see Fig. 2C for two example PFMs). NPC dispersion was associated with the proportion of NPC vertices assigned to specific networks, showing a positive relationship with the dorsal attention network (r = −0.29, p = 0.022) and negative relationships for all other networks, most strongly for visual-2 (r = 0.41, p = 0.00068) and default mode (r = 0.41, p = 0.00084). These associations mirror the geometry of NPC connectivity, with dorsal attention centrally positioned in gradient space and visual-2and default mode toward opposing extremes, such that greater peripheral network occupancy increases dispersion.

We next tested whether these network–dispersion relationships were accompanied by group differences in network representation within the NPC. Of the three networks most strongly associated with dispersion, visual-2 showed a significant (Mann–Whitney U = 376, p = 0.047; Fig. 2C), dorsal attention network a trend (p = 0.086) and default mode network no (p = 0.563)  group difference in NPC surface area. Together with the strong anticorrelation between visual-2 and dorsal attention network representation within the NPC, these patterns suggest that dyscalculia specific group differences in NPC manifest as a shift in relative network composition of the area.

Conclusion

Using complementary connectivity frameworks, we dissociated trait- and state-dependent features of functional connectivity organization in dyscalculia during a numerical task. While task-phase modulation of the whole-brain principal gradient reflected state-related increases in integration during numerical decision-making compared to pure perception, group differences were specific to the numerical parietal cortex (NPC) and were stable across task phases, indicating a trait-level alteration. Network-based analyses suggest that these NPC differences reflect selective allocation of cortical territory: in dyscalculia, greater representation of visual-2 within the NPC may reduce available space for dorsal-attention–linked networks that support numerical processing and visuo-spatial working memory. Together, these findings highlight how combining gradient and network-based approaches can refine our understanding of region-specific connectivity alterations and their potential computational consequences in numerical learning disorders.
