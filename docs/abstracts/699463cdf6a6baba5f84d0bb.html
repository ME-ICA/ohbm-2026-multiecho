Introduction

Resilience, the capacity to adapt successfully to stress, is a dynamic determinant of mental health. Despite its importance, the specific neurobiological mechanisms facilitating resilience remain debated. The existing literature points to three potential neural pathways. First, corticolimbic regulation, characterized by strong prefrontal (PFC) inhibition of the amygdala, supports cognitive reappraisal and emotional regulation1. Second, salience-mediated network switching, governed by the Salience Network (SN), facilitates cognitive flexibility by coordinating the transition between the internally-oriented Default Mode Network (DMN) and the task-positive Central Executive Network (CEN)2. Third, corticostriatal motivational circuitry, centered on the ventral striatum and its connectivity with sensorimotor networks, acts as a driver for active coping1. However, most prior studies have relied on comparing small samples of trauma-exposed individuals with clinical populations, yielding inconsistent results and limited reproducibility1,3.  Furthermore, both brain functional connectivities and resilience are known to be heritable4,5, the "genes-to-brain-to-behavior" pathways linking polygenic influence to these neural systems remain unclear. 

To bridge these gaps, we integrated Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) with resting-state functional MRI in a large-scale cohort of 28,849 UK Biobank participants. We calculated Polygenic Scores (PGS) for the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) and leveraged the Schaefer-Tian parcellation to comprehensively map functional connectivity across cortical and subcortical regions. This study aims to rigorously test which of these candidate pathways—corticolimbic, network-switching, or corticostriatal—mediates the association between genetic propensity and resilient functioning.

Methods

Phenotype: Brief Resilience Scale

Resilience was assessed using the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS), a six-item questionnaire rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Reverse-coded items were recorded prior to scoring, and each participant’s score was computed as the mean across items. To improve normality for downstream analyses, BRS scores were inverse-normal transformed separately in the brain imaging cohort (participants with complete BRS, genotype, and brain imaging data) and in the  GWAS discovery cohort (participants with BRS and genotype data but no brain imaging).

Resting state MRI data and functional connectivity

Resting-state fMRI data were processed in the UK Biobank imaging pipeline and projected to regional time series using the Schaefer 400-parcel 17-network atlas together with the Tian S1 subcortical atlas. The resulting brain imaging cohort comprised participants with usable rsfMRI and complete phenotype/genotype data (N = 28,849).

For each participant, we computed pairwise Pearson correlations between regional time series and applied Fisher’s z-transformation, yielding region-to-region FC. To obtain network-level FC, we grouped edges by network pair (including within-network edges) and performed PCA on the edge set for each network pair; PC1 scores are used in association analyses with BRS and PGS, and related loadings were saved to visualize edge-wise contributions.

Genotype quality control and genome-wide association study

Genotypes were obtained from TOPMed-imputed UK Biobank data. Standard quality control was applied to remove individuals and variants with high missingness, extreme heterozygosity, sex mismatch, and non-European ancestry, and to retain autosomal variants with minor allele frequency > 1% and low missingness. GWAS of inverse-normal-transformed BRS was conducted in a discovery cohort excluding the imaging subset, using a linear mixed model with age, sex, and genetic principal components as covariates. To avoid sample overlap, participants with usable rsfMRI were held out from GWAS and used only in the PGS-FC association analyses. 

Polygenic effects

Polygenic scores were computed in the imaging cohort using LDpred2 based on GWAS summary statistics from the discovery cohort. Scores were standardized prior to analysis.

Association between resilience, brain connectivity, and polygenic effects

We tested associations between BRS and network-level FC by regressing inverse-normal-transformed BRS on each network-level PC score while adjusting for age, sex, the first 20 genetic principal components, and imaging-related covariates. In parallel, we regressed standardized PGS on each network-level FC using the same covariate set. Multiple testing was controlled using Bonferroni correction across network pairs.

For mediation, we selected network-level FC that were significant in the PGS–FC analyses and evaluated parallel multiple mediation models with PGS as the independent variable, BRS as the outcome, and FC as mediators. Indirect and direct effects were estimated with standard errors and significance testing; the proportion of the PGS–BRS association mediated by FC was computed from the estimated paths.

Results

Genetics: In the GWAS discovery cohort, resilience exhibited significant SNP-based heritability (hSNP2= 0.0732; SE = 0.0057), supporting downstream polygenic score (PGS) construction.  In the independent brain imaging cohort, the LDpred2-derived PGS explained an additional 0.45% of the variance in resilience after adjusting for age, sex, and the first 20 genetic principal components (βstd= 0.0674, p = 3.86 × 10-31). 

Neuroimaging: Network-level functional connectivity (FC) analyses revealed convergent neurogenetic architecture associations. Both higher phenotypic resilience and higher resilience-PGS were significantly associated with stronger functional connectivity within the Salience/Ventral Attention (SN/VAN) and Control (CON) networks (Figure 2a & 2b). Notably, Despite the strong theoretical basis for limbic and reward-based models, we observed no Bonferroni-significant associations with subcortical network connectivity.

Mediation: Further analysis highlighted a specific mechanism: while SN/VAN–CON coupling tracked genetic propensity, it did not directly correlate with BRS scores. However, mediation modeling confirmed that a composite of these significant connectivity features (within SN/VAN, within CON, and their coupling) jointly mediated a significant proportion (2.34%) of the genetic influence on resilience (Figure 2c). Edge-level projections further confirmed that resilience is supported by stronger connections distributed throughout these high-level control networks. 

Conclusion

This study leveraged the UK Biobank to test three competing neurobiological models of resilience. Our findings favor the Salience-Mediated Network Switching hypothesis over purely corticolimbic or mesolimbic models. We demonstrate that the genetic propensity for resilience is not primarily driven by subcortical circuits involved in affect or reward. Instead, it is rooted in the stability of higher-order cortical networks (SN/VAN and CON). These networks appear to serve as a heritable neural scaffold, facilitating the flexible coordination required for adaptive functioning.
