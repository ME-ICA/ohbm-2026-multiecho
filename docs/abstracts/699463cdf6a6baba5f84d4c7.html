Introduction

Large-scale neuroimaging studies often collect data from multiple scanners across different sites, where variations in scanners, scanning procedures, and other conditions across sites can introduce artificial site effects. These effects are known to bias functional connectivity derived from functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) ^1. Although there are several batch-correction methods (e.g., ComBat) that have gained popularity in neuroimaging research, these are primarily applied to “vectorized” data without considering the network structures. Addressing batch effect problems from the perspective of (i) covariance harmonization (in addition to the mean and variance harmonization done in ComBat), (ii) network modeling, and (iii) high-dimensionality of whole-brain FC is a critical problem that has yet to be addressed.

In our work, we propose Sparse Latent Covariate-driven Connectome factorization (SLACC), a novel method that explicitly embeds covariate effects on means and variances of latent connectivity patterns. In batch effect problems, this allows a model-based formulation of batch effects in terms of means, variances, and covariances. Building on recent advances in blind-source separation in neuroimaging ^2 and linking it to understanding covariate effects ^3, SLACC leverages latent and sparse connectivity patterns that represent co-activations among small subsets of brain regions to disentangle biologically meaningful signals from site-specific heterogeneity. We applied SLACC to the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) dataset to evaluate whether it effectively harmonizes site effects while preserving biologically relevant associations.

Methods

Summary of the method

Our model and the harmonization procedure are outlined in the Figure. Each subject’s FC matrix is modeled by (i) a set of subject scores, each corresponding to the sparse rank-1 latent patterns, and (ii) residual noise.

  • The latent subject scores (i.e., part (i)) are modeled with covariate effects, site effects in means, and site effects in variances.
  • Residual noises (i.e., part (ii)) are modeled by site-specific variances.

Altogether, our model provides a flexible framework for understanding site effects from three different perspectives.

To achieve this goal, we adopt a penalized expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm, and the penalty is derived naturally by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Data preparation

We used the ABIDE dataset preprocessed by the Configurable Pipeline for the Analysis of Connectomes (CPAC), which includes slice-timing correction, motion realignment, and intensity normalization. The fMRI time series for a set of regions of interest (ROIs) are obtained using the Harvard-Oxford (HO) atlas consisting of 110 regions with 5995 unique edges. We subsequently map them to Yeo’s 7-network parcellation plus the subcortical network. The FC is obtained by the Pearson correlation of time series and Fisher-transformed. Due to incomplete brain coverage in 38 subjects and the limited sample size of only 5 subjects from the CMU site, we excluded them from our analysis. The final sample comprises 845 subjects across 19 sites.

Data analysis

Competing methods

We compared the harmonization performance of SLACC with ComBat available at https://github.com/andy1764/ComBatFamily. 

Full-sample analysis

We use 6 covariates to be included in all these methods: age, age², sex, diagnosis (autism), age × sex, and age × diagnosis. We applied SLACC to processed FC data and extracted latent connectivity patterns. We also evaluated how well harmonized data preserve biological variations, which was assessed by extracting a p-value for each biological covariate at each edge and counting the proportion of p-values less than 0.01.

Out-of-sample validation - latent scores

We also adopted a train-test approach to validate each method’s harmonization performance that is less subject to overfitting.

We split the data into two folds: a training set consisting of 592 images (70%) and a test set consisting of 254 images (30%), using stratified sampling for each site. We first fit SLACC to the training data and used the estimated parameters to harmonize the test data. Then:

  • Using raw test data, we extracted the latent scores (aijl) and evaluated how much latent site effects in means and variances are present by extracting the F test statistics.
  • Using harmonized test data, we constructed the harmonized likelihood model from the training fit. We then extracted the latent scores (aijl) and evaluated how much latent site effects in means and variances are present by extracting the F test statistics.

We compared these two F statistics before and after harmonization.

Out-of-sample validation - edge level

We harmonized the test data using SLACC and ComBat applied to training data, and computed the F test statistics for harmonizing the means and variances at each edge.

Results

Our model identified L=46 unique sparse latent patterns, as shown in Figure (a). Overall, approximately 48% of the entries were shrunk to 0. The resulting latent patterns sorted by hierarchical clustering imply potential utility of leveraging brain networks.

Figure (b) shows the proportion of variability explained by site effects in means and variances in each latent pattern, after applying the SLACC fit from the training data to harmonize the test data. The patterns were sorted based on the total variability (SST) of the corresponding subject scores, and SST for each latent subject score was decomposed into SSReg (variability explained by site effects) and SSE (variability unexplained by site effects). We see that site effects are present in each latent pattern, but the proportion of variance explained by sites is relatively moderate. Also, it appears that the variance heterogeneity within each latent pattern is localized in a few of them.

To evaluate the utility of SLACC in harmonizing the test data, we extracted the ANOVA F statistic computed before and after harmonization of the test data (Figure (c)). Under the null hypothesis (no site effect present), the F statistic should be centered around 1. We see that a few latent patterns that were noticeably high (e.g., F > 4) before harmonization were shrunk close to 1, demonstrating that SLACC effectively mitigates site effects in latent subject scores. However, the ones with modest F statistics (1 < F < 4) did not decrease after harmonizing using the fit from the training data.

In addition to evaluating the F statistic within each latent pattern, we also conducted edge-wise analysis and extracted ANOVA F statistics for mean and variance heterogeneity. The median F statistics for mean and variance heterogeneities were 1.39 and 1.10, respectively, for SLACC, and 1.25 and 1.19 for ComBat, suggesting that ComBat might be more beneficial for addressing mean heterogeneity in univariate analysis, and SLACC might be more useful in reducing variance heterogeneity.

Preservation of biological effects was assessed by computing the proportion of p-values less than 0.01. In the harmonized test data, ComBat had roughly 1.79% that were significant, and SLACC had 1.62%. More investigation is needed to understand this, however, because the ABIDE dataset revealed confounding between biological covariates (diagnosis status) and sites, and it is not fully validated whether a slightly lower proportion in SLACC is due to overcorrection (i.e., more effective harmonization) of site effects.

Lastly, we evaluated whether the residual variance is associated with the scan duration. FC is primarily a statistical measure in which its variance inversely depends on the number of time points. In Figure (d), there was a very high correlation (r = -0.66, p = 0.002). Although this result is expected, it implies a potential utility of including the scan duration directly into SLACC.

Conclusion

Discussion

By embedding site and covariate effects into latent factors, our method enables a more nuanced correction of site-related variations while preserving biologically meaningful individual differences. The incorporation of low-rank structure and regularization into the modeling helps reduce model complexity, leading to more reliable localization and correction of site effects. From the modeling perspective, we were able to significantly reduce model complexity while still achieving a similar degree of harmonization performance, if not better.

We have primarily focused on applying SLACC to site-effect correction, but, considering the generality of SLACC, it can be used to efficiently reduce the dimensionality of FC data where covariate effects are localized. Therefore, compared to “two-stage modeling” that is carried out by reducing dimension first (e.g., using ICA) and then testing its association with covariates, our approach seamlessly integrates this in one stage while achieving additional benefits such as sparsity. In addition, SLACC is built based on a clear data-generating process for FC that many other exploratory analysis tools do not provide.

One limitation is that our model assumes symmetric FC patterns and does not consider the “positive semi-definiteness (PSD)” of the FC, both raw and harmonized. Therefore, there is no guarantee that each harmonized image is PSD. Although this is a common problem in most batch-correction methods and is not an important factor unless the PSD property is essential in conducting brain-wide association studies, incorporating it in SLACC may potentially improve harmonization performance.
