Introduction

Stress responses reflect how individuals interpret and adapt to uncertain environments. Two fundamental dimensions—predictability (the ability to anticipate events) and controllability (the ability to control outcomes)—are central to shaping these responses. Unpredictable events heighten vigilance and autonomic arousal1, whereas the ability to exert control reduces their impact2. Computational models explain these effects by proposing that learning depends on uncertainty estimates. These include surprise, the prediction error that occurs when outcomes deviate from expectations, estimation uncertainty, the imprecision or low confidence in current beliefs, and volatility, the inferred rate of environmental change that determines how strongly beliefs should be updated3.

Research examining these dimensions separately shows distinct effects. Under low predictability, unstable cue–outcome contingencies impair belief updating and increase subjective and physiological stress, with responses tracking trial-by-trial uncertainty4. Under low controllability, the inability to influence aversive events heightens stress responses and promotes passive coping, whereas having control engages processes that buffer stress2. Computational work further shows that controllability alters how information is incorporated during learning. When outcomes are controllable, individuals exploit environmental structure more effectively, whereas loss of control shifts learning toward a more reactive mode dominated by immediate prediction errors5. These studies report dissociations between subjective experience and physiological responses, highlighting the need to characterise how predictability and controllability jointly shape the uncertainty representations that govern stress responses across systems.

Methods

Thirty healthy adults (16 women; aged 18–40 years) were recruited from the University of Melbourne. Participants reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders and provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the University of Melbourne Human Research Ethics Committee (Ethics. 2024-26010-48769-4), and participants were compensated for their time.

Participants completed a probabilistic aversive learning task across two sessions conducted on separate days, one controllable and one uncontrollable, each comprising 320 trials (Figure 1 a). Prior to testing, participants received task instructions and completed a short practice session. On each trial, participants viewed one of two rock images used as a cue, which signalled the probability of a snake outcome. After seeing the cue, they indicated whether they expected a snake, and their prediction was displayed briefly before the outcome was shown. Shock intensity reflected prediction accuracy, with correct predictions followed by low-intensity shocks and incorrect predictions followed by high-intensity shocks.

Predictability was manipulated within each session by varying cue–shock contingencies across ten blocks of 25–35 trials (Figure 1 c). Blocks were highly predictable (90%), moderately predictable (70%), or unpredictable (50%). Block order was counterbalanced, and transitions were not signalled. Participants were informed that probabilities would change over time. Controllability was varied across two separate sessions trials (Figure 1 b). In the controllable session, prediction accuracy determined shock intensity, with correct predictions followed by a low-intensity shock and incorrect predictions followed by a high-intensity shock, allowing participants to reduce aversive impact through learning. In the uncontrollable session, participants completed the same prediction task, but their responses no longer controlled the shocks they received. Instead, they were given the exact sequence of low and high shocks that had been produced by their performance in the controllable session.

Subjective stress was assessed intermittently trials (Figure 1 d). Every 4–6 trials, participants rated their current stress level on a Likert scale from 1 (not stressed) to 4 (highly stressed), yielding 65 ratings per session.

Electric shocks were delivered via a Stimulus Isolator (ADInstruments). Shock intensity was individually calibrated using a staircase procedure to determine an aversive but tolerable level. Skin conductance responses were recorded from the non-dominant hand at 1000 Hz and preprocessed in MATLAB using the PsychoPhysiological Modelling toolbox (Figure 1 d). Signals were artefact-corrected, low-pass filtered at 5 Hz, square-root transformed, and range-corrected within participant.

Trial-wise learning was modelled using the Volatile Kalman Filter (VKF), which estimates surprise, estimation uncertainty, and volatility in changing environments (Figure 1 e). Model parameters were estimated using computational Bayesian modelling with weakly informative priors. VKF-derived trajectories were used as regressors to explain subjective stress ratings, reaction times, and skin conductance responses.

Behavioural and physiological data were analysed using 2 × 3 repeated-measures ANOVAs with Controllability and Predictability as within-subject factors. Model-based analyses employed general linear models and random-effects Bayesian model selection to identify which computational quantity best accounted for stress responses under controllable and uncontrollable conditions.

Results

Behavioural performance was jointly influenced by predictability and controllability. Accuracy showed significant main effects of predictability, F(2,58)=48.27, p=4.54 × 10⁻¹³, and controllability, F(1,29)=9.28, p=0.0049. There was no interaction between the two factors, F(2,58)=1.22, p=0.30. Accuracy declined as cue outcome contingencies became less predictable. Accuracy declined as cue outcome contingencies became less predictable. Across both sessions, highly predictable (HP) blocks produced higher accuracy than moderately predictable (MP, p = 1.68 × 10⁻⁶) and unpredictable (UP, p = 1.04 × 10⁻⁵) blocks in controllable session (S1) and in uncontrollable session (S2: HP > MP, p = 3.03 × 10⁻⁴; HP > UP, p = 5.59 × 10⁻⁷), whereas accuracy did not differ significantly between moderately predictable and unpredictable blocks in either session (S1 MP vs UP, p = 1.00; S2 MP vs UP, p = 0.093).

Reaction times showed a robust effect of controllability. Participants responded faster when outcomes were uncontrollable, F(1,29)=26.37, 1.74 × 10⁻⁵, irrespective of predictability. Predictability did not significantly affect response speed, F(2,58)=2.11, p=0.13. No interaction between predictability and controllability was observed, F(2,58)=1.22, p=0.30.Post hoc analyses confirmed shorter reaction times in the uncontrollable condition for highly predictable blocks (p = 4.52 × 10⁻⁴) and moderately predictable blocks (p = 1.67 × 10⁻³), with a marginal difference in unpredictable blocks (p = 0.060).

Skin conductance responses (SCRs) showed strong main effects of controllability, F(1,174)=25.12, p=1.3×10⁻⁶, and predictability, F(2,174)=48.49, p=1.8×10⁻¹⁷, and a significant Controllability × Predictability interaction, F(2,174)=21.79, p=3.6×10⁻⁹. SCRs increased as predictability decreased across both controllable and uncontrollable conditions and were overall lower in the uncontrollable condition regardless of predictability. Under controllability, SCRs were lower for highly predictable and moderately predictable blocks than for unpredictable blocks, and highly predictable and moderately predictable blocks did not differ (p=0.96). Under uncontrollability, highly predictable blocks evoked lower SCRs than moderately predictable and unpredictable blocks (ps<0.001), whereas moderately predictable and unpredictable blocks did not differ (p=0.81). Moderate predictability dampened autonomic arousal only when participants had control, but resembled unpredictability when control was absent (Figure 2 a).

To uncover underlying mechanisms, trial-wise data were modelled using the Volatile Kalman Filter (Figure 2 b and c). Model comparison revealed a controllability-dependent double dissociation between subjective and objective measures of stress. Specifically, subjective stress ratings were best explained by volatility when outcomes were controllable (exceedance probability 0.99) and by estimation uncertainty when outcomes were uncontrollable (exceedance probability >0.90), whereas skin conductance responses showed the opposite pattern, being best explained by estimation uncertainty under control (≈0.70) and volatility under uncontrollability (≈0.72). Parameter analyses showed that higher learning rates were associated with lower accuracy in both controllable (r = −0.74, p= 6.20 × 10⁻⁶).  and uncontrollable conditions (r = −0.75, p= 5.14 × 10⁻⁶). In contrast, stronger volatility updating predicted higher accuracy (r = 0.42, p = 0.028) and faster responses (r = −0.45, p = 0.015) only when outcomes were controllable. Anxiety was associated with steeper learning rates (r = 0.48, p = 0.008) and reduced volatility updating (r = −0.38, p = 0.038) under controllability, with no effects under uncontrollability.

Conclusion

The present findings demonstrate that stress responses are shaped by the interaction between uncertainty and controllability, rather than by uncertainty alone. Predictability influenced learning performance across conditions, but its impact on physiological and subjective stress depended critically on whether participants could influence outcomes. Controllability therefore emerges as a central factor that determines how environmental structure is translated into behavioural performance, autonomic arousal, and subjective experience.

Computational modelling further showed that controllability of outcomes alters how uncertainty is organised across behavioural, physiological, and subjective measures. When outcomes were controllable, uncertainty was expressed in a functionally differentiated manner. Subjective stress ratings were primarily related to higher-order beliefs about environmental volatility, skin conductance responses tracked estimation uncertainty, and behavioural performance reflected learning dynamics only when outcomes were controllable. This pattern indicates that, under controllability, different forms of uncertainty are selectively expressed across response domains, supporting flexible regulation and adaptive behaviour.

When control was absent, this organisation changed. Moderate predictability no longer reduced autonomic arousal, reaction times were consistently faster, and subjective stress depended more strongly on estimation uncertainty. At the computational level, uncertainty was expressed differently across subjective ratings, autonomic responses, and behaviour, rather than being selectively mapped to each domain. In this context, predictive structure was present but could not be used to regulate behaviour or physiology because outcomes were no longer contingent on action.

Together, these findings provide a mechanistic account of why uncontrollable stress is especially disruptive. Loss of control does not simply increase uncertainty but changes how uncertainty is represented and deployed across cognitive, physiological, and behavioural systems. Incorporating controllability into computational models of learning and stress therefore offers a principled framework for understanding how the same environmental uncertainty can give rise to adaptive regulation when agency is preserved, and to maladaptive stress responses when it is lost.
