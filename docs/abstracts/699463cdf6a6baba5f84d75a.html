Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) manifests in demyelination, affecting white matter (WM) tracts1, including those of the cerebellum. A typical disease course begins with a clinically isolated syndrome (CIS), progresses to relapsing–remitting MS (RRMS), and in many patients evolves into secondary progressive MS (SPMS) after 10–15 years. Growing evidence suggests that the cerebellum plays a pivotal role in the onset and progression of MS symptoms2,3. Given the presence of both cognitive and motor deficits across disease stages, cerebellar involvement likely contributes to multiple aspects of MS pathology.

On the motor side, ataxia occurs in approximately 80% of MS cases and is more prevalent in progressive subtypes such as SPMS2. On the cognitive side, cerebellar relapses—symptom worsening driven by cerebellar inflammation—are associated with poorer recovery from MS relapses2,4. At the network level, functional studies reveal altered coherence between cerebellar and large-scale cerebral networks, including executive control and sensorimotor networks, during relapse-to-remission transitions in RRMS5. However, these functional interactions rely on the structural integrity of cerebellar WM tracts, which remain relatively underexplored.

It is unclear whether cerebellar tracts are differentially affected across MS subtypes, whether their longitudinal change rates differ, or whether they deteriorate faster than cerebral WM tracts. Moreover, prior studies often treat the cerebellum as a secondary region of interest and are limited by small samples and short follow-up periods.

To address these gaps, we conducted a retrospective study using diffusion MRI (dMRI) scans collected over 15 years, examining microstructural characteristics of the major cerebellar tracts, specifically the three pairs of cerebellar peduncles that form the cerebellum’s core macro-circuitry.

Methods

Sample. A clinical MRI data collection of both MS patients and clinical controls (CC) was made in the Klinikum rechts der Isar clinic of Technical University of Munich, Germany. Clinical controls, as opposed to healthy controls, were individuals who were not specifically matched with control groups; rather, these were patients admitted to the clinic due to non-MS indications. A total of 1512 unique patients (including 97 clinical controls), numbering in total 4673 scanning sessions and excluding 47 available dMRI duplicates, were scanned over the period of approximately 14 years and 7 months within the same clinic. The average inter-scan interval was 1.07 years.

The dataset included various non-canonical MS (e.g., NNO, RIS, NMO) phenotypes, which constituted only a small fraction of the data and were therefore excluded. RRMS, CIS, and SPMS patients were selected for this study.

Data selection and filtering. Subject sessions with less than 15 b1000 directions were excluded. Although constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) allows modeling with less than 15 b-directions, we chose this setting to resolve crossing fibers  at highest angular resolution at lmax = 4. Additionally, we ensure necessary dMRI-associated files are present within a patient session and select those sessions having no logged errors while using recon_all or mrinfo -spacing when extracting number of dimensions from each image.

The remaining sessions were subsequently quality-checked. Missing Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) scores were interpolated with population mean value. Sessions with negative disease durations (erroneously protocolled during studies) were rejected. Sessions having at least one broken tractography metric, namely FA or RD, were dropped. Reported results of analyses are thus based on final data subset resulting in 2165 sessions.

Imaging & preprocessing. Patients underwent four different MRI scanning experiments. Scanning machines by Philips were Ingenia, Achieva dStream, Achieva, Ingenia Elition X sorted in decreasing order by the number of scans accomplished. All MRI systems had a magnetic strength of 3 Tesla. A standardized imaging protocol was used, including a single-shot 2D echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence for diffusion-weighted MRI (dMRI) [EPI single shot, 72 axial slices, 2 mm slice thickness, field of view 224 × 224, in-plane resolution: (2.0 × 2.0) mm2, repetition time (TR) / echo time (TE) 4.88 / 0.08 s, flip angle: 90◦, multiple gradient directions, among which 16, 17 and 33 single-shell b-values: 1000 s/mm2]. All the volumes were pre-processed to correct for MRI-associated artifacts (denoising, motion correction, etc.) according to a standardized pipeline, with extensive use of FreeSurfer and MRtrix3. Fiber orientation distribution (FOD) was then estimated using CSD, implemented in MRtrix3. Resulting peaks (fiber orientations) were then used as inputs for TractSeg for cerebellar WM tracts segmentation to further extract scalar metrics upon. Fractional anisotropy (FA) and radial diffusivity (RD) were selected as dMRI metrics of interest.

Statistics. Based on TractSeg Tractometry, one hundred FA/RD segments were extracted per tract and subject. They were then averaged to result in a single scalar per each tract and subject. Python’s statsmodels and R’s lme4 were used for modeling. To model dMRI metrics cross-sectionally, univariate Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) regressed on MS subtypes was performed with a post-hoc Tukey HSD. Bonferroni correction was then used to adjust for multiple comparisons. Longitudinally, individuals’ slopes β1 were estimated using simple linear regression, then a vector of cerebellar and cerebral slopes deltas was obtained. Afterwards, weighted linear regression (WLS) was applied to test the null hypothesis that the mean slope difference (β0) between cerebellar and cerebral tracts is equal to zero. T-tests were utilized to test the significance of WLS coefficients.

Results

Cross-sectionally, an ANCOVA was conducted to determine if canonical MS subtypes (RRMS, CIS, and SPMS) affected FA / RD metrics in selected cerebellar tracts, while controlling for age, sex, disease duration, and EDSS score. The ANCOVA revealed a significant main effect for FA in left and right inferior cerebellar peduncles [F(2, 653) = 5.8188, p = 0.0031 and [F(2, 653) = 7.6261, p = 0.0005, respectively]; variance homogeneity assumptions were satisfied (p > 0.05). To reveal pairwise differences, a Tukey HSD test was conducted afterwards. For both FA ICP left and right, SPMS vs. CIS and SPMS vs. RRMS differences were significantly different, with SPMS FA values being lower in all of these scenarios [ICP left: Mdiff = -0.0226, p = 9.707 x 10-4 (SPMS - CIS), Mdiff = -0.0160, p = 2.017 x 10-3 (SPMS - RRMS); ICP right: Mdiff = -0.0241, p = 2.488 x 10-4 (SPMS - CIS), Mdiff = -0.0206, p = 2.519 x 10-5 (SPMS - RRMS)], as displayed in Figure 1. The rest of the comparisons were non-significant. Longitudinally, we demonstrate significant (p < 0.05) within-subject FA (Figure 2, panels B and D) and RD (Figure 2, panels C and E) mean slope differences between cerebellar tracts and cerebral tracts within MS subtype – RRMS or CIS, with only RRMS surviving Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli correction for multiple comparisons. Here, however, we uncorrected results for both groups. Each test, displayed as a chord on Figure 2, represents a significant t-test comparing the mean longitudinal slope of the cerebellar tract with that of a cerebral tract. In RRMS, cerebellar MCP characterized by FA shows the most rapid longitudinal alteration (decrease in FA), whereas cerebellar MCP and left SCP expressed in RD reveal the fastest increase in RD, compared to cerebral tracts. In CIS, cerebellar tracts, expressed in FA, show MCP and ICP fastest decrease in FA, although sparsely. With a similar sparsity, RD MCP rises the most within CIS individuals.

Conclusion

Taken together, we demonstrate that (1) ICP tracts of the SPMS subgroup are distinctly altered, suggesting that ICP is important for MS subtypes differentiation. Whereas there is not enough evidence to see the baseline difference between RRMS and CIS groups. Cerebellar tracts change faster than the Corpus callosum and optic radiation tracts in the RRMS group.

(2) Within individuals, it appears that over time, although the majority of the tracts in MS are diffusely affected with lesions, the MCP and left SCP tracts in RRMS are deteriorating faster compared to selected cerebral tracts (corpus callosum and optic radiation). This allows us to suggest that these tracts might be particularly vulnerable.
