Introduction

Aging is a risk factor for many diseases and can impact cognitive and physical health through multiple pathways. The increasing number of older adults will not only result in an economic burden but also create the need for better care infrastructure. Thus, there is a greater urgency to understand factors that contribute to healthy aging. One such factor is brain health. The ability to preserve or even rejuvenate areas of the brain is necessary to promote not only brain health but also cognition. Yet, the differences between healthy and abnormal brain trajectories as well as the associated cognitive changes are not well-understood. The current study aimed to fill this knowledge gap by studying offspring of centenarians. 

Offspring of centenarians have been noted as the ideal models to study healthy aging1. These offspring can delay age-related diseases and preserve cognitive and physical function.1,2 Several factors such as lifestyle, nutrition and physical activity have been shown to be independent of parental longevity status.2,3  Therefore, there must be other components that lead to this rare phenotype. Thus, our specific aim was to identify structural brain differences between those whose parents survived to an advanced age compared to those with parents of usual survival.  

Multivariate covariance-based statistics were utilized to generate a gray matter volume pattern that was associated with parental longevity. This derived pattern was then regressed against several cognitive domains. Understanding the brain pattern that contributes to parental longevity and how it relates to cognitive functions such as memory can help determine how to help older adults who will likely face health challenges later in life. Patterns or networks of the brain that are either preserved or atrophied in those with parental longevity can then be utilized as targets for the development of therapies that rehabilitate these areas during the inevitable ageing process. 

Methods

One hundred and thirty-nine older participants from the LonGenity study were examined. The LonGenity study is a prospective study focused on healthy longevity at Albert Einstein College of Medicine. Each participant underwent an MRI protocol that included a T1-weighted image. All participants were of Ashkenazi Jewish descent, ≥65 years of age and free of dementia at baseline.  Eighty-four (M Age: 77.7; 58.3% female) were offspring of parents with exceptional longevity (OPEL, having at least one parent aged 95 or above). Fifty-five (M Age: 80.1; 52.7% female) were offspring of parents with usual survival (OPUS; neither parent lived to age 95). Both groups matched from a socio-economic and educational perspective. Greater detail of this study design can be found in previous work.3 All participants provided a written informed consent, and the study obtained the approval of the Einstein’s institutional review board.

T1-weighted images were acquired using a Philips 3T Eli­tion multinuclear MRI/MRS system at the Gruss Magnetic Res­onance Research Center in Bronx, NY. A detailed description of our image processing pipeline can be found in our recent paper.4 One hundred and nineteen participants also completed cognitive assessments - including a global cognition score that was composed of 10 neuropsychological tasks based upon 7 different cognitive domains (executive function, working memory, processing speed, language, visual memory, visuospatial function, and verbal episodic memory). All scores were examined in their raw form except for the global cognitive score which was z-standardized. These tasks were completed within four months of MRI acquisition, with most completed on the same day.  

A multivariate covariance-based approach was implemented to generate a gray matter pattern associated with parental longevity. This model was adjusted for several confounding factors – age, sex, years of education, estimated total intracranial volume and white matter lesion burden (white matter hyperintensities). This approach utilizes principal components analysis (PCA) to generate components from the MRI data that are then regressed against the outcome variable of parental longevity status, OPEL or OPUS.  The ideal combination of components from the resulting PCA was then identified using the Akaike Information Criteria. Finally, a bootstrapping technique was used that involved taking n-1 subjects (N=139) to produce a new covariance pattern and repeated 1000 times. This method determined stability of the voxels in our initial pattern. A spatial map, with a z-score at each voxel, is produced with a statistical significance set at p < .05, z ± 1.96. These statistically significant voxels were considered to be associated with parental longevity sta­tus.5 Lastly the subject-specific factor scores from the PCA, representing the extent to which a participant expressed the derived pattern, was then regressed against the cognitive scores -  attention, episodic memory, executive function, language, visuomotor speed, working memory, and overall cognition. 

Results

We were able to derive, using a multivariate approach, a gray matter pattern in healthy older adults that is correlated with parental longevity shown in figure 1a. Areas in red – comprised of frontal, insular and hippocampal volumes - represent positively weighted voxels that are associated with greater volume in OPEL compared to OPUS. Areas in blue – comprised of supplementary motor, precuneus, and cerebellar volumes- represent negatively weighted voxels that are associated with less volume in OPEL compared to OPUS. The relationship between the subject-specific expression PCA scores, representing the extent to which individuals display this derived brain pattern associated with parental longevity status, and cognitive performance are displayed in Figure 1b. This figure shows several statistically significant associations - overall cognition (β = −0.56, p < .001), episodic memory (β = −0.65, p < .001), language (β= −0.43, p = .02), visuomotor speed (β = 0.20, p < .001) and attention (β = −0.22, p < 0.01).  Note that the negative beta coefficients are attributed to the negative subject-specific scores obtained in our PCA analysis – but due to the double negative rule should be interpreted in a positive fashion – i.e., greater expression of the derived gray matter pattern linked to parental longevity was associated with better cognitive performance.

Conclusion

In the final analysis, a gray matter pattern associated with parental longevity was derived in healthy older adults using a multivariate approach. This gray matter pattern also varied as a function of cognitive performance. Brain regions that were shown to be maintained in individuals with parental longevity could be a marker to understand and discover new ways to promote healthy brain aging and neurocognitive aging. For instance, the hippocampus, one of the regions included in our pattern that is known to experience atrophy particularly in early stages of Alzheimer’s disease, has exhibited growth due to the participation in exercise. Additionally, improvement in cognition can be attributed to the involvement in leisure activities. Ultimately, the study of brain aging is another tool to monitor and combat age-related disruptions that will lead to better health outcomes.
