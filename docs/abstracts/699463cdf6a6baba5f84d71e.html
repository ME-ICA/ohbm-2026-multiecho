Introduction

Neuroimaging research traditionally focuses on deriving population-level principles by comparing groups (e.g. patient-control design) or experimental conditions. This approach implicitly assumes that population averages are representative for individual subjects (ergodicity). In EEG research, this assumption is rarely evaluated directly. Assessing it requires characterizing two key dimensions of variability: intra-individual stability over time, using longitudinal recordings, and inter-individual heterogeneity, by identifying and quantifying the factors that drive differences between individuals.1 Without examining these dimensions, averaging across heterogeneous participants risks obscuring potentially meaningful individual differences and producing models that describe no one in particular. This limitation is especially problematic in clinical contexts, where decisions are made for individual patients, rather than for the statistical average of a population. Here, using within-subject experimental designs, we show that individual differences constitute a major, robust source of variance in the EEG signal and investigate potential underlying factors.

Methods

Study A is a decade-long longitudinal event-related potential (ERP) study of a visual backward masking task. Inter-individual variability and longitudinal intra-individual stability were quantified using fingerprinting analysis.2 This approach yields an identification rate, defined as the percentage of individuals who are more similar to themselves at follow-up than to any other participant. Similarity was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient applied to ERP waveforms recorded at occipital electrodes in a 0-400 ms post-stimulus time window. Statistical significance was evaluated using non-parametric permutation tests.

Study B analyzed the open ERP CORE battery of ERPs, which includes six distinct tasks (e.g., face perception, visual search…).3 Fingerprinting analysis was applied to evaluate the extent to which individual differences in evoked potentials generalize across conditions within a task and across different tasks.

Results

In Study A, ERP waveforms differed strongly between individuals but remained remarkably stable within individuals over periods of 5 and 10 years (N₀y = 193, N₅y = 56, N₁₀y = 20). This long-term stability was observed for single-electrode ERPs but was most clearly summarized using global field power (GFP) waveforms. Fingerprinting analysis between baseline and the 5-year follow-up (N = 56) yielded statistically significant identification rates of 80%. Overall, intra-individual variability over a decade was substantially lower than inter-individual variability.

In Study B, individuals showed highly similar ERP waveforms across experimental conditions within the same task, with identification rates ranging from 80% to 100% for most tasks (N = 28). Moreover, a significant proportion of individuals could be identified across entirely different visual tasks, such as face perception and a visual oddball paradigm. As a control, we showed that identification rates between auditory and visual evoked potentials were at chance level. These results indicate that a major portion of EEG signal variance is explained by stable individual differences in addition to the task-evoked effects.

Conclusion

The long-term stability of individual evoked potentials suggests that the influence of incidental factors such as subject state (e.g. fatigue, stress), learning effects, changes in cognitive strategy, or technical noise (e.g. electrode placement and impedance) is small. Furthermore, the observed cross-task generalizability indicates that the morphology of the visual response in occipital cortex represents a robust individual trait that manifests independently of specific stimuli or tasks. Taken together, these findings suggest that individual differences in EEG primarily reflect stable, trait-like characteristics rather than transient cognitive states or noise, potentially driven by anatomical variability.
