Introduction

Understanding how large-scale brain organization supports cognitive functions and behavior remains a central challenge in neuroscience. Cognitive tasks do not rely on isolated regions but emerge from coordinated interactions among distributed brain systems, whose organization constrains how information is integrated and expressed during behavior. Characterizing this organization and how it is reconfigured by task demands is therefore essential for linking brain dynamics to cognitive performance. 

Functional Harmonics (FH), obtained by extracting the eigenmodes of a Laplacian defined on connectivity matrices, have been adopted to describe large-scale brain organization through a geometric representation of cortical activity (Atasoy et al., 2016; Glomb et al., 2021). A key strength of this approach is that cortical activity is interpreted as the coordinated expression of spatial modes intrinsically defined by the brain’s functional architecture. 

Within this framework, HADES performs an eigenmode decomposition of a super-dense functional connectivity matrix and projects the resulting modes onto fMRI time series (Vohryzek et al., 2024). Unlike more classical harmonic approaches (Luppi et al., 2023) based on structural connectomes, this allows functional harmonics to be expressed and analyzed dynamically during anesthesia or psychedelic resting state (Luppi et al., 2023; Vohryzek et al., 2023). 

It remains unclear whether functional harmonics are dynamically reweighted during active cognitive engagement as a function of cognitive demand, and whether such reconfigurations carry behaviorally relevant information. Leveraging the HADES framework, we examine the temporal expression of the first twenty functional harmonics during the Human Connectome Project N-back task, enabling a systematic assessment of how task-dependent harmonic reweighting relates to individual differences in performance. 

Methods

Functional harmonics were derived from a group-level, super-dense functional connectivity matrix obtained from the HCP1200 average dataset (Figure 1A), following the HADES framework described in Vohryzek et al. (2024). This dense connectivity representation preserves fine-grained functional geometry and enables the extraction of cortical eigenmodes at a vertex level. After testing the correlation between the vertex-level and a parcel-level eigenmodes, the super-dense obtained harmonic basis was then mapped to the Schaefer-400 cortical parcellation (Figure 1B). 

We chose to select almost every subject that performed a working-memory fMRI task from 1,041 Human Connectome Project Young Adults, considering for each of them two task runs (LR and RL). The time courses of these recordings were then parcellated and projected onto the harmonic basis using dot-product morphology matching, yielding harmonic time courses (ψ₁–ψ₂₀) for each run (Figure 1C). Harmonic sign polarity was aligned across subjects to ensure consistent sign interpretation. 

Task structure was reconstructed from HCP event files, separating 0-back and 2-back blocks across four stimulus categories (body, faces, places, tools). We also defined inter-stimulus windows (ISW) as the fixation intervals between task blocks (lasting 15 seconds each). For each condition, analyses were performed separately for early and late halves of each block to capture within-condition temporal evolution, for specific stimulus categories, and in the interaction between the two. 

For each harmonic and condition, we quantified complementary dynamical metrics:  

(i) z-scored (Z-AMP), absolute and relative harmonic contribution/amplitude, i.e., how much the brain activity reflects a given harmonic at each timepoint, then averaged per condition;  

(ii) fractional occupancy (FO), defined as the proportion of time points in which a harmonic was dominant under a winner-takes-all assignment; 

(iii) harmonic lifetime (LT), defined as the duration of continuous dominance episodes. 

Metrics were computed at the level of individual stimulus conditions and aggregated within task type (0-back, 2-back, ISW) across runs. Statistical comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. We also performed a robust check, repeated split-half resampling (1000 iterations), and quantified sign and magnitude stability across subjects. 

In an attempt to relate harmonic dynamics to behavior (in terms of error-rate), we then performed a latent regression analysis in which Z-AMP, FO, and LT were jointly embedded using principal component analysis; the leading component was then regressed against subject-specific error rates separately for 0-back, 2-back, and their difference. Robustness and behavioral associations were assessed using repeated random-split cross-validation (1000 iterations, 70% training / 30% test), with all dimensionality reduction performed on training data only. 

Results

Functional harmonics exhibited structured, cognitive-dependent reconfiguration during the HCP N-back task. 
In particular, from the z-scored contribution (i.e., the correspondence between brain activity and a given harmonic, normalized so that the mean correspondence is set to zero), we observed that ψ₁ was selectively enhanced during inter-stimulus windows, whereas ψ₂ and ψ₁₆ were prominent during low-load (0-back) conditions and consistently suppressed under high load (all FDR-corrected p < 0.05). A separate group of harmonics (ψ₃, ψ₆, ψ₁₇) increased during high cognitive load and transitional periods, while a higher-order subset (ψ₅, ψ₈, ψ₁₂, ψ₁₃, ψ₁₅) showed selective enhancement during 2-back blocks (FDR-corrected p < 0.05). In contrast, ψ₇ and ψ₁₄ remained largely invariant across conditions. 

These functional groupings were highly consistent across z-scored correspondence (Figure 2A), as well as absolute and relative correspondence, and across FO (Figure 2B) and LT, indicating that relative reweighting was accompanied by systematic changes in dominance probability and temporal persistence. 

The same harmonic groupings re-emerged when latent regression models were used to relate harmonic dynamics to individual error rates, both across task conditions and between early and late task phases. Importantly, these performance-related associations were phase-specific. While early task periods showed weaker and less consistent harmonic reweighting, the late phase of each block exhibited a markedly more stable and selective harmonic configuration. Harmonics such as ψ₂ and ψ₁₆ displayed near-baseline or heterogeneous expression early in the block but converged toward their characteristic task-specific profiles in the late phase (all FDR-corrected p < 0.05). Consistent with this observation, reliable associations with error rate emerged predominantly during late task periods, indicating that the harmonic geometry relevant for performance reflects a dynamically consolidated state rather than an instantaneous response to task onset. 
 
In an initial, non–cross-validated analysis, the latent components exhibited systematic and condition-dependent associations with performance, allowing the direction of effects to be assessed. Specifically, a mid-order harmonic cluster (ψ₅, ψ₈, ψ₁₂, ψ₁₃, ψ₁₅) showed positive associations (r=0.54) with error rate during the low-load condition and negative associations (r=-0.39) during the high-load condition, whereas ψ₂ and ψ₁₆ exhibited the opposite pattern, showing negative associations with low-order condition error rate (r= -0.37) and the positive with higher-load condition error rate (r=0.32). 

To assess whether these relationships were generalized and robust, we then performed cross-validated regression analyses (70% training data / 30% test data, 1000 splits), fitting the latent components on the training data only. At the cluster level, latent representations showed stable and robust predictive associations with performance, with test-set correlations ranging from r_test ≈ 0.3 to 0.6 across conditions. The cluster 5 (ψ₅, ψ₈, ψ₁₂, ψ₁₃, ψ₁₅)  exhibited the strongest and most reliable predictive performance (0-back late: mean r_test = 0.54 ± 0.04, 95% CI [0.45, 0.61]; B2 late: mean r_test = 0.39 ± 0.04, 95% CI [0.31, 0.46]), while ψ₂/ψ₁₆ also reliably predicted error rates in both conditions (0-back late: mean r_test = 0.34 ± 0.07, 95% CI [0.15, 0.45]; B2 late: mean r_test = 0.32 ± 0.05, 95% CI [0.23, 0.41]). 

Importantly, because latent components derived via PCA are not uniquely defined in sign, the cross-validated analyses were used to assess predictive reliability rather than effect direction. Directional interpretations, therefore, rely on the non–cross-validated analyses, while cross-validation demonstrates that the harmonic information captured by these clusters generalizes unseen subjects (Figure 2C). 

Conclusion

Our analyses showed that working memory reshapes cortical dynamics not only by modulating isolated regions, but by redistributing activity across a structured harmonic space. Low-order harmonics, associated with large-scale, integrative cortical patterns, were preferentially expressed during inter-stimulus intervals (ψ₁) and low-load conditions (ψ₂), whereas higher-order harmonics (ψ₈, ψ₁₂, ψ₁₃, ψ₁₅), reflecting more spatially specific and fragmented modes, were selectively recruited under increased working memory demands. 

This load-dependent reweighting suggests that task engagement does not simply amplify or suppress activity but reorganizes the balance between global and more differentiated harmonic modes. Functional harmonics, therefore, provide a compact and interpretable basis for capturing how large-scale brain dynamics adapt to cognitive demands, linking specific harmonic configurations to task structure and behavioral performance. 

By integrating relative harmonic contribution, dominance probability, and temporal persistence, this study demonstrates that task engagement induces coherent reweighting of intrinsic geometric modes. The emergence of the same harmonic clusters across multiple metrics, together with their robust association with behavioral performance—including out-of-sample prediction—suggests that functional harmonics capture meaningful computational pathways supporting cognition. Two main trends were observed. A first set of harmonics (ψ₂ and ψ₁₆) was more prominently expressed during low-load conditions (0-back) and showed positive associations with error rates under high cognitive load, whereas a different set of harmonics (ψ₅, ψ₈, ψ₁₂, ψ₁₃, ψ₁₅) displayed the opposite relationship. 

Within the HADES framework, these findings extend the role of functional harmonics beyond static spatial descriptors or global state markers, positioning them as dynamic carriers of task-specific and behaviorally relevant brain organization. This harmonic perspective provides a principled geometric account of cognitive processing and is open for future studies integrating fast-timescale modalities, computational modeling, and clinically relevant datasets.
