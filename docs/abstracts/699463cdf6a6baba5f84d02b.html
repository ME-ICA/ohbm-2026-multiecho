Introduction

Up to half of dementia cases may be attributable to potentially modifiable lifestyle and vascular risk factors, such as high cholesterol, depression, and diabetes. Midlife represents a critical window when many of such factors emerge.1 To develop effective prevention strategies, it is important to understand the mechanisms linking these risk factors to dementia. One such mechanism is cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR), a key index of brain vascular health that reflects the ability of cerebral blood vessels to dilate in response to a vasoactive challenge. While brain vascular impairments are known to precede neurodegeneration,2 the role of CVR in the pathway from dementia risk in midlife to later brain atrophy is relatively less commonly investigated. In this study, we examined whether hippocampal CVR contributes to the association between midlife dementia risk and later-life structural brain health in older adults.

Methods

We analysed data from 154 participants in the Whitehall II Heart and Brain Study. Midlife dementia risk was assessed using the UK Biobank Dementia Risk Score (UKBDRS) based on retrospective health records collected at a mean age of 52.6±4.3 years. This score combines 11 vascular and lifestyle factors, including hypertension, high cholesterol, diabetes, depression, and history of stroke. CVR and structural brain health were measured using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on average 24.3 years later (mean age 76.9±4.4 years). CVR was assessed with blood oxygenation level dependent functional MRI during a 5% carbon dioxide inhalation challenge. Grey matter volume (GMV) was quantified from T1-weighted structural MRI, and white matter (WM) microstructure integrity was derived from diffusion tensor imaging using fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD) and axial diffusivity (L1). For CVR and GMV, values were extracted from total brain grey matter and the bilateral hippocampi. For WM microstructure, we examined a priori four major tracts: corpus callosum, cingulum bundle, fornix, and internal capsule, as well as whole-brain metrics. Linear regression models were used to test associations of 1) midlife dementia risk with later-life CVR, GMV and WM microstructure; 2) CVR with GMV and WM structure. Finally, we used mediation models to examine whether CVR mediated the associations between midlife UKBDRS and later-life brain structure (riskCVRGM; riskCVRWM). Covariates including age, sex, time interval between midlife and later-life, and brain size were adjusted accordingly. 

Results

Higher UKBDRS was associated with lower later-life CVR, lower GMV and poorer WM integrity in several brain regions. Specifically, UKBDRS was linked with lower CVR in the left (β = -0.017, p = 0.012) and right (β = -0.016, p = 0.018) hippocampus. Individuals with higher UKBDRS also showed smaller grey matter volumes in the whole-brain (β = -11.791, p = 0.008) and hippocampi (left: β = -0.131, p = 0.007; right: β = -0.085, p = 0.024). Higher UKBDRS was associated with reduced white matter microstructure integrity across multiple tracts. In the whole-brain and corpus callosum, higher UKBDRS predicted lower FA and higher MD, RD and L1 (all p < 0.001). In the cingulum and fornix, higher UKBDRS was associated with lower FA and higher MD and RD (all p < 0.035). In the internal capsule, higher UKBDRS was associated with higher MD, RD and L1 (all p < 0.046).

Lower left hippocampal CVR was associated with smaller left hippocampal volume (β = 1.212, p = 0.016). Lower mean hippocampal CVR was also associated with poorer WM microstructure integrity in the fornix (lower FA, higher MD), corpus callosum (lower FA and higher MD and RD), and internal capsule (higher MD and L1) (all p < 0.05). 

Mediation results (Figure 1) showed that lower left hippocampal CVR mediated the association between higher UKBDRS and smaller left hippocampal GMV (p = 0.045). Lower mean hippocampal CVR also mediated the associations between higher UKBDRS and poorer WM microstructure. Significant indirect effects were observed for lower FA (p = 0.041) and higher MD (p = 0.028) in the corpus callosum, lower FA in the fornix (p = 0.025), and higher MD in the internal capsule (p = 0.025).

Conclusion

Our findings highlighted a potential pathway through which midlife dementia risk contributed to later-life structural brain vulnerability (grey matter volume and white matter microstructure integrity) via reduced hippocampal CVR. This pathway suggests a key role for hippocampal CVR as a novel vascular marker of brain structural vulnerability in ageing.
