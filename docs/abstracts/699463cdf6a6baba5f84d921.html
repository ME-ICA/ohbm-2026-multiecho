Introduction

Lesion–symptom mapping identifies brain–behavior relationships but often misses the impact of small, strategic, or distributed lesions. Structural disconnection mapping improves anatomical sensitivity by capturing network-level effects, yet it remains largely associational and vulnerable to multicollinearity, reflecting the spatial correlation of lesions imposed by vascular anatomy1. In contrast, inverse inference provides a conceptually distinct approach, offering higher anatomical specificity and enabling quantitative predictive metrics, such as voxel-wise accuracy. In this study, we developed and evaluated a decoding framework based on lesion-derived disconnection maps to predict a spectrum of post-stroke deficits, moving from descriptive association toward clinically actionable prediction.

Methods

Structural imaging and clinical data were collected from 340 stroke patients (mean age 63.9 ± 10.5 years) enrolled in the multicenter GRECogVASC cohort2. All patients were assessed six months post-stroke at Amiens University Hospital and exhibited motor, executive, and/or processing speed deficits. Motor impairments were observed in 64 patients (35 left-sided, 32 right-sided), executive function deficits in 118 patients, and processing speed deficits in 137 patients.

Our analysis used two complementary inference steps—forward and inverse inference—based on probabilistic structural disconnection maps, which estimate individual disconnection profiles from normative tractography data. To address class imbalance and improve estimation stability, bootstrap bagging was applied, generating 20 balanced patient bags, each containing equal numbers of patients with and without the corresponding deficit.

For forward inference, lesions were manually segmented on 3D T1-weighted MRI and normalized to the MNI152 template. Whole-brain deterministic tractography was then performed using high-resolution diffusion-weighted imaging data from more than 400 healthy individuals (mean age = 62.87 ± 13.47 years) from the CamCAN dataset3. For each patient, white matter streamlines intersecting the lesion mask were removed from the tractography data of healthy subjects matched within five years of the patient’s age. A probabilistic structural disconnection map was then generated, with voxel values ranging from 0 to 1 representing the proportion of healthy subjects exhibiting disconnection at each location3.

Within each bootstrap sample, forward inference was conducted using Support Vector Regression Lesion-Symptom Mapping (SVR-LSM) to identify disconnection voxels associated with each functional deficit3. Analyses were performed on probabilistic disconnection maps while controlling for lesion volume and total disconnection volume. Statistical significance was assessed using 1,000 permutation tests with a threshold of p < 0.05. Resulting maps were binarized to define regions of interest for decoding. A final decoding mask was generated across all bootstrap bags by retaining voxels that survived in at least 4 bags as the minimum requirement for lesion symptom mapping. Searchlight-based multivariate pattern decoding was then applied within each bootstrap bag. Decoding was restricted to the final decoding mask and implemented with a 3 mm radius searchlight and a quadratic discriminant analysis classifier, which classified patients with and without deficits at the voxel level using a five-fold cross-validation scheme.  Majority voting across bootstrap bags produced accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity maps, identifying disconnection voxels with above-chance (50% for binary decoding) performance for motor, executive, and processing speed impairments.

Results

Motor Deficits:

Searchlight-based decoding revealed that left and right motor deficits were predominantly associated with contralateral corticospinal and projection pathways (Fig. 1). For left-hand deficits, the largest clusters were the right corticospinal tract (308 voxels; accuracy ≈ 0.73) and right internal capsule (665 voxels; accuracy ≈ 0.78), followed by the right cortico-ponto-cerebellar tract and arcuate fasciculus. All regions showed high specificity (>0.72) and moderate sensitivity (~0.84), indicating reliable discrimination of patients with left motor deficits. For right-hand deficits, the left corticospinal tract (10,94 voxels; accuracy ≈ 0.70), internal capsule, and paracentral lobule were the primary contributors, while the arcuate fasciculus, cortico-ponto-cerebellar tract, and inferior longitudinal fasciculus likely represent nonspecific associations. These results confirm that motor impairments are best predicted by disruption of core motor pathways, with additional involvement of associated projection fibers.

 

Executive Function Deficits:

Decoding of executive function deficits5 identified distributed disconnections within left-lateralized fronto-striato-thalamic and associative networks (Fig. 1). The largest clusters were in long-range tracts, including the inferior occipito-frontal fasciculus and inferior longitudinal fasciculus (>1,600 voxels; accuracy ≈ 0.56–0.60), followed by intermediate clusters in the corticospinal tract, arcuate fasciculus, internal capsule, and optic radiations (300–600 voxels; accuracy ≈ 0.57). Smaller clusters appeared in subcortical and interhemispheric structures such as the corpus callosum, posterior segment, uncinate fasciculus, and putamen (<200 voxels; accuracy ≈ 0.54–0.58). These findings indicate that executive dysfunction arises from disconnection of large-scale associative and projection networks, consistent with their role in cognitive control and integration.

 

Processing-Speed Deficits:

Decoding of processing-speed deficits5 revealed widespread left-hemispheric involvement across major projection and associative pathways (Fig. 1). The largest clusters were the inferior occipito-frontal fasciculus, inferior longitudinal fasciculus, and corticospinal tract (>3,000 voxels; accuracy ≈ 0.59–0.60), with additional large clusters in the optic radiations and internal capsule (900–1,900 voxels). Intermediate clusters were observed in the corpus callosum, arcuate fasciculus, and uncinate fasciculus (500–700 voxels), and smaller clusters appeared in the putamen, insula, fornix, and cortico-ponto-cerebellar pathway (<350 voxels). These results suggest that processing-speed deficits reflect disruptions in distributed sensorimotor, associative, and thalamo-cortical networks, highlighting the integrative nature of rapid cognitive processing.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that lesion-derived structural disconnection reliably predicts domain-specific post-stroke deficits with anatomically meaningful patterns. Motor impairments were primarily associated with disruption of contralateral corticospinal pathways, highlighting the central role of primary motor circuitry. Executive function deficits mapped onto left-lateralized fronto-striato-thalamic and long-range associative networks, emphasizing the importance of distributed tracts such as the IFOF and arcuate fasciculus for cognitive control. Processing-speed deficits were linked to widespread left-hemispheric sensorimotor and thalamo-cortical connections, supporting the notion that efficient white-matter integration underlies rapid cognitive processing. Overall, these results confirm that searchlight-based decoding provides a robust framework for linking lesion location to functional impairment and offers a predictive approach that extends beyond traditional lesion-symptom mapping.
