Introduction

The anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) is an established target for deep brain stimulation (DBS) in drug-resistant epilepsy (1); however, its network-level mechanisms remain poorly understood. Previous studies have implicated the ANT in limbic and cognitive circuits (2), but the extent to which it interacts with large-scale functional networks—and how these interactions are altered in focal epilepsy—has not been fully characterized. This study applied advanced network analysis techniques to elucidate the functional connectivity architecture of the ANT in healthy individuals and to determine its disruption in focal epilepsy. By comparing the ANT with other clinically relevant thalamic and subcortical nuclei, we aimed to identify unique network control properties that may underlie the therapeutic effects and side-effect profiles of ANT-DBS.

Methods

Study 1 (Healthy controls): Resting-state fMRI data were obtained from 60 healthy adults aged 40–60 years. Functional connectivity was quantified using our original method, the functional connectivity overlap ratio (FCOR) (3), which measures the degree of coupling between a seed region and predefined intrinsic functional networks. FCOR values were computed for the ANT relative to 14 large-scale brain networks. For comparison, identical analyses were conducted for the centromedian nucleus (CM) and subthalamic nucleus (STN), both recognized as alternative targets for epilepsy control.

Study 2 (Focal epilepsy): Resting-state fMRI data were collected from 59 patients with focal epilepsy, including mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) and frontal lobe epilepsy. ANT FCOR values were compared with those of age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Group-level differences were assessed across networks, and clinical correlations were evaluated using cognitive performance scores.

Results

In healthy participants, the ANT exhibited strong connectivity with the basal ganglia network, salience network, and multiple higher-order cognitive networks, demonstrating a connectivity profile distinct from both the CM and STN. In patients with focal epilepsy, ANT connectivity with the basal ganglia and salience networks was significantly reduced, with the most pronounced reductions observed in MTLE. Furthermore, decreased connectivity within the salience network correlated significantly with lower cognitive scores, suggesting a functional relationship between ANT-network disruption and cognitive impairment.

Conclusion

In focal epilepsy, the salience network detects abnormal seizure activity and regulates arousal and network switching; its disruption facilitates seizure propagation and contributes to cognitive impairment (4). The basal ganglia act as a “braking system,” and their dysfunction possibly exacerbates seizures (5). In the present study, the ANT functioned as an intra-thalamic hub integrating diverse large-scale networks, particularly those involving basal ganglia and salience-processing systems. Focal epilepsy—especially MTLE—disrupts these network interactions, potentially explaining both the therapeutic mechanisms and the cognitive or behavioral side effects associated with ANT-DBS. These findings provide insight into the ANT’s network-control role and highlight its relevance for individualized neuromodulation strategies in epilepsy.
