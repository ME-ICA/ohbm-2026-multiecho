Introduction

MRI has been used to describe resting-state brain connectivity differences in people with Parkinson’s disease (PwPD) compared with older adults (OA). MRI is considered the gold standard, but many PwPD do not meet the inclusion criteria to undergo this costly imaging technique. Thus, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a promising tool that can include a broader spectrum and is less costly. 

We aim to describe differences we obtain in the functional connectivity and replicate resting-state functional connectivity differences between PwPD and OA found in resting-state MRI. We hypothesized that there is a lower eigenvector centrality in PwPD than in OA in the channels corresponding to the superior temporal gyrus, the supramarginal gyrus, and the inferior parietal lobe1,2.

Methods

Data of twenty-seven PwPD (mean age 69.3±6.7) and 30 OA (mean age 69.3±7.4) were collected during a single experimental session at the uMOVE core facility, Karolinska University Hospital, Solna, Sweden, as part of the “Park-MOVE” trial (doi.org/10.48723/vscr-eq07). A NIRSport2 (NIRx) system with 16 sources, 16 detectors, and eight short-separation detectors transmitted light at 760 and 850 nm. Two measurements of 10 min were used to cover the left and right brain hemispheres (Figure 1). Clinical tests of balance (Mini Balance Evaluation Systems Test), global cognition (Montreal Cognitive Assessment), and self-report questionnaires related to health status were collected (gait ability: Walk-12G, anxiety and depression: Hospital Depression and Anxiety Scale, Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, and for the PwPD group Movement Disorder Society sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS)).

MATLAB NIRS AnalyzIR toolbox 3. Raw data was trimmed 10s at the start and end of the measurement to prevent noise from position adjustments. Applying the modified Beer-Lambert law, raw data was converted into optical density and oxygenated hemoglobin (ΔHbO2) and de-oxygenated hemoglobin (ΔHHb). Data quality was assessed using MNE-NIRS (v0.7.1) and the scalp coupling index. Eigenvector centrality computation was done in BRAPH2.0 (v2.0.0.b2). Correlation matrices were entered into the Connectivity Comparison weighted undirected pipeline. Brain atlases for BRAPH2.0 were created by exporting the projected MNI coordinates of the montage channels via AtlasViewer (v2.44.0). Differences were compared with non-parametric permutation tests (10000 permutations), using an alpha level two-tailed t-test at p < .05. To correct for multiple comparisons on the network level, false discovery rate (FDR) was applied at q < .05. The study and hypotheses were preregistered (osf.io/wtsy7/overview?view_only=a8b2c4e92aa440cc87c9448d2e03bd04).

Results

We could include 42 channels with good signal quality. Descriptively higher and lower eigenvector centrality was found widespread in the left and right hemispheres when comparing PwPD to OA. Permutation tests showed a single region with significantly lower eigenvector centrality at MNI coordinates (-39 -54 46). This region did not remain significant after FDR correction. Exploratory results showed a significant lower eigenvector centrality after FDR correction of Core-Periphery value in the prefrontal region in the left hemisphere (Figure 2).

Conclusion

We partially replicated resting-state MRI eigenvector centrality obtained network patterns in PwPD compared with OA but also found unexpected increased resting-state activity. Reasons for this can also be a slight difference in how eigenvector centrality is calculated as well as many other factors such as disease stage or medication status.
