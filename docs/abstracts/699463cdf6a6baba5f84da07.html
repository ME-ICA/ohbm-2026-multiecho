Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) is a leading cause of functional disability worldwide.^1 Negative symptoms are a major predictor of functional impairment in SZ, yet progress in their treatment has been limited, partly because their underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Emotional memory impairment is one mechanism that may contribute to negative symptoms in SZ. Emotional memories play an important role in promoting goal-directed, recreational, and social activities. Past studies indicate that impaired encoding and retrieval of emotional stimuli has been linked to worse negative symptoms, particularly the motivation and pleasure dimension (MAP; i.e., anhedonia, avolition, asociality). Emotional memory deficits may explain an apparent “anhedonia paradox” in SZ, whereby individuals with SZ display normal self-reports of positive emotion to pleasant stimuli, yet report diminished experience of positive emotion during clinical interviews that rely on retrospective reports of pleasure and place demands on episodic memory. Although past behavioral evidence provides a strong link between encoding and retrieval impairments for emotional stimuli and negative symptoms in SZ, the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying these deficits remain unclear. The present study used a well-validated validated functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) paradigm to examine the hypothesis that aberrant activation of prefrontal and medial temporal regions during encoding would contribute to emotional memory impairments at immediate and long-delay intervals and their association with negative symptoms in SZ.

Methods

24 individuals with schizophrenia (SZ) and 18 healthy controls (CN) completed interview-based negative symptom rating scales, emotional experience ratings, an incidental memory encoding fMRI paradigm where they passively viewed stimuli, and an unexpected immediate and 24-hour delay Remember-Know-New recognition memory task. During fMRI, participants passively viewed 30 pleasant, 30 unpleasant, and 30 neutral International Affective Picture System (IAPS) stimuli in four 30s blocks of 4s images per condition.^2 Following this encoding phase, an immediate recognition memory paradigm was administered with 90 targets and 45 foils from the IAPS set. This was repeated after a 24-hour delay with 45 new foils. Participants also rated valence and arousal for all images. Negative symptoms were measured via the Brief Negative Symptom Scale (BNSS), an interview-based symptom rating scale.

Scans were conducted using a 3T GE Signa HDx 3.0T MRI scanner with a 32-element quadrature phased-array coil (Invivo). A whole brain T1-weighted FSPGR volume was acquired (TR/TE = 7800/3000ms, 256x256 matrix size in a 256x256 field of view) with 160 1mm-thick sagittal slices. Two 4min whole brain T2*-weighted gradient echo echoplanar (EPI) series were acquired (TR/TE = 2000/30ms, 64x64 matrix, 192x192mm field of view) with 55 interleaved 3 mm thick axial slices.

An afni_proc.py pipeline was used^3, including slice-time correction, registration to the minimal outlier volume, censoring outlier volumes and those exhibiting >.35mm inter-volume movement, alignment to the T1 volume in Talairach space, and application of a 5mm full-width half-maximum Gaussian filter. Participants with >25% censored volumes from any condition were excluded from further analysis. Voxelwise GLM modeling was used to quantify individual brain responses while encoding pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral stimuli that were later remembered.

One sample T-tests were used to quantify responses in a priori regions of interest (ROI) previously associated with emotion memory in healthy individuals^4,5. These included the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC), ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), and areas of the medial temporal lobe (MTL). All ROIs exhibited significant activation responses at the group level (FDR corrected p < .05) and thus, were all included in further analyses.

2 x 3 Group x Valence ANOVAs were used to examine hit rates at immediate and 24-hour delay. Independent sample t-tests were used to assess differences in activation patterns between SZ and CN groups. Linear regression models were used to examine whether prefrontal and MTL activation during passive viewing of incidentally encoded emotional stimuli predicted group. Hit rate was included as a covariate to account for individual variability in memory performance, thereby allowing for assessment of group differences in neural activation independent of encoding success.

Results

The SZ group rated pleasant stimuli as significantly less positive than the CN group (t(34) = -2.09, p =.04), while groups did not differ on ratings of positive emotion for unpleasant and neutral stimuli (Unpleasant: t(27) = -1.53, p = .14; Neutral: t(26) = -1.78, p =.09). The SZ and CN groups did not statistically differ in behavioral ratings of negative emotion to pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral stimuli (.66 < p < .94). Group arousal ratings for pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral stimuli did not differ (.42 < p < .73).

Immediate recognition memory differed across stimulus valence conditions such that memory for pleasant stimuli was greater than neutral (p = .03) and memory for unpleasant stimuli was greater than neutral (p = .002; F(2, 120) = 6.46, p = .002). Therefore, the expected emotion memory modulation effect was observed for both groups at immediate assessment. Hit rates for pleasant and unpleasant stimuli did not differ at immediate assessment (p = .61). The SZ group evidenced similar hits at immediate recognition memory testing compared to CN (82% versus 87%, respectively; F(1, 120) = 2.73, p = .11). Delay recognition memory did not differ across stimulus valence conditions, such that there was no emotion memory modulation effect at delay (F(2, 117) = 0.80, p = .45). The SZ group had poorer performance at delay, such that they had fewer hit rates after a 24-hour delay compared to CN (86% versus 95%, respectively; F(1, 117) = 8.79, p = .004). There were no significant Group x Valence interactions at immediate assessment or delay, indicating that the effect of valence on hit rate did not differ by group at either time point (Immediate: F(2, 120) = 0.02, p = .98; Delay: F(2, 117) = 0.24, p = .78).

Greater left dlPFC activation during the incidental encoding task predicted better recognition memory for all stimulus valence conditions, regardless of group (Table 1). In addition, the SZ group showed abnormal prefrontal engagement during successful encoding of stimuli across pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral stimuli (Table 1). Specifically, the SZ group exhibited significantly greater left dlPFC activation during successful encoding of neutral stimuli compared to CN. This effect remained significant after controlling for memory performance during the recognition memory task. Results also indicate increased right IFG recruitment during encoding of unpleasant compared to neutral stimuli in SZ relative to CN, after controlling for memory performance. Further, among the SZ group activation in the right IFG was lower during encoding of pleasant versus unpleasant stimuli with similar memory performance compared to CN. This difference between right IFG activation during encoding of pleasant versus unpleasant stimuli was significantly associated with self-reported positive emotion for neutral stimuli and worse negative symptoms in SZ.

Memory performance, emotional experience, and brain activity during successfully encoded stimuli were significantly associated with negative symptoms in SZ (Table 2). Specifically, more severe negative symptoms were associated with lower hit rate at 24-hour delay and greater intensity of self-reported negative emotion to pleasant and neutral stimuli. Reduced activation in the vmPFC and dlPFC during encoding of unpleasant compared to neutral stimuli was significantly associated with greater negative symptoms in the SZ group. Greater activation in the vmPFC and dlPFC during encoding of pleasant compared to negative stimuli was significantly associated with greater negative symptoms in the SZ group. Reduced activation in the vlPFC during encoding of pleasant and unpleasant stimuli was significantly associated with greater negative symptoms in the SZ group. Both lateral PFC effects were driven by the motivation/pleasure dimension of negative symptoms.

Conclusion

Overall, behavioral findings replicated earlier results, suggesting that people with SZ do not display diminished arousal to pleasant stimuli compared to CN. However, a hedonic deficit was observed in the SZ group, such that SZ rated pleasant stimuli as less positive relative to CN. Findings indicate that incidental emotional recognition memory deficits are linked to abnormalities in emotional experience and negative symptoms in SZ. Imaging findings replicate the importance of the left PFC in emotion memory encoding.^4 Differences in brain activity observed between groups indicate that SZ show differential neural reactivity to pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral stimuli during the passive viewing task, and that this activation predicts subsequent recognition memory immediately after encoding. Neural differences between groups largely center on two prefrontal regions: the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and inferior frontal gyrus (IFG). Across participants, dlPFC engagement showed a memory-dependent effect. Greater dlPFC activation during incidental encoding predicted better recognition memory for pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral stimuli. This pattern suggests that successful encoding of emotional and non-emotional stimuli relies on dlPFC-mediated elaborative processing in both groups. Although memory-dependent dlPFC engagement was intact in SZ, SZ showed greater dlPFC recruitment during incidental encoding of non-emotional stimuli, reflecting excessive reactivity in a region important for cognitive control. Overactivation in the lateral PFC in SZ suggests increased effort in interpreting the neutral images or regulating emotional responses. Research has found that right IFG activity is sensitive to unpleasant emotional stimuli in healthy controls.^4 Our findings showed that this valence-specific pattern in the IFG was exaggerated in SZ. Compared to CN, SZ showed greater reactivity in the IFG while viewing unpleasant remembered stimuli and blunted reactivity in the IFG while viewing pleasant remembered stimuli. Importantly, reduced reactivity in the IFG while viewing pleasant relative to unpleasant remembered stimuli was associated with lower self-reported positive emotion for neutral stimuli and more severe negative symptoms in SZ. Hyporesponsivity within this prefrontal region to pleasant stimuli in SZ suggests less engagement of neural regions involved in inhibitory control during encoding of pleasant stimuli. Additional aberrant prefrontal activation during emotional evaluation of remembered emotional stimuli was associated with negative symptoms in SZ. Further, the pattern of prefrontal activation and negative symptom associations was valence-specific for the vmPFC and dlPFC, but arousal-specific (i.e., similar for pleasant and unpleasant stimuli compared to neutral) for the vlPFC. More severe negative symptoms were linked to increased reactivity in the vmPFC and dlPFC during unpleasant versus neutral incidental encoding and reduced reactivity in these same regions during pleasant versus unpleasant incidental encoding. In contrast, reduced reactivity in the vlPFC was associated with greater negative symptoms during encoding of both pleasant and unpleasant stimuli compared to neutral, suggesting a more generalized deficit in ventrolateral prefrontal mechanisms supporting emotional salience processing underlying negative symptoms. Together, findings suggest that SZ is associated with differential neural reactivity to pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral stimuli, and that these levels of activation predict subsequent recognition memory immediately after encoding. The results further support theoretical models proposing a role for emotional memory in negative symptoms and suggest that neuromodulation techniques, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) or cognitive training targeting emotional memory, may be effective areas for intervention in this population.
