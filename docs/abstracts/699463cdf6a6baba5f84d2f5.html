Introduction

During the FMRI Open QC Project [1], Reynolds et al. (2023) [2] first noted stripes of low TSNR in one site's subjects and then used the AFNI [3] GUI's InstaCorr tool (instant correlation) to reveal extended, nonphysiological patterns of strong correlation and anticorrelation associated with the stripes, for all subjects in that collection. As a result, all datasets for this site were classified as warranting exclusion for a standard group analysis.

Since then we have investigated this phenomenon and noticed a surprising feature that was systematically present in those raw FMRI datasets: at least one narrow line (typically several, when present) of high variance through the z-axis of the EPI dataset. We have since developed a tool in AFNI to detect these variance lines (aptly named find_variance_lines.tcsh) and to systematically study datasets that have them; they appear to all have problematically strong patterns of artifactual time series correlation both along the high-variance lines and throughout other regions in the data. To our knowledge, this artifact has not been reported previously, and we term these "variance line artifacts". While the presence of any strong artifactual signal across FMRI data is concerning, it is particularly problematic for resting state and naturalistic paradigms, which typically depend on spatial correlation patterns for interpretation of results. 

Here we present a description of this variance line artifact, of AFNI's find_variance_lines.tcsh tool, and of the prevalence of this artifact in publicly available data collections.

Methods

The basic input for AFNI's find_variance_lines.tcsh is one FMRI time series dataset in its original space.  The algorithm proceeds as follows:

  • estimate a slightly eroded data mask for the EPI volume

  • detrend each voxel time series and estimate its variance

  • estimate the 90th %ile value of variance within the mask volume

  • scale each voxel's variance value by the 90th %ile value, with a ceiling of 1

  • calculate the mean of the scaled variance along each z-axis within the eroded mask

  • label any column as "bad" if its mean is above a threshold (default: 0.97).

Since the variance pattern across any given EPI volume is generally non-uniform (e.g., due to motion effects at the edge of the brain, B0 inhomogeneity, scanner distortions, midline brightness, and coil variability), it is possible that non-artifactual features can be flagged by this tool. Because the variance line artifact is highly detrimental to FMRI results, the default settings have been chosen to have higher sensitivity for artifact detection, with lower sensitivity to false alarms. To help the user distinguish these, this program also outputs automatic images of the sagittal slice of the scaled variance map, for manual quality control (QC) evaluation.

Using this new AFNI tool, we investigated the prevalence of the variance line artifact by looking at subsets of up to ten subjects from each of 193 data collections in popularly used data repositories: 33 in the Functional Connectome Project (FCP), 24 in ABIDE-1 and 19 in ABIDE-2, and 117 in OpenNeuro. Here we provide examples of variance lines and related spatiotemporal artifacts, as well as preliminary summary statistics across the examined collections.

Results

Panels A and B in Fig 1 each show an example of finding variance lines with find_variance_lines.tcsh and associated artifactual features in the data from one example subject. These two subjects come from different publicly available repositories. Row i shows sagittal slices from the scaled variance map from find_variance_lines.tcsh, with the top and bottom of potential variance line columns highlighted in red. Row ii shows a seedbased correlation map using AFNI's InstaCorr within the raw data, highlighting the extended spatiotemporal patterns of artifactual correlation. Row iii shows how these exist as well in the final processed data. Thus, the artifacts would affect any further analyses with these data.

We applied this program to check data across sites in several widely used repositories and found a large fraction of data collections containing datasets with this artifact. For example, 6 of 24 collections in ABIDE-1 and 5 of 19 in ABIDE-2 appeared to have at least one subject with variance lines. 8 of 33 FCP collections contained some examples, as did at least 15 of 117 OpenNeuro sites.

Conclusion

While reproducibility remains an area of concern in FMRI, QC of datasets has generally been overlooked as an area for improvement. It should have more emphasis in the field. We are still assessing the extent of the effects of the variance line artifacts across repositories, but variance line artifacts appear to be present in a surprisingly large fraction of data collections.

This check for variance lines, and the ability to run InstaCorr on datasets, have been integrated into AFNI's afni_proc.py processing pipeline [4] and automatic quality control HTML [5].
