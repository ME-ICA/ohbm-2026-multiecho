Introduction

In most fMRI-research neural phenomena are studied within groups, however, the group does not have emotions, it is the individual that harbors this intimate experience. Explaining emotions by a hypothetical average gives insight into overlapping neural activation patterns, failing to explain how emotional experience arises within the unique, history-dependent physiology of individual people. Deep-imaging offers way to circumvent these averaging artifacts of conventional group studies, which tend to be obscured by between-person variability.^1

Emotional experience and emotion recognition are tightly linked to how everyone learns to predict the outer world based on one’s inner world. According to contemporary constructivist accounts emotions arise through context-sensitive predictions that integrate bodily and environmental states, prior experiences, and conceptual knowledge. From this perspective emotion recognition is not a fixed stimulus-response mapping but is an act of interpreting another person’s emotional state from one’s own learned affective model.^2

Most neuroimaging work on emotion recognition has relied on group-level analyses, which identify common hubs but provide limited insight into how the distributed networks supporting emotion perception are organized within individuals. 

The present study uses a combination of deep imaging, single-subject design with multivariate decoding of audiovisual emotion recognition to show stable, separable multivariate patterns within subjects, across scanning sessions and if decoding scores based on these patterns achieve high, permutation-validated accuracy. Demonstrating such separability would provide evidence for consistent neural patterns underlying distinct emotional decisions and a proof of concept for a single-N approach in affective neuroscience.

Methods

Participants

Data were collected from two healthy, right-handed adults (subject 1: female, subject 2: male, both aged 25 years). Inclusion criteria were Swedish language proficiency, age 18–30 years, and absence of neurological or psychiatric diagnoses. Subject 1 and 2 completed 19 and 23 respective sessions. The mean inter-session interval was 2.58 days for subject 1 and 1 day for subject 2; two longer gaps (24 and 13 days) occurred for subject 1. Some sessions were acquired on the same day (subject 1: 8; subject 2: 11).

Stimuli and Tasks

Stimuli were recordings of actors expressing nine emotions (anger, anxiety, disgust, sadness, neutral, happiness, interest, pride, relief). Each emotion had four unique recordings; neutral had eight, yielding 40 clips in three modalities (audio, video, audiovisual). Twelve sets of 40 clips were created, one per session, and reused after session 12. Each session comprised three tasks: passive viewing (always first), forced-choice emotion recognition, and valence/arousal rating (randomized). All 40 clips appeared in all modalities per task (120 trials per task). Each trial included a 4‑s modality cue, stimulus presentation (mean 2.99 s), and a 4‑s response screen.

Procedure

Before scanning, participants completed standard cognitive, personality, and emotion-understanding questionnaires. Instructions and emotion definitions were reviewed, and response procedures were practiced. Each session began with a 10‑min resting-state run followed by the task runs.

MRI Acquisition

Data were acquired on a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3T (20‑channel head coil). Functional images were collected using gradient-echo EPI (TR 2.02 s, TE 27 ms, 2×2×2 mm³ voxels, 68 slices, multiband factor 2). Each session included one resting-state (297 volumes) and three task runs (580 volumes). T1‑weighted images (1×1×1 mm³) were obtained.

Preprocessing and GLM Analysis

Data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep v24.0.1, including motion correction, slice-timing correction, susceptibility distortion correction, coregistration and resampling to MNI152 (2009c) space. A 7‑mm FWHM spatial smoothing kernel was applied. Beta maps for each emotion and modality were estimated in SPM12 using a block design from stimulus onset to response; six motion parameters were included as regressors.

Multivariate Pattern Analysis

Decoding was performed separately within each subject and focused on emotion pairs (anger vs happiness, disgust vs interest, anxiety vs pride). Analyses used only beta maps from the recognition task in the audiovisual modality. A primary decoder implemented a linear SVM (LinearSVC; C=0.1, max_iter=30,000) to obtain ROC AUC scores. A secondary decoder with identical settings used hard-label predictions to quantify accuracy and derive F1-scores. Both decoders operated on masked data comprising Jülich‑Brain V1, primary auditory cortex, and 29 spherical ROIs (12‑mm radius) centered on coordinates implicated in emotion recognition based on meta-study results^3. Feature selection used voxelwise ANOVA F‑tests within each training fold (20% of voxels for primary, 5% for permutation tests). Nilearn’s mask-corrected percentiles were 100 and 32.5, respectively. Cross-validation followed a leave-one-session-out scheme with balanced classes. Statistical significance was assessed with 1,000 Monte‑Carlo permutations per subject and emotion pair, repeating the full cross-validation pipeline. Linear SVM weight maps were projected into voxelspace for each fold and averaged to get cross‑validated discriminative maps. For each binary contrast, we report mean ROC AUC, accuracy, F1, and confusion matrices.

Imbalances

The experimental design was identical across sessions and subjects. Residual imbalances concerned the number and spacing of sessions and occasional same‑day acquisitions. Neutral stimuli were overrepresented to balance positive and negative categories; no stimuli were missing, and no trials were excluded beyond session-level quality control.

Results

Across all three binary contrasts (anger vs happiness, disgust vs interest, anxiety vs pride), decoding yielded high classification performance in both participants. Subject 1 showed ceiling or near‑ceiling performance for all contrasts (ROC AUC = 1.00 ± 0.00; accuracy = 1.00), while Subject 2 reached slightly lower but still robust levels (ROC AUC range 0.87–1.00; accuracy range 0.91–0.96). Macro F1 closely matched accuracy in both subjects, indicating balanced performance across classes.

For anger versus happiness, Subject 1 achieved perfect discrimination (ROC AUC = 1.00 ± 0.00; accuracy = 1.00), and Subject 2 also showed high performance (ROC AUC = 0.87 ± 0.34; accuracy = 0.91). For disgust versus interest and anxiety versus pride, both subjects exhibited near‑perfect separability, with ROC AUC = 1.00 ± 0.00 in all cases; accuracies were 1.00 for Subject 1 and 0.96 for Subject 2. Permutation tests confirmed that decoding performance was significantly above chance for all contrasts in both subjects, with p‑values ≤ 0.004 and permutation accuracies closely matching the observed accuracies, indicating that classification success cannot be attributed to label structure alone.

Weight‑map peak analysis revealed compact and interpretable spatial patterns. In Subject 1, the three strongest positive clusters for happiness were located in right temporal–occipital fusiform cortex, right posterior middle temporal gyrus and left angular gyrus, while the strongest negative clusters were centered on left lingual gyrus, left frontal orbital cortex and right frontal operculum. For recognizing interest, positive peaks were located in the right anterior parahippocampal gyrus, left temporal pole and left posterior superior temporal gyrus, with corresponding negative peaks in central opercular cortex, right intracalcarine cortex and right middle frontal gyrus. For pride, positive clusters were maximal in left lingual gyrus, left temporo‑occipital middle temporal gyrus and left frontal pole, whereas negative clusters were situated in left temporal fusiform cortex, left occipital pole and right angular gyrus.

In Subject 2 for happiness, positive clusters were centered in right lingual gyrus and left inferior temporal gyrus in the temporo‑occipital junction, and left centromedial amygdala while negative clusters localized to left inferior temporal cortex and left inferior frontal gyrus. For interest, the three most strongly weighted positive clusters were in left central opercular cortex, left primary auditory cortex Heschl’s gyrus and left cuneal cortex, whereas the largest negative clusters were located in left lingual gyrus, left central opercular, and left posterior inferior temporal gyrus. For pride, positive clusters peaked in left temporo‑occipital inferior temporal gyrus, right occipital fusiform gyrus and right anterior inferior temporal gyrus, with negative clusters in left temporal fusiform cortex, right occipital pole and right central opercular cortex.

Confusion matrices averaged across cross‑validation folds revealed symmetric and well‑balanced classification patterns. In Subject 1, all three emotion pairs showed perfect classification with no misclassifications in either class. In Subject 2, misclassifications were rare and evenly distributed: for anger versus happiness, both classes were correctly classified on 91% of trials, and for disgust versus interest and anxiety versus pride, per‑class accuracies were 96%, with 4% of trials assigned to the opposite class.

Overall, the results demonstrate stable, highly discriminative multivariate neural patterns for multiple audiovisual emotion contrasts within individuals. Decoding generalized robustly across sessions under leave‑one‑session‑out cross‑validation, supporting the presence of session‑invariant neural signatures for the decoded emotions.

Conclusion

The present findings show that discrete emotion pairs can be decoded with very high accuracy within individuals when deep sampling is combined with an audiovisual recognition task. Across three binary contrasts, both participants reached near‑ceiling ROC AUC and accuracy, and permutation tests confirmed that these effects are highly unlikely under a label structure based on chance. This supports single‑subject approaches in affective neuroscience, where emotions are treated as properties of individual brains rather than group averages that blur potentially meaningful variability.

The robust within‑subject ability to decode suggests that some form of reliable neural signature across scanning sessions is present in each subject when recognizing emotions. By targeting audiovisual emotion labeling and using ROIs anchored in prior meta‑analytic work on perceptual decisions about emotions, the study shows that distributed sensory and association regions can distinguish not only broad valence contrasts but also more nuanced pairs such as disgust–interest and anxiety–pride. Using leave‑one‑session‑out validation extends a precision‑mapping design to decoding, yielding individual within‑subject neural activation of audiovisual emotion recognition that generalize across sessions.

Weight mappings for both subjects reveal which voxels the SVM algorithm found informative and thus allows a comparison of these patterns. Across emotions and subjects, regions recur and show superficial similarities. Directly comparing the positive and negative weights (with negative weights corresponding to the opposite emotion of each pair) of both subjects, however, suggests emotion-specific between-subject differences. For negative happiness-weightings (i.e. positive anger weights), Subject 1 shows dominantly peak weight-values in higher-order frontal regions, while the decoder relied on temporal and inferior frontal regions for Subject 2. For pride the negative weight both subjects show similar but lateralized patterns. Positive pride-weights in Subject 1 lie within the visual association and higher-order frontal regions, while higher weightings in Subject 2 suggest a pattern that spans the fusiform and temporal gyrus.

The main limitations are the small sample (two young adults) and the restriction to three binary contrasts from a single labeling task, which preclude population‑level claims and leave generalizability to other tasks and contexts open. Multivariate analysis methods, such as SVM, further bear some other caveats and limitations that are related to the data structure. First and foremost, the relation of features to samples in fMRI data is very large, which mainly leads to overfitting and optimistic accuracy estimates. As mentioned above this study does not make the attempt to derive a generalizable model, thus overfitting is a negligible concern. However, the possibility that the decoder fits systematic noise across sessions rather than signal is still present. The permutation testing results are a first step to validate the highly accurate decoding scores, but more effort is needed to increase explainability. In the future, this can be done by theory or data driven feature reduction (mean ROI signals, PCA). Another useful approach is the testing of counterfactuals in the form of subtracting important features from the data and show that the ability to decode decreases with increasing feature-loss.  

Nonetheless, the study provides a compact proof of concept that deep, within‑individual fMRI can reveal stable, person‑specific neural patterns for distinct emotional decisions, reinforcing calls to integrate single‑N designs into the core methodological toolkit of affective neuroscience.
