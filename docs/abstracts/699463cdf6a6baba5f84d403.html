Introduction

White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are areas of increased signal observed in T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) that indicate presence of cerebral small vessel disease and have been utilized as a marker of white matter (WM) pathology in multiple neurodegenerative diseases. In Parkinson’s disease, WMHs have been linked to increased future cognitive decline.1 In aging individuals with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), higher WMHs predict conversion to Alzheimer’s dementia.2 Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a terminal neurodegenerative disease characterized by progressive loss of motor function and has no cure. Disease progression in ALS has been linked to both grey and white matter (WM) atrophy, with sclerosis of the corticospinal and corticobulbar white matter tracts as a key pathological feature of Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS).3 Despite the presence of white matter pathology in ALS, and the well established link between WMHs as MRI markers of WM damage and disease progression is in the context of other diseases, WMHs remain unexplored in ALS. The present work examines the prevalence of WMHs as well as their link to disease progression and clinical outcomes in ALS patients. 

Methods

This study utilized longitudinal MRI and clinical data from 204 ALS patients and 164 healthy controls from the Canadian ALS Neuroimaging Consortium (CALSNIC) studies.4 We used structural T1-weighted (used for image registration and processing) and FLAIR MRIs (used for accurate detection of WMHs) as well as demographic and clinical information (Fig 1A). Participants with an alternate diagnosis, such as Primary Lateral Sclerosis, or a history of brain trauma or stroke were excluded (N = 40) . Participants initially recruited as controls that presented with cognitive impairment were also excluded (N = 10). Disease severity was assessed using the Revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS-R). Cognitive impairment was assessed using the North American version of the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS). All MRI images were processed using PELICAN, our previously employed and validated pre-processing pipeline which includes WMH segmentation using BISON tool (Fig 1B).5 All preprocessing steps and WMH segmentations were visually assessed and the cases that had significant artifacts or registration and segmentation errors were excluded from the analysis (N = 6). Total and lobar WMH loads (WMHL) were extracted in mm3 in the standard space. Lobar WMHL were defined using Hammer’s atlas and utilized as regions of interest throughout analyses. ALS patients with survival data (N = 128) were categorized as "short" (N = 50) or "long" (N= 78) survivors based on their time-to-outcome from the baseline MRI with a 24-month cut-off threshold. Baseline WMH burden was compared between patients and controls. Furthermore, the available longitudinal data was employed to investigate the difference in the rate of progression of WMH burden between patients and controls, and between “short” and “long” survivor groups. Linear mixed effects models were used to investigate the contribution of WMH increase to disease progression and cognitive deficits. The results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) controlling method. Statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB 2024b.

Results

Compared to healthy controls, and controlling for age and sex, ALS patients demonstrated 34.2% (β = 0.294, p < 0.005) greater WMHL at baseline (Fig. 2A). Repeating the same linear model for regional WMH measures, all regions of interest had significantly greater baseline WMHL in ALS patients than in healthy controls after FDR correction, ranging from 30.13% to 84.59% greater WMHL (βmin = 0.263, βmax = 0.613, p < 0.05). Within the ALS group, the baseline WMHL did not differ between the short and long survival group (Fig. 2B). Longitudinally, ALS patients experienced greater WMH progression (832.5 mm3/year, p < 0.001) during the study follow up time (Fig. 2C). This increased WMH progression was also significant in all ROIs after FDR correction, and the frontal regions showed the greatest difference in longitudinal progression of WMH between healthy controls and ALS patients (Left frontal: T-stat = 7.00, p < 0.005; Right frontal: T-stat = 6.61,  p < 0.005). Furthermore, within the ALS patients, those in the short survivors group experienced significantly greater WMH progression than those in the long survivors group (813.6 mm3/year, p < 0.01) (Fig. 2D). Linear mixed effect models investigating the contribution of WMH increase to disease progression in ALS patients based on ALSFRS-R scores revealed that a 464.3 mm3 increase in WMHL corresponded to a full point drop of the ALSFRS-R score (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2E). Regionally, this relationship had the greatest effect size in the right frontal area, where a 109.4 mm3 increase in WMHL corresponded to a full point drop of the ALSFRS-R score (T-stat = 4.625, p < 0.001).  Linear mixed effect models investigating the contribution of WMH burden and progression to cognition decline also revealed a significant interaction between ALS diagnosis and WMH progression, indicating a stronger association between change in WMH and cognition in ALS patients compared to matched controls (p = 0.005) (Fig. 2F). This interaction was statistically significant after FDR correction in the right frontal, left temporal, and bilateral parietal regions (p < 0.05). 

Conclusion

Our findings show, for the first time, a higher prevalence of WMHs in ALS patients compared to matched controls, as well as evidence for a link between faster WMH progression, shorter survival, and increased disability and cognitive decline in ALS patients. Furthermore, our regional analyses indicated stronger effects for WMHs in the frontal lobe regions. Future investigations are warranted to examine the underlying etiologies of WMHs in ALS and their associated risk factors.
