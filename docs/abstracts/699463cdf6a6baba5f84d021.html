Introduction

Functional connectivity (FC) has been widely used to understand individual differences in mental health. However, research is inconsistent on whether resting-state and task-based functional MRI (fMRI) reflect the same psychopathology-related FC patterns in adolescence. Some studies have found that resting state and task-based scans reflect similar functional architecture^1, whereas others report that task conditions substantially modulate FC, suggesting FC is dependent on the brain state during scanning^2. Conflicting findings may reflect true FC differences during task and rest, or methodological artifacts stemming from the emphasis on average differences between heterogenous youth. Moreover, questions remain about whether potentially contrasting FC patterns in resting-state and task-based fMRI reflect novel, under-investigated individual differences in mental health. 

The current project aims to investigate how brain state (i.e., resting versus task) impacts FC patterns estimated with a person-specific network method to capture heterogeneity among individuals. We also investigated whether individual differences in FC between states are associated with depressive symptoms in adolescents. To address these aims, we specifically examined FC from the Triple Network Model^3 that implicated in psychopathology. We hypothesized that networks will be largely similar across resting and an emotion processing task, and that significant differences will be present (e.g., FC patterns would depend on networks, such that the salience network will be denser during emotion processing than rest). We also examined whether person-specific differences in resting versus emotion FC patterns were associated with adolescent depressive symptoms, adjusting for demographic variables (i.e., age and sex).

Methods

Data were drawn from the Study of Adolescent to Adult Neural Development, including a subset of adolescents aged 15 to 17 years from the Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study (FFCWS). The FFCWS is a population-based longitudinal cohort study, with substantial representation of marginalized youths (83% Black and Hispanic). Neuroimaging data were acquired using a 3T GE Discovery MR750 scanner with an 8-channel head coil. Participants completed a resting state scan (fixation cross) and an implicit emotion processing task (identify the gender of an actor who displayed affective emotions, including sad, happy, angry, fearful, and neutral) while undergoing fMRI scanning. A standard preprocessing pipeline using FSL version 6.0 was applied to both scans. 156 participants with good quality data from both scanning states were included in analyses. Three participants were further excluded for non-converging person-specific networks, resulting in a final sample of 153 adolescents.

We modeled FC using group iterative multiple model estimation (GIMME) for data-driven person-specific network mapping^4. GIMME is ideal for evaluating this question around individual variability in FC because it fits time-lagged and contemporaneous connections that can apply to the group- or individual-levels, and it does not average across potentially heterogenous individuals. The group-level connections are estimated if they are meaningful in most individual networks (not an average map), but they are estimated in all person-specific networks. The individual-level connections are estimated after the group-level ones to ensure the networks fit each individual’s data well. The combination of both group-level and individual-level connections allows us to evaluate commonalities as well as heterogeneity in FC patterns. 

GIMME networks were derived from both fMRI states, separately, examining connections between 18 regions-of-interest (ROIs) in the Triple Network Model, with 6 bilateral ROIs from each of the default mode (DMN), salience (SN), and frontoparietal networks (FPN). Network properties of complexity (i.e., total number of connections in a network), density for within- and between-networks (i.e., number of relevant connections within or between networks, divided by complexity), and node centrality (i.e., number of connections coming in or out of an ROI, divided by complexity) were computed. We used multilevel models to compare complexity, density, and node centrality between scanning states, with random intercept and slope of scan state. All analyses adjusted for in-scanner head motion. FDR corrections for multiple comparisons were conducted for density and centrality measures separately.

To examine whether state differences in FC patterns were associated with depressive symptoms, we created difference scores for each metric by subtracting the resting FC metric from the task FC metric. We then examined the associations between difference scores and adolescent depression (measured as a latent factor of self-report questionnaires of depression, including MFQ, CDI, YSR, and KSADS-PL) in structural equation models with linear regression paths predicting difference scores from depression. We additionally adjusted for a set of head motion and demographic covariates, including adolescent age, biological sex, race/ethnicity, and maternal education.

Results

We observed some meaningful differences in Triple Network FC across task-based and resting-state scans, despite broad similarities in network architecture. Total network complexity was similar between scans (b = -1.12, p = .257), such that a similar number of connections were estimated for both networks. Examining contemporaneous and lagged connections separately, we found that the task elicited more contemporaneous than lagged connections (b = -2.16, p = .002), whereas resting elicited more lagged connections (b = 1.09, p = .028). This may suggest that temporal dynamics of FC are characterized differently between scan states. At the node-level, most centrality metrics remained similar across task and rest (ps > .05), but the bilateral inferior parietal lobule (IPL) in the DMN showed significant differences between scan states (left IPL: b = 0.01, p = .002; right IPL: b = -0.01, p = .008). At the same time, within- and between-network density metrics did not differ significantly between scans (ps > .05 for all density metrics), suggesting that networks are contributing to the overall FC architecture similarly in both task and resting scans. 

Although density metrics were similar between scan states, the differences that did exist at the person level were meaningful for individual differences in depression. Specifically, we found a significant link between the SN density difference score and adolescent self-report depression symptoms (𝛽 = -.27, p = .001), such that a greater symptom score was linked to a lower difference score (Fig.1). Separate linear path models revealed that this association was driven by both a decrease in task-based SN density (𝛽 = -.21, p = .008) as well as an increase in resting SN density (𝛽 =.19, p = .004). This may suggest that adolescents with higher depression symptoms showed both blunted SN responsivity to emotional faces, as well as elevated responsivity during rest. We did not see any significant associations of difference scores with any demographic covariates, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, and maternal education.

Conclusion

The current project investigated heterogeneity in FC patterns of the Triple Network across resting-state and task-based fMRI scan states, and how individual differences across states may be associated with depression symptoms in adolescents. While the network architecture appeared largely consistent across resting and task states, important differences also emerged in temporal dynamics, with more lagged connections during rest than task, which is consistent with slower dynamic connectivity patterns being captured during resting. We also found significant differences in node-level connections specific to the bilateral IPL within the DMN, which has been linked with emotion processing and integration of sensory information.

Crucially, individual differences in FC patterns during resting state versus task in the SN were linked with adolescent depression symptoms. Adolescents with greater depressive symptoms showed less task-based modulations in connectivity in an emotionally salient task, suggesting blunted emotion sensitivity. In addition, greater depressive symptoms were also associated with greater connectivity during the resting state, suggesting elevated “baseline” SN connectivity. Together, these findings suggest that although no differences between networks emerged at the group level, the individual differences in FC may have significant implications for how we understand the brain’s associations with mental health outcomes.

The study has several limitations as well. First, results may be limited to the sample, which is also modest in size, and the task (implicit emotion-processing) that was conducted. Additionally, GIMME emphasizes person-specific time-linked connections among prespecified ROIs; it is not a full-brain approach. Therefore, our results may not generalize to other networks in the brain.

Overall, our findings suggest that FC patterns in the Triple Network Model are largely stable across rest and emotion processing, though some state-dependent differences exist. These differences also have significant implications for understanding how mental health is neurally reflected in adolescents. The results were made possible by GIMME, which is a powerful approach for examining person-specific scan-based differences in FC, that can be linked to individual differences in depression symptoms, going above and beyond typically employed sample averages.
