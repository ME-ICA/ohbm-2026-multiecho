Introduction

Intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging enables the simultaneous characterization of tissue diffusion and microvascular perfusion without the use of exogenous contrast agents, which yields three quantitative parameters: the true diffusion coefficient (D), the pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*), and the perfusion fraction (f), which respectively reflect molecular water diffusion, incoherent motion associated with microvascular perfusion, and the relative contribution of perfusion-related signals to the total diffusion signal.1

Despite this potential, IVIM parameter estimation in acute ischemic stroke remains challenging due to the low perfusion of ischemic tissue, which increases its susceptibility to noise, rendering IVIM metrics highly dependent on b-value sampling schemes and fitting algorithms.2 Several IVIM fitting techniques have been proposed that are computationally efficient and readily implementable in routine clinical settings, including conventional least-squares fitting (LSQ), non-negative least squares (NNLS), and constrained fitting approaches. Compared with other methods, LSQ offers computational simplicity and greater tolerance to noise.3 In contrast, NNLS and constrained fitting aim to reduce parameter cross-dependence among D, D*, and f by enforcing non-negativity or segmented constraints, thereby improving fitting stability under conditions of low signal-to-noise ratio and reduced perfusion.1,4

Accordingly, this study systematically evaluates and compares the performance of three clinically implementable IVIM fitting strategies—LSQ, NNLS, and constrained fitting—in acute ischemic stroke, with a focus on parameter robustness and the delineation of ischemic core and penumbral regions.

Methods

Eleven participants (Acute stroke: 6; Control: 5) were scanned with a 1.5T MRI (GE-Artist) at Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital (IRB111-068-B) to acquire IVIM, with a voxel size of 2.5 x 2.5 x 2.5 mm, and a b value of [0 10 30 50 100 150 200 500 800 1000]. The core and penumbra regions of the stroke lesions were manually segmented on clinical routine images by an experienced neuroradiologist.

LSQ segment fit

The LSQ steps were 1: (1) the signal of b ≥ 200 s/mm² is selected, and D is obtained by fitting the single exponential model; (2)The intercept of f is estimated by interpolation to b = 0, and (3) the double exponential model of S₀, D, and f is fitted at the same time to obtain D* (Fig. 1 A).

NNLS spectral fitting

The NNLS method is based on a previously proposed spectral IVIM framework. 1 For each voxel, the diffusion signal is decomposed into multiple diffusion- and perfusion-related components defined over a predefined range of diffusion coefficients according to the acquired b-value sampling. Non-negative least squares fitting is then used to estimate the signal contributions, yielding three grouped diffusion coefficients (Dint, Dpar, and Dperf) and their corresponding fractions (fint, fpar, and fperf), representing intermediate, parenchymal, and perfusion-related components, respectively (Fig. 1 B).

Constrained fit

The constrained fitting strategy is based on low–b-value optimization to enable rapid IVIM estimation 4: (1) diffusion parameters are estimated from high b-values (b ≥ 200 s/mm²) using a mono-exponential model, where perfusion effects are negligible. (2)The estimated diffusion coefficient (D) is fixed, and the pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*) is subsequently fitted using low b-values (0 < b < 200 s/mm²), yielding a three-parameter solution (Fig. 1 C).

Statistical Analysis

In the stroke group, IVIM parameters were sampled voxel-wise within ischemic core and penumbral masks in six patients. Distributions of D, D*, and f (including NNLS-derived components) were compared between core and penumbra across the three fitting methods, and their diagnostic performance in differentiating core from penumbra voxels was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and the area under the curve (AUC). In the control group, coefficients of variation (CVs) for D, D*, and f (including NNLS-derived components) were calculated within manually delineated white matter and gray matter regions to assess the stability and reproducibility of the parameters.

Results

Robustness

In the control group, coefficients of variation (CVs) differed across IVIM fitting methods and parameters (Table 1). For the true diffusion coefficient (D), the LSQ method showed moderate variability in both gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) (GM: 64.37 ± 4.89%; WM: 64.44 ± 5.10%). For the NNLS approach, the intermediate and perfusion-related diffusion components (Dint and Dperf) exhibited markedly high variability (GM CV ≈ 150–178%; WM CV ≈ 176%), whereas the parenchymal diffusion component (Dpar) demonstrated relatively greater stability (GM: 61.31 ± 2.55%; WM: 52.27 ± 2.11%). The constrained fitting method showed a distinct pattern, with higher variability in GM (83.00 ± 6.58%) but substantially lower variability in WM (29.80 ± 0.62%), representing the lowest CV among all diffusion-related parameters.

Voxel-wise distribution analysis

In stroke patients, voxel-wise distributions of the diffusion coefficient D (or Dpar derived from NNLS) within ischemic core and penumbral regions were compared across six cases and fitting methods. Under LSQ fitting, D values showed broad and highly overlapping distributions between core and penumbra, with pronounced right-skewed tails. The NNLS-derived parenchymal diffusion component (Dpar) exhibited a modest reduction in distributional overlap; however, substantial overlap between the two tissue classes remained.

In contrast, constrained fitting yielded a clearer separation between core and penumbra distributions. Core voxels showed a leftward shift toward lower D values with a more concentrated distribution, whereas penumbral voxels were shifted toward higher values with a larger mean, indicating improved parameter stability and physiologically plausible differentiation.

ROC analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis further quantified the ability of IVIM-derived parameters to discriminate ischemic core from penumbra (Fig. 2 B). Under LSQ fitting, D demonstrated strong discriminative performance (AUC = 0.940), whereas D* (AUC ≈ 0.54) and f (AUC ≈ 0.62) showed limited discrimination.

For NNLS fitting, the parenchymal diffusion component Dpar achieved a moderate AUC (0.723), slightly lower than LSQ-D, while exhibiting improved compartment specificity. The NNLS-derived perfusion-related components (D* and f) remained near chance-level discrimination (AUC ≈ 0.54–0.62).

Within the constrained fitting model, Dpar showed the highest discriminative performance (AUC ≈ 0.772), followed by fpar (AUC ≈ 0.665), whereas the remaining diffusion and perfusion components exhibited AUC values close to chance level (≈ 0.50–0.56).

Conclusion

Across IVIM fitting strategies, clear trade-offs were observed between parameter robustness and tissue discrimination in 1.5T MRI. While LSQ fitting achieved high discriminative performance for ischemic core–penumbra separation, it was accompanied by substantial variability and broad parameter distributions, particularly in healthy tissue. NNLS fitting improved compartment specificity but exhibited pronounced instability in perfusion-related components. In contrast, constrained fitting provided a balanced performance profile, combining reduced variability—especially in white matter—with consistent separation between core and penumbra and moderate-to-high discriminative accuracy. These findings suggest that constrained IVIM fitting, particularly for parenchymal diffusion parameters, offers a clinically favorable compromise between stability and diagnostic utility for acute ischemic stroke imaging.
