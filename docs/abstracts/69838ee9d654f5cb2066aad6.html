Introduction

Motivation fundamentally shapes learning by dynamically regulating how cognitive resources are allocated over time. According to Self-Determination Theory, motivation spans a continuum from intrinsic autonomy to extrinsic reward, with each state engaging partially overlapping but functionally distinct neural systems. While prior neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that reward-based learning recruits dopaminergic and valuation-related circuits, and autonomy-driven learning additionally engages higher-order prefrontal control networks, most existing work has treated motivational influences as static conditions rather than temporally evolving states.

In real-world learning, however, motivation fluctuates moment by moment, giving rise to dynamic transitions between curiosity-driven exploration and reward-oriented goal pursuit. How such motivational state dynamics shape exploratory behavior—and how these behavioral states are instantiated in large-scale neural network reconfiguration—remains poorly understood. Critically, autonomous exploration offers a unique window into these processes, as it allows motivated behavior to unfold continuously rather than being discretized into trial-level responses.

Here, we combine computational ethology with neuroimaging to investigate how autonomy and reward jointly modulate motivational states during learning. Using an autonomous exploration paradigm with factorial manipulation of autonomy and reward, we characterize latent features of exploration trajectories that index motivational state dynamics, and examine how these behavioral signatures relate to memory performance and state-dependent changes in functional brain networks. By linking fine-grained exploratory behavior to neural connectivity dynamics, this work aims to elucidate how motivational states during learning drive effective exploration and memory formation.

Methods

Participants and Experimental Design

The experimental design is illustrated in Figure 1A. The paradigm was adapted from prior work on autonomous learning and memory (Voss, Galvan et al., 2011a; Voss, Gonsalves et al., 2011b; Voss, Gonsalves et al., 2011c) and followed the implementation described in van Lieshout et al. (2023). The experiment consisted of two blocks, each comprising a learning phase followed by a memory phase.

A total of 63 undergraduate students from Beijing universities were recruited. Both behavioral data (exploration trajectories and memory performance) and functional MRI data were collected during task performance. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation, and the study protocol was approved by the local institutional ethics committee.

Data quality was assessed based on head motion criteria. Participants with excessive motion (mean framewise displacement, FD ≥ 0.3 mm) were excluded. The final sample included 50 participants (28 males; age range: 18–27 years, M ≈ 21.3, SD ≈ 2.2). All group-level analyses controlled for age, sex, and six rigid-body head motion parameters.

fMRI Preprocessing and First-Level Analysis

Functional MRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep (version 24.0.0) following standard pipelines for motion correction, spatial normalization to MNI152NLin6Asym space (2 mm isotropic resolution), and nuisance regression. Preprocessed functional images and subject-specific brain masks generated by fMRIPrep were used for subsequent analyses.

First-level statistical analyses were conducted using Nilearn. For each participant, task-related BOLD responses were modeled separately for the two experimental blocks using GLM. Each learning grid was categorized into one of four conditions: MOVE–REWARD, MOVE–NO REWARD, FOLLOW–REWARD, and FOLLOW–NO REWARD. Regressors were time-locked to grid onset and convolved with a canonical Glover hemodynamic response function. A cosine drift model was applied to remove low-frequency signal fluctuations (high-pass filter: 0.01 Hz).

Six head motion parameters (three translations and three rotations) derived from fMRIPrep were included as nuisance regressors. The repetition time (TR) was 1.2 s. For each block, condition-specific beta estimates were computed, and linear contrasts were specified to assess the main effect of Autonomy (MOVE > FOLLOW), the main effect of Reward (REWARD > NO REWARD), and their interaction. Contrast maps from the two blocks were combined at the subject level using a fixed-effects model by averaging contrast estimates across blocks. The resulting subject-level contrast maps were then forwarded to group-level analyses.

Modeling and Statistical Analysis

Exploration trajectories were analyzed at the grid level using a computational ethology approach. Six trajectory metrics were extracted to capture complementary aspects of exploratory behavior: curvature (path smoothness), deviation (spatial dispersion), jitter (high-frequency motor variability), entropy (spatial coverage), PCA contribution (trajectory linearity), and fractal dimension (geometric complexity). These features were used to characterize motivational states during learning and to predict subsequent memory performance. To examine how neural and behavioral factors jointly influenced learning outcomes, traditional regression models were applied, modeling memory performance as a function of task-related brain activation and trajectory-based behavioral features. Hierarchical Bayesian models were constructed to capture latent motivational states underlying observed exploration behavior and memory performance. These models incorporated individual-level parameters within a group-level structure, enabling inference on hidden cognitive states and inter-individual variability.

Results

1. Reward amplifies perceptual gain and control signals rather than isolated reward circuitry

Rather than selectively engaging classic reward-related regions alone, reward broadly enhanced a distributed perceptual–semantic–control network during learning. Compared to no-reward trials, reward elicited increased activation in the occipital pole, lateral occipital cortex, occipital and temporal fusiform gyri, and lingual gyrus, indicating strengthened low- and high-level visual processing. Parietal regions including the angular gyrus and precuneus were additionally recruited, consistent with enhanced visuospatial integration and memory-related processing. Increased activation in the middle and superior frontal gyri and frontal pole further suggested elevated executive control and goal maintenance. Moreover, anterior and posterior cingulate cortex and insular cortex were engaged, reflecting modulation of motivational salience, affective processing, and interoceptive awareness. Together, these results indicate that reward reshapes learning by globally increasing perceptual gain and control engagement rather than acting through isolated valuation circuits.

2. Autonomy induces an action-centered motivational state grounded in sensorimotor and control networks

Autonomous learning was associated with a distinct neural configuration emphasizing action execution and self-regulation. Relative to follow trials, autonomy selectively engaged the precentral and postcentral gyri, supplementary motor area, superior parietal lobule, and lateral occipital cortex, reflecting enhanced voluntary movement, sensorimotor integration, and spatial attention. Frontal and medial regions, including the superior frontal gyrus, paracingulate gyrus, frontal opercular cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex, were additionally recruited, consistent with increased cognitive control, conflict monitoring, and self-initiated decision-making. Activation of the insular cortex further suggests heightened interoceptive and self-related processing. These findings indicate that autonomy is not a weaker form of reward, but instead constitutes a qualitatively distinct motivational state centered on action control and self-monitoring during exploration.

3. High-confidence memory retrieval reflects coordinated reinstatement of representational and control systems

Successful memory retrieval with high confidence was supported by coordinated activation of posterior representational regions and anterior control areas. Robust effects were observed in the precuneus, temporo-occipital middle temporal gyrus, and lateral occipital cortex, regions implicated in visuospatial imagery, semantic integration, and episodic recollection. Additional engagement of the frontal pole suggests higher-order monitoring and evaluative processes during confident decisions, while activation of the precentral gyrus may reflect reinstatement of action-related representations encoded during learning. This pattern indicates that confident remembering relies on the integration of perceptual–mnemonic representations with frontal control mechanisms, consistent with effective encoding under motivated learning states.

Conclusion

Together, these findings demonstrate that motivation during learning operates through dynamic and dissociable neural states rather than static condition effects. Reward enhances perceptual gain and executive control by broadly engaging visual, parietal, and cingulo-frontal networks, whereas autonomy induces an action-centered motivational state grounded in sensorimotor and cognitive control systems. High-confidence memory retrieval is supported by coordinated activation of posterior representational regions and anterior monitoring mechanisms, reflecting effective encoding under motivated exploration. By linking fine-grained exploratory behavior with state-dependent neural network configurations, this work highlights how autonomy and reward differentially shape learning trajectories and memory formation.
