Introduction

Callous Unemotional (CU) traits are associated with significant clinical and neurophysiological heterogeneity that may affect treatment effectiveness. Research has identified environmental, genetic, and neurophysiological factors that play a role in the development and maintenance of these traits. Specifically, CU traits are associated with cortical gray matter volume (cGM) differences and structural and functional abnormalities in the amygdala.1,2  However, the CU traits phenotype itself is heterogeneous, and studies have identified distinct primary and secondary subtypes associated with unique etiological pathways and outcomes. Research suggests primary CU traits arise from genetically-mediated fearlessness and low arousal, while secondary CU traits emerge in the context of heightened arousal and greater exposure to adversity.3 While studies have identified physiological correlates of primary and secondary CU traits, studies of neurobiological differences in primary and secondary CU traits are less common. 

Importantly, prior studies have largely used parent report measures to derive and validate CU traits subtypes, which inflates shared method variance and may undermine construct and external validity. Further, prior studies have failed to capture hypothesized biological influences related to distinct primary vs. secondary etiological pathways to CU traits by leveraging data-driven, bottom-up analytic approaches. Thus, the current study uses weakly-supervised machine learning clustering algorithm to identify unique profiles of primary and secondary CU traits which may better characterize children based on their underlying neurobiology. We hypothesize that (1) distinct profiles of cGM and subcortical gray matter (sGM) will differentiate children with CU traits, and (2) that subtypes will differ based on their levels of internalizing  symptoms and exposure to adversity in line with theory driven definitions of primary and secondary CU traits.  

Methods

This longitudinal study used imaging data from the Adolescent, Brain, Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study4 from a subset of participants (N=461, Average Age = 119.1 months, 69.9% male). Children in the High CU (CU+, N=224) group met diagnostic criteria for one or more disruptive behavior disorder from parent report on the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (K-SADS) and scored in the 95th percentile on a validated 4-item measure of CU traits. Children in the typically developing (TD N=237) group were matched on age and sex, and did not report any clinical diagnoses or CU traits. 

The present analysis used tabulated data measuring gray matter volume across 84 ROIs from the Desikan-Killiany Atlas (cGM) and the automatically segmented brain volume (sGM), available from the National Institute for Mental Health Data Archive. All imaging data were processed by the ABCD Data Core and harmonized using Longitudinal ComBat to account for site and scanner effects. Validation measures included child reports of anxiety and depression symptoms from the K-SADS, as well as a previously described general exposome factor to measure general environmental risk. 

We implemented heterogeneity through discriminative analysis (HYRDA)5 to identify neuroanatomical  subtypes of children with CU traits. We then applied the trained model to imaging data collected at 2-year follow-up. Subsequently, we used ANCOVA to evaluate the differences between groups on relevant clinical constructs and validate identified neuroanatomical subtypes. 

Results

HYDRA revealed an optimal 2-subtype solution (ARI = 0.74). Subtype 1 (S1) exhibited globally higher cGM (F(1,456) = 83.21, p = <.001) and sGM (F(1,456) = 67.02, p = <.001) than both Subtype 2 (S2) and TD children, while S2 exhibited globally lower cGM and sGM than both TD and S1 children. While these differences were widespread across various cortical and subcortical regions, they were most pronounced in the bilateral Superior Frontal Gyrus (Figure 1). Subtypes remained relatively stable across the 2-year follow up interval (χ²(1, 148) = 52.64, p < .001). As hypothesized, S2 exhibited higher levels of depression and anxiety symptoms, as well as higher levels of exposure to adversity. 

Conclusion

This study provides evidence of a neuroanatomical basis to prior hypothesized subtypes of CU traits. Results suggest that subtypes may arise through distinct neurobiological and environment etiological pathways, and have implications for diagnosis and treatment.
