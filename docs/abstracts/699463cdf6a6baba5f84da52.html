Introduction

The ratio between the lengths of the index and the ring finger (2D:4D) is a putative developmental marker of early androgen exposure1 (lower ratio indicative of higher androgen exposure). It has been studied extensively in relation to behavioural traits, including sexual orientation and gender identity – the largest psychological sex differences.2 On average, 2D:4D is lower among cisgender heterosexual men than women; however, some research suggests that this difference is reduced or inverted in sexual and gender minority populations.1 Relatively little work has examined whether variation in 2D:4D is associated with sex-differentiated brain features, and none of these included sexual and gender minorities. 

Research on sex differences in cortical morphology indicates that cisgender women tend to show greater cortical thickness (CT), whereas cisgender men tend to show greater cortical surface area (SA).3 These sex differences may also not generalize to sexual and gender minorities4, and are often discussed in relation to hormonal influences (androgens associated with masculine profiles).5

Disentangling biological mechanisms from experiential influences is challenging; however, if digit ratio can be linked to both sex-differentiated cortical features and to psychological features (sexual attractions and gender identity), this would support the existence of a biological component contributing to variation in these aspects of brain and behavior. 

We recruited participants of a range of gender identities and sexual orientations in Thailand, and examined sex assigned at birth, gender identity, and sexual orientation as separate factors. We hypothesized that digit ratio is related to sex, gender, sexual orientation, and cortical features. We predicted that greater cortical thickness and smaller surface area are associated with higher 2D:4D, sex assigned female at birth, feminine gender identity, and attraction towards individuals assigned male at birth.  

Methods

Participants were recruited through community sexual health centers affiliated with the Research Institute for Health Sciences at Chiang Mai University, Chiang Mai, Thailand, between 2018–2022. The final sample included 260 (mean age = 25.57, SD = 5.18) adults spanning nine sexual and gender identity groups (assessed by self-report). This included individuals assigned female or male at birth: transfeminine (n = 23)  and transmasculine (n = 30), neither with history of gender-affirming hormone use; gay men (n = 30); lesbian women (n = 30); and bisexuals (men n = 30, women n = 29), dees (Thai, culturally-specific label for women dating transmasculine individuals, n =  26), as well as heterosexuals (men n = 31, women n = 31). 

Participants’ hands were scanned using a document scanner to obtain left and right hand digit ratios. Two independent raters digitally measured participants’ index and ring fingers using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/). Each rater performed measurements for each finger three times. The measurements were averaged to produce values for each participant’s index (2D) and ring finger (4D), and ratios (length of 2D divided by the length of 4D) were calculated for left and right hands separately. Inter-rater reliability for digit measurements was excellent (intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC(2,1) = .998, 95% CI [.997, .998]).

High-resolution (~1mm3) T1-weighted MRI data were acquired using a 1.5T scanner with a 16-channel head coil. The white matter and pial surfaces were reconstructed and cortical thickness and surface area were estimated vertex-wise at 40,962 vertices per hemisphere using the CIVET pipeline (https://github.com/aces/CIVET). 

A multivariate, data-driven partial least squares (PLS) approach (Python, pyls package; https://github.com/rmarkello/pyls) was used to identify latent variables capturing maximal covariance between vertex-wise cortical structure variables (CT and SA) and 2D:4D, sex, gender, and sexual orientation. Digit ratio (2D:4D) was included as a continuous variable, sex assigned at birth was coded as 1 = AFAB and 0 = AMAB; gender identity was coded as 1 = feminine and 0 = masculine; and sexual orientation was coded as −1 = attracted to AFAB, 0 = bisexual, and 1 = attracted to AMAB. Variation attributed to age and total brain volume was removed from the cortical data through residualisation prior to the PLS analysis. Statistical significance was assessed using permutation testing (5000), and the reliability was estimated through bootstrapping (5000); a bootstrap ratio (BSRs) of ±2.57 (corresponding to a 99% confidence interval) was considered significant.

Post-hoc group comparisons of brain scores and digit ratios were conducted using one-way ANOVAs followed by Tukey’s HSD tests for multiple comparisons, with groups defined by sex assigned at birth and gender identity (based on the outcome of the PLS analysis; details below).

Results

PLS identified one significant latent variable (p = .002) explaining 65.3% of the covariance between cortical structure and the investigated variables (Figure 1). Regionally greater cortical thickness, spreading across frontal, cingulate, insular, and parietal cortices, covaried positively with higher digit ratio, female sex assigned at birth, and feminine gender identity. Cortical surface area was related to the variables in either a positive or negative direction depending on the region. Small, frontally located areas positively covaried with higher digit ratio, female sex assigned at birth, and feminine gender identity. Areas identified in temporal, parietal, and occipital cortex negatively covaried with these factors. Sexual orientation did not reliably contribute to the observed pattern.

We performed post-hoc analyses after dividing the sample into four groups based on sex assigned at birth and gender identity: cismasculine, transfeminine, transmasculine, cisfeminine.

We compared latent variable (LV1) brain scores between the groups. Brain scores indicate the degree to which each individual’s cortical profile expresses the multivariate pattern identified in the primary PLS analysis. Groups differed significantly (F(3,255) = 6.13, η² = .07, p < .001). Pairwise comparisons (Tukey HSD) revealed a significant difference between the sex- and gender-congruent groups, with higher scores in cisgender AFAB individuals and lower scores in cisgender AMAB individuals (p < .001). Transfeminine and transmasculine groups showed intermediate brain scores that did not differ significantly from either cisgender group, consistent with a shift in pattern expression away from strict alignment with sex assigned at birth and toward gender identity.

Notably, there were significant differences in 2D:4D between the same four groups in both the left- and right-hand (Fs(3, 255) = 10.27 and 8.10, respectively; ps < .001). For both hands, the cismasculine group showed significantly lower digit ratios than the cisfeminine group (left: p < .001; right: p < .001). Transmasculine and transfeminine individuals occupied intermediate positions between cisgender groups in the left hand, such that neither group differed from cisfeminine individuals (ps ≥ .36), and transmasculine individuals differed only from cismasculine individuals (p = .017). For the right hand, transmasculine individuals did not differ from cismasculine individuals (p = .241) and transfeminine individuals did not differ from cisfeminine individuals (p = .998).

Conclusion

Cortical thickness and surface area covaried with 2D:4D, sex assigned at birth, and gender identity, but not sexual orientation. Cortical thickness varied in line with our predictions, with greater thickness associated with higher 2D:4D, sex assigned female at birth, and feminine gender identity. Surface area covaried with these variables positively or negatively, depending on the brain region.

Post-hoc comparisons, performed ignoring sexual orientation, further indicated that expression of the latent cortical pattern differed between sex–gender congruent groups, with cisfeminine individuals showing higher LV1 brain scores than cismasculine individuals, while sex–gender incongruent groups exhibited intermediate scores. 

This intermediate expression of the cortical pattern in sex–gender incongruent groups was largely mirrored by what we observed for 2D:4D – it was also shifted away from aligning with sex assigned at birth, and toward alignment with gender identity in those groups. 

These findings extend prior work on digit ratio beyond behavioral traits to cortical morphology, and demonstrate how established patterns of cortical variation manifest when sex and gender are considered as separate factors. If 2D:4D is indeed reflective of prenatal androgen exposure, our results support the role of androgen not only in sex differences but also in gender differences in brain structure. The observed linkage to a biological proxy that is not influenced by social experience is consistent with the idea that there is an innate biological component to sex- and gender-related differences in brain structure. These findings also bear important implications for sex and gender analysis, emphasizing the need to consider gender identity, and not only sex assigned at birth, when investigating hormone–brain–behavior relationships.
