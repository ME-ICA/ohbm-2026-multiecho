Introduction

Zero echo time (ZTE) fMRI provides a robust complementary approach for mapping brain activity during stimulus and resting state, with markedly reduced acoustic noise and susceptibility artifacts1. Due to the ultra-short echo time (TE) of 60 μs, ZTE-fMRI is insensitive to the conventional blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) contrast. Instead, its short repetition times (TR) and low flip angles ( α < 10°) have been modeled as reflecting arterial inflow effects2–4, with recent animal studies providing evidence that ZTE contrast is also sensitive to T1 changes associated with tissue oxygenation (tO2)4. To date, apart from our recent proof-of-principle demonstrations of task-based and resting-state fMRI results at a clinical 3T system1, most ZTE-fMRI implementations have been limited to preclinical ultra-high field platforms using head-only excitation coils. It is generally believed that ZTE-fMRI performance degrades in humans due to longer arterial transit times (resulting in weaker inflow contrast) and shorter tissue T1 values (leading to elevated tissue signal) at lower magnetic field strengths4. Together, these constraints represent a major barrier to routine human applications, highlighting the need for strategies that substantially improve ZTE-fMRI sensitivity at 3T.

Here, we report a novel strategy to enhance ZTE-fMRI sensitivity at 3T based on inflow effects. Specifically, we leverage the elevated tissue signal at 3T by selectively nulling the inflowing blood signal using saturation. This approach yields a ZTE-fMRI response with opposing polarity and an increased spatial extent of the detected activation than the non-saturated ZTE-fMRI. In addition, we examine the effects of oxygen inhalation across acquisition conditions to elucidate further the contrast mechanisms underlying ZTE-fMRI. Together, these results suggest that the proposed strategy holds potential to substantially improve the sensitivity and practical utility of ZTE-fMRI at clinical field strengths.

Methods

In ZTE acquisitions, to keep the gradient waveforms continuously on, the readout gradient is applied during the RF pulse, resulting in the first samples of each radial spoke being missed and creating a sampling gap at the k-space center. In this study, the ZTE sequence was based on csPETRA5, with the k-space gap filled using single-point imaging (SPI). To test the inflow hypothesis, the signal from inflowing arterial blood was suppressed in a 10-mm-wide saturation slab that was applied at the neck level every 20 ms (pulse duration: 4 ms, total saturation block length: 6.8 ms) followed by the acquisition of 10 radial spokes (Fig.1a). Dynamic 3D ZTE fMRI measurements were performed using a homogeneously distributed radial sampling scheme with the following parameters: TR/TE = 2/0.06 ms, Tenc = 1.6 ms, α=3°, FOV = (192 mm)3, Matrix size = 64x64x64, ΔV = (3 mm)3, Nspokes = 1500, Nspi = 125 SPI acquisitions. Due to the extended duration of the saturation block compared to the acquisition block, the effective temporal resolution T­acq was 4.1 s/volume. For an 8-μs-long RF pulse in the ZTE protocol, the flip angle was restricted by the system to a maximum of α=3°. Based on Bloch simulations with TR = 2 ms, as in Fig. 1(b), the gray matter reached a steady state after 3 seconds, corresponding to 1 volume (1500 spokes). As the gray matter relaxes during the periodic saturation blocks, the steady-state is reached dynamically on a 10-spoke basis (Fig. 1c).

Motivated by the findings from our previous experiments, in which oxygen inhalation substantially increased signal intensity in the activated region (Fig. 1e), we hypothesized that the ZTE-fMRI contrast is a composite effect of inflow and T1 reduction due to oxygenation. To test the hypothesis, the following set of measurements was performed in four healthy volunteers during a visual stimulus based on a block design paradigm:

1. Regular ZTE-fMRI

2. Regular ZTE-fMRI during hyperoxia 

3. ZTE-fMRI with saturation band

4. ZTE-fMRI with saturation band during hyperoxia

For the hyperoxia measurements, 16 L/min of oxygen was delivered to the subjects via an MRI-compatible oxygen flowmeter and mask. All measurements were performed at a 3T clinical MRI system (Magnetom PrismaFit, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) using a 20-channel receive-only head coil array (Head 20, Siemens).

For fMRI a visual stimulation was delivered using a flickering checkerboard pattern with a periodic block design of 57.2 s = 28.6 s (off) + 28.6 s (on), corresponding to 14 volumes = 7 volumes (off) + 7 volumes (on). The total number of volumes for measurements with saturation block was 86, resulting in a total scan time of approximately 5 min 52 sec. During the oxygen-enhanced measurements, each subject was first measured while breathing room air and with oxygen supply, and for each condition the acquisitions were performed both with and without the saturation band. The stimulus during the oxygen study was presented in an asymmetric temporal pattern, with varying intervals between onsets. The hyperoxia study included 146 volumes, resulting in a total scan time of approximately 9 min 57 sec.

All images were reconstructed using regridding and inverse Fourier transformation (iFFT), and then co-registered to the MNI-152 brain atlas. The analysis was performed using General Linear Model (GLM) in FSL. Before clustering, all Z-maps acquired with the saturation band were inverted to identify the hypothesized negative correlation.

Results

Figure 2 (a,b) presents the clustered Z-map (|Z|>3.1) and corresponding mean time courses acquired with saturation band, at both subject and group levels. Figure 2a includes results from three subjects. By inverting the polarity of Z-maps, significant negative Z-clusters were observed for all three subjects across the central region of the occipital lobe. The mean time courses of the cluster region also exhibited a strong negative correlation with the event design. The mean intensity was observed to decrease at the onset of each event block. As in Figure 2b, despite lowered Z-threshold (|Z|>2) and smaller extents, the group analysis results based on all four subjects further showed consistency with the subject-level findings. The group-level results for measurements without saturation are also shown in Figure 2b, indicating a positive correlation with the event design. The group-level saturation analysis yielded a cluster of 2606 voxels in the occipital lobe, which is 548% larger than the positive cluster detected in the regular scans without saturation.

Figure 2c shows the clustered Z-map (|Z| > 3.1) and the corresponding mean time courses from the hyperoxia experiments at the subject level. The regular ZTE-fMRI scan under room-air conditions served as the benchmark for comparison, with 783 voxels in the surviving cluster. With continuous oxygen inhalation, the activation response was enhanced, resulting in a 166% increase in cluster size when compared with the benchmark. When combined with saturation blocks, ZTE-fMRI yielded a substantially larger cluster of 8474 voxels in the Z map under room-air conditions. With oxygen supply, ZTE-fMRI with saturation blocks showed a cluster of 2668 voxels, 68.5% fewer than under room air. 

Conclusion

ZTE-fMRI with a saturation pulse of the inflowing blood demonstrated robust negative responses to the visual stimulus in the occipital lobe at both the subject and group levels. This measurement supports the hypothesis that inflowing arterial blood is the primary contrast mechanism in ZTE-fMRI, as a substantial decrease in saturation also reduces the longitudinal magnetization within the voxel. As blood flow increases during activation, a partial volume becomes saturated, resulting in a decreased measured signal intensity and thus a negative correlation with the task stimulus. As shown in Figure 2, at both the subject and group levels, an immediate drop in mean signal intensity was consistently observed at the onset of each stimulus, indicating that ZTE-fMRI can detect the activation response with a shorter delay. This finding is also consistent with our previous study, in which we observed that the ZTE-fMRI signal reacted faster than the BOLD-fMRI signal to the stimulus by 2.5±0.4 sec1, indicating an arterial response. Compared to the regular scan, a larger cluster survived in the group level analysis with the saturation band, demonstrating that ZTE has high sensitivity and spatial specificity to changes in the inflow.

RF excitation with whole‑body coils can, in principle, be viewed as saturating the inflowing blood signal. However, this is unlikely in most clinical systems because the integrated body coil is typically about 50 cm in length. When the eyes are positioned at the isocenter, the heart in an average-sized adult is located more than 30 cm away. Hence, it lies largely outside the sensitive region of the body coil.

Further study with oxygen supply at the subject level yielded additional exploratory insights into the ZTE-fMRI contrast mechanism. Without saturation, oxygen supply strongly enhanced the positive response to the event design in the designated region, with 166% larger cluster and less noisy time course. This finding implies that the inflow effect is not the sole contributor to the ZTE-fMRI contrast. However, elevated oxygen levels can also augment cerebral blood flow, potentially confounding the interpretation of enlarged activation areas. Therefore, a measurement combined with both oxygen supply and saturation blocks was performed to test the second part of the hypothesis. With the saturation blocks, ZTE-fMRI showed a significant negative cluster across the occipital lobe under room-air conditions, indicating a significant effect of the nulling inflow. After administering oxygen, the observed negative cluster decreased by 68.5%. This finding implies that the oxygen-driven signal increase may partially cancel out the saturated inflow contrast. The positive response that counteracted the saturation band effect is likely related to elevated tO₂, suggesting that T₁ changes induced by tO₂ represent an additional contributor to the ZTE-fMRI contrast, supporting the second part of our hypothesis. A recent preclinical study in mice also provided evidence for this hypothesis, showing that the contrast mechanism was driven not only by inflow effects but also by T1-shortening induced by tO24. However, the relative contributions of these two effects to the ZTE-fMRI signal remain unclear.

Given the limited number of subjects, further studies with larger cohorts and a broader range of task paradigms and stimulus designs are required to better characterize the ZTE-fMRI mechanism and confirm the statistical robustness of the present findings. Expanding both sample size and task diversity will also enable a more detailed characterization of the hemodynamic response underlying brain function.
