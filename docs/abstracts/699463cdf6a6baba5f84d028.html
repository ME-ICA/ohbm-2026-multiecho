Introduction

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a vital therapeutic tool for the treatment of neuropsychiatric disorders. The changes in excitability induced by rTMS applied to the primary motor cortex (M1), which are indexed by the amplitude of motor-evoked potentials (MEPs), are pivotal for understanding the mechanisms underlying rTMS modulation. Notably, such stimulation not only alters the local excitability of the targeted M1 but also modulates excitability in the contralateral M1, offering a critical window into remote modulation mechanisms1. However, studies on rTMS-induced contralateral M1 modulation yield inconsistent results2, 3. These discrepancies likely stem from individual differences in brain organization affecting signal propagation, yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Emerging evidence suggests that the intrinsic functional architecture mediates signal propagation following single-pulse TMS4, 5. Thus, it is crucial to investigate whether it accounts for variability in rTMS-induced remote modulation. Furthermore, stimulating non-homologous regions, including the dorsal premotor cortex (PMd) and posterior parietal cortex (PPC), also modulates contralateral M1. Therefore, this study systematically investigated whether the intrinsic functional network architecture contributes to the heterogeneity observed in rTMS-induced remote motor cortical modulation and whether such contributions are pathway-specific.

Methods

Twenty right-handed healthy volunteers were recruited for this study. The experimental design involved three rTMS sessions targeting distinct cortical sites: the left M1, left PMd, and left PPC. Intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) was applied to all targets, delivering 600 total pulses at 70% resting motor threshold. The M1 hotspot was defined as the scalp location eliciting the maximal peak-to-peak MEP amplitude in the contralateral first dorsal interosseous muscle. The PMd target was localized 2.5 cm anterior and 1 cm medial to the M1 hotspot, while the PPC target corresponded to the P3 electrode position. Changes in motor cortical excitability were quantified as the ratio of post-intervention to baseline MEP amplitude.

Prior to the rTMS sessions, structural T1-weighted images and resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) were acquired. MRI data underwent standardized preprocessing using DPABI. Seed-based correlation analysis was performed to quantify resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) between each stimulation target and the contralateral M1. Beyond direct inter-regional coupling, functional brain networks support neural information processing through network-level interaction. Therefore, we identified the engaged network for each stimulation and calculated the mean within-network connectivity of the engaged network was computed to capture intra-network interactions.  

Results

Eighteen participants completed the M1 stimulation session, while 10 and 15 participants completed the PMd and PPC sessions, respectively. At the group level, paired t-tests revealed no significant modulation of contralateral motor cortical excitability following iTBS to the left M1 (t = -0.028, p = 0.978), PMd (t = 0.819, p = 0.434), or PPC (t = 0.394, p = 0.699). This absence of a uniform group effect is consistent with previous reports highlighting substantial inter-individual variability in rTMS response. Bayesian linear regression demonstrated that rsFC between the stimulation site and the contralateral M1 was a significant predictor of individual response specifically for M1 stimulation (β = -0.45, SE = 0.22, 95% CrI = [-0.88, -0.02]). However, this predictive power did not extend to PMd (β = -0.11, SE = 0.31, 95% CrI = [-0.70, 0.54]) or PPC stimulation (β = 0.01, SE = 0.28, 95% CrI = [-0.54, 0.59]). Furthermore, network topology metrics failed to yield significant predictors when each target was analyzed independently. 

Unlike M1 stimulation, which was confined to local sensorimotor circuits, PMd and PPC stimulation engaged diverse non-sensorimotor networks, such as the frontoparietal network. This suggests that PMd and PPC stimulation may share underlying mechanistic principles involving broader network-level interactions. Consequently, we performed Bayesian mixed linear modeling on the combined PMd and PPC dataset. This analysis revealed that the mean within-network connectivity of the engaged network (β = -0.40, SE = 0.19, 95% CrI = [-0.77, -0.01]) significantly predicted individual response variability.

Conclusion

Our findings provide evidence that intrinsic functional architecture mediates the remote effects of rTMS. For M1, the negative association between bilateral rsFC and excitability changes supports the interhemispheric inhibition theory, suggesting that stronger connectivity facilitates greater transcallosal inhibition. This insight holds significant translational value for personalized stroke rehabilitation. Regarding PMd and PPC, combined analysis reveals that remote modulation depends on both within-network connectivity and whole-brain integration. These results highlight the critical role of network topology in rTMS remote modulation and suggest mechanistic distinctions between intra- and inter-network effects. Crucially, unlike previous studies that focused on immediate signal propagation following single-pulse TMS, we investigated the lasting effects induced by repetitive stimulation, which more closely aligns with clinical therapeutic interventions. Collectively, these findings advance theoretical frameworks for functional connectivity-based TMS targeting, representing a rapidly advancing approach for optimizing therapeutic efficacy.
