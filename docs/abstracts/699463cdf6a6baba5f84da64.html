Introduction

Primates are innately social creatures, and the richness of their social environment is a critical determinant of mental health, longevity, and reproductive success. While it is established that the primate brain must adapt to the demands of social living, the specific neural mechanisms that track dynamic changes in social environment—and the timescale over which they occur—remain poorly understood. 

Cross-sectional studies in both laboratory-housed1 and free-ranging macaques2 have robustly linked social group size to gray matter volume (GMV) in specific cortical regions, such as the superior temporal sulcus (STS). However, these correlational snapshots cannot distinguish between pre-existing predispositions versus experience-dependent plasticity. Furthermore, current models often overlook potential structural adaptation in subcortical brain regions, such as the thalamus and striatum, which are increasingly implicated in regulating one’s social state3. 

To address these gaps, we conducted a longitudinal, multi-modal MRI study in a macaque sample undergoing controlled, reversible transitions between single-living and social housing. This design allowed us to causally map the structural and functional plasticity that unfolds across both cortical and subcortical networks in response to social change.

Methods

We collected longitudinal structural MRI and resting-state functional MRI (rsfMRI) data from 13 male cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) across a 15-month period. To isolate environment-dependent plasticity from maturation effects, we employed an A-B-A experimental design consisting of three distinct 6-month phases: an initial Single Living baseline, a Pairwise Living phase, and a return to Single Living phase (Fig 1A). Crucially, animals underwent MRI scanning at two timepoints within each phase (spaced 3 months apart; Sessions 1–6 total), allowing us to assess intra-phase stability alongside inter-phase plasticity. At each session, anesthetized animals underwent a high-resolution T1-weighted scan and two 45-minute rsfMRI acquisitions with opposing phase-encoding directions (Fig 1B). 

T1-weighted images from each animal were used to derive longitudinal voxel-based morphometry (VBM) data. T1-weighted images were processed based on the ANTs longitudinal pipeline; an unbiased single-subject template (SST) was constructed from all six sessions for each animal. Individual sessions were nonlinearly warped to their SST, which was subsequently nonlinearly registered to the NMT v2.0 standard template4.  Cortical and subcortical gray matter for each session were segmented using NMT tissue priors, which were modulated by the Jacobian determinant from the session-to-SST warpfields. Maps were spatially smoothed (1mm FWHM Gaussian kernel) and aligned to the NMT template.

rsfMRI data underwent motion correction and distortion correction using dual phase-encoding acquisitions, registration to standard NMT space, spatial smoothing (3mm FWHM), and aCompCor denoising with 15 head motion regressors and high-pass filtering (0.01Hz).  Final session-level images were then generated by z-transforming and concatenating runs acquired with opposing phase-encoding directions. Whole-brain functional connectivity (FC) was estimated via pairwise correlations between constituent regions of CHARM (level 6) regions SARM (level 3) parcellations4.

We quantified the effect of condition (Single vs. Pairwise) on VBM and FC using Linear mixed-effects models, with session and social condition as within-subject fixed effects, and subject as a random effect. The VBM condition contrast map was thresholded at p < 0.001 and cluster corrected (cluster-extent p < 0.05) using AFNI’s 3dClustSim. The FC condition contrast matrix was thresholded at p < 0.001 and significant FC networks were identified using the Network-Based Statistic5 (NBS) with 5000 permutations.

Results

Longitudinal VBM revealed six clusters exhibiting state-dependent increases in gray matter volume during Pairwise Living (Fig 2A). Cortical expansion occurred in the right temporal cortex—specifically area TA, posterior TE, and the STS—replicating prior work on social network size1,2. Crucially, we identified novel, significant expansion in subcortical regulatory hubs, including the left thalamus, left striatum, and medulla. These volumetric gains were transient, largely reversing upon the return to the Single Living condition (Fig 2B).

NBS identified a robust, large-scale component of functional reorganization (p = 0.02; 189 regions, 279 edges) associated with social pairing (Fig 2C). This reorganization was characterized by strengthened coupling (84% of edges) between frontal integrative regions and temporal, parietal, and brainstem structures. Conversely, functional decoupling was spatially concentrated within fronto-temporal and thalamic circuits (Fig 2D). Regionally, the largest positive shifts in connectivity centered on lateral frontal areas, STS, and brainstem, while negative shifts were largely localized to the thalamus and midbrain (Fig 2E).

Finally, we examined functional connectivity specifically within structurally plastic regions. Areas encompassing the six VBM clusters displayed distinct functional profiles during Pairwise Living (Fig 2F). We observed a complex pattern of connectivity modulation across bilateral limbic structures—including the hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, and striatum—as well as the brainstem. Notably, these functional shifts spatially converged with the identified subcortical VBM clusters, paralleling the structure-function coupling observed in the right temporal cortex.

Conclusion

Our longitudinal, reversible manipulation establishes a causal link between social environment and time-dependent structural and functional re-organization across the primate social neural network. We confirm the involvement of canonical cortical nodes, replicating and extending findings of structural adaptation in the STS and temporal cortex1,2. Crucially, our VBM analysis also reveals significant plasticity in subcortical hubs, specifically the dorsal thalamus and striatum. The discovery of social-dependent structural adaptation in the thalamus strongly corroborates emerging physiological evidence in rodents that the mediodorsal thalamus functions as a critical regulator of social state3. Together, these data suggest that social environmental complexity is supported by a dynamic interplay between cortical perceptual systems and subcortical regulatory circuits.
