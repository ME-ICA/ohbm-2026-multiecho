Introduction

The principles underlying large-scale organisation of the brain remains a central open question in neuroscience. Whilst brain geometry itself is complex, and it is known that cortical folding patterns support an increase in surface area, the distribution of cortical features (e.g. myelination, cortical thickness, cell or receptor densities, functional activation) remains not fully characterised. ^1 Specific features have been shown to vary in a systemic pattern across the cortical mantle; for example, myelin content is known to be higher in unimodal (rather than transmodal) regions. However, two key areas of research remain open. Firstly, studies typically analyse the distribution of a few phenotypes at a time. To date, there has been no large-scale, broad comparison of the features underlying the organisation of the brain. Secondly, typical approaches to understanding the distributions of these phenotypes across the cortex typically involve dividing the brain into specific regions of interest. This approach (in effect, using a single parcellation and thereby restricting analysis to a particular spatial scale) fails to account for the intrinsic multiscale organisation of the brain. 

Here, we present the first study analysing a broad range of maps of brain architecture across spatial scales. We primarily use parcellation-free approaches derived from image texture analysis to quantify the self-similar organisation of hundreds of maps of cortical organisation. Self-similarity (parts of the object showing statistical similarity to the whole object at many different scales) is known to be an efficient, concise program for generating complex patterns observed in nature. ^2 Our approach permits quantification of the scaling and universality of these maps, and assessment of a preferred spatial scale of human brain organisation. 

Methods

116 brain maps from the neurosynth database were used in the primary analysis. A further 106 maps from various sources were used for further validation, including maps from neuromaps (28 maps) and the Human Connectome Project (47 maps of task activation derived from fMRI). In total, this resulted in 222 maps at the vertex level, including maps such as myelination, thickness, synapse density, receptor density, and oxygen and glucose metabolism. The medial wall of each map was excluded, and the remaining data for each map was linearly rescaled to the interval [0,1]. 

Here, the primary quantification of self-similarity across scales uses the rate of change of a local estimate of the fractal dimension over different scales and orientations.^3 In brief, for a brain map M:cortex→[0,1] to show self-similarity across scales, the root mean squared difference of the map at two positions on the cortical surface (x and y) is expected to have a power law relationship with the distance between x and y: RMS(M(x)-M(y))∝|x-y|3-d, where d is the fractional dimension of the map. Under reasonable assumptions of stationarity and isotropy, d can be viewed as a function of the distance between x and y (r=|x-y|), rather than the specific points x and y. Then, d can be simply derived from the gradient of the log-log relationship between RMS(M(x)-M(y)) and r. The range of r over which d is constant represents self-similarity over these spatial scales; higher values of d (tending away from 2 and towards 3) over this range indicate greater ‘roughness’ (or variability) over this scale. Up to normalisation or rescaling, this method is exactly analogous to the detection of fractal scaling/criticality in the Ising model of magnetism.^4 To validate the above method, we performed secondary analyses using traditional box-counting and power-spectrum approaches.

Results

We quantified the scaling properties in meta-analytic maps from the neurosynth database, using the Pearson correlation coefficient to assess whether maps showed a relationship between RMS(M(x)-M(y)) and |x-y|. Of the 116 maps analysed, 31 maps showed r>0.95 (i.e. >90% of variance explained by distance); these maps were judged to show self-similarity across a range of spatial scales. Figure 1A shows example maps showing the self-similar scaling; figure 1B shows example maps that do not have a strong power-law relationship and therefore do not show self-similarity across scales. 

Of the 31 maps showing a high degree of self-similarity, fractal dimensions ranged between 2.77 and 2.97 (dimensions of 2 and 3 indicate complete smoothness and roughness respectively). This suggests local patterns tended to vary substantially (as opposed to being locally smooth); these patterns were then replicated at varying scales across the cortical surface. 

Finally, the maps showing high degrees of self-similarity included those given by the meta-analytic terms ‘arousal’ (r=0.99), ‘fear’ (r=0.99), ‘stress’ (r=0.99), and ‘anxiety’ (r=0.98). This potentially suggests that the activation patterns in these states are related to recruitment of a whole-brain neuronal ensemble acting at different states to coordinate complex behaviours. Other high scoring maps included ‘decision making’, ‘addiction’, ‘impulsivity’, ‘navigation’, and ‘planning’. In contrast, some low scoring maps included ‘belief’, ‘expectancy’, ‘judgement’, and ‘selective attention’. 

Conclusion

Overall, we find evidence for self-similar scaling across many, but not all, features of the human brain. This suggests that various features of the brain are organised according to principles which support complicated patterns emerging from relatively simple organisational rules. We find that these patterns represent complex patterns and may be associated with the coordination of neuronal ensembles in diverse behavioural domains. Ongoing work in this domain will include inter-individual and inter-species comparison of scaling properties, e.g. in fMRI time-series (between subjects) or in gene expression levels (between species).
