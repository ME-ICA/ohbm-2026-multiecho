Introduction

Recent work by Krohn et al. introduced the complexome framework, an information-theoretic account of blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) signal dynamics in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). The study showed that participants exhibit spontaneous and transient BOLD complexity reductions, quantified as weighted permutation entropy (WPE). These ‘complexity drops’ were found to form spatially coordinated cascades and closely track functional connectivity (FC) strength across the brain. This approach provides a link between local signal dynamics and global network organisation, offering a promising framework to study how moment-to-moment changes in regional dynamics contribute to the formation and fluctuation of large-scale functional networks.1 In the original framework, complexity drops were defined using a fixed percentile WPE threshold. While effective, this binary definition introduces an arbitrary parameter and may miss smaller or temporally heterogeneous decreases in complexity.

Therefore, we developed a threshold-free method for detecting complexity drops using a multiscale Mexican-hat (Laplacian-of-Gaussian) wavelet transform, enabling continuous estimation of drop strength across time and regions.2 This approach is sensitive to the shape and temporal structure of fluctuations, rather than relying on absolute amplitude of the complexity timeseries.

Methods

Using the same HCP resting-state dataset as the original study (343 participants, two 15-min runs, TR = 0.72 s),3,4 continuous drop-strength signals were derived by convolving regional WPE time series with Mexican-hat wavelets. Wavelet scales were selected to match empirically observed cascade durations and were further validated by sigma-wise correspondence with the original threshold-based drop coincidence (sigma= 2-6).

Results

Across runs, wavelet-based drop detection closely followed fluctuations in mean WPE, confirming that it captures the expected complexity dynamics (median r = -0.68). Critically, wavelet-based drop coincidence showed a stronger correspondence with FC than the threshold-based measure (Wilcoxon Z = −21.6, p < 10⁻¹⁰²). At the edgewise level, the wavelet-based model substantially improved explained variance in FC (adjusted R² = 0.87) compared to the threshold-based approach (adjusted R² = 0.67). This suggests that continuous multiscale drop detection provides a more sensitive description of the dynamical interactions that shape large-scale connectivity.

Conclusion

In summary, continuous multiscale wavelet-based detection of complexity drops overcomes limitations of amplitude-thresholding and captures additional temporal structure inherent to spontaneous complexity dynamics. The improved correspondence with FC suggests that this method provides a more sensitive account of how local complexity fluctuations relate to large-scale connectivity. This framework offers new opportunities for studying state-dependent changes in neural dynamics, including future applications to psychedelics, anaesthesia, and sleep.
