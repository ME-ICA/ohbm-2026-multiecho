Introduction

The menopause transition is associated with systemic metabolic and vascular changes, yet its effects on brain structure and microstructure are not fully understood ^1. Neuroimaging studies of women around the time of menopausal transition are often confounded by chronological age, making it difficult to disentangle menopause-related brain differences from normative aging. Moreover, prior work has frequently focused on single imaging modalities or isolated brain regions, limiting insight into the distributed nature of menopause-related brain changes. Using data from UK Biobank ^2, we examined multimodal neuroimaging differences between menopause states in an age matched sample, and characterized how brain traits are associated with this transition in a cross sectional study.

Methods

We analyzed multimodal MRI data from individuals aged 45-55 selected from a larger cohort of the UK Biobank. Individuals were classified as postmenopausal if they answered “yes” to having undergone menopause on or before the time of imaging. Responses that included any indication of having a hysterectomy or having changed across time points from “yes” to “no” were excluded from the analyses. If an individual did not have responses at the imaging timepoint while answering “yes” to having had menopause at the second imaging timepoint and their age at menopause was greater than 1 year younger than their age at scan, they were labeled as being post-menopause. Women labeled pre-menopause indicated that they had not undergone menopause and were still menstruating at the time of scan. Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) status was assessed by answering the question, “have you ever used HRT?”, and positivity was determined by responses of “yes” or “still taking hrt” on or before the imaging timepoint. Analyses were restricted to individuals aged 45-55 years old to focus on the menopausal transition period and reduce age-related confounding. Within this age range, complete cases for pre- and post-menopausal groups were age matched in a 1:1 ratio using the MatchIt package in R, resulting in equal group sizes with comparable age distributions. Structural imaging measures from T1-weighted MRI included 34 bilateral cortical thickness and surface area regions of interest (ROIs), and 16 subcortical volumes derived with FreeSurfer v7.1.1 using the Desikan-Killiany (DK) atlas ^3 ^4. dMRI white matter microstructure measures included skeletonized diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) metrics averaged in 24 bilateral JHU atlas ROIs using the ENIGMA TBSS pipeline. Average cortical gray matter DTI FA and MD microstructure measures were calculated in 34 bilateral DK atlas ROIs. The log of white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volumes from FLAIR was also included. We compared demographic and lifestyle factors between pre- and post- menopausal individuals. Regression analyses compared imaging measures between pre- and post-menopausal groups while accounting for age, college, Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), and intracranial volume (ICV) for volumetric measures. Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied across all imaging measures to correct for multiple comparisons.

Results

Our final sample included a total of 1018 subjects (N=509 per group, mean age 52.08±1.62 years old). Postmenopausal individuals were more likely to be on hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and have lower sleep scores, BMI, and hand grip strength. Across 207 regional imaging measures included in the analysis, 43 measures (20.7%) were considered statistically different (q<0.05) between the pre- and post-menopausal statuses. The majority (67%) of these differences were found in the cortical microstructural MD, where we observed lower and widespread cortical MD in postmenopausal individuals compared to premenopausal individuals. This was in line with observed lower global cortical MD, which was also detected (d=-0.26). Higher cortical FA in postmenopausal individuals was found in the banks of the superior temporal sulcus, insula, lingual, superior temporal, posterior cingulate, and precuneus gyri. Postmenopausal individuals had higher thickness in the banks of the superior temporal sulcus, fusiform, superior temporal, precuneus, and parahippocampal gyri. Lower pars triangularis surface area, lower MD of the fornix white matter region, and lower FA in the posterior corona radiata were also observed in postmenopausal individuals compared to premenopausal. WMH volumes did not pass the significance threshold (p=0.05).  Results were largely consistent when restricting analyses to individuals not on HRT. 

Conclusion

These findings indicate that menopausal status is associated with measurable and distributed brain differences beyond chronological aging. Postmenopausal individuals exhibited lower cortical MD, higher cortical FA, and higher cortical thickness, directions often interpreted as reflecting greater preservation of tissue. However, these measures lack biological specificity, and the observed patterns may reflect any number of factors like more pathogenic inflammatory/glial related changes influenced by hormonal transitions ^5 or compensatory processes, including brain stabilization after the perimenopause transition period. A further limitation of this work surrounds the lack of information on the perimenopausal state. There may be many individuals whom we labeled as pre-menopausal undergoing hormonal changes and symptoms of menopause in a perimenopausal state, which is not well defined in this cohort. Still, these findings suggest that diffusion based microstructural metrics in the gray matter cortex may reflect alterations associated with the menopausal transition that are not fully captured by macrostructural measures alone.  Future work will include expanding our analyses to include more biophysical models of cortical microstructure and determining the extent to which brain structure and microstructure are predictive of menopausal states. Together, these results underscore the importance of considering menopausal stage in studies of women’s brain aging and motivate longitudinal investigations incorporating detailed hormonal characterization to clarify temporal dynamics and hormonal contributions to observed neuroimaging changes.
