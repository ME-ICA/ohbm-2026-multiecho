Introduction

Adolescence is marked by heightened risk for suicidal and non-suicidal self-harm behaviours. While the dual-systems model attributes this vulnerability to a maturational imbalance between relatively faster-developing limbic reward systems and slower-maturing fronto-parietal control networks1, growing neuroimaging evidence suggests that suicidality is more closely associated with distributed network abnormalities rather than focal regional differences.2 Morphometric similarity networks (MSNs)3 offer a systems-level framework for quantifying coordinated structural organization by integrating multiple morphometric features into whole-brain networks. A recent ABCD-based MSN study showed that greater differentiation—particularly between isocortical and paralimbic/subcortical modules—tracks with higher cognitive function and lower psychiatric symptom burden, suggesting that coordinated morphometric development may index both adaptive functioning and vulnerability to psychopathology.4 However, whether such large-scale structural organization is associated with suicidal and self-harm behaviours remains unclear.     

Leveraging the large-scale longitudinal ABCD dataset, this study examines how MSNs within and between brain modules relate to suicidal and self-harm behaviour and impulsivity domains associated with such behaviour. By focusing on distributed structural organization, this work offers a systems-level perspective on neurodevelopmental correlates of suicidal and self-harm behaviour in preadolescence. The study protocol was published in the University of Nottingham Research Data Repository.

Methods

This study used ABCD Study data release 5.1.5 which included full data from baseline (age 9-10) to the Year 3 follow-up (age 12-13) and approximately half of the Year 4 data.

Brain morphometric similarity networks and modular similarities

Seventeen cortical and thirteen subcortical structural and diffusion MRI image-derived phenotypes (IDPs) were extracted from the Desikan–Killiany (68 bilateral cortical ROIs) and ASEG atlases (14 bilateral subcortical ROIs), following IDP choices informed by prior work.4 MRI acquisition and processing details are available in the ABCD documentation. Participants with MRI data missing or failing the ABCD quality control criteria were excluded. ROI labels and participant IDs were harmonized across IDPs.

Within-individual z-scores were computed for each IDP. Cortical and subcortical morphometric similarity networks (MSNs) were constructed as 68×68 and 14×14 ROI–ROI Pearson correlation matrices. A cortico–subcortical MSN (68×14) was computed using the 13 IDPs available across both tissue classes. (Figure 1a)

From the MSNs, cortical modules were first derived at the individual level using Louvain community detection, which was applied again on the resulting group co-classification matrix . This yielded three cortical modules: CM1 (primary sensory/somatomotor cortices), CM2 (paralimbic/ventromedial territories including cingulate, insula, OFC, parahippocampal cortex), and CM3 (fronto-temporal associative regions). Subcortical modules were defined empirically by similarity patterns within subcortex and between cortex and subcortex while avoiding singletons: SM1 (accumbens, amygdala, caudate, hippocampus, putamen) and SM2 (pallidum, thalamus). The cortical modules closely followed the previous study by Wu et al.4 while the subcortical modules differed by SM2 containing pallidum and thalamus instead of pallidum and putamen. For each participant, intra- and inter-modular similarity was defined as the mean MSN edge weight within and between module pairs. Figure 1b visualizes the edges of the intra- and inter-module similarities for cortex, subcortex, and cortico-subcortex.

Suicidal and self-harm behaviour and impulsivity measures

A binary self-harm outcome variable was defined based on current or lifetime report of nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) or suicidal behaviour (including any preparatory action or attempt) at any assessment from the baseline (age 9–10) to Year 4 follow-up (age 13–14). Twelve ABCD impulsivity metrics (UPPS, BIS/BAS, NIH Toolbox) spanning emotional, behavioural, cognitive, and reward-related domains were analyzed. All measures were rescaled using percent-of-maximum-possible (POMP) scoring. To identify relevant impulsivity metrics, univariate logistic regression models were run between the impulsivity measures and the binary variable of any suicidal or self-harm behaviour reported between age 9-14.

Statistical modelling

Prospective associations between baseline modular similarities at age 9-10 and lifetime self-harm behaviour up to age 14 were evaluated using population-averaged logistic regression with a binomial link. Each model included one z-standardized modular similarity metric as the primary predictor. Covariates were age, sex, pubertal status (child-/parent-reported Tanner stage), race/ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, Other), and intracranial volume. Cluster-robust standard errors accounted for site-level dependence. False discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg) corrected for multiple comparisons across modular similarities.

Associations between baseline modular similarities and baseline impulsivity measures were examined using linear mixed-effects models. For each modular metric, one impulsivity domain score served as the primary fixed effect with the same covariates as above and the modular similarity served as the dependent variable. A random intercept for site accounted for clustering. Multiple comparisons were FDR-corrected.

Results

Participants

Among the 11,868 participants with baseline MRI data, 1,004 with missing values and 963 failing ABCD’s recommended imaging inclusion criteria were excluded, yielding 9,901 participants. Mean age of the participants was 119.1 ± 7.5 months, and 4,830 (48.8%) were female. Most children were in pre-puberty (50.4%) or early (23.6%) or middle (23.3%) puberty. For modelling stability, late or post-pubertal children (1.7%) were combined into a mid-puberty and above group. Similarly for race / ethnicity, Asian (2.0%) category was combined with Other (12.2%). Majority of the children were White (53.5%), Hispanic (20.3%) or Black (14.0%). Mean intracranial volume was 1,494 ± 143 mm3.

Intra- and inter-module similarities

Figure 2a (left) visualizes the signs of mean 15 intra- and inter-module similarity edges. All intra-module similarities were positive (r ≈ .26–.84). All within-cortex and within-subcortex inter-module similarities were negative, with SM1–SM2 most dissimilar (r ≈ –.65). For the cortico-subcortical inter-module similarities, CM1 (somatomotor) was negatively correlated to SM1 but positively correlated with SM2, CM2 (paralimbic) was positive correlated with both subcortical modules, and CM3 (fronto-parietal) was negatively correlated with both subcortical modules.

Modular similarity and self-harm behaviour

Between baseline (age 9-10) and Year 4 (age 13-14), 1,995 (16.8%) participants reported suicidal (n = 622) or nonsuicidal self-harm behaviour (n = 1,373) (S/SHB). We tested baseline modular similarities as predictors of lifetime S/SHB between age 9-14. 10 out of 15 similarity metrics across cortex, subcortex, and cortico-subcortex were significant after FDR correction (q<0.05, Fig 2a middle). Among these, nine significant associations showed consistent directionality: positive modular similarities in normal network architecture (n=5, 3 intramodular and 2 intermodular) were negatively associated with S/SHB, and negative intermodular similarities (n=4) were positively associated with S/SHB. The strongest associations were higher inter-module similarities between CM3-SM2 (β_std= 0.120) and CM1-CM3 (β_std=0.117), lower intra-module similarity within SM2 (β_std=-0.116), lower inter-module similarity between CM2-SM1 (β_std=-0.103), and higher inter-module similarity between SM1-SM2 (β_std=0.098). Together, these patterns suggest broad reductions in normative brain modular differentiation and integration are associated with S/SHB.

Modular similarity and impulsivity

Nine baseline trait impulsivity measures (BIS; 4 BAS subscales; 4 UPPS subscales) were positively associated with 4-year S/SHB (OR per SD 1.07–1.38), with UPPS subscales showing the strongest associations (OR 1.24–1.38). For association with the brain modular similarity metrics, BIS, BAS Drive, BAS Reward Responsiveness, UPPS Negative Urgency and Positive Urgency were nominally significant, but only BAS Reward Responsiveness survived multiple testing correction. Higher BAS Reward Responsiveness was linked to (i) greater CM1–CM2 similarity, (ii) greater CM3–SM1 similarity, and (iii) lower CM1–SM2 similarity (|β_std| ≈ .02–.03) (Figure 2a right). As with self-harm, higher impulsivity was linked to reduced positive and increased negative modular similarity, even for nominal associations.

Conclusion

This study leverages the large-scale, longitudinal ABCD dataset to characterize how structural brain network organization is associated with suicidal and non-suicidal self-harm behaviour (S/SHB) in preadolescence (age 9-14). By employing morphometric similarity networks (MSNs), we captured coordinated maturation of cortical and subcortical circuits. A distributed but consistent inverse pattern emerged in nine out of 15 similarity metrics consistent with a broad attenuation of normative organization. Reductions in normative modular integration (e.g. less intramodular similarity) and modular differentiation (i.e. higher intermodular similarity) were associated with S/SHB up to four years later. Only paralimbic-frontoparietal intermodular dissimilarity increased with higher S/SHB risk. The morphometric similarity network pattern underlying trait impulsivity linked to S/SHB showed the same directionality (inverse to the normative pattern) but was less distributed and spatially distinct from the observed S/SHB changes.

These results provide new evidence for neurodevelopmental factors in the risk of S/SHB and trait impulsivity but do not directly support the dual-systems model of adolescent vulnerability. There is partial support for associated developmental deviations of the slower-maturing fronto-parietal associative/control system (CM3) indexing lower within module similarity but both reduced and higher intermodule separation associated with harmful behaviour while trait impulsivity was as expected linked with less segregation with limbic subcortical regions.

The widespread deviations from normative similarity architecture suggest large-scale maturational deficits within and between not only the FPN but also the sensorimotor, paralimbic, and subcortical systems (CM3-CM1, CM3-CM2, CM3-SM2). The strong SM1-SM2 effect further indicates that atypical differentiation within basal ganglia-thalamic loops may contribute to risk through dysregulated gating, action selection, or affect-motivation coupling. It is thus conceivable that widespread impairments in regulatory information processing heighten propensity to reactive or maladaptive behaviours.

The observation that brain structure showed more robust associations with S/SHB than impulsivity likely reflects several factors. First, S/SHB may index a composite neurobiological vulnerability reflecting multiple risk dimensions:  limbic-control imbalance (CM2/SM2-CM3), dysregulated salience/interoceptive signalling (CM2), impaired inhibitory control (CM3-CM1, CM3-SM2, SM1-SM2), and vulnerability to social stress and rumination (CM3). This is consistent with recent work that has demonstrated widespread hypoconnectivity across multiple brain systems in adults with suicidality.2 This may also explain the limited reproducibility of multi-univariate regional analyses for S/SHB, motivating systems-level approaches such as MSN that better characterize coordinated variations across the brain.  

We note also that S/SHB is a more high-threshold, clinically salient outcome compared to self-report impulsivity, which is more susceptible to noise and situational biases. In this study the S/SHB outcome was aggregated across ages 9-14, while impulsivity was only from baseline. Lastly, logistic models predicting rare but high-impact binary outcomes can be more sensitive to subtle structural deviations that linear continuous models.

Overall, this work identifies reduced brain organization as an early neurodevelopmental marker of vulnerability for adolescent suicidal and self-harm behaviour, providing a systems-level framework that future longitudinal and multimodal studies can build upon.
