Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD) are two highly prevalent disorders associated with significant clinical impairment. Although depressive symptoms are shared across the two disorders, their underlying neurobiology may differ1. Initiatives such as the ENIGMA Consortium provide the scale needed to examine transdiagnostic brain-behavior relationships in thousands of individuals, but pooling samples with different depression scales that vary in structure, symptom emphasis, and scoring remains a challenge.

Item response theory (IRT) offers a framework for harmonizing symptom measures by modelling item-level properties and placing responses onto a shared latent metric. Prior efforts, such as PROsetta Stone, have demonstrated the feasibility of IRT-based linking but have not incorporated clinician-rated instruments widely used in mood disorder research, nor applied harmonized symptom metrics within large neuroimaging studies2. Integrating IRT-derived symptom dimensions with structural brain measures is needed to clarify shared and diagnosis-specific neural correlates of depressive symptomatology.

This study harmonized five validated and widely used depression rating scales at the item level across 54 independently collected study samples sites, generating general and symptom-specific latent dimensions for cross-disorder analyses. Associations between these harmonized IRT scores and structural MRI measures were examined in 12,072 individuals with BD, MDD, and healthy controls. To support reproducibility and translational use, we developed an open-source tool for generating harmonized IRT-derived symptom dimensions and scale-specific predicted scores using R Shiny. Overall, this work creates a reproducible method for placing diverse depression scales on a common metric and linking those harmonized symptom dimensions to structural brain differences across mood disorders.

Methods

Data were aggregated from 54 international sites participating in the ENIGMA Bipolar Disorder and Major Depressive Disorder Working Groups. The harmonized dataset included 12,072 individuals (6,789 healthy controls, 3,307 with BD, and 1,903 with MDD). All contributing sites obtained institutional ethical approval and written informed consent. Depressive symptoms were assessed using one or more of five widely used clinician- and self-report measures: the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS), Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (IDS), and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D).

MRI acquisition and processing 

T1-weighted structural MRI scans were acquired on 1.5T and 3T Siemens, Philips, and GE systems. All images underwent ENIGMA-standardized FreeSurfer processing and visual quality control. Subcortical volumes and cortical thickness and surface area were extracted using the Desikan-Killiany atlas. To minimize scanner and site variability, ComBat harmonization3 was applied separately to cortical and subcortical measures, adjusting for age, sex and diagnosis. 

Item response theory (IRT) harmonization

Item-level responses from all five depression scales were harmonized using the graded response model (GRM). Suitability for IRT was evaluated via Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) indices, Bartlett’s test of sphericity, and confirmatory factor analysis with weighted least squares mean- and variance-adjusted (WLSMV) estimation to assess unidimensionality, local independence, and monotonicity. A stepwise calibration strategy was employed: item parameters were first estimated in a calibration subsample with full scale overlap and then extended to participants with partial instrument coverage, allowing all items to be placed on a common latent metric.

Exploratory factor analysis of pooled item-level data (principal axis factoring, promax rotation) identified five symptom dimensions: cognitive–affective, mood/motivational, somatic, anxiety, and suicidal ideation. General and domain-specific latent trait IRT scores (θ values) were estimated using empirical Bayes methods.

Open source depression harmonization tool

An open-source application was developed (HARMOODS) to operationalize the calibrated GRMs and provide automated harmonization of HDRS, MADRS, and BDI item-level data (Figure 1). Implemented in R Shiny, the tool performs item validation, category standardization, scoring, and a series of QC checks. It also offers visualization modules for item-level diagnostics, score distributions, and test information functions. Harmonized θ scores and scale-specific predicted totals can be exported for downstream statistical analyses. The open-source R Shiny application is available online (https://emmacorley.shinyapps.io/irt_app/, version 1.0; accessed November 27, 2025).

Statistical Analyses

Group differences in cortical and subcortical measures were assessed using ANCOVA. Associations between harmonized IRT scores and each regional MRI measure were tested using linear models. Diagnosis-by-symptom interactions were included to assess whether associations differed between disorders. For suicidal ideation, which could not be modeled using IRT as it was measured by a single item, binary logistic regression was applied using established severity thresholds for each rating scale. Age, sex and intracranial volume were controlled for across all models. Standardized β coefficients and Cohen’s d values were computed. False discovery rate (pFDR) correction was applied across all 156 brain regions for each depression dimension.

Results

IRT suitability, model performance and score validity 

Across all five depression measures, item distributions showed excellent suitability for IRT, with strong factorability (KMO= 0.97) and robust factor loadings (0.536-0.922). Parallel analysis supported a dominant general factor, alongside additional interpretable cognitive, mood, anxiety, somatic, and suicidal ideation dimensions.

IRT-derived general depression scores were strongly correlated with original scale totals (r= 0.82-0.94). Test information curves indicated greatest precision at moderate-to-severe levels (θ≈ 0.5-2.5), improving measurement accuracy where clinical severity is most variable. Across cortical and subcortical regions, IRT-derived scores explained equal or greater variance in structural brain measures than any individual unharmonized scale.

Group differences in brain structure

Relative to controls, both BD and MDD showed widespread structural alterations. Subcortical differences were observed in the hippocampus, amygdala, and accumbens (pFDR< 0.05). Cortical alterations included lower thickness and surface area across temporal, frontal, parietal, and cingulate regions. MDD and BD also showed smaller thalamic and striatal volumes and reduced cortical volume, with the largest effects in the bilateral insula, inferior temporal cortex, and anterior cingulate (d =0.11–0.15).

Association between general depression severity and brain structure 

Higher general IRT-derived depression severity scores were associated with lower right caudate volume (β= -0.084, pFDR= 0.012) and thinner left postcentral gyrus (β= -0.087, pFDR= 0.038). Negative associations with surface area were observed in the left cuneus, precentral, postcentral and transverse temporal gyri and right caudal middle frontal cortex (β range= -0.079 to -0.094, pFDR< 0.05, Figure 2). Positive effects included increased cortical volume in the left pars triangularis and right temporal pole (β range= 0.08-0.12).

Significant diagnosis-by-severity interactions appeared in multiple subcortical regions, including the hippocampus, accumbens, amygdala, thalamus, putamen, and pallidum (pFDR < 0.05). Seven cortical regions showed interaction effects, with the strongest in the right isthmus cingulate (F= 7.043). Surface area interactions were driven primarily by stronger negative associations in MDD, especially in the left entorhinal and left superior parietal cortices (Figure 2). 

Symptom dimension associations

Symptom dimensions showed distinct structural profiles. Somatic symptoms were linked to widespread alterations, including lower thickness in the right postcentral gyrus (β= -0.105, pFDR= 0.022) and reduced surface area across occipital, cingulate, and temporal cortices (β=-0.093 to -0.127). Three-way diagnosis × symptom × region interactions indicated stronger negative associations with general depression severity in MDD for ventricular and subcortical volumes, whereas BD showed relatively stronger negative associations in frontal and temporal surface area. Anxiety symptoms were associated with greater right thalamic volume and thickness in the left supramarginal gyrus, right insula, and right pars orbitalis (pFDR <0.05), with effects most pronounced in BD; additional diagnosis-specific interactions appeared in the pallidum, thalamus, temporal pole, and superior parietal cortex, generally negative in MDD.

Cognitive–affective symptoms were linked to thinner inferior temporal, lingual, precuneus, superior frontal, and superior parietal cortices (β= -0.101 to -0.144), alongside greater right temporal pole volume, with stronger effects in MDD. Mood/motivational symptoms were associated with lower accumbens and caudate volumes (β=-0.079 to -0.107), thinner bilateral postcentral gyri, and a widespread pattern of lower cortical surface area. Suicidal ideation showed no significant effects on subcortical volume or cortical thickness but was positively associated with surface area in the left supramarginal gyrus.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that item-level harmonization of psychiatric symptomatology using IRT provides a scalable solution to a major barrier in psychiatric neuroimaging: detecting brain-symptom associations despite inconsistent measurement of depressive symptoms across multisite datasets. By calibrating five widely used depression instruments onto a shared latent metric, the harmonized scores yielded more reliable estimates of symptom severity, showed greater sensitivity to structural brain variation, and explained equal or greater neuroanatomical variance than any individual scale. Importantly, this work represents the largest study to date to combine standardized structural neuroimaging with psychometrically harmonized symptom severity across disorders, underscoring the value of aligned dimensional phenotypes for improving signal detection and interpretability in large clinical cohorts.

Structural neuroimaging results revealed both shared and diagnosis-specific signatures of depressive symptoms. General severity was associated with fronto-striatal, temporal, and sensory-motor alterations, while individual dimensions showed dissociable profiles: somatic symptoms with widespread cortical and subcortical reductions; cognitive-affective symptoms with medial prefrontal, cingulate, temporal, and inferior parietal regions; anxiety symptoms with positive associations in the insula and thalamus; and mood/motivational deficits with prefrontal and striatal regions. Diagnosis-by-symptom interactions, particularly stronger negative associations in MDD than BD across limbic, cingulate, and parietal regions, suggest that similar clinical presentations arise from partially distinct neural substrates. These patterns are consistent with meta-analytic evidence that depressive symptomatology in MDD and BD show both overlapping and distinct grey-matter abnormalities, particularly within frontal, limbic, and parietal networks4,5. 

Strengths include a uniquely large transdiagnostic sample (N=12,072) across 54 sites; item-level harmonization of clinician- and self-rated instruments; standardized ENIGMA neuroimaging processing with ComBat; and domain-specific IRT modelling enabling refined symptom-brain inferences. Limitations include the cross-sectional design, incomplete item correspondence for some scales, and the focus on structural MRI. Future work should apply this framework to longitudinal datasets, predict clinical outcomes, integrate genetic data, and extend IRT-based linking to additional tools.

To support broader adoption, we developed HARMOODs, an open-source R Shiny application for generating calibrated symptom scores. HARMOODs provides the unique ability to perform all computations locally in the user's browser. It does not store uploaded data and requires no identifiers, offering the advantage of minimal GDPR risks. Outputs are generated only client-side, and data cannot be recovered if lost. By lowering barriers to implementing harmonized symptom scoring, HARMOODs provides a practical resource for multisite research.

Overall, this study offers the most comprehensive integration of item-level depression harmonization with structural neuroimaging in mood disorders to date, establishing a framework for examining symptom-brain relationships in large, diverse cohorts and enabling globally comparable symptom scores for future multimodal and longitudinal applications.
