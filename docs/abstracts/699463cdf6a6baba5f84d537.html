Introduction

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is caused by a progressive loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the brain. Although primarily characterised as a movement disorder, it is accompanied by a wide range of non-motor symptoms that substantially contribute to disease burden and reduced quality of life. Among these, anxiety is the most prevalent and affects 31% of patients, is associated with cognitive decline, and is described by patients as being as incapacitating as motor symptoms1.

The neurobiological basis of anxiety in PD is not well understood. Pathological changes in structures in the fear and limbic circuits, which include the amygdala (responsible for fear conditioning) and hippocampus (responsible for contextual memory), can occur early in the disease process. Alterations in these structures have also been linked to general anxiety disorders in healthy controls, though results have been inconsistent. While most studies focus on functional activity and whole volumes, the specific relationship between different hippocampal sub-structures and anxiety in PD and non-PD has not been explored.

This study examines the structural differences in the amygdala as a whole, and the sub-structures of the hippocampus between patients with PD with anxiety (PDA), PD without anxiety (PDN), healthy controls with anxiety (HCA) and healthy controls without anxiety (HCN). In addition, we examine correlations of anxiety severity in PD and brain structures to better understand the neurobiology of anxiety in PD. These findings will indicate the difference in these brain structures from PD effects, and whether anxiety in PD is a specific PD phenotype similar to general anxiety. 

Methods

Participants: A total of 90 participants were included across four groups: PDA (n=21), PDN (n=24), HCA (n=20) and HCN (n=25). Participants were recruited from patient organisation databases (Join Dementia Research, Parkinson’s UK), community charities (Age UK, Anxiety UK), neurology clinics and existing University College London (UCL) databases. PD patients had to have a clinically confirmed diagnosis of PD. Participants were excluded if they had a history of psychotic disorders, alcohol or substance abuse, or any MRI contraindications such as pacemakers, aneurysm clips, cochlear implants, intrauterine devices, metal fragments in the eye, a body weight exceeding 250 lbs, pregnancy, or claustrophobia. 

Clinical Assessments: The Generalised Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7) was used across all four groups, with a score of greater than or equal to 5 indicating mild anxiety as the study threshold. Anxiety in PD groups was also assessed using the Parkinson’s Anxiety Scale (PAS), which measures persistent anxiety (PAS-A), episodic anxiety (PAS-B) and avoidance behaviour (PAS-C). Depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and cognition using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Motor symptom severity was evaluated during ‘ON’ phases using the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) Part III. Demographic and clinical variables (age, gender, disease duration, anxiety duration, Levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) and Hoehn and Yahr (HY) stage) are summarised in Table 1. Statistical comparisons for demographic/clinical data used Chi-Squared tests, t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or Kruskal-Wallis tests in MATLAB R2023b. 

MRI Acquisition & Analysis: Scanning was performed on a 3Tesla Siemens Magnetom Prisma fit MRI system with a 64-channel head coil at the Functional Imaging Laboratory (UCL). Sequences included a T1-weighted Magnetisation Prepared Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echo (MPRAGE) (176 slices; slice thickness = 1mm; FoV = 256x256mm; voxel size = 1x1x1mm; TR = 2530ms; TE = 3.34ms) and a T2-weighted turbo spin-echo (TSE) (40 slices; slice thickness = 2mm; FoV = 175x175mm; voxel size = 0.4x0.4x0.4mm; TR = 10380ms; TE = 50ms). Automated segmentation of the hippocampus was performed using Freesurfer’s recon-all command (version 6.0.0) (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) employing the T1/T2 hybrid pipeline2. Regions of interest (ROIs) included: CA1, CA2/3, CA4, fimbria, granule cell and molecular layers of the dentate gyrus (GC_DG), hippocampus-amygdala transition area (HATA), hippocampal fissure, hippocampal tail, molecular layer, parasubiculum, presubiculum, subiculum, and whole hippocampus (Figure 1.0). ROI volumes were normalised by dividing each volume by total intracranial volume (TIV) and then multiplied by 100 to generate % of TIV. Analyses focused on CA1, CA2/3, amygdala, HATA, fimbria, and the whole hippocampus. Group differences were assessed using non-parametric ANCOVAs (covariates: age, gender, TIV, and PD disease duration, where appropriate), and residual volumes (res), adjusted for these covariates, are displayed in Figure 2. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d (d). Partial Pearson correlations (r), using the same covariates, examined associations between anxiety severity (GAD-7, PAS) and ROIs within PD groups.

Results

Demographics: Groups were well-matched for age and gender. The HCA and PDA groups had significantly higher anxiety scores than the non-anxious groups. Depression scores (PHQ-9) followed a similar pattern, with anxious groups scoring higher than non-anxious groups. Anxiety duration differed significantly between the PDA and the HCA groups, with the HCA group reporting a much longer duration (PDA: 6.44 (15.96) vs. HCA: 36.71 (32.07), p = 0.01). Cognition (MoCA) was significantly worse in PDA compared to the HCN (PDA: 24.12 (2.90) vs. HCN: 27.41 (1.74), p = 0.02).

MRI Results: 

All ROI volumes are expressed as % of TIV. Due to non-normally distributed data, all values are reported as medians (IQRs). Residual volumes (res) are reported alongside raw values for clarity. 

Within our ROIs, there was no difference between HCA and HCN, and no difference between PDN and HCN. The PDA group compared to the PDN group, however, had volume reductions in the right fimbria (PDA: 0.0039 (0.0016), res: -0.00020% vs. PDN: 0.0042 (0.0015), res: -0.00018%; p = 0.03, d = 0.195), but volume enlargement in the right HATA (PDA: 0.0034 (0.0012%), res: -0.00002% vs. PDN: 0.0031% (0.0006%), res: -0.00019%; p = 0.006, d = 0.0666). 

In post hoc correlational analyses, no significant associations were found in the PD groups between motor symptom laterality and right-sided dominance of atrophy(PDA: -0.03 (0.88) vs. PDN: -0.03 (0.56), p = 0.62). 

Correlational analyses (Table 2) within the PD groups revealed significant negative correlations between anxiety severity and volume of left-sided limbic structures: GAD-7 and total PAS scores correlated moderately and negatively with left fimbria volume (r = -0.44, p = 0.004 and r = -0.34, p = 0.03, respectively), and GAD-7 score correlated negatively with left HATA volume (r = -0.46, p = 0.003). Correlational analyses within the HC groups revealed no significant correlations.

Conclusion

This study revealed a distinct pattern of limbic system atrophy in the hippocampal-amygdala interface regions in Parkinson’s disease with anxiety. Furthermore, there were significant negative correlations between higher anxiety scores and reduced volume of the right fimbria and left HATA in the combined PD groups. Although preliminary, these findings suggest that progressive limbic atrophy may contribute to the emergence of anxiety in PD. In contrast, no such associations were found in anxious healthy controls, despite longer-standing anxiety. This, and the lack of significant findings in correlational analyses for healthy controls, indicates that limbic atrophy is unlikely to be the driver for anxiety outside of PD. 

The most interesting finding in our study is the reduction in volume of the left and right fimbria in the PDA group compared to the HCA group, and the negative correlation of anxiety scores with these structures. The fimbria is a pivotal white matter pathway in the circuit of Papez that underpins normal emotional regulation3. It connects the hippocampus to other limbic structures like the thalamus and the amygdala4, and if disrupted, could cause dysfunction in downstream limbic structures. Previous literature has identified that functional changes in these key limbic structures5 have been observed in generalised anxiety disorders, which aligns with our findings. When taking into account the residual volumes displayed in Figure 2, both the left and right fimbria are smaller than expected in the PDA group after accounting for age, sex and TIV. Our findings therefore support a role for reduction in fimbria volume in the phenotype of anxiety in PD.

Limitations of this study include the use of a cross-sectional design, a modest sample size, and potential image quality issues due to movement in PD patients, all of which may reduce sensitivity. Additionally, the use of automated segmentation over manual segmentation contributed to the limitations of this study, as it was initially designed for use on healthy controls, as was the GAD-7. Furthermore, the definition of the PDA group using a GAD-7 threshold of ≥5 rather than comparison of those with very high scores to those without anxiety may have limited our ability to detect stronger group effects. 

In conclusion, these findings demonstrate a distinct pattern of limbic system alterations associated with anxiety in PD. Our findings support the view that anxiety in PD reflects underlying limbic circuitry changes based on the underlying pathology of PD, rather than a reactive psychological response. This research raises the possibility that anxiety in PD may represent a distinct clinical and pathological phenotype rather than simply a comorbidity, which may call for specific treatments, requiring future work to determine whether the observed limbic volume alterations change with intervention. Future research should therefore employ longitudinal and multimodal imaging approaches to determine the functional relevance of these structural changes, clarify subtype-specific patterns, and assess their potential as biomarkers for the emergence or progression of anxiety in PD.
