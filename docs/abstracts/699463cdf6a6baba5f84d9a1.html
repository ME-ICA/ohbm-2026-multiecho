Introduction

In the United States, nearly 3 million people receive treatment for traumatic brain injury (TBI) yearly and TBI is a leading cause of long-term disability in adults. The various pathophysiological mechanisms of TBI make multiple brain systems vulnerable to injury, causing widespread cognitive deficits, and subsequently, impairments in multiple forms of memory are common. These deficits are long-term and may continue to change over time for some individuals. Of the structures at risk during injury, the hippocampus and medial temporal lobes are highly vulnerable. Unsurprisingly, deficits in declarative memory are a highly prevalent form of memory impairment in TBI. Given recent discoveries about the contribution of the hippocampus to processing in short-term or working memory and to cognitive abilities associated with nondeclarative memory, it is likely that hippocampal damage may also contribute to deficits in multiple forms of memory. However, little is known about the microstructural integrity of the hippocampus and surrounding white matter in TBI. In this study we will evaluate hippocampal microstructure in individuals with TBI and examine how microstructural integrity correlates with relational memory ability. We approach this evaluation using diffusion-weighted imaging and a spatial reconstruction task. We hypothesize adults with chronic moderate-severe TBI will have higher hippocampal mean diffusivity, and changes in hippocampal microstructure will correlate with relational memory ability. Due to the increasing occurrence and perceived functional burden of TBI and subsequent memory deficits, it is vital to identify potential structural markers capable of informing treatment. My findings will have implications for instructional and rehabilitative strategies for healthy individuals and individuals with memory impairment to benefit memory and overall cognitive functioning.

Methods

Participants for this study include 75 participants, 38 with moderate-severe TBI (Mage = 36.00 years) and 35 without TBI [non-impaired comparison (NC); (Mage = 37.08 years; Mtime = 76.21 months since injury)] collected at Vanderbilt University Medical Center via the Vanderbilt Brain Injury Patient Registry.1 Eligible participants with TBI and NC were invited to undergo neuroimaging and cognitive assessment, providing data to be used in the current study. Participants enrolled in the TBI group were 18 years or older to limit the effects of developmental changes and younger than 55 to conservatively limit the effects of expected age-related cognitive decline. Each TBI participant sustained a single moderate-severe TBI, as determined using the Mayo Classification System.2 Injury information was determined from available medical records and a semi-structured participant interview. Participants with TBI were in the chronic phase of injury (>6 months post-injury) at the time of data collection. We also have an NC group with no history of neurological or cognitive disability who are matched pairwise to participants with TBI on sex, age, and educational attainment to reduce between-group demographic variability and ensure similar within-group demographic variability.

Hippocampal microstructure and relational memory were measured across both groups. All MRI data was collected using a 3T Philips MR 7700 with a 32-channel head coil. The T1-weighted (T1w) turbo field echo scan had 1 mm3 resolution, field of view (FOV)= 240 mm × 240 mm, 170 slices, and SENSE factor = 2.80. The multi-shell DWI echo planar imaging sequences were acquired with 23 directions at B-value = 500, 47 directions at B-value = 1500, and 70 directions at B-value = 2500. Each sequence included 7 interleaved B=0 volumes. The multiband DWI sequences (factor of 3) had a 2.14 mm3 voxel size, 63 slices, flip angle = 90°, TR = 4300 ms, TE = 91.5, and FOV = 240 mm x 240 mm. A separate scan was obtained with the reverse phase encoding for each B value. Data was collected over a one-year period from July 2023 to July 2024. For neuroimaging data, collected T1-weighted images and DWI data were preprocessed and analyzed using FSL tools. For hippocampal microstructure, mean diffusivity (MD) was used as a metric to determine microstructural changes. The spatial reconstruction (SR) task was used to measure relational memory. The SR task was completed on a Microsoft Surface Pro tablet3 the same day as neuroimaging. Participants were asked to remember the locations of novel objects presented on the screen. There were 5 trials of each set size (i.e., 2, 4, 6, 8, 10-items in each trial). Participants were first shown an instruction page and then presented with two practice trials. The first trial presents the novel objects on a white screen. After a set time corresponding to the number of items presented in the trial, the objects then disappear. After a pause of time relevant to the trial, the objects reappear at the top of the screen in a random order. The participant then moved the object from the top of the screen to a space on the screen where they believe they “studied” the object. No accuracy feedback was given. For this task, we focused on the dependent variable, accurate single-item placement, which is a measure of the participant’s ability to place the correct item in the correct studied location. This variable reflects the binding of the item to its specific location.

The primary statistical analysis to determine differences between the groups will be a linear mixed models design. For all analyses, a random intercept term for each subject will be included in the model to account for the correlations between repeated measures from the same subject. Model assumptions, including normality, will be assessed through residuals analysis. All predictor variables will be mean-centered to minimize the potential for multicollinearity.

Results

Analysis of our neuroimaging data supports a statistically significant group difference when comparing mean diffusivity between the TBI and NC groups. The TBI group (Mleft = .00086, SDleft = .000045; Mright = .00087, SDright = .000037) had higher mean diffusivity in both the left and right hippocampus compared to the NC group (Mleft = .00092, SDleft = .00015; Mright = .00095, SDright = .000091) t(53)left = -3.48, p = .001; t(50)right = -4.61, p < .001. 

These statistical tests were conducted prior to proceeding with the primary statistical analysis outlined above to ensure our hypothesis was sound. We will be proceeding with the linear mixed models design to further evaluate group differences in hippocampal microstructure with regional specificity (i.e., CA1, dentate gyrus, subiculum, and fimbria). Following this, we will also perform further analysis to correlate diffusion metrics with spatial reconstruction task performance.

Conclusion

Though this project is still ongoing, current results suggest there are indeed differences in hippocampal microstructure as measured by mean diffusivity when comparing individuals without TBI and individuals with chronic moderate-severe TBI. Further investigation is ongoing to further characterize this difference and elucidate regional specificity along with correlating these differences to relational memory ability. This research is important to support the goal of determining the relationship between hippocampal microstructural deficits and relational memory ability in patients with cognitive disorders. Additionally, this project supports the continued exploration of the potential role of neuroimaging in guiding rehabilitation strategies, and how the assessment and rehabilitation of memory impairments can impact the care of TBI patients.
