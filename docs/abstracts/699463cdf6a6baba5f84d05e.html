Introduction

Since its first introduction in the 1950s, brain simulation has been a prolific subject of research. Numerous models and tools have been developed to explain the behavior of neural populations in various contexts, for instance resting state or cognitive tasks. Among these methods, Neural Mass Models (NMM) proved their efficiency by explaining a large set of physiological or pathological cortical activities, such as epilepsy seizures1, or EEG signal generation during a motor task2. These models rely on the fact that populations of neurons sharing similar characteristics, such as excitatory or inhibitory behaviors, can be lumped into a point through a mean field approach, making it possible to simulate the electrical activity of hundreds of neurons through differential equations without high computational costs.
Modeling neural activity is a useful way to provide information about underlying functions of the brain and can explain phenomena occurring while acquiring brain signals with specific neuroimaging modalities. These modalities often have specific characteristics, and combining them is a useful way to associate these characteristics, providing a larger view of brain events during an acquisition. One of the most used bimodality is electroencephalography (EEG) associated with functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI), which combines the excellent time resolution of EEG, based on the brain electrical activity, and the good spatial resolution of fMRI, relying on the Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD) signal. One key question is that the link between these two signals is not yet fully understood, and simulation could provide insights about this link.
This study aims to assess the validity of simulated EEG-fMRI signals from the Supplementary Motor Area (SMA) during an experiment that alternates rest and motor task. To do so, we compared simulated EEG-fMRI with different noise levels and real data recorded during a Neurofeedback motor task from the dataset XP23.

Methods

The neural activity necessary to simulate EEG and fMRI signals is generated using Neurolib4, a Python library that offers a whole-brain modeling framework based on neural masses. Several classical models are already implemented in the library, such as the Wilson-Cowan model. This model is based on the interaction between excitatory and inhibitory neuronal sub-populations and outputs the firing rate (FR) of the whole population. Neurolib also adds a noise component to the simulation, generally modeled by an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, representing small variations in neural activity. Noise amplitude can be changed by tweaking the standard deviation of the process, called σ. Therefore, here, we used the Wilson-Cowan model for its versatility and widespread use in neuroimaging community.

Neurolib relies on a graph implementation to perform whole-brain simulations. The nodes of this graph are spatially defined NMMs, which interact with each other during the experiment. The relative position of these nodes is given by the Automated Anatomical Labeling 2 (AAL2) atlas: each node represents one of the 80 Regions of Interest (ROI) present in the atlas. To add biological plausibility to the simulations, the interactions between nodes are conditioned by a structural connectivity matrix named Cmat based on tractography, a functional connectivity matrix named Fmat, and a delay matrix representing the average fiber length between two ROIs. Figure 1 shows a summary of the pipeline.

During our experiment, the resting state was modeled as a 0 V constant stimulus, using an absence of synaptic input sent to all NMMs of the whole-brain model. The motor task will be modeled as a 30 Hz sinusoidal stimulus sent to the left SMA (lSMA) and right SMA (rSMA) ROIs of the AAL2 atlas, with an amplitude of 2 mV, to ensure that the stimulus has an influence on the model output. We extracted the post-synaptic potentials (PSP) and Firing Rate (FR) of each node computed during the simulation.

The simulated neural activity, represented as the PSP and FR time series, served as the basis for generating simulated EEG and fMRI signals. To estimate EEG signals, a forward model is necessary and is known as the LeadField matrix. This matrix takes several parameters, one of them being the final EEG montage. The chosen montage was a 10-20 configuration of 64 electrodes, as the one used in³, to have physiologically meaningful EEG results. The EEG signal was obtained by multiplying the total PSP of each node with the LeadField matrix.

The process used to simulate fMRI in Neurolib is based on the Balloon model5, which takes the FR as an input, and computes the BOLD signal associated to the neural activity of an experiment.

To evaluate the quality of our simulations, we compare them to a real dataset. The XP2 Dataset3 collected the bimodal EEG-fMRI data of 16 participants, acquired during a neurofeedback (NF) experiment. Each NF series consisted of eight blocks, each comprising 20 seconds of rest followed by 20 seconds of a motor task. During the task periods, participants were instructed to focus on the mental imagery of a hand movement. For our simulations, and to facilitate comparison, we kept the same block duration. However, due to computational constraints, only three blocks were simulated.

Comparisons between signals were made between three simulated runs with different noise amplitudes σ, and real EEG and fMRI signals. In order to validate the realism of simulated EEG signals, we compared their power spectral density empirically. The BOLD comparison was also made by comparing the output of the Balloon model, with the average BOLD signal for all runs of all XP2 subjects.

Results

To simulate real acquisition, and compare more efficiently the signals, we subsampled our BOLD signal afterward, with a sampling frequency of 1 Hz, based on the Time Repetition (TR) of fMRI acquisitions in the dataset XP2.

To efficiently compare EEG-BOLD signals, a post processing step was also applied to simulated EEGs. To match the post-processing present in XP2, we downsampled the simulated EEG signal to a sampling frequency of 200 Hz, and applied a band-pass filter, with a low cutoff frequency set to 0.1 Hz, and a high cutoff frequency set to 70 Hz, as higher frequencies are more likely to be induced by acquisition noise in real EEG recordings. Since no acquisition noise was generated in the simulation, no other EEG post-processing steps were required. The simulated BOLD signal was not post-processed for the same reasons. Three simulation runs were studied, each with a different neural activity noise amplitude: σ = 0.0 (noiseless), σ = 0.03 and σ = 0.1 (very noisy). The results are shown in Figure 2.

Among the three different neuronal noise values, the BOLD signal generated with σ= 0.1 had the greater visual similarities with the real BOLD signal originating from SMA. The shape of the simulated signal with heavy noise (σ=0.1) roughly matches the shape of the real signal, notably during the first occurrence of the stimulus during the experiment. However, this similarity is possible because of the noise induced in the simulation: since BOLD recordings are often noisy in real situations, adding noise to a simulated BOLD increases visual similarities. The activation delay in all simulated signals, is also similar to real data: one can note a delay of several seconds after the beginning of the motor task step, for the signal to reach its local maximum. We can still observe differences between real and simulated BOLD, notably in the amplitudes during the motor task: The simulated signals had a higher amplitude than the real signals. Moreover, When the noise level is moderate (the first two cases), weak activations are observed during the task in regions beyond the SMA, reflecting the influence of SMA activity on neighboring regions through the connectivity matrices incorporated in the model.

The differences between simulated EEG signals and real signals are more pronounced than those observed in BOLD signals. While the ground-truth Power Spectral Density (PSD) decreases linearly until 50Hz and then remains constant at -60dB, the simulated PSDs decrease exponentially to 10–20dB, indicating that frequency amplitudes are higher in the simulated data. Moreover, the simulated EEG data contain fewer frequency components and do not have the complexity of real ones: several harmonics are visible in the ground truth spectrum, especially around 5 Hz, 35 Hz, and 47 Hz approximately, whereas in the noisy simulated spectrum, only a weak harmonic peak at 30 Hz is observable, which corresponds to the frequency of the stimulus applied to rSMA and lSMA during the motor task. These peaks are clearly visible in the noiseless simulated EEG spectrum. Since no noise was applied, all the frequencies generated by the model are apparent, making the stimulus frequency more prevalent in the spectrum. Almost no information can be extract visually from the spectrum based on the simulated signal with σ = 0.1. Peaks are observed at similar frequencies, around 30 Hz, in both real and simulated EEG signals. However, the greater complexity of real EEG data may contribute to similar spectral features without a direct correspondence to the modeled sources. Differences are also present between the topoplots of β frequency band : while activation in SMA is clearly visible in noisy simulated data, we can only see a desynchronisation at the center of the topoplot, which can be explained by the contribution of other EEG sources, and the fact that rest and task steps are averaged together in the topoplots.

Conclusion

We showed that generating semi-realistic EEG-fMRI signals in a precise area of the brain during a motor task was possible with simulation tools like Neurolib, by developing a proof-of-concept pipeline generating neuronal activity. By varying the noise amplitude in our simulated EEG-fMRI signals, we also showed that neural activity noise had a high influence on the similarities between real, and simulated signals, especially in simulated BOLD. In our experiment, the stimulus was applied only to the rSMA and lSMA. Incorporating additional brain areas, such as those involved in visual attention or task comprehension, would likely produce signals closer to those observed in a motor neurofeedback experiment. This initial work paves the way for further improvements, such as employing more sophisticated NMM models like the Jansen-Rit model, generating more realistic stimulus, modeling interactions between SMA and the motor cortex, and for advancing our understanding of the relationship between EEG and BOLD signals.
