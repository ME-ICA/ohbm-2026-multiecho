Introduction

Disruptions in thalamic and basal ganglia circuits are key pathophysiological features of both Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) and Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). While resting-state functional connectivity (FC) has consistently identified subcortico-cortical dysconnectivity in these conditions, FC is inherently undirected, masking the causal dynamics of these alterations. Specifically, it remains unclear whether these network deficits arise from impaired top-down cortical control or aberrant bottom-up signaling. This distinction is crucial, as it highlights fundamentally different neurobiological mechanisms. We employed regression Dynamic Causal Modeling (rDCM)—a generative framework capable of inferring directed effective connectivity (EC) in whole-brain networks—to dissociate the directional architecture of thalamo-cortical and basal ganglia-cortical circuits in large cohorts of ADHD and ASD.

Methods

T1-weighted images from 175 ADHD patients (mean age=11.77) and 178 TD subjects (mean age=11.78) from the ADHD-200 dataset were processed using FreeSurfer. Resting-state fMRI was performed with C-PAC and included skull stripping, slice-time correction, nuisance regression (motion, white matter, CSF), and bandpass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz). Data were co-registered to MNI152, mapped to midthickness surfaces, resampled to Conte69, smoothed (6 mm FWHM), and downsampled to 10k vertices. Functional preprocessing of 103 ASD (mean age 20.85) and 107 TD (mean age 19.32) subjects from the ABIDE dataset was performed using the Connectome Computation System (CCS), C-PAC, DPARSF, and NIAK. The thalamus and basal ganglia were subdivided using the Tian atlas (8 and 10 subdivisions per hemisphere). Group differences in FC were first assessed at the whole-structure level and then refined using dominance analysis to identify subdivisions driving the effects (Fig. 1a). Directional influences were estimated using rDCM among key cortical-subcortical nodes. (Fig. 2a)

Results

While FC revealed overlapping spatial dysconnectivity, rDCM uncovered opposing directional architectures. In ADHD, FC showed mixed alterations: increases in lateral prefrontal and parietal regions and decreases in medial prefrontal and temporal areas. Basal ganglia–cortical circuits exhibited hyperconnectivity in lateral frontal and insular regions and hypoconnectivity in the posterior cingulate and occipital cortex. Dominance analysis identified the dorsal caudate and putamen as primary drivers of these abnormalities (Fig. 1b, c). ADHD was characterized by a unidirectional bias: projections from the dorsal thalamus to the cortex and from the cortex to the basal ganglia were preserved (Fig. 2b, c). In ASD, connectivity alterations were more widespread, showing robust increases linking the thalamus and basal ganglia with dorsomedial/dorsolateral prefrontal, parietal, and temporal cortices, together with focal decreases in ventromedial prefrontal and occipitotemporal regions (Fig. 1b, c). Effective connectivity was bidirectional and distributed, suggesting atypical reciprocal coupling across subcortical hubs. (Fig. 2b, c)

Conclusion

ASD and ADHD exhibit distinct modes of subcortico–cortical dysconnectivity. ADHD is characterized by unidirectional communication, whereas ASD shows broad reciprocal reorganization. These findings extend prior models of disrupted hierarchical control and highlight candidate biomarkers for differential diagnosis and circuit-specific intervention strategies in neurodevelopmental disorders.
