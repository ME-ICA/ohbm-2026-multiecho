Introduction

Amyloid deposition is a core biological process driving Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology and is well described within the Amyloid–Tau–Neurodegeneration (ATN) framework, which reconceptualizes AD from a clinical syndrome to a biologically defined disease.(1) Amyloid accumulation begins years before overt cognitive decline; however, substantial heterogeneity in amyloid burden exists even at early clinical stages. Although structural brain changes are traditionally considered downstream events in the AD cascade, growing evidence indicates that regional gray-matter alterations emerge early and provide complementary information about Aβ accumulation. In parallel, olfactory dysfunction—one of the earliest sensory deficits in AD—has been proposed as a sensitive behavioral marker of underlying neuropathology.(2,3)

We hypothesized that individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) would exhibit (i) greater amyloid burden and (ii) regionally specific gray-matter alterations compared with healthy controls (HC), and that both distribution-based and region-based imaging features would discriminate HC from MCI above chance levels. We further hypothesized that olfactory performance would be impaired in MCI and would provide additional discriminatory value beyond imaging measures alone, reflecting early network-level vulnerability associated with AD pathology.

Methods

Seventy-three participants (32 healthy controls [HC], 41 mild cognitive impairment [MCI]) completed standardized olfactory testing, structural MRI (SPM 12 & Freesurfer), and amyloid PET imaging (Freesurfer and Clinica) using [¹⁸F]-florbetapir. Participant-level SUVR histograms were constructed using predefined bins, with bin counts normalized by each participant’s total count yielding unit-area probability distributions. The data used to construct histograms were taken from normalized brain images that have had the CSF and white matter masked out using Freesurfer-generated masks. Group-level distributions were derived by averaging normalized bin frequencies within each diagnostic group (Figure 1.). Wasserstein distance was then computed to quantify distributional differences in amyloid burden between HC and MCI.(4)

Using a nested modeling framework, generalized linear models (GLMs) evaluated classification performance via the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Models were first fit using imaging features alone and then extended to include olfactory performance to assess incremental discriminatory value. Five-fold cross-validation was applied to all models to ensure robust performance estimates. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Results

Amyloid SUVR distributions differed between HC and MCI, showing clear separation at the distributional level. Wasserstein distance–based features, embedded using multidimensional scaling (MDS), yielded low-dimensional representations that captured meaningful between-group variation. Generalized linear models using these amyloid distribution features demonstrated an AUC of 0.76 in discrimination ability between HC and MCI (Figure 2.A.). Including olfactory identification improved results slightly to an AUC of 0.8.

Gray-matter volumes were summarized using principal component analysis (PCA) applied to regional gray-matter measures within the olfactory-default mode network.(5) The first two components explaining the dominant variance across regions. Models using gray-matter PCA features alone showed modest discrimination performance (AUC 0.503). However, the inclusion of olfactory identification scores significantly improved classification accuracy to around 0.644 (Figure 2.B.).

Combining models, integrating gray-matter features and amyloid distribution features resulted in a discriminatory ability around 0.714 while adding olfactory identification increased this to about 0.759 (Figure 2.C. & D.). The highest classification performance was observed in the amyloid distribution and olfactory identification model.

Conclusion

Here, we demonstrate that distribution-based amyloid features and regional gray-matter measures each achieve above-chance (50%) discrimination between healthy controls (HC) and individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Across both molecular and structural imaging modalities, the inclusion of olfactory ability significantly improved model fit and classification performance. These findings underscore the potential role of olfactory performance as a complementary behavioral marker for early disease characterization.

Although the present analyses were conducted within the available cohort, future studies in larger, independent samples will be necessary to further establish the robustness and generalizability of these results. Nevertheless, the consistent incremental contribution of olfactory performance across imaging modalities highlights its value within multimodal frameworks that integrate behavioral measures with imaging biomarkers to characterize early disease progression.
