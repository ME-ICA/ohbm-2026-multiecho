Introduction

Catatonia is a severe psychomotor syndrome associated with mood, psychotic, and neurodevelopmental disorders, as well as non-psychiatric medical conditions1,2. Its clinical heterogeneity leads to underdiagnosis and significant morbidity and mortality. Identifying diagnostic biomarkers is therefore a critical challenge that must be addressed to improve early intervention and reduce the burden of disease. 

At the molecular level, catatonia is hypothesized to stem from an imbalance between cortical excitatory and inhibitory activity, involving glutamatergic and GABAergic neurotransmission3. As such, electroencephalography (EEG) could provide an accessible, non-invasive means to quantify anomalies in cortical activity4 that could be relevant for catatonia diagnosis. Patients with catatonia routinely undergo EEG in clinical practice as part of the initial differential diagnosis to rule out epilepsy but it can also provide quantitative markers of the temporal dynamics of neuronal networks, and neuronal excitation/inhibition imbalance. To date, however, no published studies have investigated quantitative EEG measures in catatonia.

We studied quantitative EEG markers: power spectral density in each frequency band (PSD - reflecting excitation/inhibition balance), alpha peak frequency (PAF - related to deviations in neurodevelopmental trajectories), and C and D microstates (previously linked respectively to self-referential and externally-oriented attentional modes). We therefore hypothesized that this imbalance would manifest as abnormalities in PSD distribution, a decrease in peak alpha frequency, and an increase in the representation of C over D microstate classes. 

To test the relevance of these markers in a real-world setting, we compared them between patients with and without catatonia (respectively N=102 and N=519), extracted from a transdiagnostic hospital-based cohort of patients with schizophrenia and/or mood disorders.

Methods

We screened all individuals who received a diagnosis of schizophrenia or mood spectrum disorders (based on ICD-10 diagnostic codes) and who also underwent EEG recordings between 2019 and 2024, extracted from the GHU Paris Psychiatry and Neurosciences data warehouse (N = 850). To identify patients with catatonia, we used an automated textual research looking for the expression “catato*”. Each of the resulting medical records was then manually checked to confirm the diagnosis of catatonia according to DSM-5 criteria. This study was approved by the GHU Paris Research Ethics Committee on February / 06 / 2024 (agreement number 2024-CER-A-010 – TEMPO project).

EEG data were recorded using 21 scalp electrodes following the international 10–20 system, on a Micromed® set-up during 20 minutes. 

Preprocessing was done using the MNE-Python software   according to the OHBM COBIDAS MEEG good practice recommendations The EEG preprocessing utilized a multi-step automated algorithm employed in recent studies. EEG signals underwent bandpass filtering (1-100 Hz). Bad EEG channels were identified using the pyprep algorithm, which employs a RANSAC-based method, and then interpolated using spherical spline interpolation from neighboring good channels. Electrodes were then re-referenced to the common average. Artefacts were detected using independent component analysis (ICA) combined with the IClabel algorithm. ICA decomposition was performed with the number of components set to the number of EEG channels (i.e., 19). To account for the high level of noise associated with this clinical data, we set stringent thresholds to keep components labeled as “brain-related” (> 70%) and reject those labeled as artefacts (> 80%). A second bandpass filter (1-45 Hz) was then applied. Only initial sequences at rest with eyes closed and before stimulation procedures were used in the main analysis (~7 min). Next, EEG signals were segmented into 2-second epochs. The Autoreject toolbox was used to interpolate or reject bad epochs. If there were three or more interpolated channels or two interpolated channels that encompassed the entire occipital region, the participant was excluded from the analysis (N=78). A total of 621 individuals passed these quality checks including 102 with and 519 without catatonia. Their demographic and clinical characteristics are reported. For each EEG recording, the following preprocessing criteria were extracted: channel interpolation number (number of interpolated channels out of the total number of channels), ICA rejection rate (number of ICA components rejected out of the 19 generated) and epoch rejection rate (number of epochs rejected out of the total number of epochs). 

Overall PSD was extracted using Welch’s method on 2-second epochs. Microstate analysis was conducted using the Pycrostates library in the alpha band (8-12Hz) given the resting-state eyes-closed design of our study. 

For each feature extracted from the resting-state EEG, we performed a linear regression analysis on catatonia status with the following covariables: age, sex, treatment (total equivalent doses of diazepam, olanzapine, and fluoxetine, which were not correlated to each other, and associated diagnoses (i.e., neurodevelopmental and neurological disorders). A subgroup analysis was conducted in patients stratified based on their specific psychiatric diagnoses: psychotic disorders (N=417; 59 patients with catatonia versus 358 without catatonia) and mood disorders (N=184; 33 patients with catatonia versus 151 without catatonia). To further control for the effects of benzodiazepines on EEG, we repeated the analysis in a subgroup of patients without benzodiazepines (N = 349; 51 patients with catatonia versus 298 without catatonia). 

Results

A total of 621 individuals with EEG recordings were included and analyzed. Of these, 102 had catatonia during the EEG recording, while 519 had never been diagnosed with catatonia. Patients with catatonia were older than those without catatonia (47.5 ± 19 vs. 41.7 ± 18.2 years; Z-score = 2.83, p = 0.005), more likely to be women (57% vs. 44%; X2= -2.35, p = 0.01), had a lower prevalence of psychotic disorders (58% vs. 69%; X2 = 2.19, p = 0.02), and a higher prevalence of neurodevelopmental disorders (16% vs. 7%; X2 = -2.95, p = 0.003) and neurological disorders (15% vs. 6%; X2 = -2.89, p = 0.003). No significant differences were found for mood disorders. Regarding treatment at the time of EEG, patients with catatonia received higher doses of diazepam equivalents (10.8 vs. 5.9 Z-score=1.419, p=0.05). No significant differences were observed in olanzapine or fluoxetine equivalent dosages. Among patients with diagnosed catatonia, a Bush Francis Catatonia Rating Scale score was available for 33 of them at the time of the EEG, showing a mean of 21±6.

The overall distribution of topomaps (Figure 2) were aligned with physiological expectations, showing predominant occipital activity in the alpha band, and midline-centered theta activity. 

Patients with catatonia exhibited increased power in the delta band (T = 2.37, pFDR = 0.03), decreased power in the alpha band (T = -3.55, pFDR = 0.002), and increased power in the gamma band (T = 3.14, pFDR = 0.008) compared to non-catatonia patients (Figure 2), after correction for age, sex, medication (total equivalent doses of diazepam, olanzapine, and fluoxetine), and associated neurological and neurodevelopmental diagnoses. No significant differences were observed in other frequency bands. 

Using the same linear regression model, catatonia patients exhibited a reduced peak alpha frequency (T=-2.60, p= 0.009) compared to non-catatonia patients. 

The global explained variance (GEV) of the microstates maps extracted from the 50 randomly selected patients was 60%. Topographies were consistent with the literature. Catatonia patients exhibited a higher mean duration of microstate C than non-catatonia patients (T=2.17, p =0.03), after correction for age, sex, medication (total equivalent doses of diazepam, olanzapine, and fluoxetine), and associated neurological and neurodevelopmental diagnoses. 

To ensure that the results were not driven by the effect of benzodiazepines which are known to affect EEG, analysis were replicated in a subgroup not receiving any benzodiazepines (N= 349 including 51 patients with catatonia and 298 without catatonia). 

Concerning the PSD distribution, in the subgroup of patients without benzodiazepines (N=349), the increase in gamma power remained significant (T = 3.08, pFDR = 0.011). The relative distribution of other frequencies was also preserved despite a lack of significance after correction, with a decrease in alpha power (T = -2.11, pFDR = 0.088) and an increase in delta power (T = 1.09, pFDR = 0.458). Catatonia patients also exhibited a reduced peak alpha frequency in the subgroup of patients without benzodiazepines (T = -2.46, p = 0.014). Concerning microstates analysis, in the subgroup of patients without benzodiazepines, this increase in class C mean duration remained significant (T = 2.99, p = 0.003), and we also observed a significant increase in class C time coverage in catatonia (T = 1.97, p = 0.049). 

Conclusion

In this study, quantitative measures of resting-state EEG were compared for the first time between patients with and without catatonia from a large transdiagnostic hospital-based population with schizophrenia and mood disorders. Using routine clinical EEG, we identified significant differences in power spectral densities, peak alpha frequency, and microstate class C between the groups.

The relative distribution of PSD across frequency bands at rest evolves with age, shifting from dominant low-frequency bands in childhood to alpha dominance in adulthood, reflecting an increased inhibitory tone in the excitation/inhibition balance. The observed reduction in alpha power in individuals with catatonia could therefore reflect a disruption in the development of this inhibitory tone. Moreover, the reduction in peak alpha frequency (PAF) observed in this cohort reinforces this hypothesis of a disrupted neurodevelopmental trajectory: higher PAF is associated with better cognitive abilities, it physiologically increases during development, and lower PAF values have previously been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders. This link between atypical neurodevelopment and the onset of catatonia is further supported by its high prevalence in individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders, and by brain imaging studies indicating atypical brain development—deviations in sulcation and gyrification indices in patients with catatonia5. In this cohort, also, neurodevelopmental disorders were more represented in the catatonia group. 

We observed an increase in the lower bands of resting-state gamma power in patients with catatonia. The elevation of resting-state gamma activity is consistent with the hypothesis of an imbalance between cortical excitatory and inhibitory activity in catatonia. Finally, we observed an increase in the average duration of microstate C. fMRI studies suggested that microstate C may be related to the default mode network, associated with negative thoughts, mind wandering, self-related thoughts, as well as emotional and interoceptive processing. Notably, the default mode network has been shown to be disrupted in catatonia. Several authors consider catatonia to be a primitive fear response, an increase in microstate C may thus reflect excessive baseline activation of self-referential and emotional processing networks at the neurophysiological level—processes that are notably disrupted in catatonia.

Taken together, altered low- and mid-frequency power likely reflects dysfunction within long-range cingulo-thalamo-cortical circuits, whereas changes in fast-frequency power may reflect more local disruptions in prefrontal interneuron function. 

Several methodological considerations need to be pointed out. First, the study population was retrospectively selected from a hospital-based real-world health data warehouse, selection bias may have reduced the differences between the two groups. The waxing and waning course of catatonia also makes the timing of EEG critical, we manually verified each file to ensure that EEGs were performed during a documented catatonic episode with catatonia symptoms. Regarding benzodiazepines, in addition to add the treatment as covariable, a subgroup analysis was conducted on patients who had not received benzodiazepines, in which most of the results were replicated. From a technical perspective, EEG recordings were acquired during routine clinical practice, we established strict thresholds to remove non-brain signals. Caution is warranted when interpreting gamma-band results, as gamma activity is typically minimal during eyes-closed rest and is more reliably associated with higher-order cognitive processes. 

These results shed light on pathophysiology with a probable neurodevelopmentally-related excitation/inhibition dysregulation. Importantly, our study shows that routine clinical EEG can be leveraged for diagnostic biomarker development, potentially enhancing early detection and treatment.
