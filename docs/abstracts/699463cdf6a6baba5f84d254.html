Introduction

Whole-brain functional connectivity (FC) supports fundamental cognitive processes and has been reported to be broadly atypical in autism. However, the specific nature of these differences in FC between autistic and non-autistic individuals remain inconsistent across literature1, which might be related to limitations of resting-state and fixed task paradigms. Movie-watching paradigms allow for the temporal anchoring of neural responses compared to resting state while imposing fewer constraints and offering greater ecological validity than traditional task-based paradigms by providing more naturalistic stimuli2. Emerging evidence suggests that FC during movie watching can better predict behavioural phenotypes compared to resting-state and task-based approaches3. Furthermore, using movie stimuli which vary in social and cognitive demands could reveal condition-specific FC responses which may be different in autistic individuals. 

In the current study, we clustered whole-brain FC into 7 sub-networks (as defined by the Yeo-7 atlas4) and characterised within-network connectivity of FC in these sub-networks during 6 movie clips spanning different levels of cognitive load and social content. We tested the hypothesis that within-network connectivity in autistic and non-autistic adults would respond differently to different levels of cognitive and social demands.

Methods

Ten autistic (5 female) and fifteen non-autistic adults (10 female) (mean age ± standard deviation = 31.32 ± 8.01, median (range) = 29 (20 - 48)) underwent high-resolution fMRI scanning. fMRI data were collected on a 7 T MRI system (Siemens MAGNETOM Terra.X) with 8Tx/32Rx head coil (Nova medical)(resolution = 1.793 × 1.793 × 1.800 mm3 voxel size, repetition time (TR) = 1000 ms, flip angle= 45°, Echo time (TE) = 25 ms, multiband factor = 5) during 6 movie clips. Three of the movies including human faces and social content were categorised as social conditions whilst the other three including only abstract shapes and objects as non-social conditions (Fig 1A). Two of the three movie clips of each condition contained a plot or story that was cognitively demanding (i.e. was cognitively high demanding and defined as “high cognitive load”) whilst the other clip did not contain a complex storyline/plot (“low cognitive load”).  After their scan, participants completed the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ)5, which we used to characterise their individual autistic profiles on a continuous scale as opposed to a clinical diagnosis only.

We first denoised fMRI data with NORDIC and used FSL TOPUP to correct for susceptibility distortion. The fMRI data were then preprocessed with the standard fMRIPrep pipeline, registered to the standard MNI152NLin2009cAsym space. Next, we high-pass filtered the registered data at 0.008 hz, denoised the data with FSL ICA-FIX, spatially smoothed the signal with 3.6 mm Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel using fslmaths, and regressed out the mean timeseries of the white matter and cerebrospinal fluid.

For each movie clip, we extracted the averaged timeseries in 400 ROIs as defined by Schaefer’s atlas and we computed whole-brain FC matrices with Pearson’s correlation between each pair of ROIs (Fig 1B). Non-negative FC matrices were thresholded successively over a range of network costs: 5% to 50%, at 1% steps. Each of the 400 ROIs was assigned to one of the 7 Yeo sub-networks and within-network connectivity of each sub-network was calculated as the mean correlation coefficient of within-network ROI pairs over the available cost range. Linear mixed effects (LME) models were used to examine effects of group (autistic/non-autistic), social/non-social conditions, high/low cognitive loads, and their interactions on within-network connectivity. Interaction effects between AQ scores and social conditions or cognitive loads were further tested when the corresponding group × cognitive-load or group × social-condition interactions were significant. For visualisation purposes only, all the participants were divided into groups of higher (> median) and lower (<= median) AQ scores in post-hoc stratification. p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons across the 7 Yeo sub-networks.

Results

We observed a significant group × cognitive-load interaction (pFDR = 0.033) on within-network connectivity of the frontoparietal sub-network (Fig 2A) which was increased during higher cognitive load video clips in autistic but not non-autistic individuals. There was also a significant AQ × cognitive-load interaction with the frontoparietal network (pFDR = 0.011)(Fig 2B), whereby its within-network connectivity was increased by higher cognitive load in individuals with higher but not lower AQ scores. In addition, we observed a significant group × cognitive-load interaction effect on within-network connectivity of the salience network (pFDR = 0.002) (Fig 2C) which was decreased during viewing of higher cognitive load videos in non-autistic but not in autistic individuals. The effect of AQ × cognitive-load interaction on the salience network was also significant (pFDR = 0.034) (Fig 2D): within-network connectivity was reduced in individuals with lower AQ scores more than those with higher scores. No significant group × social-condition interaction effects were found.

Conclusion

In conclusion, when the cognitive demanding of stimuli increased, autistic individuals presented changes (increased within-network connectivity) mostly in their frontoparietal sub-network, which supports higher order executive functions; whilst non-autistic individuals showed changes (decreased within-network connectivity) mostly within the salience sub-network, associated with processing of salient and sensory information. Our findings suggest different brain functional reconfiguration strategies in autism when watching content with higher cognitive demands under naturalistic stimuli, potentially related to atypical salience and sensory processing.
