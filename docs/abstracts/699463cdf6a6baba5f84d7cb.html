Introduction

Music and speech are often treated as categorically distinct, yet several theoretical frameworks argue for shared cognitive and affective mechanisms. The musical protolanguage hypothesis1 and the musi-linguistic continuum2 propose that speech and music overlap in their use of pitch, rhythm, and timbre to convey aesthetic and emotional meaning. A particularly compelling phenomenon in this context is the experience of chills - fleeting, pleasurable bodily sensations often accompanied by piloerection (i.e., goosebumps) - which are regarded as a neurophysiological marker of aesthetic processing. While music-elicited chills have been extensively investigated3 using psychophysiological and neuroimaging methods, comparable responses to spoken language remain largely unexplored.

Recent work in the field of phonaesthetics has sparked the interest in investigating language through an empirical aesthetics viewpoint. Specific phonetic-prosodic configurations in speech may elicit chills (phonetic chills), potentially reflecting the propositional-aesthetic dimension4 in speech perception. In which language typically occupies the propositional end of the propositional-aesthetic dimension, yet even ordinary speech can convey aesthetic value, revealing beauty as an intrinsic potential of linguistic expression. Empirical evidence based on objective physiological measurements is scarce. The present study investigates whether non-melodic speech can elicit chills comparable to those observed during music listening, using skin conductance responses (SCRs) as an index of arousal and emotional processing. By focusing on speech perception rather than music per se, this work aims to position language within broader models of aesthetic and emotional processing that are central to the perception of aesthetics and creativity. This project extends earlier discussions of the phonetic chill phenomenon and its potential role in language attitudes.5

Methods

Seventy-eight participants (N=78) took part in a laboratory experiment combining self-report and physiological measurements. Sixteen auditory stimuli were presented: eight music stimuli and eight speech stimuli in different languages. Each stimulus lasted 40 seconds. Participants provided post-hoc chill ratings (via continuous sliders) as well as continuous self-reports (by pressing the spacebar) during listening. The electrodes were positioned on the distal phalanges of the index and middle finger of the left hand of the participants. The lighting and the temperature in the room were kept constant and participants had a breathing exercise as their first task in order to acclimate them to the lab setting. The experiment was followed by an in-depth questionnaire.

Electrodermal activity (EDA) was recorded using a g.HIamp biosignal amplifier (g.tec Medical Engineering) at 256 Hz, alongside pulse, plethysmogram, and SpO₂ recordings. Raw data were stored in the device’s native HDF5 format, containing continuous physiological samples and asynchronous hardware triggers. The EDA channel (µS) was extracted and converted into a continuous time series with timestamps in seconds. Trigger ticks were converted to seconds using the device sampling rate.

All preprocessing and analysis were conducted in Python. To harmonize artifact detection and phasic decomposition, the EDA signal was downsampled to 8 Hz using linear interpolation, consistent with established EDA processing practices. Motion artifacts were identified using the MIT Media Lab’s EDA-Explorer pipeline, a machine-learning-based classifier that labels artifact-contaminated 5 second windows. Artifact segments were linearly interpolated; if the classifier failed to return valid labels, the signal was conservatively retained without artifact rejection.

The cleaned 8 Hz signal was low-pass filtered at 1.0 Hz (4th-order Butterworth) and decomposed into tonic and phasic components using the cvxEDA algorithm as implemented in NeuroKit2. Phasic activity reflects rapid sympathetic responses, while the tonic component captures slower baseline changes. Discrete skin conductance responses (SCRs) were additionally identified using NeuroKit2’s peak detection algorithm.

Event-related analyses were aligned to stimulus onset triggers. For each trial, the EDA analysis window spanned 0.25–40.25 seconds post-trigger, capturing the full stimulus-evoked response while excluding pre-roll activity. Within this window, three complementary SCR features were extracted from the phasic signal: (i) SCR count (number of detected peaks), (ii) summed phasic amplitude at peak samples (proxy for response magnitude), and (iii) integrated SCR (area under the phasic curve), indexing overall sympathetic drive. Features were exported for statistical analysis and within-participant comparisons across music and speech stimuli.

In order to distinguish between SCRs that were elicited by the stimulus and purely orientation responses a cut-off of 4 seconds was chosen to label the first responses as ‘orientation’ towards a new stimulus – which was not elicited by the sound unfolding. This is due to the slow latency of SCRs – estimated around 1-4 seconds. The longest suggested latency was applied in this case since acoustic stimuli – as opposed to visual stimuli simply need time to unfold. (Figure 1 illustrates the difference in an orientation response and a ‘true’ Chill SPR). 

All analyses relied on open-source software (NeuroKit2, EDA-Explorer, NumPy, SciPy, pandas). Scripts were executed in standardized cloud environments to ensure reproducibility.

Results

Both music and speech stimuli elicited measurable SCRs consistent with chill-related autonomic responses. Music stimuli produced a significantly higher mean number of chills across participants (M = 1.21; SD = 1.38) compared to speech (M = 0.89; SD = 1.03), as well as larger summed SCR amplitudes. This difference was most pronounced when early orienting responses were treated separately from later chill-related SCRs. Considering the magnitude of the response, the summed phasic amplitude at peak samples was analysed. Similar to the occurrences, music (M = 2.06; SD = 3.20) showed a significantly greater amplitude than speech (M = 1.57; SD = 2.38). The results are summarized in figure 2. 

Critically, speech stimuli reliably elicited delayed SCRs characteristic of chills rather than simple orienting responses. Individual case analyses showed highly similar SCR profiles across music and speech stimuli within the same participant, including comparable latency and waveform morphology. Across participants, substantial interindividual variability was observed, consistent with previous estimates that only a subset of the population reliably experiences chills.

Conclusion

The present findings provide physiological evidence that non-melodic speech can elicit chill-like autonomic responses, supporting the existence of phonetic chills as a genuine psychophysiological phenomenon. While music remains more potent in both frequency and intensity of chills, speech perception engages overlapping autonomic mechanisms associated with aesthetic and emotional processing. These results align with models proposing a musi-linguistic continuum and a propositional–aesthetic dimension in language processing.

By demonstrating measurable SCR correlates of chills during speech perception, this study contributes to ongoing efforts in human brain mapping to integrate language into broader neuroaesthetic frameworks. The findings highlight speech as a biologically grounded aesthetic stimulus and underscore the value of psychophysiological measures in bridging music and language research.
