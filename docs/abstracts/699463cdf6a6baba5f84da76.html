Introduction

Cardiac pulsatility is a ubiquitous physiological process that interacts with the cerebral vasculature and contributes structured variability to functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) signals. In brain imaging, cardiac effects are modeled as global physiological noise or removed using peripheral regressors, under the assumption that they contain no spatially or temporally meaningful information. This assumption may be an oversimplification of cardiac–cerebral interactions, particularly in association cortex, where vascular architecture and compliance are heterogeneous1.

The cortex is organized into large-scale networks that support distinct cognitive domains and serve as canonical research frameworks across aging and neurological and psychiatric disorders. Less appreciated is that these same cortical territories also differ in vascular properties. Recognizing this dual organization highlights the need for approaches that quantify cardiac-driven pulsatile activity within the established cortical networks.

A key challenge in studying heartbeat-related blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI effects is temporal undersampling. Cardiac pulsatility occurs at frequencies that approach or exceed the temporal resolution of conventional fMRI acquisitions, leading to aliasing of fast vascular fluctuations into slower frequency bands2. Such aliased signals overlap with timescales commonly examined by resting-state and task-based fMRI, raising the possibility that unmodeled cardiac dynamics contribute to apparent low-frequency BOLD variability.

We characterize cortical pulsatile activity (CPA) by time-locking BOLD signals to cerebral ballistocardiographic (BCG) events identified from simultaneously acquired electroencephalogram (EEG). By anchoring analyses to cardiac mechanics measured at the head and leveraging the spatiotemporal structure of multi-slice fMRI data within network-defined regions of interest, we measure CPA-related BOLD dynamics with improved resolution.

Methods

Simultaneous EEG–fMRI data were acquired during task-free, resting-state scans in older adult participants without dementia (n = 70; aged 55–80; 50% female). Exclusion criteria included major systemic illnesses and psychiatric disorders. Each participant completed three resting-state scans of 10 minutes each.

EEG data were recorded using a high-density 128-channel system (Brain Products GmbH) and preprocessed to remove gradient artifacts using a 31-sample moving-average subtraction method. The EEG data were then analyzed to identify cerebral BCG activity, which reflects cardiac pulsation–related motion of the head and electrodes within the static magnetic field during simultaneous EEG–fMRI3. This motion induces voltage changes in EEG electrodes via electromagnetic coupling, yielding signals that are time-locked to pulse arrival at the head with millisecond-scale precision. Because the travel time of the cardiac pulse from the heart to the head varies across individuals and physiological states, reliance on peripheral cardiac recordings can introduce temporal uncertainty. By contrast, BCG signals provide pulse timing anchored to cardiac mechanics as experienced at the level of cerebral vasculature, serving as electrophysiological markers of cerebral pulse arrival that can be used in event-related fMRI analyses.

Independent component analysis was applied to the EEG data to isolate cardiac-related components, yielding multiple candidate BCG components within each session. A canonical BCG component was selected based on the temporal regularity and stability of detected pulse events, quantified using variability metrics of inter-peak intervals. This procedure enabled robust identification of cerebral pulse timing without assuming correspondence to a specific cardiac phase at the heart. Pulse events were defined via peak detection on the canonical component, producing a session-specific event series for subsequent fMRI analyses.

fMRI data were acquired on a Siemens Prisma 3 Tesla MRI scanner using a 64-channel head coil, with a multiband EPI sequence (TR = 800 ms) and temporally staggered slice acquisition, in which eight slices were acquired approximately every 89 ms. fMRI data were coregistered to individual T1-weighted anatomical images and resampled into subject-specific anatomical volume space. Regions of interest (ROIs) derived from a cortical network atlas were mapped into volumetric space and used to extract ROI-specific BOLD signals.

BOLD signals were time-locked to BCG events and analyzed using selective averaging, whereby event-centered BOLD time courses were extracted and averaged across repeated cardiac events to estimate pulse-locked CPA responses. Event-related analyses leveraged the intrinsic temporal structure of the multi-slice acquisition, together with aggregation across repeated BCG events at the level of each ROI, to recover pulse-locked BOLD dynamics at effective sub-TR temporal resolution without modifying the acquisition sequence.

Analyses were conducted within ROIs defined by the Yeo resting-state network atlas and used for quantifying CPA. Within each participant, the pulse-locked BOLD response exhibited a clear negative peak, which served as a subject-specific temporal landmark for data-driven alignment across sessions and individuals. To enable group-level analyses across participants with differing heart rates, CPA responses were normalized with respect to individual average cardiac period prior to aggregation.

Statistical analyses employed repeated-measures analysis of variance with CPA-evoked BOLD amplitude as the dependent measure. Peak amplitudes were used to compare the large-scale networks (network and ROI as factors). For each network, time, position along anterior/posterior brain axis, and hemisphere were used as factors to assess the spatiotemporal organization and propagation of CPA across cortical networks.

Results

Average BOLD signals time-locked to EEG-derived BCG events revealed robust CPA-related BOLD dynamics that were highly consistent across the cohort. In all participants, the CPA-related response was characterized by a transient negative deflection followed by a more gradual recovery toward baseline. The CPA-related waveform unfolded on a sub-second timescale, with a rapid negative deflection occurring within a few hundred milliseconds of the pulse event and a slower recovery toward baseline completed within the same cardiac cycle. This waveform was clearly identifiable at the individual-subject level and enabled reliable peak-based alignment across sessions and individuals without reliance on external timing assumptions. This sub-second temporal profile contrasted sharply with the multi-second dynamics of the typical BOLD response evoked by neural activity.

The temporal width of BOLD deflections scaled with participant-specific inter-beat interval (p < .05), consistent with direct coupling to cardiac pulsatility. Importantly, these CPA-related dynamics were not spatially uniform. The largest BOLD deflections were consistently observed in frontal and parietal regions constituting the default mode and executive control networks, whereas lower-level cognitive regions, such as those comprising the dorsal attention network, exhibited smaller amplitudes. There was a significant main effect of network (p < .05), and the follow-up analyses showed significant contrasts for these two networks vs. other networks (p < .05).

Spatiotemporal mapping revealed structured organization of CPA-related BOLD changes across the default and executive networks. Following peak-based alignment, CPA-related BOLD deflections showed a systematic anterior-to-posterior temporal progression across the frontal and parietal regions. Repeated-measures analyses of variance demonstrated significant interactions between time and anterior/posterior position (p < .05), confirming organized propagation of CPA-related BOLD dynamics rather than global or random fluctuations.

These effects were robust at the individual-subject level and reproducible across sessions, indicating that CPA-related BOLD dynamics represent a stable physiological feature rather than transient noise. The observed spatial pattern aligned with known heterogeneity in vascular architecture and compliance across cortical regions1.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates that cardiac mechanics give rise to structured, network-specific BOLD cortical pulsatile activity (BOLD CPA) that is robust, reproducible, and spatiotemporally organized across cortex. By anchoring fMRI analyses to cerebral pulse timing derived from high-density EEG, we show that heartbeats evoke a stereotyped, pulse-locked negative BOLD deflection with a consistent temporal profile across individuals, establishing BOLD CPA as a stable physiological feature.

The observed BOLD CPA dynamics are most consistent with pulse-driven vascular mechanisms. Cardiac pulsatility induces rapid, impulse-like perturbations to cerebral blood volume and intravascular signal fraction4, leading to transient susceptibility changes in the BOLD signal. Because these effects are mechanically driven rather than metabolically mediated, they can occur on sub-second timescales. The resulting CPA-related waveform exhibits an asymmetric temporal profile, with a rapid signal decrease followed by slower recovery, consistent with impulse–relaxation behavior in compliant vascular systems. The preferential expression of BOLD CPA in frontoparietal association networks is consistent with regional differences in vascular organization and baseline hemodynamic properties across cortex1, suggesting that local vascular characteristics may shape how cardiac pulsatility manifests in the BOLD signal.

These findings refine how cardiac-related effects should be treated in fMRI analysis. BOLD CPA exhibited a non-sinusoidal temporal profile and systematic regional timing differences that are not well captured by standard physiological correction approaches based on global sinusoidal regressors, even when augmented with temporal derivatives. As a result, CPA-related dynamics would be only partially modeled, leaving structured residuals that vary across cortical regions and would be largest in association cortex, where BOLD CPA deflections are strongest.

This has direct implications for interpreting regionally selective fMRI findings. Apparent region-specific alterations in BOLD signal properties or functional connectivity—particularly within parietal and other association cortices—could, in some cases, reflect differences in BOLD CPA expression or differential effectiveness of physiological modeling rather than localized changes in neural activity. Explicit consideration of regional CPA parameters may therefore be critical for accurate interpretation of spatially selective fMRI effects in both healthy and clinical populations.

The present findings extend prior work using ultra-fast but low-spatial-resolution imaging approaches, such as magnetic resonance encephalography (MREG5), by enabling network-level mapping of CPA with whole-brain coverage using standard fMRI acquisitions. We achieve this through EEG-derived cerebral pulse timing and event-related aggregation, bridging vascular physiology with the spatial frameworks commonly used in cognitive neuroimaging. Although simultaneous EEG–fMRI is technically demanding, cardiac pulsatility-related signals are an intrinsic feature of such datasets, and cerebral pulse timing can be recovered with high temporal precision as a natural byproduct of the acquisition. In this context, BOLD CPA represents an additional physiological dimension that can be quantified at minimal additional cost when explicitly modeled.

By establishing the basic spatiotemporal properties of BOLD CPA in healthy older adults, this work provides a foundation for future studies of cardiac–vascular dynamics in aging and neurodegenerative disease. Incorporating BOLD CPA into analysis pipelines has the potential to improve the interpretability of resting-state and task-based fMRI measures by more effectively disentangling vascular and neural contributions to BOLD signal variability.
