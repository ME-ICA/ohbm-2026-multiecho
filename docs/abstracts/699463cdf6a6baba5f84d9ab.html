Introduction

Visuospatial dysfunction in Parkinson’s disease (PD) represents a largely unmet therapeutic need, impacting daily activities, mood, and fall risk.1 While cortical changes have been extensively studied, thalamic involvement remains unexplored. Recent evidence highlights selective vulnerability of the mediodorsal (MD), anteroventral (AV), and pulvinar (Pul) thalamic nuclei across neurodegenerative disorders.2 Art Therapy (AT) may address visuospatial deficits by engaging sophisticated neurologic mechanisms including shape recognition, sensory-motor integration, and eye-hand coordination.3 However, the neural substrates underlying individual variability in treatment response remain unknown.

Methods

We applied advanced subcortical segmentation (sTHOMAS)4 to baseline (sess-1) and post-intervention (sess-2) T1-weighted MRI from 24 PD patients (H&Y 2-3) participating in a longitudinal AT intervention study. AT consisted of 20 weekly sessions (90 min each) including progressive visuospatial tasks. Bilateral volumes were extracted for whole thalamus,10 thalamic nuclei, the habenula and mammillothalamic tract (MTT). ROI volumes were residualized for ICV, age, and sex via linear regression. Residual features underwent Principal Component Analysis (PCA) (retaining ≥90% cumulative variance). Silhouette analysis across k=2-6. Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) clustering was applied to PCA scores to identify possible morphometrical subtypes. Cluster stability was validated via: (1) bootstrap consensus clustering (N=1000 iterations, 80% subsetting) (Fig.1), (2) Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), and (3) Random Forest LOOCV cross-validation (accuracy, kappa, feature importance). Clinical assessments at sess-1 and sess-2 included: UPDRS, H&Y, PDQ-39, MoCA, Benton Visual Retention Test, TUG, and SAPS-PD. Paired comparisons evaluated within-cluster longitudinal changes; Spearman correlations examined structure-function relationships between Δvolumes and Δclinical scores. Linear mixed models tested cluster×session interactions adjusting for total intracranial volume, age, and sex. All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4). Statistical significance was set at α=0.05 (95% confidence interval).

Results

Silhouette analysis identified k=2 as the optimal cluster solution (average silhouette=0.245), satisfying minimum cluster size requirements (≥6 subjects per cluster). PAM clustering applied to PCA scores identified two neuroanatomical subtypes (Fig. 2): Cluster 1 (n=11) and Cluster 2 (n=13). Despite moderate silhouette values (0.245), comprehensive validation confirmed exceptional cluster stability and biological validity: high consensus scores (0.84 and 0.87), perfect PAM-consensus agreement (ARI=1.0), and Random Forest cross-validation reproducibility (accuracy=83.3%, κ=0.67), with top discriminative features: CM (importance=100), whole thalamus (92.6), MD-PF (62.0), VLp (44.8). Cluster 2 exhibited significantly larger baseline volumes across multiple thalamic structures: whole thalamus (+1437 mm³, d=-1.94), CM (d=-1.77), MD-PF (d=-1.60), and other nuclei (AV, VLp, Pul; all p<0.01, d>1.3). Critically, these substantial volumetric differences were not reflected in baseline motor severity: UPDRS-III (Cluster 1: 40.18 vs Cluster 2: 36.38, p>0.05) and H&Y staging were comparable between clusters, suggesting that thalamic architecture encodes clinically-latent information on disease heterogeneity. Modest baseline differences emerged only in non-motor domains, Cluster 2 showed worse PDQ-39 mood/stigma (p=0.03), while Cluster 1 demonstrated better visuospatial performance (MoCA-visual, Benton; p<0.05). The predictive value of baseline thalamic profiling became evident through longitudinal analysis, revealing divergent response patterns to AT. Cluster 1 (lower thalamic volumes) showed predominant cognitive and quality-of-life improvements after AT: PDQ-39 ADL (-2.00, p=0.001), total score (-5.45, p=0.02), emotions (-1.55, p=0.04); MoCA (+1.00, p=0.04); alongside motor gains: UPDRS-III (-5.09, p=0.03), total (-7.36, p=0.05). In contrast, Cluster 2 (higher thalamic volumes) exhibited primarily selective motor improvements: UPDRS-III (-5.31, p=0.03), total (-7.46, p=0.03), TUG cost (p=0.03), and reduced PDQ-39 stigma (-0.54, p=0.05), with minimal cognitive changes. The differential trajectories were confirmed by significant between-cluster differences in Δbenton (p=0.02), indicating divergent visuospatial plasticity. Within-cluster correlations revealed distinct mechanisms: Cluster 1 showed strongest ΔMD-PF/ΔVLp associations with cognitive-behavioral changes (|ρ|>0.77, all p<0.005); Cluster 2 exhibited ΔAV/ΔCM correlations with motor/perceptual symptoms (|ρ|>0.72, all p<0.01). Mixed models confirmed stable cluster differences across time (cluster×session interactions: p>0.05 for most variables), with Cluster 2 maintaining +1317 mm³ thalamic advantage (p=6.8×10⁻⁸), indicating that baseline volumetric profiles represent stable endophenotypes rather than transient states.

Conclusion

Advanced thalamic nuclei segmentation identified clinically latent PD subtypes undetectable by baseline motor assessments, yet strongly predictive of response to art therapy. Larger thalamic volumes were associated with predominantly motor benefits, while smaller volumes predicted broader cognitive-behavioral improvements, suggesting distinct pathophysiological and neuroplastic mechanisms. Thalamic nuclei profiling may therefore enable early prediction of complementary therapy outcomes and support precision medicine in PD.5
