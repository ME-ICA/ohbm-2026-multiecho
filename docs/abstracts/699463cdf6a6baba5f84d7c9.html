Introduction

This work aims to study the influence of input data, i.e. raw T1 MRI images and/or their derivatives, on the learning of a 3-dimensional convolutional neural network (3D CNN). To test the interaction between input data and cerebral atrophy patterns, we conducted binary classifications (patients vs healthy subjects) in two settings of pathologies presenting different atrophy patterns.

Methods

We used two MRI acquisition databases for our study:

– The Multiple System Atrophy (MSA) database: comprising 126 HC and 92 MSA patients from three MSA reference centres in France (1, 2, 3).

– The Alzheimer's disease (AD) database: comprising 714 HC and 254 AD patients, from the ADNI database (4).

We applied the following image analysis pipeline: [i] Normalisation of T1 volumes in MNI space; [ii] Extraction of normalised volumes (T1) from the brain; [iii] Segmentation of grey density (GD), white density (WD) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from normalised T1 images. The learning capabilities of the 3D CNN (5) were evaluated for the binary classification of MSA/HC patients and AD/HC patients, based on a single image type (monoparametric study) and several image types (multiparametric study).

Results

We evaluated the classification capability of the 3D CNN on a training set with balanced patient and SC groups. This set was divided into two distinct subsets: a training set and a test set, in order to evaluate the performance of the model. The single-parameter approach, shown in Figure 1, first allowed us to confirm the classification capabilities of the 3D CNN with an average accuracy >0.870 for the MSA/HC task and >0.730 for the AD/HC task. The best performance in terms of average accuracy was obtained for WD maps with 0.938 for MSA/HC classification and 0.823 for the AD/HC classification. We note, for the MSA/HC classification, a greater difference between sensitivity and specificity when using our parametric maps. For the AD/HC study, we observe a reversal in classification capability, with specificity yielding better results than sensitivity when using parametric maps. We will only discuss the biparametric study here and show the results of all multiparametric approaches in Table 1. The most notable results in terms of average accuracy are the T1-WD combinations for the MSA/HC classification with a value of 0.917 and T1-CSF for the AD/HC classification with a value of 0.817.

Conclusion

Thanks to these two classifications, we observed that the T1 image already allows for correct classification. We note that the influence of these parametric maps is slightly different depending on the atrophy pattern. In the context of AD/HC classification, it can be noted that CSF images seem to perform better. Whereas for MSA/HC classification, parametric maps show better specificity. This result could be related to the use of tissue probability maps based on healthy subjects to calculate the parametric maps for all participants. We observe a trend towards improvement in the capabilities of our model when using a T1-parametric map combination, even when the maps provide lower performance compared to T1 images. In conclusion, this work shows an improvement in classification capabilities when combining T1 images with parametric maps derived directly from them, even with pathologies showing different patterns of atrophy.
