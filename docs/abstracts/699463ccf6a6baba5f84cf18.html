Introduction

Physical function declines with age and is influenced by factors such as physical activity and diet. These factors contribute to aging pathology through cellular and molecular mechanisms that result in accumulating damage over time. Damage can be conceptualized as “entropy”, or disorder. The observation that biological systems tend to become more entropic with age has led several to draw parallels with the 2nd Law of Thermodynamics,1 which dictates that the entropy of a system must increase over time. Conceptualizing aging as a potentially ameliorable increase in entropy has gained attention in recent years, but primarily in the study of microscopic biological systems. To what extent the 2nd Law analogy extends to aging biology at larger scales is an open question.

The brain is one system to which the concept of increasing entropy with age may be particularly appropriate. Network neuroscience has led to identification of distinct communities within brain networks that associate with specific cognitive and physical functions.2 Physical function has been shown to be related to the spatial organization of the sensorimotor network in older adults.3 However, prior investigations used methodology that required a priori specification of expected, “ideal” community structure. This limits the scope of analyses to a few specific brain regions. 

Here, we apply a novel method 4 for mapping the spatial entropy of brain network communities to a cohort of older adults (n = 190) and younger adults (n = 30). We compared the entropy maps of older and younger adults, hypothesizing that older adults would have higher entropy than younger adults across the whole brain. We also examined the relationship between physical function and brain network entropy in older adults, hypothesizing that better physical function would be associated with lower entropy in the sensorimotor network. In exploratory analyses, we assessed the relationships between brain entropy and markers of general health.

Methods

Data was collected by the Brain Networks and Mobility (B-NET) study, an observational study of cognitively normal community-dwelling older adults (n=190) aged 70 and older. Participants provided extensive information about their health history and completed physical testing and brain MRI scanning. Physical function was quantified by the expanded short physical performance battery (eSPPB). In addition to the older adults, a cohort of younger adults (n=30) aged 22-36 years were enrolled.

Images were collected on a Siemens 3T Skyra MRI scanner with a 32-channel head coil. An anatomical image was collected using T1-weighted 3D volumetric MPRAGE sequence (TR = 2000ms, TE = 2.98 ms, number of slices = 192, 1.0mm isotropic voxels). Resting-state functional MRI data were collected using BOLD-weighted echo planar imaging sequence (TR = 2000ms, TE = 25ms, number of slices = 35, voxel dimension = 4.0x4.0x5.0mm). 

SPM12 was used for structural image segmentation. Gray and white matter segmented images were summed and any voxel with at least 0.5 probability was retained as brain parenchyma. Non-brain tissue were excluded from brain masks. Structural images were masked and spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template using ANTs. 

BOLD-weighted images underwent distortion correction using FMRIB’s “Topup” Software Library. The first ten volumes of the BOLD images were removed to allow for signal normalization. Slice time correction and realignment of the functional images was completed using SPM12. BOLD images were coregistered to native anatomical images, then warped to MNI space using the transformation derived from ANTs. Data were band-pass filtered (0.009 – 0.08 Hz). Global average signal from white matter, gray matter, CSF, and the 6 rigid-body motion parameters were regressed out from the filtered data. Finally, motion correction was applied using motion scrubbing.

Functional brain networks were generated by finding the Pearson correlation of the BOLD signal time series for every pair of voxels for each participant. The result was an NxN correlation matrix where N is the number of network nodes (N ≅ 20,000). This correlation matrix was converted to an adjacency matrix Aij by thresholding connections to retain only the strongest edges that satisfied the equation S = log(N)/log(k), where k is mean degree and S was set to 2.5. Retained connections were set equal to 1. 

Communities were identified in each participant’s network such that each voxel (node) was assigned to a single community. Community partitioning was optimized using the modularity metric Q. A dynamic Markov process was used to identify the network community partitioning that maximized Q. 

Spatial maps of voxel community assignments in brain space were used to quantify the spatial entropy of communities in a localized area (Figure 1). For each voxel i, a spatial “neighborhood” encompassing all voxels within three voxels from i was identified. The neighborhood spatial entropy was then quantified using O’Neill’s entropy (HO),5 a variation of Shannon’s entropy adapted for spatial analyses.

SPM12 was used for statistical analyses of entropy maps. Two-sample t-tests were used to compare the entropy maps of older adults with younger adults. Within the older cohort, we also assessed how various factors (eSPPB and BMI) were related to brain network entropy maps using regression. Maps were thresholded at the voxel level at p<0.005 and corrected for multiple comparisons using cluster correction at p<0.05. The smallest statistically significant cluster size was 7264 voxels for the age cohort t-test, 155 voxels for eSPPB, and 6285 for BMI.

Results

Spatial entropy maps from the cohort of older adults were compared to maps from the cohort of younger adults using two-sample t-tests. Figure 2a shows results from these tests with both thresholded and unthresholded images. Warm colors indicate regions with higher entropy in older adults relative to younger adults. In older adults, spatial entropy was significantly higher in many regions including the sensorimotor cortex, basal ganglia, hippocampus, thalamus, and occipital lobe. There were no regions with significantly higher spatial entropy in younger adults compared to older adults. 

Next, eSPPB scores from the older participants were regressed against entropy maps to determine whether physical function was associated with brain network spatial entropy. Figure 2b shows maps of T-values from the regression. Better eSPPB scores were associated with lower entropy in cortical and subcortical regions including the sensorimotor cortex, basal ganglia, and bilateral middle frontal gyri. Better eSPPB was not significantly associated with higher entropy in any brain regions. 

We used measures of general health from B-NET participants to determine whether general health markers were significantly related to brain entropy. Previous work from the B-NET study found that BMI was related to community structure consistency in the sensorimotor network.3 A regression model was fit with BMI as the independent variable of interest and entropy maps as the outcome. Figure 2c shows maps of T-values from the regression. Higher BMI was associated with higher entropy in portions of every cortical lobe, including the left sensorimotor cortex and the bilateral posterior insula. Higher BMI was not significantly associated with lower entropy in any brain regions.

Conclusion

We found that brain entropy was higher in older adults than in younger adults, particularly in the sensorimotor cortex, basal ganglia, hippocampus, thalamus, and occipital lobe. Higher entropy in regions including the sensorimotor cortex, basal ganglia, and middle frontal gyrus was associated with worse physical function in older adults. Finally, BMI was associated with greater entropy in regions including the sensorimotor cortex, occipital lobe, and posterior insula. We interpret findings as evidence that spatial entropy in resting-state brain network community landscapes may be a marker of declining physical function. Modifiable factors, such as losing excess weight, may help to reduce or prevent further increase in brain entropy, which may in turn improve aging outcomes. 

            The finding that entropy is higher in the brains of older adults compared to younger adults was in line with our hypothesized outcome. Regions that had the most pronounced difference in entropy included regions associated with motor control, including the motor cortex and basal ganglia, as well as the visual cortex. Based on prior work,4 we expect that elevated entropy in a region would be associated with deficits in the functions for which that region is most directly responsible. This view is consistent with the finding that older adults have higher entropy in regions associated with motor control, as the older adults also had significantly worse eSPPB scores than the younger adults. Additionally, it is consistent with the finding that older adults have higher entropy in the visual cortex, as older adults reported significantly worse visual function than younger adults. 

            As hypothesized, better physical function was associated with lower entropy in the sensorimotor network. Physical function was also associated with lower entropy in the basal ganglia, which was not surprising given that the basal ganglia are known to be crucial for modulatory motor control. Additionally, we observed that better physical function was associated with lower entropy in the bilateral middle frontal gyri. These regions are typically associated with various cognitive functions rather than motor function, so we did not expect this relationship. Nevertheless, this finding provides a basis for future exploration of this region’s role in physical function. The middle frontal gyrus is routinely targeted using techniques such as transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS). Future work may assess whether tDCS reduces entropy in older adults, and whether entropy reduction is accompanied by physical function improvements. 

            The finding that BMI is related to higher brain entropy - including in regions associated with physical function – suggests that it may be worthwhile to test whether behavioral and/or pharmacological interventions modify brain entropy and whether changes in brain entropy are related to favorable aging outcomes. For example, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogs are increasingly popular for weight loss, though there is still debate regarding the benefits and risks of prescribing these drugs in older adults. Future work may benefit from considering how these drugs impact the brain entropy of older adults.

            An important limitation to findings presented here is that data used in analyses were cross-sectional. Therefore, causality in relationships between variables of interest and brain entropy cannot be determined. Future work investigating targeted interventions will help establish directionality in these relationships.
