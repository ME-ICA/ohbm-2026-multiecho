Introduction

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that usually presents after midlife and is associated with a diffuse pattern of brain atrophy on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)1. Recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified numerous genetic risk variants with small cumulative effects that, when aggregated into a polygenic risk score (PRS), explain up to 36% of PD heritability2. However, the biological consequences of most of these genes remain poorly understood. In this project, we used data from the UK Biobank (UKB)3 to investigate how genetic risk for PD shapes brain anatomy and behavior. We combined genetic, neuroimaging, and behavioral data to uncover the neural and clinical correlates of the genetically determined high-risk state and differentiate between potential genetic factors that affect neurodevelopmental processes and those that contribute to later-life vulnerability toward PD.

Methods

To identify the effects of genetic risk of PD on the brain, we studied MRI, genetic, and behavioral data of 46,349 healthy participants aged 44 to 85 years old from the UKB. PD PRS was calculated using the PRS-CS method4 and the association between PD-PRS and cortical surface area, cortical thickness, subcortical volume, subcortical susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI), and white matter fractional anisotropy were examined using linear regression. All analyses were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) approach while accounting for the following confounds: age, age squared, sex, interaction between age confounds and sex, number of days since the start of image acquisition, number of days squared, average head motion during resting and task functional MRI, table position (z-axis) inside the scanner, scan site, genotyping batch, and the top 15 population genetic principal components. Similarly, we assessed the associations between PD-PRS and behavioral and demographic measures relevant to PD. Potentially causal relationships between brain structure and PD diagnosis were then investigated through Mendelian randomization. We further compared our maps of PD-PRS effect on cortical surface area and thickness to those of the ENIGMA PD consortium (3,096 PD patients, 1,262 controls)5 using the “spin-tests” method to account for spatial autocorrelation.

PD risk genes were then stratified based on their functions into three distinct components of mitochondrial, autophagy, and lysosomal (Mito-ALP) genes, and their pathway-specific neuroanatomical associations were assessed using linear regression. To further characterize biological processes underlying these distinct neuroanatomical signatures of genetic risk, we performed gene-set enrichment analyses using multimarker analysis of genomic annotation (MAGMA) to identify overrepresented biological pathways in each genetic variant subset. Finally, we investigated the developmental gene expression trajectories of Mito-ALP pathways using BrainSpan RNA-sequencing data spanning fetal stages through adulthood and compared them to the expression patterns of other PD risk genes.

Results

PD-PRS showed widespread positive associations with cortical surface area (SA), subcortical volumes, and white matter fractional anisotropy (FA). Conversely, PD-PRS showed more restricted negative associations with cortical thickness in prefrontal, temporal, and limbic areas (Figure 1a). Mendelian randomization revealed that increased cortical SA and larger subcortical volumes have a potentially causal effect on PD. The cortical regions showing the greatest surface area expansion due to PD genetic risk also show the greatest atrophy in PD (derived from ENIGMA PD maps). Higher PD-PRS was further associated with greater educational attainment, fluid intelligence, and household income, as well as reduced smoking and lower body mass index (Figure 1b).

Pathway-specific analyses revealed that non-Mito-ALP variants were responsible for most brain size associations, while mitochondrial, autophagy, and lysosomal (Mito-ALP) pathways showed limited neuroanatomical correlates (Figure 1c). Gene expression trajectory analysis further revealed distinct patterns of expression for Mito-ALP pathway genes, showing significantly lower expression during fetal stages compared to non-Mito-ALP PD risk genes with peak expression prenatally (Figure 1d). Gene-set enrichment analyses identified that non-Mito-ALP variants were enriched for neurodevelopmental processes including microtubule organization and cell division, while Mito-ALP variants were enriched for processes such as dopamine biosynthesis and oxidative stress. Genetic correlation analyses further confirmed that PD risk variants share genetic architecture with genes influencing brain size which are primarily driven by non-Mito-ALP variants.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal a link between PD genetic risk and brain size, as evidenced by greater cortical surface area  and white matter density. These structural differences are established during neurodevelopment and may create a vulnerability to later-life neurodegeneration as evidenced by the spatial overlap between regions showing genetic risk-related expansion and those most atrophied in PD. In addition, the association between higher PD genetic risk and favorable cognitive and socioeconomic markers further suggests that genetic variants promoting larger brains may confer early-life advantages while simultaneously increasing vulnerability to neurodegeneration in later life.

Pathway-specific analyses demonstrated that these neuroanatomical signatures are primarily driven by non-Mito-ALP genes. Moreover, non-Mito-ALP genes showed higher expression during fetal stages and were enriched for neurodevelopmental processes. These findings suggest that non-Mito-ALP and Mito-ALP genes contribute to PD development through distinct mechanisms: non-Mito-ALP genes may increase PD risk via brain growth during development, whereas Mito-ALP genes contribute to disease risk through other mechanisms, possibly related to late-life cellular homeostasis. These results provide new insights into how genetic risk factors might shape brain structure and contribute to PD susceptibility, highlighting the complex interplay between developmental and pathway-specific mechanisms in PD pathogenesis.
