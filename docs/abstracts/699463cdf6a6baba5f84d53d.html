Introduction

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a heterogeneous condition positioned between typical aging and dementia, marked by measurable cognitive decline with preserved everyday functioning 1. Individuals with MCI are at increased risk for dementia and often present additional disturbances in mood, postural balance, executive function, speech-in-noise perception, and social communication, accompanied by hippocampal atrophy and altered hippocampal-whole-brain functional connectivity 2,3. Despite these multidomain vulnerabilities, few interventions simultaneously target cognitive challenge, coordinated movement, and emotional engagement in early-stage MCI 4. Musical training and psychomotor training, delivered in stimulating group formats, offer a promising means to support cognition, sensorimotor, and social functioning while potentially engaging hippocampal circuitry. 

In this randomized controlled trial (COPE: Countervail cOgnitive imPairmEnt), we tested whether six months of intensive musical or psychomotor training in individuals with MCI, relative to passive healthy controls matched for age, sex, and education level, would improve cognitive and sensorimotor performance and preserve or modulate hippocampal volume and connectivity. Although only a marginal effect on global cognition emerged in the music group, the results indicate complementary domain-specific gains across cognitive and sensorimotor domains and suggest training-related support of hippocampal integrity across both groups. These findings indicate that engaging, low-risk group activities, such as musical and psychomotor training, are promising early-stage interventions for individuals at elevated dementia risk.

Methods

MCI patients were recruited and diagnosed at two university memory clinics. Inclusion criteria required an MMSE score ≥ 23 or an MoCA score ≥ 17 to exclude dementia, and no major neurological or psychiatric comorbidities. MCI patients were randomly assigned to music training (MUS) or psychomotor training (PSY), using stratified randomization to balance age, baseline global cognition, sex, and MCI subtype (amnestic vs. non-amnestic).

Both interventions took place in small groups twice weekly for 45 minutes over 6 months, with daily homework. MUS involved progressive training on a 13-note tongue drum, from bimanual rhythmic-melodic patterns to playing entire melodies, score reading, and ensemble playing. PSY focused on body awareness, postural control, and memorizing progressively complex, meaningful movement sequences. A passive healthy control group (HC), matched for age, sex, and education, served as a reference for learning effects and normal aging.

Baseline assessments included 40 participants (25 MCI, 15 HC), and 6-month behavioral analyses 33 participants (19 MCI: MUS = 11, PSY = 8; 14 HC). The behavioral battery comprised the COGTEL Weighted Score (WS, primary outcome) 5, speech-in-noise (SIN) perception, attention, manual dexterity, one-leg balance, motor inhibition, and hand/foot laterality recognition. The COGTEL is a validated global cognition measure, highly correlated with the MMSE (r = 0.93), but covering more domains and thus avoiding ceiling effects in healthy older adults.

MRI was acquired on 3T Siemens Prisma scanners with MP2RAGE T1-weighted imaging and resting-state (RS) fMRI. Hippocampal regions of interest were defined from an independent baseline contrast showing reduced gray matter in MCI versus HC. Longitudinal voxel-based morphometry extracted gray-matter volume in these ROIs, and seed-based RS fMRI assessed hippocampal connectivity. Longitudinal structural models included 27 participants (13 MCI, 14 HC), and connectivity models 26 participants (12 MCI, 14 HC). Statistical analyses comprised linear mixed-effects models and ANCOVAs, controlling for age, site, and intracranial volume, with subject as a random effect.

The primary outcome was improvement in COGTEL-WS in MCI. Secondary outcomes comprised improvement in other cognitive and sensorimotor tests, COGTEL subtests, and maintenance of structural and functional MRI of hippocampal networks.

 

Results

Over 6 months, MUS showed marginal COGTEL-WS gain (Δ = 4.2, p = .06), but no change in PSY or HC. In MUS, categorical fluency and verbal short-term memory improved, and memory gains scaled with homework duration. PSY enhanced manual dexterity, balance, and body-schema awareness. Across 10 cognitive and sensorimotor scores, MCI participants improved relative to HC (F(1, 28.4) = 4.2, p < .05, Hedges' g = −.35, computed as HC - MCI).

At baseline, MCI patients showed reduced bilateral hippocampal gray-matter volume relative to HC, but neither group exhibited longitudinal atrophy over six months. Left-hemispheric hippocampal functional connectivity remained stable across all groups. In contrast, right-hemispheric hippocampal network connectivity declined in HC but was preserved in both MUS and PSY. This pattern indicates a relative right hippocampal functional advantage in MCI patients who received 6-month multimodal interventions, suggesting an attenuation of age-related functional disconnection.

Conclusion

Six months of MUS training yielded a marginal advantage in global cognition. Both MUS and PSY produced domain-specific cognitive and sensorimotor benefits: MUS supported verbal and auditory-cognitive functions, whereas PSY enhanced proprioceptive and balance abilities. Across both groups, right hippocampal functional connectivity stabilized relative to natural age-related decline. These findings suggest that engaging, nonpharmacological multimodal activities may offer low-risk strategies to preserve function during prodromal MCI.
