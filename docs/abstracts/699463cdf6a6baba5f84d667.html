Introduction

The brain age gap (BAG), estimated from brain imaging data, is widely considered as a general indicator of brain health and is influenced by diverse genetic and phenotypic factors. Several studies have examined the genetic architecture of the BAG. However, trait-relevant cell types and the spatial distributions of these aging-accelerating cells remain elusive. To elucidate the composition and spatial organization of cells of brain aging acceleration, we generated a single-cell spatial transcriptomic atlas of the mouse brain spanning postnatal development to late adulthood, covering 12 distinct ages from postnatal day 7 (P7) to month 27. By integrating BAG-associated GWAS results with this single-cell spatial transcriptomic data, we performed genetically informed spatial mapping of cells for complex traits (gsMap) analysis, revealing a strong association between brain aging and specific cell types including oligodendrocytes in the corpus callosum and epithelial cells in the choroid plexus. These findings were further validated through MAGMA analysis of human single-cell data, which consistently implicated oligodendrocytes and committed oligodendrocyte precursor cells. Moreover, we constructed a single-cell spatial aging clock to decode the acceleration effect of expression of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) genes and other secretory protein genes in brain on neighboring cell. These results spatially resolved transcriptomics with genetic insights to delineate key cell types and regional mechanisms driving brain aging, offering a framework for understanding cell–cell interactions in aging and potential targets for intervention.

Methods

Mouse Brain Tissue Processing for Spatial Transcriptomics

Whole brains were collected from male C57BL/6J mice spanning 12 developmental and aging time points (postnatal day 7 to month 27). Following approved protocols, animals were perfused transcardially with phosphate-buffered saline, and brains were rapidly dissected. Each brain was embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound within a metal frame and snap-frozen on dry ice. Frozen blocks were stored at -80°C until sectioning. Coronal sections (10 µm thickness) were cut using a cryostat (Thermo Fisher) maintained at -20°C. Two consecutive sections per biological replicate were selected for Stereo-seq capture, targeting regions containing cortex, hippocampus, striatum, thalamus, and corpus callosum. Prior to mounting, sections were flattened and transferred onto pre-cooled Stereo-seq chips (1×1 cm or 1×2 cm arrays). For quality control, additional adjacent sections were collected for RNA extraction and integrity assessment (RIN ≥ 7 required).

Library Preparation and Sequencing

Chip-mounted sections underwent methanol fixation, fluorescent staining for registration, permeabilization (0.1% pepsin, 37°C, 15 min), on-chip reverse transcription, and PCR amplification (15 cycles). Libraries were prepared by Tn5 tagmentation and index PCR (13 cycles), then sequenced on an MGI DNBSEQ-TX platform (100 bp paired-end).

Data Processing and Segmentation

Raw data were processed using SAW v4.0: spatial barcodes were aligned, low-quality reads filtered, and reads aligned to mm10. UMI-count matrices were generated. For single-cell analysis, a spatial density image was aligned to the staining image. Cell segmentation used Cellpose 2.0 with a 6 µm cytoplasmic expansion. Cells with <150 genes were filtered out.

Cell Type Annotation and Clustering  

Cells expressing fewer than 150 genes were excluded. Initial cell‑type labels were assigned using a semi‑supervised deep‑learning framework trained on annotated reference datasets. Batch effects were corrected across all sections using scVI, followed by community‑based clustering with the Leiden algorithm. Clusters were annotated by integrating preliminary labels with established marker genes. To resolve finer subtypes, each major cell class was processed separately with scVI for batch integration and re‑clustered with Leiden. Subclass‑specific marker genes were identified through differential expression analysis against a sampled background of all cells.

Integrative Genetic Analysis with MAGMA and gsMap.

Brain age gap (BAG) genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics were obtained from published large-scale neuroimaging genetics studies. MAGMA (v1.08) was used for gene-level and gene-property analyses. SNPs were mapped to genes within a 35 kb upstream/10 kb downstream window. Gene-based associations were computed via the SNP-wise mean model, accounting for linkage disequilibrium using the 1000 Genomes European reference panel. For cell-type enrichment, gene-level z-scores were regressed against cell-type specificity metrics in a linear model adjusting for gene size, density, and other covariates. The genetically informed spatial mapping (gsMap) method was applied to integrate BAG GWAS signals with our spatial transcriptomic data, generating continuous spatial maps of trait relevance across brain regions and cell types. Type I error control was validated through permutation testing, confirming appropriate false positive rate calibration.

Results

1. Lifespan cell-type-specific transcriptomic signatures of healthy ageing in mice

To systematically delineate the cellular and molecular dynamics of brain ageing, we profiled the healthy mouse brain across lifespan using single-cell spatial transcriptomics. We constructed a high-resolution atlas by sampling male C57BL/6J mice at 12 critical developmental and ageing stages: postnatal day 7 (P7), P14, and months 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 17, 22, and 27. The Stereo-seq was employed to capture transcripts at the subcellular level in these sections, allowing us to map the spatial localization of RNA molecules across all profiled brain regions.

Following rigorous quality control, we obtained high-quality transcriptomic data for over 3 million individual cells across all time points. Cell types were comprehensively annotated using a tiered, semi-supervised strategy integrated with canonical marker genes. Our atlas identified and manually curated 21 major cell classes. The neuronal clusters included cholinergic and monoaminergic neurons (CHOs), dentate gyrus granule neurons (DGGRCs), diencephalon and mesencephalon excitatory neurons (MEGLUs), diencephalon and mesencephalon inhibitory neurons (DEINHs), glutamatergic neuroblasts (GNBLs), non-glutamatergic neuroblasts (NGNBLs), peptidergic neurons (PEPs), telencephalon inhibitory interneurons (TEINHs), telencephalon projecting excitatory neurons (TEGLUs), and telencephalon projecting inhibitory neurons (MSNs). The immune clusters included macrophages (MACs). The glial clusters included astrocytes (ACs), microglia (MGLs), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), oligodendrocytes (OLGs), and ependymal cells (EPENs). The vascular clusters included choroid plexus epithelial cells (CHORs), endothelial cells (ENs), pericytes (PERs), smooth muscle cells (SMCs), and vascular and leptomeningeal cells (VLMs). 

2. BAG-associated cell types revealed by integrative genomic-spatial mapping

To pinpoint the specific cell populations contributing to accelerated brain aging, we integrated the latest brain age gap (BAG) genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics with our mouse spatial transcriptomic atlas. The BAG GWAS data, derived from neuroimaging and genetic analysis of over 28,000 individuals, identified significant genetic loci associated with the discrepancy between chronological age and predicted brain age.

First, we employed the MAGMA framework on a comprehensive human single-cell RNA-seq reference of the adult brain to identify cell types that were significantly enriched for BAG-associated genetic SNPs. The results robustly implicated cells within the oligodendrocyte lineage. Notably, mature oligodendrocytes (OLs) and committed oligodendrocyte precursor cells (COPs) emerged as the most strongly associated cell populations (Top clusters: cluster 41, P =2.52 × 10-7, cluster 47, P =8.41 × 10-7, cluster 46, P =9.79 × 10-7).

To spatially localize these genetically implicated cells within the brain architecture, we next performed gsMap analysis by integrating the BAG GWAS results with our spatially resolved single-cell data from the mouse brain, generating a continuous mapping of trait-relevance across all segmented cells and anatomical regions. The gsMap analysis revealed a strong association between brain aging and specific cell types including oligodendrocytes in the corpus callosum and epithelial cells in the choroid plexus, consisting with MAGMA analysis. This integrative approach delineated a core set of BAG-associated cell types oligodendrocytes (particularly in white matter tracts) and choroid plexus epithelial cells. The convergence of evidence from human molecular data (MAGMA) and mouse spatial transcriptomics (gsMap) strongly implicates these populations in the genetic predisposition to accelerated brain aging, highlighting their roles in myelin maintenance and blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier function, respectively, as key processes in brain ageing.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides a spatially resolved and genetically informed perspective on the cellular drivers of brain aging. By generating a comprehensive single-cell spatial transcriptomic atlas of the mouse brain across the lifespan and integrating it with human brain age gap (BAG) GWAS data, we identified oligodendrocytes—especially those localized in the corpus callosum—and choroid plexus epithelial cells as key cellular substrates of accelerated brain aging. Cross-species validation through MAGMA analysis further reinforced the central role of oligodendrocyte lineage cells. Moreover, the development of a single-cell spatial aging clock enabled us to dissect the paracrine influence of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors and other secretory molecules on neighboring cells, revealing a spatially structured microenvironmental dimension of brain aging.

Our work thus moves beyond genetic association to spatially map and functionally contextualize the cell types most relevant to brain aging. These findings offer a multiscale framework for understanding how cell–cell interactions within specific brain regions contribute to aging trajectories and highlight potential cellular and micro-environmental targets for interventions aimed at preserving brain health during aging.
