Introduction

Internalizing problems, such as anxiety and low mood, are increasingly common in youth and may signal early stages of emerging mental illness. These early stages represent a critical window of opportunity for prevention. Effective early intervention requires identifying which cognitive and neural processes serve as early targets before symptoms progress into more severe psychopathology. 

One process consistently linked to internalizing disorders is self-referential processing, often assessed with a Self-Referent Encoding Task (SRET)1. Individuals with depression typically endorse more negative and fewer positive trait adjectives than healthy controls2. Such self-related negativity biases also predict future increases in depressive symptoms, suggesting a cognitive vulnerability for developing depression3. Neural correlates of self-referential processing are found in cortical midline structures, particularly default mode network (DMN) regions. Aberrant DMN function has been linked to internalizing disorders, with increased DMN activity during self-referential processing observed in individuals with depression4. However, most evidence comes from studies of established mental illness. It remains unknown whether these cognitive and neural patterns are already present during subclinical stages. This gap is critical: without understanding the mechanisms that precede clinical onset, efforts to develop targeted early-stage preventive interventions remain constrained. 

The present study investigates self-referential processing and its neural correlates in help-seeking youth with internalizing problems (aged 16-25) and matched healthy controls (HCs). We test whether help-seeking youth show stronger negativity biases in self-referential processing compared to HCs, and whether this is associated with stronger DMN activity. We further assess whether increased internalizing symptoms are associated with greater negative self-referential processing and heightened DMN activation.

Methods

This poster is part of the ongoing PRYME study5, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) that examines how early-stage mindfulness-based intervention (MBI) impacts internalizing problems and associated cognitive processes, including self-referential processing, in help-seeking youth (16–25 years old). Here, we present preliminary baseline findings on self-referential processing biases and their neural correlates in help-seeking youth with internalizing problems and matched healthy controls (HCs). 

Self-referential processing was assessed with a Self-Referent Encoding Task (SRET), which measures endorsement and recall of positive and negative trait adjectives across self, other, and semantic conditions. Data include 136 help-seeking and 42 HC participants collected between July 2023 and December 2025. A 3-way mixed ANOVA with Group (help-seeking vs. HC), Condition (self vs. other) and Valence (positive vs. negative) tested group differences. Linear regressions with Conditionand Valence assessed associations with internalizing symptoms. Whole-brain and small-volume corrected (SVC) fMRI analyses were conducted with FSL FEAT (FLAME1; Z > 3.1, p < .05) to examine group differences and associations with internalizing symptoms for self vs. semantic and negative vs. positive self-referential processing. In addition, the specificity for self-referential processing and endorsement was assessed.

Results

Preliminary behavioral results show a significant GroupxConditionxValence interaction (p<.05), with help-seeking youth endorsing fewer postitive and more negative traits as self-relevant than HCs in the self condition (Fig. 1a). Furthermore, a significant InternalizingxConditionxValence (p<.05) was found. Higher levels of internalizing symptoms are associated with increased negative self-endorsement. In addition, higher levels of internalizing symptoms are associated with reduced positive endorsement in both self and other conditions (Fig. 1b).

Our fMRI analyses showed a main task effect, with significant DMN activation during self vs. semantic processing. Help-seeking participants showed higher mPFC activation during negative vs. positive self-referential processing compared to HCs. In addition, internalizing symptom severity was positively associated with medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and insula activity during negative vs. positive self‑referential processing (Fig. 1c). These associations were found to be specific for self-referential vs. other-referential processing (Fig. 1d).  

Conclusion

Research on self-referential processing has focused predominantly on clinical populations, leaving early, subclinical stages less understood. This gap is crucial, as adolescents and young adults with internalizing problems are at elevated risk for later disorders, and early biases in self‑referential processing may contribute to this progression and represent targets for early intervention. This study investigates these processes in help‑seeking youth and HCs using the SRET. Preliminary findings indicate that help‑seeking youth link more negative and fewer positive traits to themselves compared to HCs, and higher internalizing symptoms are associated with stronger self‑related negativity biases. Neural results support this pattern, showing increased mPFC and insula activity with greater internalizing symptom severity during negative self‑referential processing. These findings support the role of self-referential processing as a potential mechanism in the development of internalizing disorders and could inform early interventions that target these mechanisms (such as mindfulness or compassion-based interventions) aimed at preventing progression to more severe psychopathology.
