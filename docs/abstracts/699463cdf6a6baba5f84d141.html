Introduction

Mathematical cognition comprises multiple component processes such as calculation, reasoning, and identification, yet the complete underlying dynamic brain network mechanisms remain poorly understood. Although previous studies suggest that distinct mathematical tasks may rely on separate neural circuits1, direct electrophysiological evidence regarding the complete temporally structured information flow among multiple brain regions during these tasks is still lacking.

This study systematically compared neural activity and directed information flow during three mathematical tasks — numerical calculation, reasoning, and identification, by combining high spatiotemporal resolution intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) with Granger causality analysis. Focusing on key nodes of the mathematical processing network, including posterior cortical regions (occipital and parietal lobes) and medial temporal lobe (hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus)2,3, we aimed to address: Do different mathematical tasks correspond to distinct regional activation profiles? Do they exhibit task-specific temporal dynamics in information transmission?

By characterizing dissociable patterns of neural activity and information flow dynamics across calculation, reasoning, and identification tasks, this study provides novel causal evidence regarding the brain network organization of mathematical cognition, and lays the groundwork for developing network-based intervention strategies for mathematical difficulties.

Methods

Data were collected from nine epilepsy patients who had undergone iEEG electrode implantation, with electrode placement determined solely by clinical necessity (see Fig.1A). The iEEG data were recorded at the sampling rate of 2048 Hz. All participants voluntarily enrolled and provided written informed consent.

Pre-implantation anatomical T1-weighted MRI scans were preprocessed and parcellated using Freesurfer (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/), then resulting anatomical files were imported into BrainStorm (https://neuroimage.usc.edu/brainstorm) to serve as an anatomical reference. Post-implantation CT scans were loaded into BrainStorm and normalized to the aforementioned anatomical reference, and electrode contacts were manually localized on a contact-by contact basis. The anatomical location label of each contact was automatically extracted based on brain parcellations derived from the AAL atlas.

In experiment, each trial began with a 1000 millisecond presentation of “+” fixation cue, followed by the sequential presentation of three premise numbers, each displayed for either 300 or 1000 milliseconds (see Fig. 1B). Depending on the experimental condition (numerical reasoning, numerical calculation, or numerical identification), participants were required to verbally report their answer. Specifically, in the numerical reasoning condition, participants inferred the test number based on an implicit rule derived from three presented numbers; in the numerical calculation condition, they summed the three numbers; and in the numerical identification condition, they simply judged whether the digit “5” was present among the three numbers and responded with “5” or “0”. Each participant completed 45 trials for each of the three experimental conditions. With each experimental trial treated as a processing unit, baseline correction was first performed using the signal recorded during the fixation period (the final 500 milliseconds). Next, the average signal from all contacts on the same electrode shaft was calculated as a common reference, which was then subtracted from the signal of each individual contact on that shaft. Subsequently, signals from all trials were subjected to a two-step filtering procedure: a 0–160 Hz band-pass filter was applied to isolate high-gamma activity, followed by 50 Hz, 100 Hz, and 150 Hz notch filters to remove power line interference. Finally, the filtered signals were down-sampled to 256 Hz. Following preprocessing, event-related potential (ERP) waveforms were analyzed and compared across the three experimental conditions using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Additionally, Granger causality analysis was conducted to investigate directed functional connectivity between different brain regions.

Results

This study reveals differential engagement of brain regions across distinct mathematical cognitive tasks. The coordinated activation of the occipito-temporal visual association cortex, parietal regions, and medial temporal lobe (hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus) suggests that numerical processing relies on a distributed brain network. Importantly, during the processing stage following the third stimulus presentation, task-specific modulation of regional activity was clearly observed: the calculation task elicited the strongest activity in the hippocampus (F(2, 132) = 3.89, p = 0.023, see Fig. 2A), the numerical identification task evoked the largest response in the parahippocampal gyrus (F(2, 132) = 4.93, p = 0.009), and the numerical reasoning task produced the most prominent activation in the parietal lobe (F(2, 132) = 3.79, p = 0.025). To examine the directional flow of neural signals during task execution, Granger causality analysis was conducted among the above five brain regions (see Fig. 2B). The results revealed temporally structured information transfer pathways across tasks, with distinct patterns emerging depending on the task type.

For calculation task, following Stimulus 1, a significant information flow from the occipital lobe to the hippocampus (GC = 0.055, p = 0.047) was observed, reflecting the initial encoding and storage of visual numerical information into the working memory system. After Stimulus 2, the pathway expanded into two parallel streams: occipital-to-parietal (GC = 0.005, p = 0.041) and hippocampal-to-parietal (GC = 0.003, p = 0.041), indicating that the parietal lobe simultaneously integrated external visual input and internal mnemonic information to prepare for arithmetic operations. During Stimulus 3 processing, these pathways remained active, accompanied by a newly emerged significant flow from the parahippocampal gyrus to the hippocampus (GC = 0.008, p = 0.034), suggesting the integration of long-term memory or contextual information in the final decision stage4.

However, the reasoning task exhibited a distinct and more complex spatiotemporal profile, characterized by an earlier and broader network engagement. The information flow pattern in the reasoning task reveals a dynamic network centered on relational detection and rule construction. Pre-stimulus activation from the temporal lobe to the parahippocampal gyrus indicates an early engagement of relational processing (GC = 0.022, p < 0.001). From Stimulus 1, parallel pathways supported both visual encoding (occipital to hippocampus, GC = 0.031, p = 0.047) and numerical relation analysis (occipital to parietal, GC = 0.012, p = 0.050). After Stimulus 2, the parahppocampal to hippocampal pathway (GC = 0.008, p = 0.044) marked the initial formation and temporary storage of relational hypotheses. By Stimulus 3, the network engaged in full integration: the temporal lobe contributed high‑level rule categories5, the hippocampus retrieved stored relational assumptions, and the parietal lobe performed matching and verification, forming a closed loop for relational reasoning.

In contrast to the complex arithmetic or reasoning processing tasks, the numerical identification task exhibited a more direct and efficient processing circuit. A key feature was the early and sustained activation of the temporal to parahippocampal pathway (GC = 0.026, p = 0.050), likely reflecting the retrieval and maintenance of the target template (the digit “5”) from long-term memory. Visual information (occipital) was simultaneously encoded into working memory (hippocampus, GC = 0.027, p = 0.038) and directly routed to the parahippocampal gyrus for online matching (GC = 0.010, p = 0.044). By the third stimulus, the parietal lobe was recruited (GC = 0.002, p = 0.028), possibly aiding decision integration, and the final parahippocampal to hippocampal pathway (GC = 0.011, p = 0.039) aggregated the matching results to prepare the verbal response.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the brain activation patterns and information flow underlying three mathematical cognitive tasks — calculation, reasoning, and identification — by integrating event-related potential (ERP) analysis with Granger causality. The results demonstrate that each task is associated not only with distinct regional activation but also with fundamentally different dynamic processing circuits: calculation follows a hierarchical “encode-maintain-operate” sequence; reasoning engages an early-integrated network centered on relational construction and hypothesis testing; and identification relies on an efficient “template load–online match–decision output” loop. These findings confirm that mathematical cognition is not supported by a single system but rather by multiple functionally specialized and temporally structured brain networks, providing direct evidence for the neural computational division of labor in higher cognition. Future studies may explore the plasticity of these networks in mathematical learning difficulties and interventions.
