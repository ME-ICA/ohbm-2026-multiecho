Introduction

While noxious stimulus-evoked brain activations have been studied extensively in individuals with and without chronic pain1, the subtle nuances of stimulus-specific activations between different noxious stimulus modalities (e.g. noxious pressure vs. heat) have been less well characterized. Functional MRI studies have also explored the differences between populations at rest or for one modality using co-activation pattern (CAP) analysis, a method that looks at which regions activate together in time throughout a scan or during task periods, but has not been examined between modalities. Mechanical and thermal stimuli induce responses in overlapping (e.g. C polymodal nociceptors) and distinct (e.g. innocuous warm fibers, for heat) populations of nerve fibers that alter the qualitative perception of pain2.

Methods

In this study we applied different noxious stimulus modalities to 52 participants in separate experimental runs that held constant stimulus timing and changes in physical intensity across conditions. To acquire co-activation patterns, resting state functional imaging data (TR= 2 sec, FoV = 220mm, Slice Thickness= 2mm, Voxel size 2 x 2 x 2mm) was collected from participants before experimental pain stimulation. Participants were then stimulated using a 20 second on/off quantitative sensory testing (QST) protocol for 6 minutes with either a TSA 2 thermal stimulator (Medoc; Israel) or a pressure controlled pressure cuff (Hokanson; Bellevue, WA). We examined CAP differences in both people with and without temporomandibular disorder (TMD) between mechanical (n=46)and thermal stimuli (n=38). Following the stimulation session, participants were prompted to report their average pain rating. 

After preprocessing imaging data in FSL, data was denoised using a trained data set in FIX, and time series extracted into nodes defined by the Schaefer 400 atlas parcellation with subcortical regions added from the Human Connectome Project atlas. NeuroCAPs 3, a python-packed for performing co-activation pattern analysis, was used to determine the number of CAPs per group using the elbow method and calculate CAP states per participant. Fractional occupancy, Dwell, and event-locked probability were calculated using MATLAB to determine which CAP represented co-activations during task periods. Using task-related CAPs for each modality, we calculated and compared between and within each modality the mean network activity, percent of ROI activation/de-activation, and the CAP differences.

Results

No CAP differences were found between people with and without TMD for thermal stimulation. We found uncorrected differences for  mechanical pain between groups in CAP amplitude and mean network activity. People without TMD were found to have higher CAP amplitude and higher mean network activity in the dorsal attention (DAN), salience (SN), and default-mode networks (DMN). Examining modality differences in task-related CAPs, we found significant differences in network level activity between mechanical and thermal-induced pain stimulation. Mechanical pain was found to utilize the DAN, SN, and somatomotor networks more than thermal pain. Conversely, thermal pain was found to utilize the limbic, DMN, and executive control networks more than thermal pain. We also examined the ascending and descending pain networks and found significant differences in the utilization of each for each modality. The ascending pain network was found to have higher activity during mechanical stimulation, while the descending pain network had higher activity during thermal pain. 

Conclusion

These findings show that neural representations of experimental pain differ depending on the type of experimental pain given and increase the field's understanding of perceptual differences between different modalities. Thermal pain was found to involve more descending pain pathways which matches previous findings that perception and top-down modulation are involved with heat pain stimulation 4. Likewise, mechanical pain was found to induce higher activity in networks associated with ascending pain as well as sensory processing. Since TMD participants had lower activations in the task-related CAP, but reported higher pain from stimulation, this may be due to a mechanically similar nature between the spontaneous joint and muscular pain induced by TMD and the experimental mechanical cuff pain. This study demonstrates that the brain coactivation patterns associated with experimentally induced pain are not ubiquitous across modalities and that the type of modality used to induce experimental pain affects which regions will be most active during imaging studies.
