Introduction

Ischemia is an important aspect of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and leads to white matter damage which prominently manifests as white matter hyperintensities (WMH) on FLAIR MRI. WMH are clinically relevant: They are used as exclusion criteria in clinical trials of monoclonal antibodies, contribute to early diagnosis of vascular dementia, and are a potential biomarker in novel therapeutic approaches that target ischemia1. WMH have been extensively investigated and linked to cognitive decline2, however they are almost exclusively assessed by their volume on FLAIR MRI or with visual rating scales such as the Fazekas scale that reflect the size and confluence of WMH3. Importantly, the underlying pathology of WMH is heterogeneous4 and invisible on FLAIR MRI. Therefore morphological assessments of volume and confluence may not sufficiently reflect the effect of WMH on cognitive impairment, and tissue microstructure metrics from diffusion MRI could be a useful complement. We address this question by examining the microstructural heterogeneity of WMH in a large memory clinic cohort and by investigating the relationship between cognitive performance and WMH volume/confluence and microstructure.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited to the study Quantitative MRI in NHS Memory Clinics (QMIN-MC; n = 892, mean age = 72, 48% female). Participant diagnoses included among others Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, vascular dementia, mixed dementia and mild cognitive impairment. Cognitive abilities were tested with the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination, which assesses attention, memory, language, verbal fluency and visuospatial function.

Acquisition

MRI was performed with a 3T Magnetom Prisma and 64-channel head coil (Siemens Medical Systems, Germany). Multi-shell diffusion MRI (dMRI) was performed with a single-shot SE-EPI sequence with b-values of 0, 300, 1000 and 2000 s/mm2 in 6, 8, 30 and 60 gradient directions. Additionally, we acquired FLAIR (voxel size = 1.0 mm3, TR = 5000 ms , TE = 386 ms, TI = 1800 ms) and MPRAGE images (voxel size = 1.0 mm3, TR = 2000 ms , TE = 1.95 ms, TI = 880 ms, flip angle = 8°). Total acquisition time was 14:22 minutes.

Processing

Preprocessing was administered with Micapipe including denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, motion and distortion correction and bias field correction. White matter hyperintensities were automatically segmented from FLAIR and MPRAGE images with FSL TRUENET. WMH volume was defined as the total number of WMH voxels while confluence (i.e. the extent to which discrete lesions have merged into larger confluent lesions) was calculated with a novel algorithm yielding a score between 0 and 1 as a quantitative alternative for Fazekas grading (Schmidt et al., in preparation). 

NODDI (Neurite Orientation Dispersion and Density Imaging) microstructural maps, namely orientation dispersion index (ODI), neurite density index (NDI) and free water fraction (FWF), were derived from dMRI images with the AMICO implementation (Accelerated Microstructure Imaging via Convex Optimization). In order to analyse NODDI metrics within WMH, WMH masks were applied to NODDI maps and the average across all selected voxels was computed for each participant. 

We then generated control images or every subject so that within-WMH NODDI metrics could be compared to healthy white matter in identical locations in the brain: 20 subjects with no WMH were selected and contributed to an ODI, NDI and FWF group template image. Template images were registered to each subject’s native space and were masked with each subject’s WMH mask to create individual controls (Figure 1).

WMH volume and confluence as well as NODDI metrics within WMH and within healthy control WM then entered statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

To investigate the association of microstructure metrics (ODI, NDI, FWF) with morphological metrics (WMH volume, confluence), Pearson correlations were calculated for each pair of metrics. Differences between microstructure metrics within WMH and within healthy white matter were tested with t-tests.

To examine how well microstructure and morphological metrics explained cognitive performance, we conducted a regression of each cognitive score against age, sex, WMH volume, WMH confluence, ODI, NDI and FWF. FDR corrections for multiple comparisons were applied.

Results

WMH differed from normal white matter in all three metrics ODI, NDI and FWF (ODI: t = 9.45, p < 0.001; NDI: t = 65.72, p < 0.001; FWF: t = 54.50, p < 0.001). The three metrics correlated moderately with WMH volume and moderately–strongly with WMH confluence: Larger/more confluent WMH displayed lower ODI (r = 0.70), lower NDI (r = 0.65) and higher FWF (r = 0.49).

An effect on cognitive performance was identified for ODI within WMH (total score p = 0.004, attention p = 0.02, verbal fluency p = 0.02, language p = 0.02, memory p = 0.03, visuospatial abilities p = 0.03) and FWF within WMH (verbal fluency p = 0.004), but not for NDI, volume or confluence (Figure 2).

Conclusion

Discussion

ODI, NDI and FWF within WMH differed significantly from the metrics within normal white matter. Furthermore, the metrics varied depending on WMH volume and confluence. This is in keeping with previous dMRI studies suggesting microstructural heterogeneity of WMH5. Interestingly, ODI displayed a nonlinear trajectory: In weakly confluent WMH it was higher than in normal appearing white matter, but lower in highly confluent WMH. This suggests a potential mechanism whereby neurite orientation dispersion increases initially in damaged white matter, but later decreases as axonal loss progresses, which is in line with the decreasing neurite density index in more severe WMH.

Importantly, only microstructure and not volume/confluence of WMH affected cognitive performance. This finding indicates that it is not the size or spatial distribution of WMH per se that contributes to cognitive impairment, but rather the degree of tissue damage. For WMH to be a useful biomarker in clinical trials, dementia diagnosis and studies of ischemia-targeting therapeutic approaches, they need to be investigated with measures beyond volume or confluence. Microstructure information is essential and therefore diffusion MRI should be performed when WMH are examined.

The limitation of our study is the cross-sectional design which does not allow to analyse whether larger/more confluent WMH show stronger microstructural changes because they are older lesions or because of the underlying pathology. A strength is that we leverage a large diverse real-world clinical cohort and a novel analysis method (WMH confluence metric) to address the clinically relevant question of WMH heterogeneity.

Conclusion

White matter hyperintensities (WMH), commonly caused by ischemia in dementia and linked to cognitive decline, are increasingly used in diagnosis and clinical trials. Due to their pathological heterogeneity, standard morphological assessment with volume or visual rating scales could be insufficient. We demonstrate that WMH are considerably heterogeneous and affect cognition through their microstructure, not their morphology. When WMH are used for diagnosis or clinical trials, their microstructural assessment with diffusion MRI would usefully complement volume/visual assessment that is currently standard.
