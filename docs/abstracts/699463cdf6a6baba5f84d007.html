Introduction

Sleep is crucial for maintaining healthy brain function1,2. Poor sleep is associated with a wide range of health problems, including neurodegenerative diseases. One major factor that influences sleep is aging. Aging not only reduces the quantity and quality of sleep but also alters its microstructure. The strength of non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep neural oscillations, such as sleep spindles and slow waves, which play a pivotal role in maintaining sleep and supporting cognitive functions, is lower in older adults than in young adults, and these reductions are associated with increased Amyloid-beta pathology and poorer memory performance3.

Another factor that bridges sleep and cognitive health is the dynamics of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)2. Poor sleep quality impairs the clearance of CSF tracers from the brain4. Furthermore, sleep neural oscillations drive CSF dynamics in healthy young adults5. Slow waves and sleep spindles exhibited tight coupling with CSF fluctuations. Moreover, these oscillations recruited brain regions critical for sleep maintenance and memory: slow waves during slow-wave sleep preferentially activated the prefrontal cortex, whereas sensorimotor regions, including the visual cortex, were more strongly recruited during light sleep.

These findings suggest that neural oscillations, CSF dynamics, and hemodynamic responses are tightly coordinated during sleep. However, how these are affected by aging has remained unclear.

The present study investigated how aging alters sleep neural oscillations, their associated hemodynamic responses and CSF dynamics. To address this, we utilized a simultaneous polysomnography (PSG) and sparse functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) method5. This multimodal method has been shown to provide greater specificity and sensitivity than either technique alone1, allowing us to examine the interactions among neural activity, hemodynamic responses, and CSF fluctuations during sleep in both young and older participants.

Methods

21 healthy young (9 females, mean 22.8±3.5 years old) and 23 healthy older participants (10 females, mean 66.3±5.4 years old) participated in this study. All participants had two 90-minute early afternoon naps with PSG in a 3T Siemens Prisma MRI scanner. These two nap sessions took place approximately one week apart.

MRI data were acquired using a sparse GE-EPI sequence (TR/TE=3000/30ms, FA=85°, Multiband=3, 42 transverse slices, voxel size = 3x3x3mm3, 1800 volumes, B1+RMS=0.5μT, MR acquisition time=0.9s, quiet period=2.1s). An anatomical image, respiration and heartbeat signals were also acquired. PSG data were acquired using a BrainAmp MRplus EEG amplifier (Brain Products, Gilching, Germany) with a 5 kHz sampling rate and an MR-compatible 32-channel EEG cap containing 27 EEGs, 2 EOGs, 2 EMGs, and 1 ECG (EasyCap, Wörthsee, Germany).

PSG data were corrected for the MRI-related artefacts, such as MRI gradient and cardiac-pulse artefacts5. Sleep stages were scored into wakefulness (stage W), Non-Rapid Eye Movement (NREM) sleep stages 1-3 (N1-N3), and Rapid Eye Movement (REM) sleep. Sleep spindles and slow waves were detected from N2 and N3 stages.

MRI data were denoised using cardiac and respiration, and motion corrected5. Event-related fMRI GLM analyses were used to compare the aging effect on the hemodynamic response using FSL FEAT v6.00. Based on each sleep-specific event (onset and duration for slow waves and sleep spindles) detected from the simultaneous EEG data, event-specific first-level analysis was performed employing one regressor: 1) the event onset and duration. The regressor of interest was convolved with the double-gamma HRF. Motion parameters estimated during image realignment (3 translations and 3 rotations around the x, y, and z axes) were included in the matrix as confounding regressors of no interest. Additionally, since fMRI signal amplitudes vary across different sleep stages, the mean MRI signal inside the white matter region and the mean MRI signal in the cerebrospinal fluid region were included in the GLM as nuisance regressors. For each event of each participant, the event-specific first-level results were combined across sleep sessions using a second-level, fixed-effect analysis to calculate an average response per participant. For each event, these second-level results in different age groups were subsequently combined and compared at the group level using an FLAME mixed-effect analysis. In a cluster-based threshold statistical analysis, the resulting group-level BOLD Z statistic images were thresholded using Z > 3.1 and a cluster-corrected significance threshold of P < 0.05.

The CSF signal was extracted by averaging across all voxels in the lateral ventricles. The grey matter (GM) signal was extracted by averaging all voxels in the whole grey matter. The CSF signal was segmented based on each sleep event (slow waves and spindles). First, -9 to 0s before the onset of the events was defined as a baseline. This baseline signal was averaged and subtracted from 0 to 30s after the onset of the events. CSF and GM signals were averaged across the number of each event for each sleep session and then averaged across the sleep sessions and participants. To test the effect of aging, two-tailed independent t-tests (P < 0.05) with False discovery rate (FDR) corrections were applied to each time point of the event-related fMRI time course signals.

To investigate the relationship between the CSF and neurovascular signals, voxel-wise correlation maps between the CSF signal from the lateral ventricles and all voxels were computed using Pearson’s correlation separately for each nap session. These correlation maps were averaged across the sleep sessions and participants and compared between the age groups.

Results

We first confirmed that aging alters both the quantity and quality of sleep. The average sleep duration (mean ± SE) was 65.4 ± 3.8 min for the young, and 42.4 ± 3.5 min for the older participants. The average sleep efficiency (mean ± SE) was 72.9 ± 4.1% for the young participants, and 47.1 ± 3.9% for the older participants. The proportions of stages W, N1, N2, N3, and REM sleep (mean ± SE) were 26.9 ± 4.2%, 25.8 ± 1.9%, 34.4 ± 2.7%, 10.1 ± 2.2%, and 2.7 ± 0.7% for the young participants, respectively, whereas those for the older participants were 51.8 ± 3.9%, 23.9 ± 1.7%, 21.5 ± 2.5%, 0.6 ± 0.3%, and 1.0 ± 0.4%. The number of spindles (mean ± SE) was 255.6 ± 34.0 for the young and 119.6 ± 14.6 for the older participants. The number of slow waves (mean ± SE) was 104.5 ± 25.7 for the young, and 3.4 ± 1.1 for the older participants. These confirm that aging reduces both the quantity and quality of sleep, and that the occurrences of sleep spindles and slow waves during NREM sleep are significantly decreased.

We next examined how aging alters cortical and subcortical activation associated with sleep neural oscillations. The fMRI GLM analyses revealed clear age-dependent differences in brain networks recruited by both sleep spindles and slow waves. The young participants showed stronger activations in the thalamus and prefrontal regions, whereas the older participants exhibited greater activations in the temporal regions. For slow waves, the young participants showed stronger prefrontal activation, while the older participants showed increased activation in the visual and temporal regions. These indicate that aging results in a shift in recruitment of sleep neural oscillation-driven networks. With aging, brain regions supporting sleep maintenance and homeostasis become less active, whereas sensory cortices show more activation.

We next tested the effects of aging on the sleep neural oscillations and CSF dynamics. Slow waves triggered the CSF signal fluctuation similarly across the age groups, peaking at approximately 8s and returning to the baseline at ~15s after the onset of slow waves, replicating our previous study5. However, the older participants exhibited a smaller CSF signal fluctuation than the young participants. Sleep spindles were also followed by much reduced CSF signal changes in the older participants than in the young ones. In the young participants, the CSF signal peaked at ~10s and lasted for more than 30s. However, in the older participants, the signal peaked at ~8s, then quickly returned to baseline, showing substantially briefer CSF signal changes.

Finally, we tested whether aging alters the interaction between hemodynamic responses and CSF signals. In the young participants, widespread negative correlations between the CSF and GM signals were found. By contrast, in the older participants, the negative correlations that were evident in the young participants were no longer found in any specific regions. Thus, the relationship between the CSF signals and hemodynamic responses was markedly changed by aging.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that aging alters the strengths of sleep neural oscillations, cortical and subcortical brain activation, CSF dynamics, and their interactions. Aging reduced the activations in the thalamus and frontal regions. Since the thalamus and the frontal regions are involved in gating environmental signals and maintaining sleep, the present results suggest that aging weakens cortico-thalamic gating. In line with these findings, older participants increased the activations in the sensory regions, including the temporal and visual regions, involved in auditory and visual processing. Additionally, our results demonstrate that CSF dynamics during sleep are also altered by aging. The older participants showed reduced CSF fluctuations evoked by the slow waves and sleep spindles as compared to the younger participants.

Altogether, the present study reveals that aging alters the interactions among neural activity, hemodynamic responses, and CSF dynamics during sleep. How these are associated with cognitive decline or metabolite clearance2 needs to be investigated in future research.
