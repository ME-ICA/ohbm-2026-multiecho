Introduction

Disease progression models often use cross-sectional data across a number of disease-relevant features to estimate longitudinal trajectories in order to learn the underlying disease trajectories across individuals. The classical approach is to model disease progression as a latent permutation of events, with each event corresponding to an abnormality being detected in a feature. These feature abnormalities usually occur through a linear and monotonic progression. Current approaches, like the original Event-Based Modeling (EBM)3 and Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) EBM4, use maximum likelihood approaches to estimate the most likely ordering of features. However, estimating event permutations can be computationally expensive, and these methods face challenges when it comes to computational efficiency and interpretability, especially when dealing with large feature sets. This challenge makes the models infeasible for use with upwards of tens of thousands of brain-wide imaging features. We recently introduced the Fast Event-Based Model (FastEBM)1 to enable a high-dimensional, interpretable, and noise-robust modeling of disease progression and event permutation using a manifold learning approach. In this work, we extend FastEBM to allow feature ordering without stage information as an input, and incorporate uncertainty quantification to assess the confidence of predictions. We validate our new implementation by performing a comparison on synthetic data and data from The Alzheimer’s Disease Prediction Of Longitudinal Evolution (TADPOLE) challenge5.

Methods

Given a set of subjects (n) with an arbitrary number of features (p), FastEBM constructs a subject-wise similarity matrix, S, using a locally-weighted Gaussian kernel. 

S(x,y) = exp(-||x-y||2/(ϵ2x+ϵ2y))

Where x and y are subjects with their biomarkers z-scored relative to a control group, ||.|| denotes the Euclidean norm, and ϵx denotes the distance of subject x to its k-th nearest neighbor. FastEBM now offers the option to use a weight coefficient derived from pathology severity (e.g. the clinical continuum for Alzheimer’s disease) to scale the subject similarities, or a z-scoring option, where features are z-scored relative to a user-defined control group. Kernel S is then normalized to handle the potential sampling bias in the data

W(x,y) = S(x,y)/(D(x)D(y))

Where D(x) is the row sum for subject x in S(x,y). Then, a symmetric stochastic transition matrix T is calculated by normalizing W as follows

T(x,y) = E(x)-1/2W(x,y) E(y)-1/2 

Where again E(x) is the row sum of transition matrix T for subject x. Matrix T corresponds to an ergodic and regular Markov chain for which we calculate its fundamental matrix defined as

N = I + ∑∞n=1(Tn-A) = (I-T+A)-1

Where A is the stationary matrix of the chain. Each entry of the fundamental matrix, nij, can be interpreted as the expected number of times the chain is in state i if it started in state j. Using the fundamental matrix, we can examine the system’s behavior over time, and order the subjects based on how closely their behavior is to a reference subject. To calculate such ordering, we define a dynamic progression score, dps(x,y), based on the Euclidean distance between two subjects x and y as

dps2(x,y) := ||N(x, .) - N(y, .)||2

Where N(x, .) is a row-wise feature representation of subject x in N. The subject ordering is relative to a reference subject. To have an outlier-robust subject ordering, here we use the median of controls as the reference subject. From the subject orderings, we derive feature trajectories along the pseudotime axis defined by the dps score and use the change point detection algorithm to infer feature orderings. Validation:

To validate FastEBM, we compared our method to both KDE-EBM and the original EBM (using the pyEBM package) on simulated and real data. To maximize the sensitivity to recover fine structure of the manifold we used k=1 nearest neighbor for all analyses below such that the resulting similarity matrix is connected. For uncertainty quantification, in order to preserve the shape of the manifold and to prevent generating a singular transition matrix from having repeated subjects in our samples, we used the subsampling method and randomly sampled 0.8 of data without replacement in each iteration. Original EBM uses bootstrapping for uncertainty quantification. Subsampling and bootstrapping were done for 1000 iterations for each comparison. 

Real data:

We used the data provided as part of The Alzheimer’s Disease Prediction Of Longitudinal Evolution (TADPOLE)5 challenge for biological validity. TADPOLE consists of 1,737 subjects with 523 cognitively normal (CN), 872 with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and 523 dementia subjects. We used 7 biomarkers from CSF measures, brain region volumes, and cognitive scores. We regressed out age and sex fitted on the CN group from cognitive scores and CSF measures. Age, sex, and ICV were regressed out of brain volumes.

Simulated data:

For simulated data, to test FastEBM’s ability to infer high-dimensional event sequences, we used Kendall’s Tau rank correlation coefficient to compare the inferred ordering to the ground truth. For the simulated dataset we used a sigmoid function to generate 100 biomarker trajectories with Gaussian noise added (noise level  σ = 0.5) to each feature independently across 2000 subjects. The final dataset has an equal number of patients and controls. Fig. 1B shows an example of biomarker trajectories used to generate a 5-feature dataset. 

Results

The positional variance diagram (Fig. 2A) shows the uncertainty in estimating biomarker ordering across the three methods for TADPOLE dataset. Using FastEBM, ADAS13 and MMSE have close event centers with the lowest standard deviation in ordering. Similarly, hippocampus volume, whole brain volume, and PTAU are close to each other with whole brain volume having the highest standard deviation in rank. Fig. 2B shows the event-center variance diagram for each method. It can be seen that for FastEBM the average ordering starts with amyloid- 42 (ABETA) (mean rank  SD: 1.00 ± 1.29) followed by total tau (TAU) (1.57 ± 1.27) and phosphorylated tau (PTAU) (2.14 ± 1.05), then structural MRI markers: whole brain volume (2.4 ± 1.42) and hippocampus volume (2.96 ± 1.29), and finally cognitive scores, ADAS13 (5.15 ± 0.46) and MMSE (5.79 ±  0.43). This ordering is consistent with the hypothetical model of Alzheimer’s disease progression proposed by Jack2 where amyloid-β accumulation is the earliest detectable event, followed by tau pathology, brain atrophy, and cognitive decline. KDE-EBM consistently ranks MMSE first with no standard deviation (0 ± 0), followed by ADAS13 (1.01 ± 0.11). TAU (3.09 ± 0.77) and PTAU (3.149 ± 0.82) are ranked after with close event centers, followed by hippocampus volume (3.76  1.60), ABETA (4.59  1.40), and whole brain volume (5.40 ± 0.61). Original EBM ranks cognitive scores early in the disease progression timeline with close center followed by a mixture of tau marker abnormality and brain volume loss (Fig. 2B). Subsampling iterations for FastEBM 1458.49 took seconds while bootstrapping on KDE-EBM and EBM took 2414.14 and 4950.64 seconds respectively. 

Fig. 2C shows the Kendall’s Tau for each sampling iteration on the synthetic dataset. FastEBM achieves an average Kendall’s Tau of 0.979 ± 0.003 with a runtime of 4311.87 seconds (1.2 hours). In contrast, KDE-EBM achieves an mean Kendall’s Tau of -0.184 ± 0.072 with a runtime of 160547.16 seconds (44.5 hours). KDE-EBM’s lower performance can be attributed to the parameters we used to maintain a feasible runtime. Specifically, we reduced the greedy initializations and Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling to 10 and 200, respectively. Finally, original EBM achieves an average accuracy of 0.783 ± 0.040 across the 1,000 bootstrap iterations with a runtime of 179684.78 seconds (49.9 hours).

Conclusion

In this work, we present FastEBM, a manifold-learning based framework for modeling z-score trajectories that leverages median-based references for outlier robustness and incorporates subsampling for uncertainty quantification.  Using a subset of ADNI (TADPOLE) and anchoring our analysis to the median of controls, our method effectively recovers an ordering consistent with biological and clinical trajectories, where z-score deviations in amyloid-β levels precede tau accumulation, followed by structural brain atrophy and subsequent cognitive score decline. z-score deviations, anchored by the robust median, enables the recovery of a smooth, fine-grained subject ordering, overcoming the potential instability due to outliers and choppiness that is characteristic of manifolds derived from discrete staging and mean-based references. Further, using a simulated dataset with medium noise level, FastEBM is able to maintain the accuracy for biomarker orderings. On simulated high-dimensional data, subsampling iterations allows up to 41 times faster uncertainty quantification using FastEBM, and 3 times faster inference on TADPOLE. Lastly, we want to acknowledge some limitations of our current framework. While subsampling is computationally efficient and it does not distort the manifold by repeatedly sampling the same subject and thus generating a non-singular transition matrix, it relies on the assumption that 80% of the data are dense enough to capture the true topology of the underlying distribution. Consequently, specialized manifold-preserving sampling strategies may yield more theoretically consistent confidence intervals. Further, our framework assumes a single subtype and provides a single feature permutation across all subjects. We plan to address these limitations in future works.
