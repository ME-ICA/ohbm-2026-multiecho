Introduction

Brain activity is widely assessed with electrophysiological recordings, such as electroencephalography and magnetoencephalography (EEG/MEG). While it is commonly assumed that pyramidal neurons are the main generators of EEG/MEG signals, the cellular mechanisms underlying these signals in humans remain poorly understood.

Methods

In this study, we combined EEG/MEG with histological data and high-resolution 7T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to bridge the functional and anatomical perspectives on brain organization. We performed two analyses: across regions and across participants. For across-region analysis, we used independent datasets, including EEG (LEMON dataset, 209 participants1), MEG (Cam-CAN dataset, 507 participants2,3), high-resolution quantitative MRI (10 participants4), and histological sections stained for cell bodies (i.e., cytoarchitecture) from the BigBrain data set5. We correlated averaged-over-participants distributions in a pairwise fashion across modalities. The electrophysiological variables included alpha power, low beta power, and high beta power; MRI variables, R1 and R2*, provided proxies for myelin and iron content at different cortical depths; and cytoarchitectonic variables quantified layer-wise thickness and staining profile derivatives (mean and skewness of a staining profile). For across-participant analysis, we collected a multimodal dataset including resting-state MEG and high-resolution quantitative MRI from 31 participants. We correlated resting-state alpha power with myelin and iron estimates derived from depth-specific latent components obtained via factor analysis within each region of interest (parcellated into Julich Brain Atlas 3.1).

Results

Results revealed that across regions, alpha power correlated positively with the thickness of layer IV and with myelin and iron estimates at mid-cortical depth as measured by R2*. By contrast, across participants, alpha power and R2* at mid-cortical depth correlated negatively, most prominently in the frontal and central cortical regions. The opposite directions of correlations suggest that different factors may influence inter-individual and intra-individual differences. We propose that variable trends can be explained by microcircuits that include excitatory and inhibitory neurons and their fibers. Simulations with a Wilson–Cowan neural mass model support the idea that regional differences in alpha are dominated by excitatory population strength, whereas inter‑individual variability is more sensitive to inhibitory drive.

Conclusion

The current study contributes to ongoing research aiming at revealing the structural laminar correlates of electrophysiological signals, particularly neural oscillations, in the human brain. Moreover, the results suggest that structure–function coupling occurs through distinct underlying mechanisms at inter‑ versus intra‑individual levels, prompting the question of which factors drive these differences in electrophysiological signals, e.g., oscillations.
