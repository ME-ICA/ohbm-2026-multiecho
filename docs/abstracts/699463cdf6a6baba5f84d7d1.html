Introduction

Current diagnosis and monitoring of Alzheimer’s disease rely primarily on Amyloid beta and Tau pathology and on markers of structural atrophy. To accelerate therapeutic development, there is a need for biomarkers that are sensitive, reliable, and capable of tracking disease progression. Magnetoencephalography offers a promising avenue because it directly measures population level neural activity and is sensitive to alterations in synaptic transmission. Prior MEG studies consistently report a slowing of the power spectrum in Alzheimer’s disease, reflected in increased delta (1-4 Hz) and theta (4-8 Hz) power and reduced alpha (8-13 Hz) and beta (13-30 Hz) power compared with healthy controls ) 1. These findings, however, are based on static time averaged spectra despite the inherently dynamic nature of MEG signals, which arise from transient activity within large scale networks. Network level dynamics have been examined in Alzheimer’s disease and show reduced activation and connectivity within the default mode network (DMN) 2. Yet it is unclear how oscillatory slowing in AD relates to changes in network activity, nor how these develop longitudinally. Here, we investigated how static and dynamic power spectra and network dynamics differ in AD patients and how they change over the disease.  

Methods

We used data from the New Therapeutics in Alzheimer’s Disease (NTAD) study 3, including participants aged 50-85 with mild cognitive impairment or early AD (n=66, biomarker positive) and normal cognition (n=34, biomarker negative). Additionally, patients were followed longitudinally at 2 weeks after baseline (test/re-test, n=22), and at one year (n=44) and two years (n=26; cognitive and neuropsychiatric tests only) after baseline. Here, we focus on the MEG data recorded during resting wakefulness (eyes closed). Independent Cam-CAN 4 data (N=328, age 50-88) were used to model confounds.

MEG data we preprocessed and source reconstructed using osl-ephys and MNE Python. We used LCMV beamforming on an 8mm grid for source reconstruction and combined individual dipoles into a 52-parcel adaptation of the Glasser parcellation. Parcel time courses were then orthogonalized to remove source leakage. Sign ambiguity of individual subjects’ parcels was resolved by iteratively flipping signs such that subjects’ covariance was optimally aligned. Static power spectra were calculated using a Hanning taper for low frequencies (1-30 Hz), and a multitaper (10 Hz smoothing) for high frequencies (30-120 Hz).

For studying large scale networks, we fine-tuned a time-delay embedded hidden Markov model (TDE-HMM) with 10 states initially trained on the Cam-CAN cohort (N=612) 5 to the NTAD data and extracted HMM features including power spectra and dynamics (e.g., fractional occupancy (FO), lifetimes (LT), etc.) from both datasets. Power spectra were averaged across parcels to boost signal-to-noise ration.

Confounds including age, sex, and scanner effects were modelled with general linear models on the combined NTAD + Cam-CAN data; the NTAD residuals were then used for further analysis.

Static and dynamic power spectra, and network dynamics were compared cross-sectionally (i.e., AD patients vs. Controls), and longitudinally (AD patients at baseline vs. annual follow-up) in a mass-univariate GLM framework. Statistical significance was determined by comparing t-statistics to the maximum statistic of 5000 non-parametric permutations at alpha=0.05. In addition, test/re-test reliability was established using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) on the patients’ baseline and two-week data. An ICC ≥ 0.75 is seen as good reliability.  

For effect size estimates, we calculated the Cohen’s f2 score. This score assesses the difference in explained variance between a full model (RAB2), and a model where the regressor of interest (A) is excluded (RB2), normalised by the full model’s unexplained variance: (R2AB - R2A)/(1 - R2AB). Effect sizes are deemed moderate for Cohen’s f2 ≥ 0.15, and high for Cohen’s f2 ≥ 0.35. 

Results

We replicated previous findings of oscillatory slowing in AD: increased delta/theta and decreased alpha/beta power (Figure 1A). These effects were robust at the group level and small to moderate in size (Cohen’s f2 < 0.35; Figure 1B). Moreover, we observed that both effect size and test/re-test reliability of the parcel-average power spectrum was as high as the best individual parcel, and higher than the average individual parcel spectrum (Figure 1C). We did not establish any significant longitudinal differences in the static power spectrum.

A similar oscillatory slowing appeared in dynamic network spectra, with sensitivity varying by network and frequency band. Effect sizes exceeded those of static spectra in 5/10 states and were strongest in a state with high power in right-lateralised temporo-parietal regions (Cohen’s f2 > 0.6) in the alpha/beta band (Figure 1D-F). Differences in network dynamics were dominated by three states reflecting the anterior (state 2, 7) and posterior (state 1) parts of the DMN, respectively (Figure 1G). A reduction in posterior DMN activity replicates previous findings 2. Additionally, we report an increase in activation of the anterior DMN. Test/re-test reliability of the network spectra were generally high (>0.75), but more variable for the dynamics. The anterior and posterior DMN states had good reliability.

Longitudinally, we only found changes in the anterior DMN (State 2). In particular, high gamma power (116-120 Hz) was reduced in frontal brain areas in this state. A post-hoc analysis with a high sampling frequency showed a significant reduction in frontal gamma power between 116 and 200 Hz (Figure 1H). Interestingly, the more gamma power was reduced in a patient from baseline to the annual follow-up, the better they retained their memory performance (ACE-R Memory) in the following year (R = -0.51, p = 0.007; Figure 1I-J).

Conclusion

We showed that oscillatory slowing in AD is present across large scale networks, but importantly, effect sizes in individual network spectra are up to twice as high as in static spectra. Moreover, we find opposing effects in anterior and posterior parts of the DMN: the posterior DMN is visited less frequently in AD, in line with previous observations, but the anterior DMN is visited more frequently. Crucially, the anterior DMN showed longitudinal changes: switching rates were higher and high gamma (116-200 Hz) power was reduced in frontal brain areas. The amount of reduction in high gamma power predicted memory decline the following year.

These results indicate that brain network dynamics are sensitive to amyloid status and disease progression, and that network spectra are more sensitive and reliable than static spectra. This suggests promise as a biomarker tool. Future analyses will examine whether these biomarkers can be used in a multivariate context for prediction of individual diagnosis and/or disease progression.
