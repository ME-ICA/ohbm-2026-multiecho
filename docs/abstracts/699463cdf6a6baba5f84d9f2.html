Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a neuropsychiatric condition characterized by intrusive thoughts, repetitive behaviors, and difficulty in tolerating unpredictability. Neuroimaging studies have implicated prefrontal and parietal cortices in the disorder, highlighting alterations in processes that support flexible timing and inhibitory control.1,2 Recent work has reported abnormal regional activation during inhibitory tasks,3 but more importantly disrupted communication between large-scale networks,4 with a lack of network flexibility in adapting to unpredictable environments.5 This corpus of studies suggests that OCD is characterized by disordered regional brain responses as well dysfunctional connectivity. Individual data sets have rarely been investigated to inquire whether there is conformity in the two types of results. Here, we investigated these questions in fMRI data collected using a task that manipulated timing and inhibitory demands in youth with OCD (compared to controls). 

Methods

Gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) fMRI data were obtained [3 Tesla Siemens Verio system; 12-channel volume head coil (TR: 2.6 s, TE: 29 ms, FOV: 256×256mm2, acquisition matrix: 128×128, 36 axial slices, voxel dimensions: 2×2 x 3 mm3); 3D T1-weighted anatomical MRI image was also acquired (TR: 2200 ms, TI: 778 ms, TE: 3 ms, flip angle = 13°, FOV: 256×256mm2, 256 axial slices of thickness = 1.0 mm, matrix = 256×256)] in a group of youth with OCD (n=42) and healthy controls (n=40). All subjects provided consent or assent, and methods were approved by the WSU Institutional Review Board. During fMRI, participants completed a visuo-motor integration task. The task was constructed using a factorial combination of Response mode (Always Respond, i.e., “Go” vs. Selectively Inhibit, i.e., “No-Go”) and Inter-stimulus interval (ISI)(Fixed, i.e., “Periodic” vs. Variable, i.e., “Random”). fMRI data were preprocessed using a standardized pipeline in SPM12 pipeline including AC–PC alignment, motion correction, co‑registration, normalization to MNI space, reslicing to 2 mm isotropic resolution, smoothing with an 8 mm FWHM kernel, and high‑pass filtering with a 128 s cutoff. Vector onsets for each block of trials were convolved with the canonical HRF to recover condition-related response across the brain. We used a functionally defined cerebral parcellation scheme (Brainnetome). Regional responses (246 regions) were estimated based on the beta values for the overall response condition. Network activity was modeled using standard functional connectivity methods (zero lag) computed from time series associated with each condition (30,135 unique connectivity estimates). Regional response and network connectivity for all pairs were using mixed three-way mixed ANOVAs with Response Mode and ISI modelled as repeated measures, and group modeled as non-repeated measure. We were primarily interested in identifying two-way interactions between Group and Response Mode, and Group and ISI.

Results

Analyses of regional activation intensity revealed clear group and condition effects. OCD participants displayed reduced activation in the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), a region involved in rule‑based action selection and cognitive control.4 Under the Group × ISI interaction, OCD participants showed increased activation in several parietal regions relative to controls as stimulus timing became less predictable. For the Group × Response Mode interaction, OCD participants exhibited greater activation in motor and parietal regions (precentral gyrus, superior parietal lobule, inferior parietal lobule) as well as prefrontal regions (right inferior frontal gyrus, pre‑supplementary motor area) when inhibitory responses were required (Go to No‑Go)(Figure 1a and 1b, left). More extensive dysfunctional connectivity was observed under the interaction terms (Figure 1a and 1b, right). OCD participants needed increased connectivity between regions in the frontal, premotor, cingulate, striatal, and limbic circuits, suggesting compensatory engagement of control‑heavy and emotionally loaded networks (not shown). The most salient interaction was observed under Group x Response Mode (Figure 1b, right). As inhibitory response demands increased, OCD participants were characterized by compensatory increases in connectivity across regions in the frontal, motor, parietal and thalamic circuits.

The network figures illustrate pathways where functional connectivity differences were significant under the Group x ISI and Group x Response Mode interactions. 

Conclusion

Youth with OCD exhibit regional and connectivity deficits under changes in timing and inhibitory demands. At the level of brain regions, OCD participants showed reduced inferior frontal gyrus activation, but unpredictable stimulus timing elicited increased activation in premotor cortex and IFG, alongside exaggerated recruitment of parietal and motor regions during variable ISI inhibitory demand. Functional connectivity effects were widespread and largely noted for inefficiently increased network connectivity under increase in inhibitory demand. These complementary findings suggest a partial dissociation between regional and network effects in OCD. As with many other neuropsychiatric conditions (e.g., schizophrenia), they suggest that the nature of neural dysfunction is highly nuanced and subject to task demands.
