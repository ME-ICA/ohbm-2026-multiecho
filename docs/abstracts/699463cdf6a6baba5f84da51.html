Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of long-term disability, yet the neural basis of brain recovery remains unclear, hindering brain-based therapeutics. Advanced imaging, like diffusion and resting-state fMRI, has been used to measure structural and functional connectomes in stroke patients but faces challenges: patient discomfort, limited resources, lack of required expertise to process such images, and increased noise in these modalities due to stroke pathology. Most stroke patients, however, undergo clinical MRI, which can be used to delineate the stroke lesion. This work leverages two lab-developed tools to estimate structural disconnection (eSDC) and functional connectivity (eFC) directly from lesion masks only to then be used in connectome-impairment mapping. The NeMo Tool is a database of tractography results from healthy controls that removes streamlines passing through a lesion mask to provide eSDC.1 The Krakencoder, a joint autoencoder framework that can enable mapping between structure and function, can be used to estimate FC from the NeMo Tool’s estimates of SDC.2 These eSDCs and eFCs offer a novel way to capture the impact of lesions on brain network dynamics without acquiring advanced MRI in patient populations. Here, we predict motor scores from eSDC and eFC and compare the accuracy to predictions from observed FC (oFC) derived from resting-state fMRI obtained directly in the patient. Accurate prediction of outcomes using estimated connectome measures has the potential to inform efficient, personalized recovery strategies using only clinically acquired MRI.

Methods

The data used in this project is from a cohort of 132 adults aged 18–71 (45% female) who had ischemic stroke and were admitted to the School of Medicine at Washington University. The data includes MRI and a battery of neuropsychological tests. Scanning was performed with a Siemens 3T Tim-Trio scanner and included resting-state fMRI (TR =  2000ms, 4mm voxels, 6-8 scans for 30 mins total).3 Lesion masks were created in a semi-automated way and hand-checked by a clinician for validity. Lesion masks were then fed into the NeMo Tool to obtain eSDC and these outputs then fed into the Krakencoder to obtain eFC. Resting-state  fMRI was processed using standard pipelines and an oFC was obtained for each stroke subject. Ridge regression was used to predict motor score recovery from eSDC, eFC and oFC; all models included covariates of age, sex, lesion volume, physical therapy hours, and smoking status  (Fig. 1).

Results

The NeMo Tool’s eSDC and the NeMo+Krakencoder-derived eFC resulted in significantly more accurate predictions of motor scores at baseline, 3 months, and 1 year (right-sided outcomes) post-stroke compared to both models based on oFC and a covariate-only model (eSDC pFDR< 0.001; eFC pFDR<0.001; Fig. 2A.). These results highlight the NeMo and Krakencoder's ability to capture behaviorally meaningful structural and functional brain network disruptions due to stroke using only lesion masks. Figure 2B visualizes the most predictive network-level connections for left and right motor scores across modalities at the acute timepoint. Each ribbon shows whether stronger connectivity (or stronger disconnection, for eSDC) between two networks is associated with better (red) or worse (blue) behavioral scores, based on the Haufe-transformed regression weights. Notably, eSDC predictions show a more lateralized pattern, with prominent involvement of brainstem connections, potentially reflecting disruption of descending motor pathways. eFC predictions emphasize frontoparietal network interactions, consistent with higher-order cortical contributions to motor performance and prior work implicating frontoparietal activity in post-stroke motor recovery.4

Conclusion

We demonstrate that our novel pipeline for estimating functional and structural network disruptions from lesion masks can provide a time-efficient yet behaviorally relevant alternative to advanced connectome MRI in patient populations. This pipeline offers clinicians a practical tool for better understanding how lesions can impact brain networks and ultimately impairment, possibly enabling more accurate prognosis and recovery planning. This approach preserves inter-individual differences, allowing personalized predictions of motor recovery, and enhancing the potential for tailored interventions in stroke rehabilitation.
