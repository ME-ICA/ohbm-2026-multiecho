Introduction

Autism is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental condition and has been consistently linked to white matter differences in the brain using diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI). Previous dMRI studies in autism have reported white matter microstructural differences using the traditional reconstruction model, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI).1-2 Many such studies report lower fractional anisotropy (FA) in autism, most consistently in the corpus callosum; other affected white matter tracts are inconsistently identified across studies. These inconsistent results may be due to modest sample sizes, small effect sizes, and low statistical power, as well as methodological limitations such as failure to account for crossing fibers. The advanced tensor distribution function (TDF) model addresses the limitations of DTI by fitting a continuous mixture of tensors at every voxel, modeling complex fiber orientations.2 Here, we conducted a preliminary investigation of white matter microstructure differences in autism in a large, multi-site sample using tractography and the TDF microstructure model along with DTI microstructural metrics.

Methods

We analyzed 3D dMRI brain scans from 768 participants aggregated across 17 publicly available sites, including sites from the NDA, ABIDE, HBN and POND. Our sample consisted of 447 participants with autism and 321 neurotypical participants, with age ranging from 3 to 24 years (age: 12.15 ± 3.71 years; 32.3% female). All participants had a complete dMRI scan and complete information on diagnosis, age, sex, and full-scale intelligence quotient. For sites that had siblings or longitudinal scans, we selected one sibling or one time-point per family or participant. This approach maintained the independence of data points. Preprocessing was performed using the ENIGMA-DTI protocol.3 A tensor distribution function-derived FA (FATDF) and standard DTI metrics – FA (FADTI), and mean (MD), radial (RD), and axial diffusivity (AD) – were computed at each voxel. As in our prior work using Bundle Analytics (BUAN), whole brain tractograms were generated by using the constrained spherical deconvolution model and local deterministic tractography.3-4 Next, individual white matter tracts were extracted for each participant using their whole brain tractogram and model bundles from the HCP-842 template. We analyzed 13 major white matter tracts: the commissural tracts included forceps minor, forceps major, and midbody of the corpus callosum; association tracts included the bilateral averaged arcuate fasciculus (AF), extreme capsule (EMC), inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), middle longitudinal fasciculus (MdLF), and uncinate fasciculus (UF); projection tracts consisted of the bilateral corticospinal tract (CST), frontopontine tract (FPT), optic radiation (OR), and occipito-pontine tract (OPT). TDF and DTI metrics were mapped onto all streamline points and averaged to obtain one value per bundle for each subject. Before running linear regression, we harmonized the data across sites using Combat. We then tested the main effect of diagnosis and interactions with age and sex, when including standard nuisance covariates and correcting for multiple comparisons. We also tested associations between Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS) scores and diffusion metrics in a subset of participants with autism who had SRS data (248 participants with autism; 6-18 years; 33.1% female). In a follow-up set of complementary analyses, we assessed the global sensitivity of each microstructure metric with increasing sample sizes. For these analyses, we computed along-tract profiles with a segment length of 5mm and tested diagnostic group differences in FATDF, FADTI, MD, AD, and RD using a linear mixed-effects model at each segment. We then used repeated bootstrap sampling to examine how the proportion of white matter segments that survive a global FDR correction vary depending on the sample size.5 Specifically, for each sample size, subjects from each group were resampled with replacement across 500 bootstrap iterations and the segment-wise p-values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR across all bundles. We report the global FDR proportion, which reflects the proportion of all bundle segments that pass the global FDR correction threshold.

Results

The autism group displayed lower FATDF in autism compared to the neurotypical in two commissural tracts, – forceps minor and forceps major – four association tracts, – AF, UF, IFOF, and ILF – and 3 projection tracts – CST, OPT, and OR. FADTI was lower in autism for all three commissural tracts – forceps minor, forceps major, and midbody of the corpus callosum – and two association tracts – AF and UF – compared to the neurotypical group (Figure 1, above). Higher RD was found in autism in one commissural tract – forceps minor – and two association tracts – ILF and UF. There were no significant results for diagnosis by age and diagnosis by sex. A significant diagnosis-sex-age interaction was found for MD in the AF. In the SRS subset analysis, higher autistic traits in the autism group were associated with lower FADTI, higher MD, and higher AD in association and projection tracts. Specifically, this was found in the OR for FADTI, EMC and FPT for the MD metric, and EMC, UF, CST, and FPT for AD. For the complementary analysis where we examined the sensitivity of each microstructure metric, we found that the global FDR proportion increased with sample size overall for all metrics (Figure 1, below). RD showed the highest proportions passing global FDR followed by FADTI and FATDF.

Conclusion

In this preliminary study, we leveraged a multi-site sample and advanced diffusion modeling to characterize white matter microstructural differences in autism. Using both conventional DTI metrics and the TDF, we observed widespread differences in commissural, association, and projection tracts. Compared to the neurotypical group, the autism group showed lower FADTI and higher RD in commissural and association tracts as well as lower  FATDF in commissural, association, and projection tracts. We also found that autism traits were linked to differences in association and projection tracts for the FADTI, MD, and AD metrics. These microstructure alterations across multiple white matter tracts, particularly in frontal and temporal regions, align with findings from previous T1 brain MRI studies which found that autism was associated with greater cortical thickness in frontal areas and lower thickness in temporal areas. This regional overlap across structural and diffusion modalities support how the frontal and temporal lobes may be especially key areas to understand brain difference in autism. Moreover, the consistent increase in the global FDR proportion across brain metrics with larger sample sizes suggests greater statistical power to detect effects in larger cohorts. Together, these findings suggest that autism may be linked with widely distributed white matter alterations, and that integrating advanced modeling approaches with tractography in a large sample enables a more comprehensive characterization. The advanced single shell model TDF provides complementary insights to DTI by characterizing complex fiber architecture and in this study, was combined with tractography-based tract profiling. The next steps for this study include examining additional white matter tracts with more spatial specificity to better understand whether these alterations are localized to specific regions of the individual tracts. Moreover, future studies can look into how the different segment lengths for tractometry provide additional insights into understanding autism.5
