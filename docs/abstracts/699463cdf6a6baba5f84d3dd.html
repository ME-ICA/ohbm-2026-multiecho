Introduction

Understanding the laminar organization of cortical processing is essential for advancing our knowledge of brain function. While invasive electrophysiological recordings provide insights into layer-specific activity in animals1 and select patient populations2, non-invasive approaches applicable to healthy humans remain limited. Simultaneous electroencephalography (EEG) and functional MRI (fMRI) offer a promising avenue for bridging this gap. However, conventional gradient echo (GE) BOLD fMRI is confounded by superficial layer bias due to draining vein effects3, limiting its utility for layer-specific investigations. Vascular Space Occupancy (VASO) contrast offers improved spatial specificity for laminar fMRI but has not previously been combined with EEG due to technical and safety challenges. Here, we present the first demonstration of simultaneous EEG-VASO acquisition at 7 Tesla (7T), establishing the feasibility, safety, and functional sensitivity of this novel multimodal approach.

Methods

All data were acquired using a 7T Philips Achieva MR scanner (Philips Medical Systems) with a volume transmit coil (Nova Medical). The EEG system comprised of BrainAmp MRplus EEG amplifiers, ExG amplifier (Brain Products GmbH) and 64-channel MR compatible EEG cap with 4 carbon wire loops (EasyCap GmbH).

VASO sequence: A slice selective slab-inversion (SS-SI) VASO sequence with a double variable flip angle (VFA) 3D-EPI readout was used. A 3D-EPI readout chosen over a 2D-EPI readout since it has a lower specific absorption rate (SAR), higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and provides the ability to accelerate both in-plane and through-plane [1mm3, FOV=156(AP)x153(RL), SENSE=3 (AP) TR/TE=19/52ms, EPI factor=19, TRvolume=5s, HS=0.75, 18 axial slices]. 

To improve the functional sensitivity to the VASO contrast, a range of times for the first readout (TI1) were acquired during a finger-tapping paradigm (Fig. 1A). Following this, VASO data was collected in the visual cortex using two 16-channel surface receive coils (for higher SNR and acceleration) during a 8Hz flashing checkerboard paradigm (full field, 8 Hz reversal)4 (Fig. 1B).

EEG-VASO Heating Tests: VASO-fMRI uses repeated adiabatic inversion pulses and so heating tests were required. These were performed on a conductive (0.5Ω-1m-1) head shaped phantom (electrode impedance<15kΩ) with the EEG cap connected using conductive gel. Fibre-optic thermometers (Luxtron) monitored (1 Hz sampling rate) heating at six locations: cap electrodes (Cz, TP7, FCz & T7), the EEG cable bundle and EEG connector box (Fig. 1C). Temperature recordings were taken with the VASO sequence run for 17 and 76 volumes dependent on thermometer location. The change in temperature across each scan period was then assessed.

Pilot experiment: EEG-VASO data were simultaneously acquired during an eyes-open eyes-closed (EOEC) paradigm with two 16 channel receive coils on a single subject as a proof of concept. The EEG cap and surface coil positioning, and planning of the image acquisition were performed as previously described in Marsh et al4. The functional run comprised 60 volumes plus a noise scan (total acquisition time of 5 minutes 5 second). Cardiac and respiratory traces were recorded throughout each functional run using the scanner’s vector cardiogram (VCG) and respiratory belt. A T1weighted structural (PSIR) was also collected (0.7 mm isotropic, 252×250 matrix (M x P), 175 slices, TI1/TI2 = 780/2280 ms, SENSE: 2.2 x 2 (P x S direction)). After the EEG-fMRI session, the locations of the EEG electrodes and subjects head shape were digitally recorded using a Polhemus isotrack 3D system.

Analysis: 

The VASO and BOLD data underwent thermal denoising using NORDIC, data were then motion corrected (FSL, FLIRT) and the VASO data were corrected for BOLD contamination. For the finger-tapping and checkerboard paradigms the VASO-CBV and BOLD data were each input into a fixed effects GLM using a HRF convolved with a boxcar of the stimulus timings with the motion parameters as nuisance regressors. For the EEG-VASO EOEC paradigm, regressors were generated by convolving the standard HRF with the EEG alpha power time-course from the peak response to the EOEC stimulus as previously described4.

Results

Figure 1A shows the VASO-CBV and BOLD response to the finger-tapping task across a range of inversion times (TI1 = 1100-1250ms), with optimal VASO sensitivity at TI1=1150ms and BOLD sensitivity. Figure 1B shows results of the optimised SS-SI-VASO sequence applied to the visual cortex using the surface receive coils.

Figure 1C shows heating test results for the SS-SI-VASO sequence (RF average/maximum power 7.6/33W, SAR/head ≤46%, B1+rms=0.90 μT). There was no clear heating at any thermometer location, with a maximum heating over a functional run in channel FCz of 0.25°C. No channel had a heating change close to 1°C, VASO in combination with EEG was regarded as safe for human studies5.

Figure 2 shows the alpha power to the EOEC paradigm localises to the occipital cortex (Fig. 2A) with the time-course showing a strong alpha power modulation (Fig. 2B). Using the EEG alpha time-course as a regressor in a GLM results in negative VASO-BOLD-EEG (Fig. 2C) and positive VASO-CBV-EEG correlations (Fig. 2D) localised to grey matter.

Conclusion

A 1 mm isotropic 3D-EPI VASO sequence was optimised to measure activation during a motor task and visual task. The z-stat maps for both BOLD and VASO show significant activation in the motor and visual cortex for the motor and visual tasks, respectively (Fig. 1A&1B).

We show that for the 3D-EPI VASO sequence implemented in this work (which had a B1 RMS = 0.9 μT, SAR/head = 46%) the maximum heating observed over a 6-minute 20s period was ~0.25 ̊C (Fig. 1C). The greatest heating effect was observed in the FCz electrode.

These findings are in general agreement with other work at 7T that considered safety of using EEG with 7T MRI5 although it is important to note that, to our knowledge, this is the first safety test of EEG with a VASO sequence at any field strength. High SAR values can arise from the increase B1 needed for VASO (i.e. SAR/head = 46%) compared with standard gradient echo 3D-EPI (i.e. SAR/head = 18%) and therefore these safety tests were vital.

We show that the EEG data quality (Fig. 2) is not compromised to any greater degree for the SS-SI-VASO sequence than other MR sequences previously evaluated for fMRI at 7T4. Therefore, we are able to present the first results of modulations in CBV which are correlated with modulations in EEG alpha power. We show that the modulations in CBV are more spatially specific than the BOLD signal modulations (Fig.2d compared to Fig.2c), as expected. 

There are known benefits of VASO as a non-invasive method for understanding the functional role of different cortical layers for different tasks. This study provides the first step to the possibility of investigating the functional role of different electrophysiology signals across cortical layers with EEG-VASO. This will overcome the challenges of correcting for the draining vein effect in layer studies using EEG-BOLD4. This is likely to become an important tool to increase our understanding of how electrophysiology signals are generated, what their function roles are and what changes in disease.
