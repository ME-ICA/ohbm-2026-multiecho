Introduction

Psilocybin (PSIL) is a classic psychedelic compound that has shown rapid and enduring therapeutic effects across several psychiatric disorders. Recent longitudinal precision fMRI work demonstrates that acute PSIL produces markedly larger and longer-lasting spatial desynchronization of cortical activity than a stimulant control (Methylphenidate, MTP), reflecting a reconfiguration of whole-brain functional organization with its strongest and most consistent effects centered on the Default Mode Network (DMN).1 However, how PSIL alters the role of the DMN within whole-brain organization remains unclear. A recent systems-level framework proposes that DMN activity is counterbalanced by an opposing Action-Mode Network (AMN), which supports externally oriented engagement, arousal, sustained goal maintenance, and action planning. In this framework, pronounced DMN desynchronization under PSIL raises the possibility that its effects reflect a reweighting of the coupling between DMN and AMN, rather than a disruption confined to DMN circuitry alone. Importantly, the DMN is also distinguished by its position at the apex of the brain’s intrinsic temporal hierarchy, operating at the longest Intrinsic Neural Timescales (INT) relative to other large-scale networks. A perturbation that preferentially targets the DMN may therefore have consequences beyond spatial desynchronization, potentially reshaping the temporal organization of whole-brain activity. Accordingly, in this study, we focus on default-action coupling and investigate how PSIL alters INT and the temporal hierarchy that accompanies default-action organization across the cortex.

Methods

Six healthy adults (18–45 years)1 completed imaging under three conditions: a non-drug baseline, PSIL (25 mg), and MTP (40 mg). For drug sessions, MRI acquisition started approximately one hour after drug ingestion. Across conditions, a given participant could contribute multiple imaging sessions. In total, 32 baseline, 6 MTP, and 10 PSIL sessions were retained for FC analyses.

To characterize PSIL-induced reconfiguration of large-scale brain organization, we first examined parcel-wise cortical FC using the Schaefer 400 parcellation.2 We then applied the dimensional-reduction algorithm (i.e. gradient analysis) to unthresholded FC matrices for the baseline, MTP, and PSIL conditions using diffusion map embedding.  We opted to use unthresholded FC to avoid introducing sparsity or correlation thresholds that might systematically down-weight between-network connectivity (Fig. 1). Using the canonical gradient architecture introduced by Margulies et al.,3 we defined a DMN-focused ROI from the top 10% of vertices on the unimodal-transmodal Gradient 1 (G1). To quantify how PSIL alters the balance between default and action mode networks, we then assessed the alignment between FC profile and the default-action axis (defined by Gradient 3 in Margulies et al.3).

Additionally, we examined INT from resting-state fMRI time series to measure the temporal persistence of intrinsic activity. For each vertex, we estimated the autocorrelation function (ACF) and quantified INT as the sum of consecutive positive ACF values multiplied by the repetition time. Finally, to relate changes in intrinsic activity to cognitive constructs across different conditions, we performed meta-analytic decoding using Neurosynth. For each session and each time point, we computed spatial correlations between the whole-brain activity pattern and Neurosynth maps for 123 cognitive terms.4 The cognitive term with the highest correlation was assigned as the matching label for that time point, and the corresponding correlation value was taken as its matching score. Within each session, we then summarized, for each term, its occurrence frequency and mean matching score. At the condition level (baseline, MTP, PSIL), we aggregated matching scores across sessions and computed a weighted matching score for each cognitive term by multiplying its relative occurrence frequency by its mean matching score, yielding a condition-specific profile of dominant cognitive terms (Fig. 2).

Results

At the whole-brain FC level (Fig. 1A), the PSIL condition showed an overall higher FC magnitude and a marked reduction in negative correlations compared with both the baseline and MTP conditions. These differences were particularly pronounced for between-network connectivity, which exhibited broadly increased strength under PSIL. Network-level analyses indicated that the reduction in negative FC was most prominent between the DMN and attention/action-related networks, including the dorsal attention and ventral attention networks (Fig. 1B).

Functional gradient analyses revealed that the spatial organization of the group-level G1 was highly similar across conditions and recapitulated the canonical unimodal–transmodal axis. However, the contribution of G1 to overall functional organization differed substantially across states. Specifically, G1 exhibited stronger eigenvalues at baseline and under MTP (baseline: λ = 0.45, MTP: λ = 0.43), whereas under PSIL the G1 eigenvalue was substantially reduced (λ = 0.04), indicating a weakened principal gradient structure. For Gradient 2 (G2), the baseline and MTP conditions produced maps that closely resembled a task-positive vs. task-negative arrangement, forming an axis spanning from AMN toward the DMN. In contrast, in the PSIL condition, the dominant axis captured by G2 was reoriented, exhibiting a more pronounced anterior–posterior gradient.

To assess DMN-AMN coupling directly, we examined FC profiles seeded from a DMN-focused ROI. In the baseline and MTP conditions, FC patterns were clearly structured along an action–default axis, with the expected strong anticorrelation between DMN and AMN. Under PSIL, however, this canonical anticorrelation was substantially attenuated with the FC profile exhibiting reduced cross-network differentiation (Fig 2B). At the individual level, spatial similarity between each session’s FC profile and the action-default axis (Fig 2C-D) was consistently positive across sessions in the baseline and MTP conditions, but was markedly reduced under PSIL.

Whole-brain INT maps under the baseline and MTP conditions closely followed the hierarchical unimodal-transmodal functional axis, with shorter timescales in primary sensory and motor regions and longer timescales in higher-order association cortex, particularly within the DMN. In contrast, under the PSIL condition, this spatial organization was substantially disrupted under PSIL with a global increase in INT across the cortex (Fig. 2F).

Neurosynth-based meta-analytic decoding (Fig. 2E) revealed that the five most frequent cognitive terms were the same across conditions at the broad categories. However, their weighted matching scores differed across conditions. In the baseline and MTP conditions, memory-related terms such as autobiographical memory, memory retrieval, and navigation showed higher weighted matching scores. In contrast, under PSIL, the dominant weighted matching scores shifted toward more basic sensorimotor-related terms, including movement and pain.

Conclusion

Together, these findings demonstrate that psilocybin induces a profound reorganization of large-scale brain function that extends beyond global spatial desynchronization. PSIL attenuates the canonical anticorrelation between default-mode and action-related systems, and reshapes the low-dimensional geometry of functional connectivity. This spatial reconfiguration is accompanied by a breakdown of the intrinsic temporal hierarchy, characterized by globally prolonged neural timescales. Meta-analytic decoding further reveals a shift from introspection-related cognitive content toward more basic sensorimotor-oriented states during PSIL. Collectively, these results suggest that psilocybin acts as a potent perturbation of the brain’s default-action operating regime, reshaping how information is integrated across both space and time, and in turn altering the dominant modes of spontaneous cognitive expression.
