Introduction

In decision theory and reinforcement learning, it is typically assumed that agents aim to maximize either expected reward or a subjective transformation of it—expected utility. Correlates of subjective transformations of reward have also been argued to apply to the dopaminergic reward system.1 This transformation—the utility function—is treated as a stable psychological trait: it may differ between individuals but is assumed to be stable within an individual over time. Its curvature is then taken to define risk preference: concave utility reflecting risk aversion, linear utility reflecting risk neutrality, and convex utility reflecting risk-seeking. An alternative account from ergodicity economics proposes that, rather than maximizing expected utility, agents aim to maximize time-average growth rates. On this view, risk-taking behavior is shaped more by the structure of the environment than by idiosyncratic psychological differences. In particular, this framework predicts approximately risk-neutral behavior in additive environments, but substantially greater risk aversion in multiplicative environments, where wealth compounds over time.2 These shifts arise not from a change in intrinsic preference, but from optimizing the time average.

Here, we ask whether human risk-taking maximizes time average growth rates, and how this is encoded by the brain. We test this by imposing different wealth dynamics within a gambling task, recording both behavioral and neural responses. 32 participants made a series of risky choices under either additive or multiplicative dynamics while we acquired functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data. The goal of the fMRI analysis presented here is: (1) to infer and map voxel-wise risk preferences based on neural activity, (2) to assess whether these neural signatures are consistent with observed risk-taking, and (3) to assess how well these preferences approximate theoretical optimality. 

Methods

We collected behavioral and fMRI data from 38 participants (6 excluded). Informed consent was obtained from all participants as approved by the Ethics Committee of Region Hovedstaden (protocol H-22046090) and in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.

The study took place over two days; on each day, participants performed a different condition of a gambling task in the scanner, one with additive and the other with multiplicative gambles. FMRI data were collected on a Siemens PRISMA 3T scanner; each volume consisted of 27 axial slices of 2 mm thickness and was collected using an EPI gradient echo sequence (TE = 32ms, TR = 2.024s, flip angle = 90°). For alignment of the fMRI images, we used standard T1-weighted images. FMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPREP version 24.0.1.

Similar to Skjold et al.3 the experiment consisted of a learning and a decision task. The learning task was a classical conditioning experiment, where participants, endowed with 1000 points, had to learn the effect of nine different fractal images on their current points. Each fractal had a deterministic effect on the participants’ points. After 45 trials, the points were reset. We tested participants’ ability to learn the fractals by presenting them with 15 pairs of fractals, where they should identify the one that has a better effect on their points (without feedback). After three learning blocks, participants performed 160 trials of the decision task. Participants were presented with two gambles: one on the left, and one on the right side of the screen, consisting of two fractals each (Fig. 1). Participants chose between these gambles using the button box. The chosen gamble was realized by the outcome of a fair coin toss landing on the upper or lower fractal, and the effect on the points was displayed by rolling the current points up or down.

For both behavioral and imaging analyses, we model risk preferences with an isoelastic function (as detailed in Skjold et al.3). The function’s shape is defined by a single parameter 𝜂, where values less than 0 indicate risk-seeking, 0 indicates risk neutrality, and values larger than 0 indicate risk aversion. Values of 0 and 1 are special cases, as these indicate linear and logarithmic transformations. These are the values predicted under the ergodicity model for additive and multiplicative dynamics, respectively.

To infer risk preferences from the voxel-wise neural data, we modelled changes in utility at the trial level for a grid of possible values of 𝜂, which we refer to as the utility regressor (UR). These changes were set to onset at the time the points updated and convolved with an HRF. GLM analyses were conducted using SPM12. For each participant, session, and UR, a design matrix was generated, in which we modelled the fractals, the four coin locations and the wealth updating as box-car regressors with time on screen as duration and left and right responses as onset regressors. A design matrix without an UR served as a null model.

For each design matrix, we estimated the voxel-wise cross-validated log model evidence (cvLME) using the MACS toolbox4 and aggregated these in a 4-dimensional array (with dimensions: participant, session, 𝜂, and voxels).

On the group-level, we performed a fixed-effects Bayesian model selection by first calculating the log BayesFactor (BF) of each 𝜂-model against the null model. We then summed over the participant dimension, and selected the 𝜂 with the highest BF for each voxel and session (Fig. 2A).

For the region-level, we calculated the log BF of each 𝜂-model against the null model and only considering voxels, where at least one 𝜂-model had a BF > 10. For these surviving voxels, we ensured positivity by subtracting the smallest log BF from all parameters and then divided by the sum, creating a probability weighting over 𝜂. These estimates were then summed and normalized over voxels. We also summed over participants and normalized again to create a group estimate.

Results

Here, we describe the preliminary results of the parameter estimation of 𝜂, discretized for the values -0.25 to 1.25 in increments of 0.05, as detailed in the methods.

In Fig. 2A we show the results of the parameter estimation on the group level. The thresholded maps show two main findings. The main clusters surviving the threshold encompass the striatum and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex in both the additive and multiplicative conditions. The regions are typically associated with reward processing and value encoding. Secondly, the group analyses maps indicate that the best fitting parameters of the isoelastic utility function are around 0 in the additive condition and are around 1 in the multiplicative condition. Thus, corresponding to linear and logarithmic risk-preferences, respectively, as predicted under the ergodicity model. 

In Fig. 2B and C, we show the region-wise parameter estimates, weighted by their evidence. In B, we display the individual traces and the group-level results. While there is more noise than in the group level analysis, we also see that for most participants, the strongest weighted evidence for the striatum (putamen, caudate, nucleus accumbens) and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex are around time-optimality (close to 0 in the additive and close to 1 in the multiplicative condition). In other words, these estimates are close to what would be predicted if the brain were computing the quantities necessary for maximizing time average growth rates. 

In Fig. 2C, we show bivariate plots of the joint probabilities of 𝜂 over the sessions for both the group and individual estimates. This plot reiterates the results from 2B and stresses further, that indeed on the individual level substantive shifts in voxel-wise risk preferences happen between the two sessions. Furthermore, these shifts are generally in the direction of time-optimal behavior and away from the values which would be predicted under a model of stable risk preferences (indicated by the diagonal).

Finally, in Fig. 2D we show the results of the behavioral analyses which uses hierarchical Bayesian models to estimate the 𝜂-parameter of the isoelastic utility function. As can be seen, the neural analyses mirror the outcomes of the behavioral analyses, in that participants approach the parameter values that are time optimal. In the behavioral analysis, the estimates for the additive parameters are slightly negative, whereas the estimates in the neural analysis are slightly more positive, though both are close to the predicted zero point. An exception are group estimates of ventromedial prefrontal cortex, which seem to be more inline with the behavioral results.

Conclusion

In this study, we show that risk preferences can be estimated directly from neural data, and that the resulting estimates are consistent with time optimal behavior. Specifically, we find that evoked reward responses in the striatum and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) are best explained by a transformation of wealth that is close to the theoretical optimal transformation for maximizing the growth rate of wealth. 

The credibility of this result is supported by its convergence with independently modelled behavioral estimates. Although behavioral data were not directly included in the neural models, approximately the same parameter values were estimated. Moreover, the behavioral analysis reported here constitutes a third replication of an environment-dependent shift in risk preferences.3,5 Together, these findings provide further evidence that risk preferences can adjust quickly, substantively, and near-optimally to align with maximizing the time average growth of wealth. This supports the view that environmental dynamics, rather than stable psychological traits, emerge as the dominant driver of observable risk preferences.3,5

Methodologically, by estimating risk parameters directly from fMRI data by using cross-validated log model evidence, we show that voxel-level signals are better explained by models that include (rather than exclude) utility-change regressors. The voxels showing utility-like responses are concentrated in canonical reward and value-encoding regions (striatum and vmPFC), providing face validity to this modelling approach.

Finally, the comparison of additive and multiplicative sessions suggests that these regions encode a transformed representation of reward rather than absolute reward changes. That the implied transformations cohere with time-optimal predictions may therefore represent an important case in which a principled optimality theory predicts the form of human reward responses. Whether this effect replicates broadly, generalizes across tasks and populations, and withstands expanded model comparisons and alternative explanations remains for future work.
