Introduction

There is significant interest in cerebellar involvement in both the motor and non-motor symptoms of neurodevelopmental disorders. This was first motivated by early structural MRI studies of individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) in whom hypoplasia of the cerebellar vermis was first reported1 and its relation to impairments in associative learning as measured using trace eye blink conditioning in autistic children with intellectual disability2. An important principle of cerebellar function is that the cerebellum does not function alone, but rather functions through dynamic interactions with the brain stem and thalamo-cortical systems. Cerebellar interactions with other brain areas are rapid and occur in short bursts at defined frequencies relative to sensory or motor events, as evidenced from invasive electrophysiology studies in experimental animals. Although the cerebellum has historically been considered a motor structure that places descending motor commands into the machine language of motor units3, a current view is that the cerebellum operates in concert with the precentral, premotor, and prefrontal cortices through recurrent activity in discrete loops in order to optimize both motor and cognitive functioning. In macaques, trans-synaptic viral tracing demonstrated that postsynaptic projections of dentate neurons target both the motor and non-motor thalamus and that cerebellar lobules that trans-synaptically innervate the prefrontal cortex receive trans-synaptic feedback from their cerebral target via the pons4. The looped circuit anatomy allows the cerebellum to reciprocally interact with an expanse of frontal cortex anterior to M1 that includes Brodmann’s areas 46 and 95. With the objective of characterizing these interactions in humans, and since associative learning and motor ability rely upon cerebellar and frontal lobe connections, we used the eyeblink conditioning task (EBC) to probe cerebro-cerebellar interactions in healthy human adults. 

Methods

Eight healthy adults of either sex underwent 2 sessions of trace EBC consisting of a 200 ms, binaural, tone conditioned stimulus (CS) and a 100 ms corneal air-puff unconditioned stimulus (US) delivered to the right eye. Trace EBC trials used a 700 ms CS-US interval in which a 500 ms, no-stimulation trace period was interposed between the CS and US (20 ± 5 s intertrial interval). As a measure of associative learning, conditioned responses (CRs) were defined as eyeblinks within the CS-US interval having onsets later than 80 ms post CS onset. During the second trace EBC session, we recorded magnetoencephalography (MEG). A SAM Beamformer (as implemented in the BrainWave MATLAB toolbox, https://github.com/cheynelab) was used to extract the time course of alpha activity (7-12 Hz) in the cerebellum and cerebral cortex, time-locked to the eyeblink onset (baseline = -2 to -1.7 s). Coherence between cortical and cerebellar sources was computed using custom MATLAB code. 

Results

During MEG recording, the average percentage of trials with CRs was 58.2 ± 10.1% and the average CR onset latency was 472 ± 36 ms, indicating that the subjects adapted the CR timing to within the trace interval. Increases in alpha power during the CS-US interval prior to CR onset were found (Fig 1) in the right cerebellum (MNI coordinates 17, -88, -38), left frontal eye field (FEF, Brodmann Area 6, MNI coordinates 47, -9, 55), right FEF (Brodmann Area 6, MNI coordinates 54, -7, 41), and the supplementary motor area (SMA, MNI coordinates -3, -13, 72).  In addition, left cerebellar activation was observed (MNI coordinates -34, -83, -38). Among those sources, significant increases in coherence during the CS-US interval over a baseline period of equal length were found (Fig 2). Significant pairs were: (i) left and right cerebellum (p = 0.023), and (ii) left FEF and SMA (p = 0.03). 

Conclusion

 Our analyses support the hypothesis that trace EBC in the human brain involves dynamic interactions within the cerebellum and within the premotor areas of the frontal cortex in the alpha band. Because the two cerebellar hemispheres are not directly connected to one another, bilateral coherence of alpha activity within the cerebellum must be underlain by a common input that drives isochronous 10 Hz activity during learning, such as the inferior olive. Our MEG findings indicate that coherent activity during associative learning results both from reciprocal interconnections, as in the cerebral cortex, and by common input, as in the cerebellar cortex.  Additional analyses will describe these physiological processes further.
