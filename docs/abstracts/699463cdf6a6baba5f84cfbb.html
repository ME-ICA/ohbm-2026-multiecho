Introduction

Vision Transformers (ViTs) are increasingly explored for structural MRI classification in neurodegenerative disease. Their global attention mechanisms allow them to dynamically integrate information from spatially distant brain regions, theoretically aligning with the network-based nature of distributed atrophy patterns.1,2 Unlike Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) constrained by local receptive fields, ViTs tokenize volumes into sequences where attention capture non-local associations without explicit neuroanatomical priors. However, this weaker inductive bias makes them fragile on the small, heterogeneous cohorts typical of clinical research, with high sensitivity to initialization, hyperparameters, and sampling noise, a critical issue for differential diagnosis where phenotypes overlap and labels are imperfect.

Consequently, promising benchmarks on Alzheimer’s disease (AD) vs. controls often mask these instabilities, leading to performance overestimation in multi-class settings involving frontotemporal dementia (FTD) variants, notably the behavioral variant (bvFTD) and primary progressive aphasia subtypes: semantic (svPPA) and nonfluent/agrammatic (nfvPPA) variants. These syndromes share partially overlapping atrophy patterns and are clinically defined using multimodal assessments, making MRI-only prediction unstable. Transformer models frequently show inconsistent convergence, large variance across seeds, and reduced robustness to domain shift.

In this work, we examine ViT stability and robustness for MRI-based differential diagnosis. Using ADNI+ALLFTD for in-domain training and NIFD+NACC for out-of-domain evaluation, we quantify how variance evolves with task complexity and test a targeted stabilization protocol spanning optimization, architecture, and evaluation. We further adopt uncertainty-aware reporting (bootstrapping, calibration analysis, probability of false outperformance) to assess reliability beyond point estimates.3,4

Methods

We evaluated the stability and robustness of ViTs for MRI-based differential diagnosis using ADNI and ALLFTD as in-domain (ID), and two external cohorts, NIFD and NACC, for out-of-domain (OOD) testing. We study how task complexity impacts training stability by moving from a coarse 3-class setting (CN/AD/FTD) to a fine-grained 5-class differential diagnosis task (CN/AD/bvFTD/svPPA/nfvPPA). In the 3-class task, FTD is formed by merging bvFTD, svPPA, and nfvPPA into a single class.

T1-weighted MRI preprocessing. All scans underwent a unified pipeline including N4 bias correction, skull stripping, affine + diffeomorphic registration to MNI space, 1\,mm isotropic resampling, intensity z-normalization, and fixed field-of-view cropping. To avoid data leakage, a single baseline scan was selected per subject; CN subjects were required to remain stable across longitudinal visits.

Models. We benchmarked 3D ViT\textsuperscript{1}, Swin-3D\textsuperscript{2}, Swin with deformable patch locations (Swin-DPL)\textsuperscript{5}, a hybrid CNN-Transformer (MedViT-3D), a 3D CNN (ResNet-18), and an anatomy-driven baseline combining segmented structures from AssemblyNet ensemble of U-Nets with a Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier. Architectures were selected to maintain comparable parameter counts (\(\sim\)25--40M). Swin-DPL served as the primary testbed for stabilization experiments because it isolates optimization challenges intrinsic to hierarchical attention mechanisms.

Training protocol. All models were trained from scratch using AdamW, cosine learning-rate decay, warmup, early stopping, and mixed-precision training. To avoid confounding architectural changes with optimization variability, hyperparameters were kept fixed across seeds and folds. We performed patient-level stratified 10-fold cross-validation, preserving age, sex, diagnosis, and dataset proportions. OOD performance was computed by averaging the softmax outputs of the ten fold-trained models.

Stabilization strategies. We evaluated a comprehensive set of strategies targeting the specific vulnerabilities of ViTs on small datasets. We categorized them into three families based on their objective:

  1. Data-centric regularization (combating overfitting): extensive 3D MRI-specific transforms (affine/elastic deformations, bias-field, noise), balanced sampling to mitigate collapse on minority FTD subtypes, MixUp to enforce decision boundary linearity, and label smoothing;
  2. Optimization stability (improving convergence): Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of weights, LayerScale, stable initialization, Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM), and ShakeDrop;
  3. Inference reliability (assessing robustness): Test-Time Augmentation (TTA), temperature scaling for calibration, and snapshot ensembling (averaging top-\(K=12\) validation checkpoints).

Individual and cumulative effects were assessed. The full training protocol combined 3D augmentation, EMA, label smoothing, balanced sampling, and MixUp, while the final model was evaluated with TTA, temperature scaling, and checkpoint ensembling.

Uncertainty-aware evaluation. To quantify model sensitivity to random initialization and sampling, all experiments were repeated across multiple seeds. We used patient-level bootstrapping (10{,}000 replicates) to compute confidence intervals without parametric assumptions. Model stability was assessed using the normalized coefficient of variation (nCV), proportional to the ratio standard-deviation over mean, over 5 different trainings, enabling fair comparison across classes of unequal size. Statistical significance was evaluated with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (ID), McNemar tests (OOD), and the probability of false outperformance (PFO)4

Results

Baseline experiments revealed substantial instability in Transformer-based models when moving from a simple 3-class setting (CN/AD/FTD) to the full 5-class differential diagnosis task (CN/AD/bvFTD/svPPA/nfvPPA). Pure ViTs showed inconsistent convergence and frequent collapse on minority classes. This suggests that without explicit spatial constraints, global attention layers struggle to capture the fine-grained, localized atrophy patterns required to distinguish overlapping PPA phenotypes. In contrast, the 3D ResNet and the anatomy-driven SVM baseline remained stable across seeds and folds, highlighting the resilience of convolutional priors under severe class imbalance.

Across architectures, Swin-DPL provided the strongest baseline among pure Transformers, but still showed wide seed-dependent variability in Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) and Macro-F1. Bootstrapping revealed that global confidence intervals did not widen substantially with increased task complexity, indicating that bootstrap variance is primarily driven by sample size, not optimization difficulty. However, per-class nCV exposed large instability for svPPA and nfvPPA (nCV \(>\) 0.1), demonstrating that global metrics mask subtype-specific volatility.

Applying stabilization strategies showed that data-centric components dominate performance gains. Effectively, these strategies act as a surrogate for inductive bias, forcing the model to learn anatomical invariances (e.g., through aggressive augmentation) that are otherwise absent in the pure Transformer architecture. MRI-specific 3D augmentation produced the only consistent improvement in both in-domain and out-of-domain discrimination metrics. EMA and label smoothing improved convergence but often worsened calibration (higher ECE/Brier), emphasizing a calibration vs.\ discrimination trade-off. Balanced sampling and MixUp contributed modest average improvements yet were necessary to prevent boundary collapse on rare PPA variants.

The full stabilization protocol (3D augmentation + EMA + label smoothing + balanced sampling + MixUp) substantially reduced inter-seed variance and improved MCC and Macro-F1. For Swin-DPL, this protocol narrowed the gap with the ResNet-18 baseline in-domain, and improved OOD Macro-F1 without modifying backbone architecture. Wilcoxon and McNemar tests confirmed that several of these improvements reached statistical significance, whereas intermediate variants (e.g., augmentation+EMA) produced numerical gains that failed to exceed stochastic variability.

Inference-time analyses showed diverging effects. Snapshot ensembling (top-\(K\) checkpoints) consistently reduced variance across all metrics and provided the most stable OOD performance. Temperature scaling improved calibration without altering discrimination, reducing OOD Brier scores.

In contrast, Test-Time Augmentation (TTA) proved unreliable: while mildly improving in-domain stability, it degraded OOD discrimination and increased inter-seed variability, indicating poor alignment between TTA transforms and external domain characteristics.

Architectural stabilization strategies commonly used in large-scale vision models were largely ineffective. Post-LN normalization and SAM severely degraded convergence, while LayerScale, stable initialization, and ShakeDrop produced no significant improvement. This suggests that the dominant factors limiting reliability are optimization noise and data scarcity rather than architectural deficiencies.

Overall, the results demonstrate that (1) instability grows sharply for FTD subtypes even when global metrics appear stable, (2) Transformer performance is highly sensitive to seed-dependent noise unless explicitly stabilized, and (3) targeted optimization and augmentation protocols enable hierarchical Transformers to recover robustness approaching CNN baselines, particularly under OOD distribution shifts.

Conclusion

This study provides a systematic assessment of the stability and robustness of Vision Transformers for MRI-based differential diagnosis of neurodegenerative diseases. While Transformer architectures are increasingly adopted for their capacity to model long-range structural dependencies and their architectural flexibility for multimodal integration, our analyses reveal that their performance in realistic clinical settings is strongly constrained by optimization instability, class imbalance, and limited sample sizes, particularly when distinguishing FTD subtypes with overlapping atrophy patterns.

Across all architectures, pure and hierarchical ViTs exhibited the highest seed-dependent variability, with frequent degradation on minority classes despite strong average accuracy. In contrast, CNNs and anatomy-driven models remained comparatively stable, underscoring the value of spatial inductive biases in low-sample and heterogeneous environments. Crucially, instability was not adequately captured by aggregate metrics or global bootstrap confidence intervals; fine-grained nCV analysis demonstrated that rare PPA phenotypes systematically amplified variance even when global performance appeared stable.

The stabilization experiments show that domain-specific data augmentation and optimization smoothing dominate the reliability gains. A targeted protocol combining extensive 3D augmentation, EMA, label smoothing, balanced sampling, and MixUp significantly reduced variance and improved the consistency of Swin-based models across seeds and domain shifts. These improvements were validated statistically using paired tests and the probability of false outperformance, confirming that several observed gains exceeded stochastic variability. Inference-time ensembling further strengthened robustness, whereas standard test-time augmentation unexpectedly degraded OOD performance, highlighting that inference strategies must be carefully matched to domain characteristics.

Overall, the findings indicate that Transformer-based classifiers can approach or match the robustness of strong CNN baselines when equipped with an appropriate stabilization protocol, even without architectural modifications or pretraining. However, this requires explicit variance control, uncertainty-aware evaluation, and calibration monitoring, elements often absent from standard reporting practices.

Looking forward, two directions appear most promising. First, large-scale self-supervised pretraining on multi-site MRI datasets may inject inductive biases currently missing from pure Transformers, potentially reducing their sensitivity to initialization and sampling noise. Second, incorporating non-imaging clinical covariates into multimodal Transformer frameworks may help resolve ambiguous phenotypes where MRI alone is insufficient, particularly within the PPA spectrum.

In summary, deploying Transformers for clinical neuroimaging requires more than architectural innovation: robust optimization, variance reduction, and uncertainty quantification are essential for producing reliable and reproducible models in differential diagnosis tasks.
