Introduction

The white matter (WM) consists of bundles of myelinated axons that transmit spiking activity from neuronal cell bodies to axon terminals in distant brain regions, and thus plays a critical role in brain function. Despite its importance, our understanding of the WM is largely based on structural connectivity or microstructural properties, while its functional physiology remains poorly characterized. Recent findings show that the blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) signal is detectable in WM [1], opening new opportunities to study WM physiology. However, the BOLD signal depends on multiple physiological factors—such as the orientation of fibres relative to the main magnetic field (θ) and cerebral blood volume (CBV)—which makes the hemodynamic response in WM difficult to interpret. In addition, the development of apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC)–based fMRI, which measures diffusivity changes suggested to reflect neuronal swelling associated with activity [2], offers a complementary means to probe WM physiology. Nevertheless, how this hypothetical neuronal swelling interacts with vascular-genic signals—and how these interactions manifest in both BOLD-fMRI and ADC-fMRI—remains unclear. In this study, we leverage a white-matter simulation framework to disentangle how different physiological factors contribute to both ADC-based and BOLD-based fMRI, providing new insights into the functional physiology of WM.

Methods

We performed all simulations by building on our recently published BOLDSwimSuite Toolbox [3], adding white-matter–specific modules. This involves incorporating axon packing (using the AxonPacking package) and adopting WM biophysical parameters. To model the WM environment, the ADC for blood and fibres was set to 0.001 (intravascular default) and 0.0005 mm2/s [3], respectively, and the magnetic susceptibility for blood and fibres was set to –1.5 × 10-8 [4] and 3 × 10-8 cgs [5], respectively. The simulation pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 1. A 0.3-mm isotropic 2D voxel containing 5000 fibres was used to generate the WM fMRI signal. Fibers were randomly assigned to the “vessel” class to achieve the desired CBV, with their orientations either parallel to the fibres or randomly distributed. A spin-echo (SE) sequence with TE = 70 ms was used, with an analytical approach to compute the local magnetic field offset and a Monte Carlo simulation with 600 steps to model diffusion.

Simulations were performed for the scenarios summarized below, with fixed parameters specified in each subplot in Fig. 2. Both SE and GRE signals are specifically stated. The tested physiological factors included:

Fibre and vascular orientation effects

  • θ from 0 to π/2 in steps of π/128

Fibre-genic effects

  • Fibre susceptibility from –10% to 10% in steps of 0.5%

  • Total ADC from –10% to 10% in steps of 0.5%

Vascular-genic effects

  • Fraction of parallel vessels (parallel relative to fibre direction) from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.01

  • Vascular susceptibility from –10% to 10% in steps of 0.5%

  • CBV from 0 to 0.04 in steps of 0.001

To further evaluate potential interactions between fibre- and vascular-genic effects during neural activity, we examined two combinations of determinants of the BOLD signal: vascular susceptibility (±10%) plus fibre ADC (±10%), and vascular susceptibility (±10%) plus fibre susceptibility (±10%) (approximating fibre size changes). SE (sampled at 70 ms) and GRE (sampled at 35 ms) signal changes were plotted as percentages change relative to baseline (0% change).

To assess how distinguishable the SE BOLD signal decays are when generated from different physiological variables, we computed a customized similarity metric (equation shown in Fig. 2), combining correlation (signal shape) and root mean standard error (RMSE, signal magnitude). Similarity against a reference signal was evaluated across all physiological factors listed above under four temporal SNR levels (assuming Gaussian noise), where the reference signal was generated with all default parameters (zero offset) : noise-free, tSNR = 20, tSNR = 10, and tSNR = 5 (MATLAB, awgn).

To evaluate whether this framework can also be used to simulate ADC-fMRI, we added diffusion weighting to the SE signal with diffusion gradients (X and Y directions). For the single-refocused sequence, we used an SE sequence with TE = 64 ms and 1200 Monte Carlo steps. For the twice-refocused sequence [5], we used pulse timings of [0, 32, 92] ms and a longer simulation of 2000 steps.

Results

The shape of the SE signal evolution changes markedly with different fibre orientations (θ), with the signal peaking around π/4. The GRE signal is higher at θ = π/2 than at θ = 0, whereas the SE signal is higher at θ = 0 than at θ = π/2 (Fig. 2a).

Both GRE and SE BOLD signals increase with increases in fibre susceptibility and ADC, with susceptibility (1.7% per 10%) exerting a larger effect than ADC (0.6% per 10%) (Fig. 2b–c).

When fibres are oriented at π/2 relative to the main magnetic field, the signal is higher for fewer vessels aligned parallel to the fibres (3.5% higher BOLD signal for fully randomly oriented vessels compared with fully parallel vessels) (Fig. 2d). The signal also increases with decreases in vascular susceptibility (0.6% per 10%, Fig. 2e). Lower CBV results in higher signals for both GRE (11% higher for CBV = 0 than CBV = 0.04) and SE (8% higher for CBV = 0 than CBV = 0.04) (Fig. 2f).

Joint-effect analyses indicate that, for the vascular susceptibility plus fibre susceptibility scenario, the SE and GRE BOLD signal variations range is approximately –3% to 3% for both SE and GRE, with fibre susceptibility exerting the larger influence (Fig. 2g–h). For the vascular susceptibility plus fibre ADC scenario, the SE and GRE BOLD signal variations range is –1% to 1% for both SE and GRE, with vascular susceptibility having the stronger effect, though the ADC effect remains notable (roughly half the effect of vascular susceptibility) (Fig. 2i–j).

The similarity analysis suggests that in a noise-free scenario, all physiological factors can be distinguished using a simple spin-echo curve (Fig. 2k). With added noise, factors remain distinguishable down to tSNR = 10 (Fig. 2l–m), with the clearest separation for θ and CBV effects. At tSNR = 5, signals from different θ values are no longer distinguishable; however, the CBV effect remains robust (Fig. 2n).

Conclusion

To better understand WM function, it is essential to investigate its physiological mechanisms in addition to structure and microstructure. In this study, we applied a biophysical framework for forward modeling of the fMRI signal to examine how the fMRI signal can be influenced by various aspects of WM physiology. We found that all physiological factors influence both the shape and intensity of the fMRI signal. Moreover, this signal does not entirely represent a hemodynamic response, as the fibre-genic signal also has a strong impact on typically measured GRE-BOLD responses; for example, a 10% increase in ADC can lead to an increase in the BOLD responses from 0.5% to 1%. These findings may help explain the complex hemodynamic responses in WM [1], reflecting a combination of ADC-related effects [2] and conventional BOLD hemodynamics responses.

Furthermore, our results demonstrate that BOLD signals corresponding to different physiological factors can be distinguished based on their spin-echo decay features, providing grounds for feasibility of physiological fingerprinting in WM. Among the factors tested, orientation and CBV effects were the most robustly distinguishable, likely due in part to their relatively larger dynamic ranges in the WM. The framework presented here not only enhances our understanding of emerging WM fMRI signals (BOLD- or ADC-based) but also provides a pathway to map WM physiological mechanisms, with broad clinical potential for WM-related diseases.
