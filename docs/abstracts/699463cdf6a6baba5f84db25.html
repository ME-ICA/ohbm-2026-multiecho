Introduction

Epilepsy affects over 50 million people worldwide, with Temporal Lobe Epilepsy (TLE) being the most common drug-resistant adult form.1 Patients often experience reduced quality of life and cognitive functioning due to recurring, unprovoked seizures. While surgical resection can be effective, about one-third of patients are MRI-negative, complicating seizure-focus localization and surgical planning. Detecting subtle hippocampal abnormalities is challenging on conventional 3T MRI, where limited spatial resolution and tissue contrast hinder precise delineation of subfields, each with distinct vulnerability patterns in TLE. Higher-field MRI improves anatomical detail and supports accurate localization of seizure focus, critical for prognosis and surgical outcome. 

Advanced diffusion MRI (dMRI) parameters, such as Microscopic Fractional Anisotropy (µFA), capture intracellular microstructural integrity independent of fibre orientation, unlike standard diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics.2 µFA cannot be reliably derived from standard dMRI sequences, but b-tensor encoding enables its estimation by encoding diffusion along multiple directions simultaneously. Because of this, µFA is thought to increase sensitivity to neurite density, an advantage given the complex micro-architecture of hippocampal subfields. However, partial volume effects are common in regions near ventricles, such as the hippocampus, which can bias µFA. Applying a Free Water Elimination (FWE) technique can likely improve tissue microstructure specificity.2 

In this study, we combine µFA with FWE using ultra-high-field 7T MRI, improving signal-to-noise ratio and delineation of hippocampal anatomy. A custom spiral dMRI acquisition further reduces geometric distortions and signal dropout. Using these optimized methods, we examine if FWE-corrected µFA can detect lateralized and subfield-specific microstructural abnormalities in TLE, identifying potential biomarkers to support clinical decision-making. 

Methods

Participants

We recruited 26 TLE patients (13 unilateral left (L-TLE), 6 unilateral right (R-TLE), 7 bilateral (B-TLE); 21 male, 5 female, mean age 34.6 ± 9.7 years) and 20 age- and sex- matched healthy controls (15 male, 5 female; mean age 32.2 ± 6.7 years). All procedures were approved by the Health Sciences Research Ethics Board at Western University, and scans were conducted at the Centre for Functional and Metabolic Mapping using the 7T Siemens Magnetom head-only MRI system. 

MRI Acquisition

We acquired T1-weighted (T1w) anatomical images using a Magnetization-Prepared 2 Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echoes (MP2RAGE) sequence with repetition time (TR) = 6000ms, echo time (TE) = 2.27ms, inversion times (TI 1 and TI 2) = 860ms, 2700ms, and voxel size = 0.7mm³. 

dMRI was acquired using a custom spiral trajectory b-tensor encoded sequence incorporating both linear tensor encoding (LTE) and spherical tensor encoding (STE); (TR= 6500ms, TE=82ms, voxel size = 1.5mm³, slice acceleration = 2).3 LTE, the standard encoding in dMRI used to derive DTI metrics such as fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD), included: (b=150/1000/2000 s/mm², with 6/26/26 diffusion directions). STE, which is required to support robust estimation of µFA, included: (b=2000s/mm² with 30 repetitions). The protocol includes 10 interleaved b0 volumes.

Image Processing

Raw diffusion data underwent the DESIGNER preprocessing pipeline, which included denoising, Rician bias correction, Gibbs ringing correction, motion correction, and distortion mitigation. µFA maps, with and without FWE, were computed using MatMRI. Hippocampal subfields, subiculum, CA1-CA4, dentate gyrus (DG), were automatically segmented from the T1w images using HippUnfold, after which hippocampal volumes were extracted. 

Registration 

Mean b0 images were rigidly registered for each participant’s skull-stripped T1w anatomical image using ANTs with a Mattes mutual information metric, linear interpolation, and a multi-resolution optimization (200 x 140 x 50 iterations). The resulting affine transform was then applied to all other diffusion-derived maps, aligning them to the T1w space for subfield-level analysis. DTI metrics and µFA values were extracted for each participant across both the left and right hemispheres, and by hippocampal subfield, using MatMRI.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python at both the whole-hippocampus and hippocampal subfield levels. Tests included paired t-tests (left vs right hemisphere), independent t-tests (TLE vs controls), and subgroup comparisons (L-TLE, R-TLE, B-TLE). All p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR), with significance thresholds set at p<0.05 (*), p<0.01 (**), and p<0.001 (***).

Results

Across the hippocampal formation, TLE patients expressed reduced subfield volumes relative to healthy controls, with the greatest reductions in L-TLE patients. Significant reductions were observed in the CA1 (791.38 ± 234.18 mm³ vs 957.83 ± 108.34 mm³, **), DG 114.77 ± 42.71 mm³ vs 137.13 ± 14.04 mm³, *), and subiculum (447.40 ± 163.35 mm³ vs 537.26 ± 60.64 mm³, *). 

To determine if microstructural alterations accompanied these volumetric differences, we first evaluated µFA with and without FWE. When combining all participants (controls and TLE), the FWE increased mean µFA values by 12.0% across subfields (0.563 ± 0.082 to 0.630 ± 0.090, ***), consistent with the removal of extracellular free-water contamination. Thus, all subsequent analyses used FWE-corrected µFA.

Across both hemispheres, all TLE lateralization groups displayed lower µFA values compared to healthy controls, indicating global microstructural abnormalities in epilepsy. Subfield-level analyses revealed reduced µFA across nearly all hippocampal subregions, supporting diffuse microstructural involvement in TLE, regardless of seizure-onset lateralization.

L-TLE patients displayed the most extensive reductions in µFA across the hippocampal formation. Significant decreases were observed in CA1-CA4 and DG regions, with the largest effect sizes in the CA1 (0.612 ± 0.066 vs 0.657 ± 0.040, **), CA4 (0.592 ± 0.079 vs 0.654 ± 0.035, **), and DG (0.593 ± 0.073 vs 0.651 ± 0.030, **), while the subiculum remained relatively preserved. B-TLE patients had significant decreases in the CA3 (0.615 ± 0.043 vs 0.652 ± 0.033, *), CA4 (0.608 ± 0.062 vs 0.654 ± 0.035, *) and DG (0.613 ± 0.050 vs 0.651 ± 0.030, *). R-TLE patients showed decreased µFA across the hippocampal subfields; however, with no significance. 

When examining the left hippocampus alone, only the L-TLE patients exhibited pronounced reductions in the CA1 (0.613 ± 0.088 vs 0.682 ± 0.028, *), CA3 (0.620 ± 0.067 vs 0.670 ± 0.026, *), CA4 (0.589 ± 0.107 vs 0.670 ± 0.036, *), and DG (0.590 ± 0.099 vs 0.669 ± 0.023, *), again highlighting a pronounced left-hemispheric vulnerability, mirroring the volumetric findings. Examining the right hippocampus revealed reductions for the L-TLE and B-TLE groups in the CA3 (0.605 ± 0.035, *; 0.599 ± 0.020, ** vs 0.633 ± 0.029), CA4 (0.596 ± 0.037, **; 0.590 ± 0.022, ** vs 0.638 ± 0.024), and DG (0.597 ± 0.032, ** ; 0.592 ± 0.023, ** vs 0.633 ± 0.026). 

Overall, these findings demonstrate lateralization-dependent subfield microstructural abnormalities, with L-TLE displaying the most extensive abnormalities, B-TLE exhibiting selective involvement across both hemispheres, and R-TLE showing partial and milder effects.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the value of combining advanced image acquisition techniques, such as high-field 7T MRI with a custom spiral diffusion sequence and b-tensor encoding, to better characterize hippocampal microstructure. Applying the FWE technique allows mitigation of partial-volume contamination from CSF, particularly in hippocampal subfields that border the temporal horn and nearby ventricles. Group differences in µFA remained significant even without FWE, indicating that observed microstructural abnormalities reflect true tissue alterations rather than extracellular free-water. Notably, µFA revealed larger and more consistent differences than hippocampal volume measures, underscoring its sensitivity to subtle subfield-specific pathology, while complementing volumetric findings.

Across the full hippocampus, L-TLE patients showed the most pronounced reductions in µFA, B-TLE exhibited selective subfield involvement, and R-TLE displayed milder, non-significant reductions. Hemisphere-specific analyses confirmed widespread deficits in the left hippocampus for L-TLE and reductions in CA3, CA4, and DG for L-TLE and B-TLE in the right hippocampus. Together, these findings indicate that combined volumetric and µFA analyses provide a sensitive framework for detecting subfield- and hemisphere-specific pathology in TLE.

These observed dMRI findings align with histopathological reports demonstrating axonal degeneration, with potential contributions from neuron loss and gliosis across hippocampal subfields in TLE. Importantly, prior post-surgical tissue studies have shown that these cellular changes do not occur uniformly, but follow subfield- and hemisphere-dependent gradients that reflect seizure lateralization and disease burden.4 Notably, CA1, CA3, and CA4 are the most common sites of cell loss in hippocampal sclerosis, which was reflected by the µFA findings.4

Overall, µFA at high-field 7T MRI combined with FWE represents a promising non-invasive biomarker for detecting subtle hippocampal pathology in MRI-negative or structurally ambiguous cases of TLE. The ability to reveal lateralization-specific differences suggests potential clinical utility in presurgical evaluation. There is also potential to determine hippocampal sclerosis subtypes in vivo, which are based on the subfield-specific pattern of cellular changes as assessed by histopathology.5 Such characterization could play a role in the development of subtype-specific precision treatments, and highlight the potential of ultra-high resolution diffusion imaging for probing microstructural alterations within the complex architecture of the human hippocampus.
