Introduction

Functional connectomes – graphs of region-to-region co-activation – are assessed from the statistical dependence of fMRI time series and are typically quantified as the Pearson correlation of the BOLD timeseries. However, the Pearson correlation between two regions is confounded by unmodeled influences of other regions and prone to false-positives. Furthermore, connectomes constructed from Pearson correlation are fully connected and undirected, and are known to have high correlation with motion1. The density and symmetricity of Pearson correlation connectomes challenge neuroscientific principles of brain connectivity. The scientific and clinical value of analyses using functional connectivity relies on having valid representations of the connectome that are statistically rigorous, related to outcomes of interest, and free from confounds. Common goals of functional connectivity analyses include the ability to identify individual participants based on their functional connectivity and the similarity of structural and functional connectivity. 

An alternative method for calculating the functional connectivity between brain regions is to explicitly model connectivity as a regularized multivariate linear model predicting the timeseries of a target region from all other regions. The model weights from the prediction of each timeseries form the edge weights in a functional connectome. One important advantage of an explicit model is the ability to quantify the accuracy of these models using held out timeseries. Another advantage is the ability to identify sparsity when using sparsity-promoting regularizers to fit the models. Because the sparsity is rigorously included in the optimization, such models also avoid the need for ad hoc thresholds to generate graphs for subsequent analysis. Here, we introduce a framework for fitting and evaluating model-based connectomes, demonstrating their accuracy, sparsity and validity. 

Methods

The Human Connectome Project - Young Adult (HCP-YA) 1200 participant release minimally pre-processed (“MinProc”) resting state functional MRI data was parcellated using the Schaefer 100 atlas for 100 randomly selected participants (Glasser et al. 2013). This data was also post-processed using XCP-D to evaluate the robustness of these analyses across processing decisions2. In XCP-D, data was despiked; a 36-parameter set of confounds related to head motion, white matter signal, cerebrospinal fluid signal and global signal were regressed out; a bandpass filter was applied (0.01-0.08 Hz). The HCP-YA diffusion data was processed using QSIRecon to generate structural connectomes from SIFT2-weighted streamline counts in between regions3. 

For each participant, the rows of a connectome were populated by either Pearson correlations or with linear model coefficients obtained by regressing the time-series of the corresponding region of interest (ROI) against time-series of all other ROIs. Linear models were fit to the data using two regularization methods–Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)4, and the Union of Intersections (“UoI”) framework5. LASSO fits an ordinary least squares model and adds an additional penalty of the sum of the absolute value of model weights. This results in weights of zero for non-informative features, essentially dropping that feature from the model. Union of Intersections performs model selection by fitting models to bootstrapped resamples of the data using a range of LASSO penalties. Features that have model weights that are non-zero across all bootstraps for each LASSO penalty are selected to fit to bootstrap resamples of the data. The best fitting feature set (as determined by the Bayesian Information Criterion; BIC) for each bootstrap is then averaged together to form the final model. In general, UoI out-performs LASSO in terms of lower false-positive and false-negative edge detection, as well as reduced bias and variance in the resulting edge weights5.

For LASSO and UoI models, model accuracy was evaluated using cross-validation between sessions: the model was fit in one session and then used to predict the timeseries in the other session. The best fit model was chosen using the BIC in the training data/session, and accuracy was quantified using cross-validated Coefficient of Determination (R2) in each region of interest and each subject in a held-out test session. This procedure was repeated with each session serving as train and test data. 

Sparsity was evaluated by taking a ratio of non-zero edges over the total possible edges. Test-retest reliability was evaluated with the image intraclass coefficient (“I2C2”) between sessions. Correlation with motion (“QCFC”) was evaluated by correlating the session level connectome edges with session level root mean squared head motion, with age and sex included as covariates. Structure function coupling (“SFC”) was calculated by correlating participant-level functional connectivity matrices – in this case on data concatenated from both sessions – with structural connectivity matrices derived from tractography data.  Participant identification (“fingerprinting”) was calculated by correlating a target participant’s session-level connectome with connectomes of every participant and their own connectome from the other session. Correct identification is recorded when the highest correlated connectome is from the target participant. Confidence intervals were determined by bootstrapping. These comparisons were done between connectomes calculated with LASSO, UoI and Pearson correlations, except for sparsity and accuracy which can not be assessed for Pearson correlation connectomes. 

Results

LASSO and UoI models show similar timeseries accuracy when comparing the average accuracy for all nodes across all participants (XCP-D: LASSO R2 =0.49, UoI R2=0.46; MinProc: LASSO R2=0.60, UoI R2 = 0.58). Despite this, MinProc connectomes are substantially sparser than XCP-D connectomes, with a lower selection ratio than MinProc for both models (XCP-D: UoI = 0.70, LASSO = 0.86; MinProc: UoI = 0.32 and LASSO = 0.43; Fig 2a). In addition, these results indicate that UoI is able to capture the same amount of variance with fewer features regardless of processing pipeline. Model accuracy has a distinct spatial pattern, with low accuracies particularly around regions with air-tissue interfaces (Fig 2b), suggesting a direct relationship between data SNR and model accuracy that can be achieved. Supporting this hypothesis, there is a large correlation between the average accuracy per node and tSNR per node from the minimally pre-processed data (XCPD: UoI r = 0.73, LASSO r = 0.73; MinProc: UoI r = 0.79, LASSO r = 0.79; Fig 2c ). 

Processing type has a large influence on the reliability of model-based connectomes, but no impact on the reliability of Pearson-based connectomes (XCPD: LASSO I2C2 = 0.09 [0.08, 0.10], UoI I2C2 = 0.08 [0.07, 0.10], Pearson I2C2 = 0.45 [0.51, 0.48]; MinProc: LASSO I2C2 = 0.35 [0.32, 0.38], UoI I2C2 = 0.28 [0.24, 0.31], Pearson I2C2 = 0.44 [0.35, 0.52]; Fig 2d). While Pearson-based connectomes are reliable regardless of processing pipeline, model-based connectomes are far more reliable when used with MinProc than with XCP-D. 

Across processing pipelines, model-based connectomes are more correlated with structural connectomes than Pearson-based connectomes (Fig 2e). Processing pipeline made little difference for this metric (XCP-D: UoI r = 0.52, LASSO r = 0.52, Pearson r = 0.39; MinProc: UoI r = 0.57, LASSO: r = 0.57, Pearson r = 0.36). 

After processing data with XCP-D there is minimal difference between the connectome types and the number edges significantly correlated with motion (UoI: 12.84%, LASSO: 13.0%, Pearson: 15.48%). However, there is a three-fold increase in the number of edges that are significantly correlated with motion for Pearson connectomes as compared to model-based connectomes when the MinProc data is used (UoI: 9.46%, LASSO: 9.34%, Pearson: 33.32%).

There is no difference in the fingerprinting accuracy between the connectome types for XCP-D processed data, with overlapping 95% confidence intervals (LASSO: 0.67 [0.59, 0.75], UoI: 0.69 [0.61, 77], Pearson: 0.78 [0.71, 0.86]). In contrast, for MinProc Pearson connectomes have an accuracy of almost 30% less than the model-based alternatives (LASSO: 0.88 [0.82, 0.92], UoI: 0.85 [0.79, 0.91], Pearson: 0.53 [0.43, 0.61 ]; Fig 2f). 

Conclusion

To summarize, model-based connectomes present a new approach to analysis of functional connectivity from MRI data. While many previous approaches are qualitative in nature, model-based approaches provide an opportunity to quantify the accuracy of a connectome with respect to the data that was measured (e.g., with BIC), and an opportunity to validate this accuracy on held-out data (e.g., in a second scan or second session). Meanwhile, model-based connectomes also retain many of the psychometric properties and empirical findings that are considered strengths of the Pearson-based connectomes: high fingerprinting accuracy, higher structure-function accuracy, and lower sensitivity to motion-related artifacts. Moreover, model-based connectomes are statistically directed, providing new opportunities for modeling the properties of the connectome as a dynamical system that are not accessible in the undirected Pearson-based connectomes. We also found that model-based connectomes are overall less reliable than Pearson-based connectomes, using multivariate reliability measurements. While this may seem like a drawback at first glance, a model of the connectome may also  be consistently inaccurate. Indeed, finger-printing accuracy is higher with model-based connectomes, suggesting that they capture meaningful individual variability that is not well-captured by Pearson connectomes. 

 All common methods for calculating functional connectivity have drawbacks that impede the inferences we are capable of drawing from analyses that use those connectomes. In particular, as with many human brain mapping methods, there is a challenge of analytic flexibility, where choice of analysis pipeline affects inferences made based on the data. Model-based connectomes are explicit about how connectivity is estimated, and are able to be evaluated for validity quantified by cross-validated accuracy on held out timeseries. This also serves as a solid basis to improve these models by incorporating more information about the measurements (e.g., from structural data, hemodynamics, etc.).
