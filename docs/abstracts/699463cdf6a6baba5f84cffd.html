Introduction

Pain relief is associated with appetitive reward, while incipient pain elicits a negative emotional response, suggesting an overlap between pain processing and reward systems. Moreover, individuals with depression exhibit dysfunctional reward processing, with anhedonia as a core symptom. Within the reward system, the Nucleus Accumbens (NAcc) and Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) are key subcortical regions involved in reward valuation and motivation1,2. Altered connectivity of these regions has been reported in pain conditions3and in anhedonia4, a core symptom of depression. Although some studies have investigated reward system alterations in chronic pain, research specifically focusing on patients with fibromyalgia (FM), particularly in direct comparison to major depressive disorder (MDD), remains limited.  Our study aims to fill this gap by directly comparing women with fibromyalgia and women with major depression disorder, examining shared and disorder-specific resting-state connectivity alterations of the NAcc and VTA.

Methods

We included 66 women with fibromyalgia, 33 patients with major depressive disorder and 52 age-matched healthy controls. Participants completed standardized clinical assessments and underwent resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). For data preprocessing and denoising we used the CONN-toolbox following the default pipeline. Seed-based functional connectivity analyses targeted the NAcc and the VTA as regions of interest (ROI). Post-hoc analyses examined (1) the association between functional connectivity patterns and participant movement, quantified via AX3 accelerometer data, and (2) the relationship between connectivity alterations and affective symptom profiles. To examine affective symptom-related effects, a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using scores from the PHQ-9, the RRS–Depression subscale, and the PANAS Negative Affect subscale. The first principal component was extracted and used to correlate with the neuroimaging data.

Results

For the Nucleus Accumbens seed, both fibromyalgia patients and patients with major depressive disorder showed increased functional connectivity with the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC)–precuneus compared to healthy controls (pFWE-cluster < 0.05, whole-brain; p-voxel < 0.001).        
 For the Ventral Tegmental Area seed, patients with depression exhibited increased connectivity with the left postcentral gyrus (S1) relative to fibromyalgia patients (pFWE-cluster < 0.05, whole-brain; p-voxel < 0.001).          
Higher severity of negative affect symptoms was associated with increased NAcc–left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (left dlPFC) connectivity across patients with fibromyalgia and major depressive disorder, statistically controlling for group, showing a trend at the cluster level (pFWE = 0.065; whole-brain; p-voxel < 0.001). No significant associations were observed for the VTA seed.          
Regarding movement behaviour, we observed a negative correlation between VTA–S1 connectivity and daily step count (r = –0.52, p = 0.02), present only in the major depressive disorder group. 

Conclusion

Our study reveals a shared brain connectivity alteration in both women with fibromyalgia and women with major depression characterized by increased connectivity between the NAcc and the PCC, a core region of the default mode network, involved in self-referential processing and  mentalizing. Although PCC connectivity was not associated with clinical symptoms, NAcc connectivity with the left dlPFC showed a trend-level association with higher negative-affect severity in patients.  This finding aligns with evidence that dlPFC alterations contribute to maladaptive negative thinking in depression, including reduced BOLD-signal variability5.         

In contrast, we also identified disorder-specific connectivity alterations: patients with major depressive disorder showed increased functional connectivity between the VTA and somatosensory–motor regions relative to fibromyalgia patients. Notably, this alteration was was correlated with lower daily step counts within the MDD group.
