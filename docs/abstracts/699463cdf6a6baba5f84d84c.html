Introduction

Non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs), including exercise, cognitive training, and combined or multimodal interventions, reliably slow cognitive decline and reduce dementia risk in older adults.1 Despite distinct theoretical foundations, diverse NPIs consistently produce similar global cognitive gains and far-transfer effects, suggesting shared neural mechanisms. However, existing accounts remain siloed, region-focused, and modality-specific, failing to explain this convergence.

Recent neuroimaging evidence links age-related cognitive decline to large-scale network dedifferentiation and disrupted functional organization. From this perspective, NPIs may act by re-differentiating whole-brain information-processing organization, restoring differentiated and efficient system-level interactions. Intrinsic neural timescales, the INT thus provides a principled index of such organization, capturing a cortical hierarchy in which short timescales support sensory encoding and segregation, while long timescales enable integration.2

However, conventional static INT measures reflect only average organization and may obscure dynamic shifts in regional roles and inter-regional collaboration.3 To overcome this limitation, we computed dynamic INT (dINT) as time series of ACWdecay values using short sliding windows. We then constructed a collaborative network by correlating regional dINT, indexing whether regions operate synchronously at similar intrinsic timescales or independently. This dINT-based framework captures time-varying collaboration of whole-brain information-processing. This approach allows us to test: whether aging- and intervention-related cognitive changes are mediated by reconfiguration of large-scale information-processing organization; whether distinct NPIs converge on shared network-level mechanisms; and whether such functional organization changes serve as mechanistically interpretable neuromarkers of cognitive gains in older adults.

Methods

Participants and study design

We pooled data from four registered NPIs to examine shared effects on large-scale brain information-processing organization. Older adults completing structured NPIs with usable MRI data were assigned to the Training Group (TG), while control-arm participants were assigned to the Control Group (CG). All underwent baseline and post-intervention cognitive assessments and resting-state MRI. After exclusions, the final sample included 171 community-dwelling adults aged 53–80 years (112 training, 59 control; Chinese Clinical Trial Registry). Inclusion ensured normal cognition, intact daily function, and absence of neurological or psychiatric disorders.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

T1-weighted and resting-state fMRI data were acquired on 3T scanners with consistent parameters. Pre-processing included motion and distortion correction, anatomical registration, surface projection, nuisance regression, temporal filtering, spatial smoothing, and frame scrubbing. Parcel-wise BOLD time series were extracted using a Schaefer 400-parcel atlas, which were mapped into 7 networks.4 Participants with insufficient usable data were excluded; mean framewise displacement (FD) was retained as a covariate.

Dynamic intrinsic neural timescales and the cortical manifold of collaborative network

For each parcel, BOLD time series were segmented into 30-TR (60 seconds) sliding windows. Autocorrelation functions were estimated in each window and summarized as ACWdecay values, producing a ACWdecay series representing dINT. Larger ACWdecay indicates integrative processing; smaller ACWdecay indicates fast, sensory-driven processing. Inter-parcel correlations of dINT time series produced 400 × 400 collaborative network for each participant, Fisher z-transformed and harmonized to reduce site effects. Group-level networks were obtained by averaging participant matrices.

Dimensionality reduction of group-level networks yielded cortical manifolds. The first two principal components (PC1, PC2) captured dominant gradients of functional organization, embedding each parcel as a coordinate. Pre- and post-intervention manifolds were compared to quantify hierarchical reorganization. The workflow has been shown in Figure 1.

Group- and subgroup-level (robustness) analyses

Parcel-level cosine dissimilarity quantified shifts in relative functional role; Euclidean distance captured specialization changes. TG vs. CG comparisons identified intervention-specific reorganization. In robustness analysis, within TG, participants were stratified by cognitive benefit (ΔMoCA > 0 vs. ≤0) to distinguish reorganization linked to cognitive gains from non-specific adaptation. Subgroup manifolds and parcel shifts were compared to isolate effective networks.

Network-level metrics

Global brain chronocoherence (GBC) was computed from collaborative networks (sum of the z-values within or between networks). Within-network GBC indexed temporal alignment and functional specialization; between-network GBC captured cross-network integration. Changes in GBC across groups/subgroups identified network-level reorganization.

Associations with cognition

Linear mixed-effects models tested associations between GBC and MoCA across all participants and timepoints. Within- or between-network GBC, age, sex, education, and head motion were fixed effects; participant was a random effect to account for repeated measures. Models were run for all networks to identify organization ~ cognition relationships.

Results

Re-differentiation of functional organization

Comparing the Training Group (TG) with the Control Group (CG), we first observed intervention-specific re-differentiation of large-scale functional organization. At the group level, the variance explained by the first two principal components of the cortical manifold increased from 33.46% to 36.46% in the TG, whereas it decreased in the CG from 31.09% to 28.64%, reflecting aging-related decline. Permutation testing confirmed that the increase in TG’s PC2 was significant (p = 0.001), indicating that NPIs enhance the functional hierarchy of the brain. Network-level contrasts further revealed that the distance between the somato/motor (SMN) and visual networks increased in TG, suggesting enhanced segregation between these sensory networks.

Network-specific mechanisms: functional specialization and role optimization

Intervention effects were network-specific and distinct along two mechanistic dimensions. Euclidean distance highlighted functional specialization, with the visual and SMN networks showing the largest post-intervention changes (one-way ANOVA, F(6, 393) = 29.124, p < 0.0001, partial η² = 0.308), while cosine dissimilarity captured role optimization, most pronounced in the dorsal attention network (DAN) and limbic network (F(6, 393) = 25.210, p < 0.0001, partial η² = 0.278). We also confirmed that sensory-related regions in TG became more “sensory-like,” i.e., functionally segregated from higher-order regions, reflecting specialization. These effects remained significant after controlling for aging-related changes.

Robustness analyses: cognitive benefit stratification

Within TG, participants were stratified by cognitive improvement (ΔMoCA > 0, n = 57) or no benefit (ΔMoCA ≤ 0, n = 40). Functional re-differentiation was evident only in the benefit subgroup: explained variance was larger than no benefit subgroup and their cortical manifold diverged from CG post-intervention, indicating maintenance of hierarchical organization. The no benefit subgroup remained similar to CG2. Network-specific effects were confirmed in the benefit subgroup: functional specialization in the visual and SMN networks, and role optimization in the DAN and limbic networks. Cosine dissimilarity and Euclidean distance analyses showed larger angle shifts in DAN and limbic networks, and larger distance changes in visual and SMN networks (all ps < 0.001). We further revealed that the visual and SMN networks expanded along PC2, forming a wider unimodal axis, while the DAN shifted toward more sensory-processing roles and the limbic network toward integrative transmodal roles.

Network-level collaboration and cognition

At the population level (n = 171), sensory networks displayed primarily within-network synchronized collaboration, whereas associative networks were more hierarchical and showed stronger long-timescale collaboration with the default mode network (DMN). Linear mixed-effects modeling linked network organization to global cognition (MoCA). As shown in Figure 2, Higher within-network collaboration in the DAN showed a trend-level association with better cognition (β = 0.078, 95% CI = -0.007–0.163, p = 0.073), whereas within-network collaboration in visual or SMN networks and between-network collaboration in the limbic network were not significant at this threshold.

Conclusion

In summary, our stepwise analyses revealed a clear chain linking interventions, brain functional organization in information-processing, and global cognition. Non-pharmacological interventions strengthen whole-brain functional organization, as reflected by increased variance along principal components, an effect replicated in the successful intervention subgroup and counteracting aging-related decline. These global changes manifest at the network level: sensory networks become more specialized, the dorsal attention network shifts toward sensory roles, and the limbic network diverges from lower-level processing, reflecting both functional specialization and role optimization. Functionally, within-network collaboration of the dorsal attention network shows the strongest potential to predict global cognition, consistent with previous DAN-cognition relationship study,5 providing a mechanistic link between intervention-induced reorganization of hierarchical information-processing networks and cognitive improvement.
