Introduction

The ability to read is essential in industrialised societies. Some deaf children face challenges in learning to read, due to limited access to spoken or signed language during early development. Most hearing children learn to read by linking speech sounds to letter forms. A key factor in this process is the ability to access and manipulate well-specified, abstract ‘phonological representations’ of spoken language1. This skill is an important predictor of reading in hearing children, but the role that English phonology plays in learning to read in deaf children is less clear2. One way that deaf people might acquire phonological awareness is through visual speech (speechreading/ lipreading). Correlations are frequently observed between speechreading and text reading in deaf children and adults, and hearing children3. This may suggest that visual speech provides a visual source of information about spoken articulations that establishes better specified phonological representations and/or promotes greater awareness of phonological structure that supports reading. We used fMRI and Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) to test this hypothesis by assessing whether visual speech evokes neural representations of phonological structure that are shared across different language forms – specifically auditory speech in hearing adults and text in deaf adults. We predicted that shared representations of phonological structure would be found in the superior and middle temporal cortex (STC/MTC) in both groups, and that better text readers would have neural representations of phonology that were more closely aligned across language forms. 

Methods

Twenty-five right-handed hearing adults with good speechreading ability and twenty-two right-handed severely to profoundly deaf adults with mixed language backgrounds were scanned with fMRI at 3T. Participant’s reading ability was measured outside the scanner using the Vernon-Warden reading test. In the scanner, participants were presented with eight consonant-vowel-consonant words (beam, beat, boom, boot, real, reef, rule, roof) that shared phonological structure and were easily discriminable as visual speech. Hearing and deaf participants were presented with different stimuli types: hearing participants viewed the words as auditory and visual speech (without sound); deaf participants viewed them as dynamic text and visual speech (without sound). Dynamic text was animated letter-by-letter with letters appearing sequentially and earlier letters fading as later ones appeared. The aim of this was to promote a phonological reading strategy. During scanning participants performed an occasional one-back 2AFC task (13% of trials), selecting which of two written words matched the previously presented stimulus.  Each unique stimulus was modelled by a separate regressor, and beta weights were used to compute the cross-validated Mahalanobis (crossnobis) distance between each individual stimulus. Distances were calculated for each word within and across the different stimulus types for each group. A two-step RSA approach was used. First, a whole-brain volumetric searchlight identified brain regions showing reliable non-zero within stimulus type distances (e.g. within auditory and visual speech for the hearing group and within visual speech and text for the deaf group). Second, in these regions, the searchlight distances were averaged across locations to generate a summary RDM for each participant and these averaged distances were correlated with representational models. These models were tested against the within and across stimulus type distances.  Kendall’s Tau-a rank correlations were computed between the neural distances and a Shared Phonemes model, predicting dissimilarity based on the number of shared phonemes.  The coefficients were converted to fisher Z values and submitted to one sample t-tests. In regions that the Shared Phonemes model was a significant fit, we fit two further models: (1) a Sublexical model excluding same-word comparisons and (2) a Lexical model, predicting unique representations for each word.  Evidence for shared sublexical representations of phonological structure was attained by significant fits to the sublexical model across stimulus type (e.g. in the visual speech-auditory speech and the visual speech to text distances respectively for the hearing and deaf groups). Fits to the Shared Phoneme model were correlated with reading performance measured outside the scanner to assess the functional relevance of the abstract neural representations of phonology to reading for each group. 

Results

In the hearing participants, using searchlight RSA, we identified six clusters with reliable non-zero within-stimulus type distances for visual speech and auditory speech. These were located in bilateral STC/MTC, left inferior frontal cortex and bilateral occipitotemporal cortex. However, only patterns in the STC/MTC were a significant fit to the Shared Phonemes model in both the within and across stimulus type distances. We further tested for a fit to the Sublexical model in this region. This model was also a significant fit in the right STC/MTC [peak at 57 -31 5] in the within and across-stimulus distances providing clear evidence of shared sublexical representations of auditory and visual speech in the right STC/MTC. In the left STC/MTC [peak at -54 -10 5], there was no evidence of a strong fit to the Sublexical model, but there was a significant fit to the Lexical model.  For deaf participants, a searchlight analysis identified eight clusters with reliable non-zero within-stimulus type distances for text and visual speech. These spread across left STC/MTC and right MTC and temporoparietal cortex, bilateral inferior frontal cortex and bilateral occipitotemporal cortex. The left STC/MTC cluster [peak at -51 -43 14] was the only region in which there was a significant fit to the Shared Phonemes model in both the within and across-stimulus distances. There was also a fit to the Sublexical model in the across and within stimulus type distances in this region providing clear evidence for shared sublexical representations of visual speech and text. Finally, we tested the functional relevance of these shared neural representations of phonological structure, finding a significant correlation between the fit to the Shared Phonemes model in the across-stimulus distances with reading scores collected outside the scanner in each group in the left and right STC/MTC respectively for the deaf and hearing participants. 

Conclusion

We investigated how visual speech contributes to text reading in deaf and hearing adults by examining whether neural representations of phonology were shared across language forms (auditory, visual speech, and text) and measuring whether the degree of alignment between these representations was related to reading proficiency.  This was indeed the case, providing neurobiological evidence that abstract neural representations of phonology are related to reading proficiency irrespective of hearing status. In hearing participants, we found shared sublexical phonological representations for auditory and visual speech in the right superior/middle temporal cortex (STC/MTC). These results align with recent work showing significant cross-modal phonological similarity for visual and auditory non-word syllables. However, our data go further by showing that these shared sublexical representations are related to reading proficiency. For deaf readers, the role of phonology has been long debated. Previous neuroimaging work has emphasised reduced precision in phonological processing but strong orthographic representations in deaf readers4. Using a different methodological approach - identifying regions where text and visual speech evoke shared phonological codes – we found evidence for shared phonological representations in the left posterior STC/MTC that relate to reading outcomes. Future work should determine the extent to which the use of dynamic text and a linguistically diverse sample of deaf adults contributed to detecting these findings. To summarise, across deaf and hearing adults, we show that reading proficiency was associated with the degree to which phonological representations are shared between visual speech and other language forms. These findings provide neurobiological evidence linking multimodal phonological processing to reading ability and point to the potential of visual speech training to support readers with diverse language experiences.
