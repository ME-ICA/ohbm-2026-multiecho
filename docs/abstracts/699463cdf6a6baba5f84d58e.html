Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterised by cognitive and/or behavioural impairments. Cognitive impairment encompasses persistent difficulties with social communication and interaction, while behavioural impairment encompasses restricted and repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or activities. Diagnosis of ASD is usually a multidisciplinary and time-consuming process, involving mainly neuropsychological assessments and, occasionally, neuroimaging assessments. Consequently, achieving a fast and precise diagnosis remains challenging. Particularly for ASD, neuroimaging is increasingly being adopted to search for atypical connectivity patterns in the human brain, as well as changes in the morphology of brain regions and dysfunctions in brain networks. Additionally, the combination of underconnectivity within certain brain networks and overconnectivity in others is frequently observed in ASD. In this context, the adoption of innovative analyses of intrinsic functional magnetic resonance imaging data (fMRI) is a promising strategy. In particular, the hemodynamic deconvolution may be useful to mitigate the blurring effect of hemodynamic responses, since it is a mathematical approach to recover a neuronal-related signal. Furthermore, unconventional brain connectivity metrics can be adopted to identify more intricate statistical functional dependencies between different brain regions. Finally, the use of machine learning classifiers can be particularly helpful in the automated diagnosis of ASD. In summary, the purpose of this work is to evaluate and compare the usefulness of adopting a combination of hemodynamic deconvolution and unconventional connectivity metrics to distinguish between ASD subjects and typical controls, using resting-state fMRI. To this end, two deconvolution approaches, five unconventional brain connectivity metrics, and nine machine learning classifiers for the automated diagnosis of ASD were considered.

Methods

Raw data were obtained from the publicly available database ABIDE I Preprocessed (Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange),1 which is a compilation of preprocessed resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data (rs-fMRI) obtained from seventeen international institutions. The timeseries for each subject was extracted, and the blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal was calculated for each of the 116 brain regions of interest (ROIs) of the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas. Quality control procedures were performed, mainly to exclude subjects with unfeasible data. The resulting dataset included 346 ASD subjects and 369 typical controls. Subsequently, the following hemodynamic deconvolution approaches were computed:

(a) Blind Deconvolution (BD)2: assumes that the resting-state fMRI BOLD signal is an intrinsic, spontaneous event-related signal. It extracts the corresponding HRF (hemodynamic response function). These computations were performed with in-house codes using the SPM12 and the Blind Deconvolution rs-HRF toolbox: resting-state Hemodynamic Response Function for MATLAB®.2

(b) Paradigm Free Mapping (PFM)3: adopts sparse regression and stability selection, demonstrating the ability to identify the neuronal activity without predefined models. The main codes were improved and adapted to meet the objectives of this work, and the SPAMS (a SPArse Modeling Software) toolbox was also adopted to optimise the solution of sparse estimation problems.

After completing all the computations with regard to the deconvolution approaches, the following functional connectivity metrics were calculated: first, the commonly used Pearson’s correlation (PC); then, the unconventional metrics, namely: Pearson’s correlation with time delay (Corr), non-linear correlation coefficient (H2), mutual information in time domain (MIT), transfer entropy (TE), and coherence (Coh), using the MULAN toolbox.4 Additionally, for each functional connectivity metric, two different methods were also considered:

(a) connectivity perspective: the metric calculation considered only the pairwise connections, represented by the letter B, for bivariate; or the metric calculation considered the pairwise connections while taking into consideration the influence of the remaining ROIs, by means of calculating the matrix inversion of the bivariate results, represented by the letter P, for partial. Furthermore, in an effort to improve P calculations, the initial 116 brain ROIs were grouped into forty main brain regions to reduce the number of variables, and then two strategies were adopted: first, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was computed to identify the most relevant ROIs within each of the forty brain regions; and second, the arithmetic mean of the timeseries values of the corresponding grouped ROIs was calculated for each brain region.

(b) connectivity direction: the metric calculation considered that the connectivity between two ROIs was directed, represented by the letter D, or undirected, represented by the letter U.

Finally, nine machine learning classifiers were adopted for the task of distinguishing ASD subjects from typical controls, using the MALINI toolbox5: K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN); Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA); Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA); Naïve Bayes; Random Forest; Sparse Logistic Regression (SLR); Ridge Logistic Regression (RLR); Linear Support Vector Machines (LinearSVM); and Multilayer Perceptron Neural Network (MLP-NN). The following classification performance metrics were considered: accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), specificity, balanced accuracy and F1-score. One-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test was performed for large samples, or Shapiro-Wilk normality test for small samples. Friedman nonparametric analysis of variance test was performed, together with Wilcoxon signed rank post-hoc test, or Kruskal-Wallis post-hoc test for paired data, whenever applicable.

Results

Taking into consideration the six brain functional connectivity metrics and their particular methods, as well as the groups of hemodynamic deconvolution approaches adopted in this work, 228 combinations were obtained for the evaluation of the automated diagnosis of ASD using the selected machine learning classifiers.

In terms of statistical analyses of the classification performance for the commonly used metric Accuracy, the classifiers Ridge Logistic Regression (RLR), Linear Support Vector Machines (LinearSVM), and Sparse Logistic Regression (SLR) demonstrated significantly superior performance, with values above 70%, while K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), NaïveBayes, and Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA) exhibited significantly inferior performance, with values around 63% (Friedman nonparametric analysis of variance test p<0.001; Wilcoxon signed rank post-hoc test p<0.05). Additionally, bivariate (B) computations led to higher accuracies compared to partial (P) computations.

Particularly for the Ridge Logistic Regression (RLR) classifier and the classification performance metric Accuracy, the metrics BPC, BH2D, and BCorrD showed significantly higher accuracies; conversely, the metrics PCorrU-a, PCohW1-a, and PCohW2-a demonstrated significantly lower accuracies (Friedman nonparametric analysis of variance test p<0.001; Kruskal-Wallis post-hoc test p<0.05). Regarding the hemodynamic deconvolution approaches, the non-deconvolved group exhibited significantly superior performance in terms of accuracy when compared to Blind Deconvolution (BD) groups (Friedman nonparametric analysis of variance test p=0.0118; Wilcoxon signed rank post-hoc test p<0.05). In relation to the brain functional connectivity metrics and also with respect to the accuracy performance metric, the non-deconvolved group demonstrated a superior performance overall, except for TE metric. BD groups performed better for metrics such as PC, Corr and H2. Conversely, the Paradigm Free Mapping (PFM) group performed better for metrics of the entropy family, such as MIT and TE, as well as PC.

Furthermore, with regard to the remaining classification performance metrics, it is worth highlighting the following overall results:

(a) Precision: the highest values were above 70% and were obtained using the classifiers Random Forest and SLR, taking into consideration the brain functional connectivity metric H2 and the non-deconvolved group.

(b) Recall (sensitivity): the highest values were above 80% and were obtained using the classifier Multilayer Perceptron Neural Network (MLP-NN), considering the connectivity metric Pearson’s correlation and the PFM group.

(c) Specificity: the highest values were above 80% and were obtained using the classifier Random Forest, considering the connectivity metric H2 and the non-deconvolved group.

(d) Balanced accuracy: the highest values were above 77% and were obtained using the classifier QDA, considering the connectivity metric MIT with partial (P) computations and the non-deconvolved group.

(e) F1-score: the highest values were above 72% and were obtained using the classifiers RLR and SLR, considering the connectivity metric H2 and the non-deconvolved group.

Conclusion

Due to the complexity of the Autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and the fact that it is essentially a behavioural condition, improving its automated diagnosis using intrinsic functional magnetic resonance imaging data (fMRI) remains a major challenge. Additionally, the diagnostic process becomes even more complicated by symptoms that overlap with those of other conditions and comorbidities. Taking into consideration the hemodynamic deconvolution approaches and the brain functional connectivity metrics that were explored in this work, a large number of combinations were obtained for evaluation in the context of the automated diagnosis of ASD using machine learning classifiers. With regard to the overall results of the Accuracy performance metric, the most relevant values observed were similar to or better than those reported in other studies, particularly when using the classifier Ridge Logistic Regression (RLR) and the public database ABIDE, with values above 70%. Interestingly, the highest accuracies were observed in the non-deconvolved group. Furthermore, unconventional brain functional connectivity metrics such as H2 and MIT demonstrate potential and could be a useful strategy for identifying ASD biomarkers in future studies. An effort was made in order to assess more relevant brain regions of interest (ROIs) using partial (P) computations. Interestingly, values above 77% for the Balanced accuracy classification performance metric were observed using the classifier QDA together with P computations, indicating that there is still much to explore. In summary, adopting unconventional brain functional connectivity metrics and hemodynamic deconvolution approaches makes the analysis more versatile and potentially more powerful. On the one hand, these metrics could overcome the limitations of linear approaches by capturing complex, nonlinear and directional relationships. They are also more robust to temporal delays and noise, which is particularly relevant in neurophysiological studies. On the other hand, these deconvolution approaches provide a more direct approximation of neuronal activity by reducing the impact of hemodynamic responses. They also enable functional mapping without the need for experimental paradigms, which is useful when studying clinical, paediatric or cognitively impaired populations. Finally, a considerable limitation of this work relates to the ABIDE database. While public datasets are a good source of large samples for neuroimaging studies, providing a large number of subjects, which is beneficial for machine learning computations, they are also very heterogeneous. This makes it even more difficult to identify ASD biomarkers and highlights the need for greater reproducibility and generalisability of data. Several strategies can be explored in future work, such as optimising the parameters of the classifiers already adopted, using additional classifiers, improving the analyses of the partial (P) computations, conducting a deeper interpretation of the results, among others. Improving the automated diagnosis of ASD remains particularly challenging; however, there are many strategies to be explored.
