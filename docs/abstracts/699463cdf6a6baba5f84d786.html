Introduction

Episodic memory is the ability to remember specific past events, along with their temporal and spatial context. Prior work has highlighted the role of medial temporal, prefrontal, and parietal regions in encoding, consolidating, and retrieving such memories. Growing evidence shows that episodic memory is supported by large-scale brain networks rather than isolated regions, and both resting-state and structural studies have begun to examine how network properties contribute to interindividual variability. Resting-state findings indicate that intrinsic connectivity within medial temporal and default-mode subnetworks, as well as connectivity between the hippocampus and prefrontal and parietal regions, relates to individual differences in memory performance. Diffusion MRI and graph-theoretical studies further suggest that memory is linked to the organization and efficiency of white-matter networks1, which may capture stable individual differences more reliably than functional networks.

A major challenge in the field is the reliability of brain–behaviour associations. Large-scale work shows that effect sizes are typically small and only stabilize with samples in the thousands2. 

In a recent fMRI network-based analysis of 1,498 young adults, we found that visual episodic memory performance is related to responsivity in nine functional networks identified through independent component analysis3. In the present study, we tested whether structural connectivity within these same networks explains interindividual differences in memory. Using tractography, we quantified within-network structural connectivity in a subsample of 1,166 participants with diffusion data and examined its association with free-recall performance while controlling for age and sex.

Methods

The data analysed in this study come from a single-centre, large-scale project on healthy young adults collected between 2008 and 2015. Of the 1,832 recruited participants, we excluded individuals with missing diffusion data (n = 449), missing or poor-quality T1-weighted scans (n = 143), and unsuccessful connectome reconstructions (n = 74). The final sample consisted of 1,166 participants (730 females, 436 males; age 18–35). All participants were free of neurological or psychiatric conditions, provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Canton Basel.

After a brief training session, participants entered the MRI scanner for one run of a picture-encoding task, followed by a separate working-memory task acquired with fMRI. Approximately 20 minutes later, they completed an unannounced free-recall test outside the scanner. Participants then returned for a second MRI session, including structural and diffusion imaging. The present work focuses on free-recall performance together with anatomical and diffusion MRI data.

Episodic memory was assessed using a picture-free recall paradigm. During encoding, participants viewed 72 affective images (24 negative, 24 neutral, 24 positive) selected from the International Affective Picture System and 24 scrambled control images containing geometrical figures. Images were presented for 2.5 s in a quasi-randomized, event-related design, with a 500 ms fixation and jittered inter-trial intervals of 9–12 s. During the inter-trial interval, participants rated valence and arousal on a three-point scale; scrambled images required size/shape judgments. About 20 minutes after scanning, participants freely described all images they remembered. Primacy, recency, and training items were excluded. Two independent raters scored recall responses (inter-rater reliability >98%), and the number of correctly recalled images served as the episodic memory outcome.

MRI data were acquired on a Siemens Magnetom Verio 3T system with a 12-channel head coil. T1-weighted images were collected using an MPRAGE sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 3.37 ms, TI = 1000 ms, flip angle = 8°, 176 sagittal slices, 1 mm isotropic). Diffusion-weighted images were acquired with single-shot EPI (64 directions, b = 900 s/mm²; one b0; TR = 9000 ms, TE = 82 ms; voxel size 2.5 mm isotropic; GRAPPA = 2).

Preprocessing was performed with QSIPrep 0.19.1. Whole-brain fiber orientations were reconstructed using QSIrecon’s mrtrix_singleshell_ss3t_ACT-hsvs workflow, including multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution and generation of 10 million anatomically constrained streamlines via MRtrix3 iFOD2, seeded at the gray–white matter interface.

We investigated nine functional networks previously identified as showing encoding-related responsivity predictive of episodic memory (Figure 1). Networks were defined by clusters with z > 3 and ≥50 voxels. Because a subset of participants overlapped with Geissmann et al. (2023), functional network clusters were transformed from group-level MNI space to each subject’s QSIPrep AC–PC space using combined MNI-to-DARTEL and DARTEL-to-T1w transformations, followed by rigid alignment to AC–PC space. For each network, we extracted within-network structural connectivity by counting streamlines between all node pairs, normalizing adjacency matrices by total streamline count.

Associations between structural connectivity and episodic memory performance were examined using multiple linear regressions that linked within-network edge weights to free-recall scores, while controlling for age and sex. Correction for multiple comparisons was done across all edges and ICs. 

Results

We examined the association between within-IC structural connectivity and episodic memory performance. Connectivity strength was extracted for nine independent components identified in a prior functionally based analysis. For each IC, we ran multiple regression models with memory performance as the dependent variable, adjusting for age and sex. After correcting for multiple comparisons (FDR, q < 0.05), no associations remained significant.

Several ICs, however, showed nominally significant effects (uncorrected p < 0.05). Figure 2a summarizes these results by displaying the t-values of the regression coefficients for all edges within each IC in block-diagonal heatmaps, and Figure 2b presents the corresponding –log₁₀(p) values.

The strongest nominal association emerged in IC-6 (multimodal integration; t = 2.71, p = 0.0067) for the edge between N17 (right caudal anterior cingulate cortex and right rostral anterior cingulate cortex) and N18 (right superior temporal cortex and adjacent right cerebral white matter). IC-50 (extended left frontoparietal; t = 2.26, p = 0.023) also showed an effect for the edge connecting N44 (right cerebellar cortex and right cerebellar white matter) and N46 (left isthmus cingulate cortex and left cerebellar cortex).

IC-37 (posterior default mode network; t = –2.19, p = 0.028) showed a nominal association for the edge between N34 (right caudal middle frontal cortex and adjacent white matter) and N35 (left inferior parietal cortex and adjacent white matter).

IC-54 (insula–occipital–temporal network) contributed three additional nominally significant edges (t=-2.13 to 2.10, p=0.033-0.039). These involved:

N3(right lingual cortex and adjacent white matter) with N7(left cerebellar cortex and left cerebellar white matter).

N6(left lateral occipital cortex and adjacent white matter) with N8(left superior temporal cortex and left insula).

N1(right superior frontal cortex and right rostral middle frontal cortex) with N11(right and left cerebellar cortex). 

Finally, IC-21 (medial frontoparietal) showed a weaker nominal effect (t = 2.04, p = 0.040) for the edge linking N23 (left middle temporal cortex and adjacent white matter) and N25 (left isthmus cingulate cortex and left praecuneus).

Conclusion

A total of 1166 participants were included in this study to investigate the structural connectivity association within nine functionally defined brain networks explaining interindividual differences in episodic memory performance. In our previous work, we identified specific functional connectivity patterns within these nine network components whose responsivity during encoding was robustly associated with episodic memory performance. However, the present analysis revealed that within-network structural connectivity was not significantly associated with memory performance at the given sample size. Several nominal associations emerged, but none survived correction for multiple comparisons. Given the larger sample size, this suggests that the structural differences between individuals within these networks were small and only weakly associated with episodic memory performance. 

Across the nine networks investigated, only five showed nominal associations linked to memory, and these were modest effects that did not survive correction. The associations that did appear were spread across regions involved in emotion, sensory processing, language, and memory, including areas within IC 6, IC 21, and IC 50, as well as parts of the left frontoparietal network (IC 37). A few regions in IC 54, such as the lingual gyrus and the cerebellum, also showed ties to memory or working-memory performance. 

Based on the largest nominal effect (IC 6, t = 2.71, r ≈ 0.08), power calculations indicate that even samples substantially larger than the present one (on the order of several thousand participants for stringent α levels) would be required for such associations to survive multiple-comparison correction, and they would still explain less than 1% of the variance in episodic memory. 

Taken together with our previous fMRI findings, these results suggest that interindividual variability in episodic memory in healthy young adults is more strongly related to dynamic encoding-related network responsivity rather than to stable white-matter connectivity within these networks.

 

Limitations

Although this study offers valuable insights, several limitations should be considered. First, the episodic memory assessment was done 20 minutes after encoding. A longer delay, such as a follow-up after several days, might reveal differences in memory retention and network involvement. Second, structural connectivity was estimated using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). More advanced scanning techniques, including those with higher spatial and angular resolution, could provide a more precise mapping of white matter architecture and potentially reveal subtler relationships between structural networks and memory performance. Third, as the sample consisted of young, healthy adults, our findings cannot readily be generalized to older or clinical populations.
