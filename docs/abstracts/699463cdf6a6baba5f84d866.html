Introduction

Structural connectivity mapping derived from diffusion MRI is a key tool for investigating how white matter (WM) pathways support brain function. However, recent debates have raised concerns about the reliability of these techniques in regions affected by focal disruption, since they assume that microstructural features remain consistent along each pathway. When a streamline, an estimated fiber trajectory connecting brain regions, crosses both healthy and lesioned voxels, this assumption breaks down. Because the method estimates connections' strength between brain regions by fitting the signal along each fiber pathway, even a small error in one location can propagate across the brain. When a pathway passes through a lesion, its contribution may be redistributed beyond the affected area, causing systematic over- or underestimation of connection strengths1. In this way, a minor inaccuracy in a focal lesion can propagate along the pathway and distort estimates in distant regions. A promising solution2 incorporates an additional compartment for pathological tissue, using lesion masks that indicate the location of damaged tissue, to better capture local microstructural changes and yield more reliable estimates. However, this approach depends on high-quality lesion masks, which are not always part of the clinical routine, may be incomplete or inaccurate, and often require manual delineation, making the process time-consuming, user-dependent, and hard to generalize. In this work, we extended the COMMIT3 to eliminate the dependency on manually drawn lesion masks by automatically detecting signal degeneration directly from diffusion MRI. Our framework introduces an optimization strategy with an additional regularization term dedicated to the pathological compartment. This encourages sparse, localized solutions consistent with lesion-like signal attenuation, effectively isolating focal degeneration without prior anatomical labeling. 

Methods

The original enhanced model's2 strategy stabilizes connectivity estimates, but it critically depends on lesion masks, which are not always available in clinical or research settings. 

To overcome this limitation, we generate voxel-wise penalization maps, which encode the likelihood of pathological involvement. In simulation settings, these maps are derived from synthetic FLAIR images, in which voxel intensities are sampled from Gaussian models of healthy WM and multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions. In clinical data, penalization maps are instead computed from acquired FLAIR images.
In both cases, intensities are evaluated within eroded white-matter masks to reduce partial-volume effects, and voxels equal or exceeding the 99th percentile of the WM intensity distribution are labeled as abnormal. Once binarized, these maps define a restricted set of voxels, denoted as Ω, in which lesion-like signal contributions are allowed to be modeled. To ensure that only a minimal subset of candidate voxels contributes to the final solution, we introduced a regularization term4 in the COMMIT’s cost function. This encourages the model to select only the most plausible lesion-like contributions to explain the signal, while shrinking less informative coefficients to zero (Figure 1). This approach preserves accurate connectivity estimates while mitigating overfitting or bias propagation without relying on pre-defined lesion masks.

To evaluate our model, we simulated focal WM alterations in 44 subjects acquired on a 3T system from the Human Connectome Project5 (HCP) test-retest dataset, fitting the model on the MR signal. We introduced multiple lesions scattered throughout WM, with four levels of signal attenuation—100% (complete loss, profound), 75% (severe), 50% (moderate), and 25% (mild)—to evaluate detection sensitivity, with total lesion loads covering approximately 5% of the volume (consistent with typical MS pathology).

In all simulations, structural connectomes, network representations of the brain in which nodes correspond to gray matter regions and edges reflect the strength of WM connections, were reconstructed using anatomically constrained tractography seeded at the gray matter-white matter interface, generating 3 millions of streamlines. Network metrics, including global efficiency, mean strength, and modularity, were computed to evaluate topology-level alterations induced by the simulated lesions. Statistical significance across conditions, (without lesion modeling, with the original multi-compartment model, and with the proposed automatic framework) was assessed using permutation-based testing. 

Finally, we validated the approach on a clinical cohort of 83 healthy controls (HC) (age 37.7±12.9 y/o, 45 F) and 107 MS patients (age 45.2±14.6 y/o, 59 F), including 71 relapsing-remitting (RRMS) and 36 progressive (PMS). The ethical review committee of the University Hospital Basel (Institutional Review Board of Northwest Switzerland) approved the study, and all participants entered the study after written consent. Connectomes were reconstructed using the same pipeline as for the simulations, and network metrics were compared between healthy controls and patient groups. Statistical analyses accounted for age, sex, and white matter volume, ensuring that the observed differences were attributable to microstructural disruption rather than confounds.

Crucially, the performance of the proposed automatic multi-compartment model was benchmarked against the original multi-compartment model. We also tested the results obtained with the original COMMIT formulation to provide a more comprehensive view of the experiments.

Results

Across all simulated scenarios, the proposed automatic lesion-aware framework demonstrated a clear improvement in detecting microstructural damage compared with both the standard COMMIT implementation and the original multi-compartment model. In simulations involving a single focal lesion, the model reliably captured reductions in streamline contributions even at mild levels of signal attenuation, showing higher sensitivity than the original framework. When multiple lesions were scattered across white matter, the automatic approach consistently reflected global disruptions in network topology, with pronounced decreases in global efficiency and mean strength, alongside increased modularity, indicating a fragmentation of the network into more segregated subunits. Importantly, the framework successfully minimized false positives (FP), i.e., voxels incorrectly identified as lesioned that do not overlap with the ground truth, and false negatives (FN), i.e., voxels that are truly lesioned but not captured by the model.

In terms of signal fidelity, the automatic framework substantially reduced the root mean square error in lesion voxels, bringing the predicted diffusion signal into close agreement with the measured signal, while the standard COMMIT model failed to account for lesion-induced deviations, producing markedly elevated errors in affected regions. Comparisons with the original multi-compartment model confirmed that the automatic approach matched or exceeded its performance, while eliminating the need for manually delineated lesion masks.

Validation in a clinical MS cohort confirmed these findings. Both RRMS and PMS patients exhibited statistically significant reductions in mean strength and global efficiency, and increases in modularity, when compared with healthy controls. These changes indicate a global weakening of brain connectivity, less efficient communication across regions, and a more fragmented network structure. These effects were most pronounced with the proposed framework, compared to the original multi-compartment model; while the standard COMMIT approach largely failed to detect any network alterations (Figure 2). The results demonstrate that the method robustly captures subtle and severe pathological changes, providing a faithful representation of lesion impact on structural connectivity in both controlled simulations and real-world clinical data. 

Conclusion

Our results suggest that the proposed automatic framework could represent a substantial advancement in microstructure-informed tractography. By removing the dependency on manually delineated lesion masks, the model may gain increased autonomy and applicability, particularly in settings where mask availability is limited or absent. Its ability to automatically detect focal signal degeneration ensures that connectivity estimates are no longer biased by mask-driven assumptions, overcoming a key limitation of previous multi-compartment approaches.

Across simulations, the automatic model consistently detected lesions at all severity levels, from mild to profound, while maintaining minimal FP and FN. Compared with the original multi-compartment approach, it achieved comparable local fitting accuracy, confirming that independence from external lesion masks does not compromise fidelity. At the network level, reductions in global efficiency and mean strength, along with increases in modularity, were consistently identified, showing the model’s capacity to translate local disruptions into topology-wide changes. 

Clinical validation in 107 individuals with MS further reinforced these findings. The automatic model detected significant connectivity impairments in both RRMS and PMS, highlighting its sensitivity compared with the standard multi-compartment model. While this may reflect detection of lesion effects not captured in manual segmentations, some contributions could arise from voxels with non-lesion signal along noisy streamlines. Therefore, observed increases in apparent impairment should be interpreted cautiously. Although we did not formally quantify associations with lesion load, it is plausible that sensitivity is influenced by lesion location and extent, potentially explaining differences relative to mask-based approaches. 

The structural network alterations identified by our automatic framework often appear more pronounced than those detected by the previous models. Rather than indicating a deterioration of network metrics, this reflects enhanced sensitivity: the method detects subtle microstructural disruptions extending beyond overt lesions, including diffuse axonal injury and other forms of WM compromise, such as Wallerian degeneration or subclinical pathology. In this sense, our framework may not merely replicate known lesions, but could capture a broader spectrum of structural vulnerability, potentially providing a more comprehensive and biologically plausible assessment of network integrity. It should be noted, however, that in patients with MS, part of this apparent divergence could also stem from the inherent limitations of manual lesion segmentation: even with meticulous delineation, a few voxels may occasionally be missed or inconsistently labeled across subjects, introducing a small human-derived bias.

By combining sparse regularization, voxel-wise penalization maps, and biologically informed weighting of streamline contributions, this framework balances specificity and sensitivity: isolating pathological components without over-penalizing healthy tissue. Its versatility suggests potential applications beyond MS, in other neurodegenerative disorders or conditions with microstructural alterations where lesion masks are unavailable.

In conclusion, he automatic lesion-aware microstructure-informed tractography framework not only reproduces the strengths of mask-dependent multi-compartment models but also extends them, capturing subtle and widespread network-level disruptions with high reliability. By autonomously detecting pathological signal alterations and translating them into meaningful connectomic insights, it provides a powerful and clinically feasible tool for mapping the human connectome under pathological conditions, shifting the focus from identifying lesions to quantifying their true impact on brain network integrity.
