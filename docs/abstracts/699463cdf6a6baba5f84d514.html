Introduction

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) reflect microvascular damage and are associated with ageing and cerebrovascular risk (CVR) factors (e.g. hypertension and diabetes). Typically WMHs are summarised with total volume or volume within regions of interest. If voxel-wise mapping is done, a mass-univariate model (MUM) is used, where logistic regression independently fits at each voxel. However, a MUM cannot capture spatial dependence and often results in unstable estimates and frequent convergence failures, especially in low-incidence voxels. While Bayesian spatial models address spatial dependence via spatially varying coefficients, their reliance on MCMC posterior inference limits scalability for large datasets and typically requires GPU resources.^2 To overcome these limitations, we propose a novel and efficient spatial generalised linear model (S-GLM) that leverages pseudo-likelihood estimation with a sandwich estimator, combined with an approximate optimisation strategy that is computationally efficient and scalable to large neuroimaging datasets.

Methods

Our S-GLM uses a log link function, where log-intensity is modelled spatially using 3D cubic B-spline bases, along with (standardised) spatially varying risk factors covariates (including polynomial terms up to cubic). We factorise the voxel-wise S-GLM coefficient updates for dimension reduction and use Taylor approximations for intractable terms. Standard second-order optimization methods are computationally impractical and gradient-based methods are slow, therefore, we implemented a scalable approximate preconditioned gradient method to accelerate convergence. We adopt a Poisson model, motivated by its success in point process modelling and its mathematical equivalence to a binomial model for low-rate binary lesion data.^4 This framework allows voxel-wise hypothesis testing of individual risk factors effects and comparisons between risk factors, employing a robust sandwich estimator for reliable standard error estimation under model misspecification (e.g., spatial dependence or overdispersion).

Results

Extensive simulation experiments validated the accuracy and scalability of our scalable approximate model factorisation and preconditioned gradient optimisation, showing minimal differences compared to MUM-GLM. We find that standard error estimates based on Fisher Information inflate false positives, whereas the sandwich estimator controls false positives and provides more robust and accurate inference. We applied this framework to UK Biobank subjects with subject-specific risk factors (age, gender and CVR factors) and a refined lesion mask of 14,807 voxels.^3 Using a spatial spline parameterization (10mm knot spacing, 889 basis elements), we estimate relative risk (RR) and absolute risk difference (RD) in lesion probability for each risk factor relative to a reference mean level. We first evaluate the model fit with just N=50 subjects. Figure 1a-b shows that the MUM-GLM is severely constrained by count rates too low to provide an estimate, while our S-GLM provides sensible estimates of lesion incidence, similar to those obtained with substantially larger sample size (cf. Fig 2a); Figure 1c-d further demonstrates that, with N=50 subjects, the S-GLM identifies a greater number of voxels with significant p-values, which are also more spatially clustered. With N=13,677 subjects, Figure 2 shows RR and RD maps for age and CVR risk factors. A 10-year increase in age is associated with a substantially stronger risk increase (average RR increase of 184.61%) than a one-unit increase in CVR factor (average RR increase of 22.83%), while both factors exhibit consistent spatial patterns of lesion increase around the lateral ventricles.

Conclusion

We have presented a novel, efficient Spatial Generalised Linear Model to capture spatial dependence while employing an approximate factorisation to scale to large datasets and substantially reduce computation time. This framework has two stages: (i) preconditioned gradient optimization to estimate voxel-wise effects of individual risk factors, and (ii) voxel-wise hypothesis testing using robust sandwich standard errors to assess and flexibly compare risk factor effects. Our experiments demonstrated its significantly more computationally efficient than existing Bayesian spatial models, while offering strong interpretability and not requiring GPU implementation.
