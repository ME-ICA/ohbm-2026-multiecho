Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can show signs of small vessel disease in the brain, but it cannot distinguish the microscopic changes that cause these signs. White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are areas of increased signal on T2-weighted and FLAIR MRIs and are one of the main MRI markers of small vessel disease related to cognitive impairment and dementia. Etiologically, WMHs have been linked to a multitude of underlying pathologies, including edema, demyelination, inflammation, and Wallerian degeneration. Since WMHs in different brain regions and different disease populations can be due to different underlying pathologies, it is critical to develop frameworks that enable combined MRI and neuropathology examinations, with the aim of developing pathology-informed MRI markers of WMH etiologies. Here, we present our postmortem multi-marker MRI and histology assessment framework developed to enable multi-modal assessments of WMH substrates in aging and neurodegenerative disease populations.

Methods

Postmortem brain samples of normal aging individuals or patients with neurodegenerative disorders were obtained from the Douglas Brain Bank (N=63). Intact hemispheres were imaged in a 3T Siemens human scanner (Figure 1) using our multi-modal protocol (Dadar et al., 2024, Aperture Neuro), including structural T1-weighted (resolution: 0.7 mm3) (Figure 1A) and T2-weighted (resolution: 0.64mm3) images (Figure 1B) used for WMH and tissue segmentation, a multi-echo gradient echo sequence used for relaxometry (resolution: 1mm3), and a Gradient And Spin Echo (GRASE) sequence (resolution: 1.8mm3) used to derive myelin water fraction (MWF) maps (Figure 1C). 3T MRIs of intact hemispheres were segmented into cortical and subcortical gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) using SynthSeg, followed by manual correction and addition of WMH masks using Display tool from MINC toolkit (Figure 1D). Average T1 and T2* relaxation times and MWF values were extracted from WMHs and normal appearing white matter (NAWM). We also obtained brains from the anatomy department of the Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières (N=9) that were also imaged using the 3T MRI protocol. Tissue blocks with WMHs were then extracted from the intact hemispheres and imaged in a Bruker 7T preclinical scanner with comparable sequences at higher resolution (0.13×0.13×0.5mm). 7T block MRIs were manually registered to the 3T hemisphere MRIs (Figure 1.E). Blocks were then sectioned and stained for the four main brain cell populations with 4 immunohistochemistry (IHC) markers (Neurons=NeuN, Astrocytes=GFAP, Microglia=Iba1, Myelin=PLP) and 4 histochemistry (HC) stains (Bielschowsky, H&E, Prussian blue, and Luxol fast blue) (Figure 2A). Histology sections were then manually registered to the 7T MRI of the blocks (Frigon et al., 2025, Aperture Neuro) and to the corresponding masks that were also manually segmented to delineate GM, NAWM, and WMHs using Display (Figure 2B). Five ROIs of the GM, NAWM and WMH were extracted from the high-resolution microscopy section of the images (Figure 2C-E). Intensity of the labeling was extracted using ImageJ and averaged to assess the microscopic cellular and molecular features of the WMHs compared to NAWM. Paired t-tests were used to compare the derived MRI and IHC/HC markers between WMHs and NAWM in the full sample and across each diagnostic group.

Results

WMH areas showed significantly higher T1 (t = 15.79, p < 0.0001) and T2* (t = 17.05, p < 0.0001) relaxation times, and lower MWF (t = -20.45, p < 0.0001) compared to NAWM in full sample as well as in the Alzheimer’s disease (tT1 = 14.65, tT2 = 12.38, tMWF = -19.83, all p-values < 0.0001) and Parkinson’s disease and Lewy body dementia (tT1 = 7.41, tT2 = 8.51, tMWF = -9.27, all p-values < 0.0001) subsamples. Preliminary data examining the IHC/HC markers in one normal aging specimen also showed decrease in PLP staining (p = 0.002; Figure 2.C) as well as increased GFAP (p = 0.07; Figure 2.D) and Bielchowsky (p = 0.06; Figure 2.E) staining in WMH compared to NAWM. No significant differences were observed in Iba1 staining levels between WMH and NAWM ROIs.

Conclusion

In this work, we present our postmortem multi-marker MRI and neuropathology assessment framework. We found significant differences in both quantitative MRI measures and IHC markers between WMHs and NAWM tissues. Future work will involve comparison of IHC markers across neurodegenerative disorders, comparison of severity of different markers between different disorders, as well as examining the association between these markers and their MRI counterparts.
