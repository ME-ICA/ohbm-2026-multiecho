Introduction

The cerebellum plays a central role in motor learning and coordination, and its involvement in higher cognitive functions, including attention, emotion, and behaviour, is increasingly recognised. Its cortex has a distinctive cytoarchitecture consisting of three layers: the molecular layer, the Purkinje cell layer, and the granular layer.1 However, these layers cannot be directly resolved with standard in-vivo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) due to typical spatial resolutions of approximately 0.7-1.0 mm. 

Despite this limitation, the approximately 1-mm-thick, highly folded cerebellar gray matter (GM) ribbon exhibits systematic intensity variations. While the individual cellular layers cannot be explicitly delineated, the GM ribbon can be subdivided into two types of voxels based on partial-volume effects: (i) molecular-layer-like voxels, which are dominated by partial volumes of GM and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), with relatively lower T1-weighted signal intensity; and (ii) granular-layer-like voxels, which are characterised by partial volumes of GM and white matter (WM) and higher T1-weighted signal intensity. This separation can be used to improve the segmentation of the cerebellar structures. 

In SPM and its cerebellum-specific SUIT toolbox2, the cerebellum is defined by a tissue probability map (TPM) with a central core of WM and a thick surrounding area of GM, where lobular branches are barely visible (Fig. 1). Although this definition allows for quite robust processing, it restricts the accuracy of structural analysis and the application of the internal classification-based registration tools like Dartel or Shooting. 

To overcome this limitation, we modified the SUIT toolbox's cerebellar segmentation routine to include an intensity-based subdivision of cerebellar GM.

Methods

Data

For template creation, we used high resolution data from the following OpenNeuro repositories: ds000113, ds001168, ds001942, ds002702, ds003097, ds004835, ds004928, ds005216. The images have a resolution between 0.6 and 0.8 mm and were acquired on 3 and 7 Tesla scanners. Manual quality control was performed, and in many cases images had to be removed due to local signal loss, especially for 7 Tesla data. 

Processing

We first used a spatial adaptive non-local-mean (SANLM) filter to remove noise. Next, we divided the SUIT-derived GM segmentation into the two sub-classes. The outer class describes the GM-CSF partial volume area, roughly corresponding to the molecular layer. The inner class outlines the partial volume of GM and WM, and corresponds to the granular layer. We assumed that the volume of both classes are globally and locally balanced and applied a k-means clustering approach with four classes (to handle also remaining pure CSF and WM voxels). This enabled further correction of inhomogeneities and artefacts by a local adaptive segmentation scheme.3 Finally, the cerebellar mask was refined by region growing to integrate missed cerebellar structures and to remove non-cerebellar parts from the initial SUIT segmentation. Affine images were subsequently exported to 0.5 mm to run the Shooting template creation4 on the previously described dataset of 193 high quality brain scans.

Results

The new approach enables the reconstruction of the fine structures of the lobular branches, even in regions with challenging signal intensity. 

Fig. 2 shows the label map of the final SUIT Dartel template based on the classic segmentation (A) and the label map of the final Shooting template based on the new segmentation (B).  

Conclusion

In this study, we expanded the cerebellum-specific SUIT toolbox in SPM to enhance the anatomical representation of the human cerebellum in structural MRI. By explicitly accounting for partial-volume effects within the cerebellar gray matter ribbon, we demonstrate that coarse subdivision into two intensity-defined compartments allows for more precise segmentation and registration of cerebellar structures. 

The resulting improvements have direct implications for subsequent analyses. Specifically, the refined segmentation facilitates voxel-based morphometry (VBM), improves anatomical correspondence for functional MRI mapping, and creation of cerebellar surface models.
