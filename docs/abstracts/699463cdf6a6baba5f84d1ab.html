Introduction

The brain encodes pain information in various ways. One of the most widely studied is the amplitude coding scheme, in which the magnitude of BOLD responses or neuronal firing rates increases as the intensity of nociceptive stimuli grows1. This scheme assumes that a fixed population of neural units represents pain across all contexts. However, some studies have shown that higher-intensity stimuli may activate additional brain regions beyond increasing activation in the same regions2,3, suggesting that the fixed-population assumption may be incorrect and that pain-encoding regions may spread spatially with increasing pain intensity. A critical methodological issue in these studies is that conclusions are often based on counting statistically significant voxels or neurons using frequentist P-values, which cannot provide evidence for a null effect that a given region does not respond to low-intensity painful stimuli. Additionally, the small sample sizes in previous studies raise concerns about replicability and generalizability. To address these issues and test the spatial-spreading hypothesis of pain encoding in the brain, we employed a Bayes factor-based approach to analyze two large fMRI datasets (total N = 210) in which participants received painful heat stimuli of different intensities.

Methods

Two fMRI datasets were analyzed (Dataset 1: N = 97 [50 females]; Dataset 2: N = 113 [68 females]). In Dataset 1 (Fig. 1A), participants received interspersed heat stimuli or aversive auditory stimuli of three intensity levels and reported perceived intensity and unpleasantness ratings (heat: 47℃, 48℃, 49℃; auditory: three levels of nails scratching a chalkboard, or aversive sounds [attacks, screaming, and crying] from the International Affective Digital Sounds database with the highest arousal and lowest pleasure)4. In Dataset 2 (Fig. 1B), participants completed three tasks: somatic pain, vicarious pain, and cognitive effort, each with three intensity levels5. Participants first viewed a low- or high-intensity social cue indicating the upcoming stimulus (somatic pain, vicarious pain, or cognitive effort [i.e., mental rotation task]), then reported expected intensity ratings, received the corresponding stimuli, and lastly gave perceived intensity ratings. Dataset 1 was collected on a 3T Siemens Trio MRI scanner at the University of Colorado Boulder (voxel size in functional images: 3 × 3 × 3 mm³ or 3.4 × 3.4 × 3.4 mm³). Dataset 2 was collected on a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma MRI scanner at Dartmouth College (voxel size in functional images: 2.7 × 2.7 × 2.7 mm³).

Single-trial beta maps for each stimulus were estimated using the Least Squares All approach (Dataset 1) or the Least Squares Separate approach (Dataset 2). These maps were averaged for each intensity level and participant, and submitted to one-sample t tests. T maps for each intensity level were converted to Bayes factor (BF) maps and thresholded at BF = 6.3. Voxels passing thresholds were classified into six main classes: (1) "Low+", showing activation only in the low-intensity condition (t > 0, BF > 6.3 for Low, but BF < 1/6.3 for High); (2) "Low-", showing deactivation only in the low-intensity condition (t < 0, BF > 6.3 for Low, but BF < 1/6.3 for High); (3) "High+", showing activation only in the high-intensity conditions (t > 0, BF > 6.3 for High, but BF < 1/6.3 for Low); (4) "High-", showing deactivation only in the high-intensity condition (t < 0, BF > 6.3 for High, but BF < 1/6.3 for Low); (5) "Both+", showing activation in both conditions (t > 0, BF > 6.3 for High and Low); and (6) "Both-", showing deactivation in both conditions (t < 0, BF > 6.3 for High and Low). Other logically possible classes (e.g., 1/6.3 < BF < 6.3) were left out as they were rare and not relevant for our purposes.

Results

In Dataset 1, we found that a wide range of brain regions encoded the intensity of painful stimuli. BF-based classification showed that most of these regions responded to both low- and high-intensity stimuli. Importantly, parts of the S1, dlPFC, dmPFC, superior temporal gyrus, inferior temporal gyri, cuneus, and cerebellum were uniquely activated by higher-intensity painful stimuli but not by lower-intensity stimuli (Fig. 2A). The number of "High+" voxels (yellow voxels in Fig. 2) increased as intensity differences grew. After controlling for stimulus intensity, we observed more voxels activated only in trials with higher pain ratings than lower pain ratings, indicating effects of pain ratings beyond stimulus intensity. Similar findings were obtained in Dataset 2 (Fig. 2B). Critically, we still observed voxels uniquely activated by high-intensity painful stimuli when minimal smoothing was applied in both datasets (Fig. 2B), although more aggressive smoothing increased the number of voxels responding only to high-intensity stimuli, suggesting that spatial smoothing could not fully explain our findings. Curiously, little spatial spreading was found in the aversive auditory task in Dataset 1 or in the vicarious pain and cognitive effort tasks in Dataset 2 (Fig. 2C), indicating that the brain does not necessarily encode cognitive or affective load through spatial spreading.

Conclusion

Brain regions encoding pain spread spatially as nociceptive stimulus intensity and perceived pain increase. These findings demonstrate a topological spreading coding scheme for pain information in the brain, deepening our understanding of the neural representation of pain and informing attempts to build activation-topology-aware signatures of pain.
