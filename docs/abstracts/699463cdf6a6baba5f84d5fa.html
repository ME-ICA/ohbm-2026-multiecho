Introduction

The basal forebrain (BF), including the nucleus basalis of Meynert and medial septum, provides widespread cholinergic input to the cortex and hippocampus, critically modulating cortical state, attention, and memory. Cholinergic activity promotes cortical desynchronization, enhancing sensory processing and cognitive flexibility, while loss of cholinergic tone is strongly linked to cognitive decline.

Transcranial focused ultrasound stimulation (TUS) is a noninvasive technique capable of precisely targeting deep brain regions with high spatial specificity. Applied to the BF, TUS is used to test whether it can modulate cholinergic output and causally influence cortical dynamics in humans.

Here, we use an online TUS protocol adapted from Murphy et al. (2024), which demonstrated acute effects on neuronal activity lasting several tens of seconds after stimulation, to modulate activity in the left BF and its cholinergic output. By applying this protocol repeatedly within a session, we also expect cumulative offline after-effects to emerge. As non-invasive proxies of cholinergic activity, we assess EEG to capture changes in oscillatory synchronization and pupillometry, since pupil dilation is modulated by cholinergic tone via the parasympathetic pathway. Additionally, the insula serves as an active control site, and ECG is recorded to assess heart rate changes as potential biomarkers of neuromodulation.

We hypothesize that TUS targeting the left BF will causally modulate cortical cholinergic tone in humans, with an increase in cholinergic tone resulting in alpha desynchronization and theta synchronization, detectable during and after stimulation in resting EEG, along with increased pupil dilation. These effects are expected to be specific to BF stimulation and not observed with TUS targeting the active control site (insula), which is hypothesized to preferentially modulate autonomic and interoceptive markers.

Methods

The study was conducted using a within-subject design in healthy young adults. Each participant underwent two TUS sessions: one targeting the BF and the other targeting the insula as an active control, in pseudo-randomized order. Both targets were accessed via the temporal bone window, requiring minimal adjustments to transducer placement. Axial steering was largely sufficient to switch between targets, making it difficult for participants to distinguish experimental conditions and ensuring effective blinding. All participants provided informed consent, and the study was conducted with institutional ethical approval.

TUS was delivered using a four-element, 545 kHz annular array transducer (CTX-545, NeuroFUS), placed over the left temporal window and coupled with a silicone pad. The transducer was positioned using MR-informed neuronavigation (Localite), guided by a robotic arm (Cobot, Axilum Robotics), to ensure precise targeting throughout the experiment. Translational and rotational deviations relative to the initial position were determined via individual acoustic simulations (k-Plan). These deviations were continuously monitored and automatically compensated.

Each session included pre- and post-stimulation resting-state EEG, ECG, and pupillometry recordings, as well as continuous recording during 40 trials of sonication. TUS parameters were as follows: pulse duration of 90 ms, with a 20-ms Tukey ramp (10 ms at the beginning and 10 ms at the end of the pulse), pulse repetition interval of 400 ms, pulse-train duration of 5.2 s, and inter-trial interval of 40 s, following the methodology of Murphy et al. (2024). The Tukey ramp was applied to each pulse to minimize potential auditory confounds. Simulations of in-situ spatial-peak pulse-average (ISPPA) values estimated a range of 1–5 W/cm², with variability dependent on skull properties. Participant safety was ensured by adhering to the following limits according to ITRUSST safety recommendations (Aubry et al., 2025): mechanical index < 1.9 and thermal dose < 2 CEM43.

To distinguish effects, online EEG was examined during each TUS trial to detect transient changes in oscillatory activity, while offline EEG was compared between pre- and post-stimulation resting-state recordings. Offline analyses focused on alpha and theta band power over frontal, central, and parietal regions, as well as whole-head topographies, to assess sustained modulation induced by TUS beyond the stimulation period.

Interim analyses were based on 12 of a planned 24 participants. Offline EEG and online HEP results reported here reflect this subset, with full-sample analyses expected to be completed and presented by the time of the conference.

Results

Resting-state EEG analyses revealed a significant reduction in alpha-band power following BF TUS relative to pre-stimulation baseline. The alpha decrease was widespread, with the strongest effects over centro-parietal regions, consistent with the broad cortical projections of the BF.

Analyses of heartbeat-evoked potentials (HEPs) and ECG-derived metrics, including heart rate and heart rate variability, revealed no significant differences between BF and insula sessions in the interim cohort. Overall, BF TUS selectively induced offline alpha suppression, whereas insula stimulation elicited no comparable EEG effects. These findings suggest that BF TUS can produce target-specific modulation of cortical activity detectable in resting-state EEG, but it did not produce measurable changes in autonomic nervous system activity in this interim sample.

Conclusion

We tested the hypothesis that robotically guided, MRI-planned TUS targeting the BF would modulate cortical oscillatory activity via central cholinergic pathways, with minimal effects on peripheral autonomic measures, using a within-subject design and the insula as an active control. Interim analyses (n = 12 of a planned 24) showed that BF TUS produced widespread reductions in resting-state alpha power, strongest over centro-parietal regions, whereas insula stimulation did not elicit comparable EEG changes. No significant changes were observed in heartbeat-evoked potentials or ECG metrics, indicating limited peripheral autonomic engagement. These findings suggest that BF TUS primarily modulates cortical state and that offline EEG provides a more sensitive measure than online recordings, which are affected by trial-by-trial variability and stimulation-related artifacts.

Completion of the full sample will enable comprehensive analyses of both online and offline EEG, along with ECG and pupillometry measures, using advanced EEG signal processing and statistical modeling. Incorporating covariates such as skull-dependent variability, in-situ intensity, and skull thickness will allow a more precise assessment of how delivered acoustic energy from TUS modulates neural activity and downstream physiological responses, thereby deepening our understanding of TUS neuromodulation mechanisms.

In summary, interim results demonstrate that BF-targeted TUS reproducibly modulates cortical oscillatory state without measurable autonomic effects, supporting a central cholinergic mechanism. Insula stimulation did not produce equivalent effects. This multimodal framework, integrating EEG, HEP, pupillometry, and individualized acoustic simulations, provides a rigorous platform for validating engagement of deep neuromodulatory nuclei and establishes a foundation for translational studies of cholinergic modulation in aging and neurodegeneration.
