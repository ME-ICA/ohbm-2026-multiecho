Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and cerebrovascular disease (CeVD) are among the leading causes of cognitive impairment in older adults, and are frequently co-morbid. While AD and CeVD pathologies can interact to influence cognitive function1, converging evidence has suggested that AD and CeVD have additive rather than synergistic effects on cognition and brain function and structure. In one autopsy study2, among AD patients with similar dementia severity, those with CeVD were reported to exhibit lower AD burden than those without CeVD. Correspondingly, our previous work has demonstrated differential influences of AD and CeVD burden on brain functional connectivity (FC)3. Further, brain FC disruptions associated with CeVD have been shown to relate to both baseline and longitudinal cognitive decline, underscoring the utility of using neuroimaging to track disease progression and cognitive decline4.

            To date, no study has systematically characterized distinct brain phenotypes arising from the influence of AD and CeVD in a fully data-driven manner in older adults. Here, our study sought to identify brain phenotypes of AD and CeVD in older adults spanning the cognitive spectrum. We accomplished this by first applying similarity network fusion to integrate regional grey matter volumes, whole-brain functional connectivity, AD blood biomarkers and CeVD MRI markers, then performing spectral clustering to delineate subtypes with distinct grey matter volume, functional connectivity, AD and CeVD marker profiles. We then examined how these subtypes differ in terms of their clinical, brain and cognitive characteristics.

Methods

We studied 329 older adults across the cognitive spectrum with varying degrees of AD and CeVD burden from the Singapore Memory Ageing and Cognition Centre (MACC) Harmonization cohort with available T1, resting-state fMRI, AD plasma biomarker (neurofilament light (NfL), phosphorylated tau at threonine 217 (p-tau217) and phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (p-tau181)) and CeVD MRI marker data (age-related white matter changes, number of lacunes, number of microbleeds and number of cortical infarcts). Individual whole-brain functional connectivity matrices were obtained from preprocessed resting-state fMRI data using 432 regions-of-interest (ROIs), while regional grey matter volumes were obtained via voxel-based morphometry using the same 432 ROIs parcellation. We then applied a similarity network fusion and spectral clustering approach5 on the regional grey matter volumes, functional connectivity, AD plasma biomarker and CeVD MRI marker data to identify subtypes with different brain and pathology profiles. Specifically, following previous procedure5, between-subjects similarity networks were first computed for each of the four data types. Smilarity network fusion was subsequently applied to integrate and iteratively fuse these similarity networks into a single fused similarity network, followed by spectral clustering on the fused network to identify homogeneous subtypes. To ensure reproducible clustering results, we repeated the above similarity network fusion subtyping step 1000 times on 90% bootstrapped samples. The final clustering result was obtained by applying spectral clustering on the consensus matrix. Finally, we examined how the clinical subtypes differed from a healthy control group (dementia-free individuals in the healthy subtype) in terms of their plasma biomarker, brain and cognitive characteristics by performing linear regression analyses with group (clinical subtype versus healthy control) as predictor of interest, and age, sex, education, handedness, diagnosis (and total intracranial volumes for brain measures) as nuisance covariates.

Results

From the SNF subtyping, we identified a stable clustering solution with three subtypes: a healthy subtype with low AD and CeVD marker burden, a CeVD subtype with high burden across all CeVD markers, and an AD subtype with high p-tau181 and p-tau217 levels. In particular, when compared against a healthy control group comprising individuals in the healthy subtype who are dementia-free, the AD subtype showed higher NfL, p-tau181 and p-tau217 levels as well as higher number of microbleeds (Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.05) and cortical infarcts (uncorrected p < 0.05), while the CeVD subtype showed higher NfL levels and higher burden in all CeVD markers (Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.05) (Fig 1A). Further, the CeVD subtype exhibited widespread whole-brain grey matter atrophy and functional connectivity disruptions particularly between subcortical and cortical regions compared to healthy controls (uncorrected p < 0.05). In contrast, the AD subtype exhibited more circumscribed grey matter atrophy in temporal and default network regions, as well as greater functional connectivity disruptions in cortical networks, including higher functional connectivity between cortical networks and lower functional connectivity within cortical networks (such as the default network) (uncorrected p < 0.05) (Fig 1B).

            Additionally, the AD and CeVD subtypes showed different cardiac biomarker and cognitive profiles both at baseline (Fig 1C) and over time (Fig 1D). Compared to healthy controls, the CeVD subtype showed higher baseline plasma cardiac biomarker (high-sensitivity Troponin T (hsTNT) and growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15)) levels (Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.05), lower baseline global and domain-specific cognitive performance including visuoconstruction and visuomotor speed (uncorrected p < 0.05), as well as faster rates of decline over time in executive function (Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.05) and verbal and visual memory (uncorrected p < 0.05). On the other hand, the AD subtype showed significantly higher baseline dementia severity, lower baseline global and domain-specific cognitive performance particularly in verbal and visual memory, as well as faster rates of decline over time in dementia severity and performance across multiple cognitive domains (Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.05).

Conclusion

Our study identified subtypes of AD and CeVD with distinct brain, biomarker and cognitive profiles. Consistent with past findings, the AD subtype showed poorer memory function as well as atrophy within circumscribed temporal and default regions, while the CeVD subtype showed elevated levels of cardiac plasma markers (hsTNT and GDF15), more widespread atrophy and disrupted subcortical-cortical functional connectivity. Further, both AD and CeVD subtypes showed faster longitudinal declines in cognitive performance compared to healthy controls. These findings highlight the utility of using subtyping to guide more tailored management strategies for prognostic predictions and personalized treatment planning. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are underway to investigate how different subtypes with distinct brain and biomarker profiles differentially respond to intervention as well as relate to long-term health and clinical outcomes.
