Introduction

The human brain processes narrative stories by integrating information across multiple hierarchical levels, from audio to semantic.1 While our previous research presented at OHBM2025 demonstrated that sentence and paragraph-level embeddings using Sentence Transformers (SBERT)2 correlate with cortical activity during narrative comprehension, human language processing fundamentally begins with auditory stimuli. Recent advancements in automatic speech recognition (ASR) models, such as OpenAI's Whisper, process speech through acoustic-to-speech-to-language representations. Embeddings from these internal encoder and decoder layers can be used to construct predictive models of brain activity during narrative story listening.3 However, despite Whisper capturing acoustic–phonetic features and SBERT providing semantic representations, their relative contributions to predicting cortical responses during narrative comprehension and the extent to which their integration captures complementary neural processes remain untested. Here, we hypothesize that Whisper embeddings are more closely tied to acoustic and phonetic properties of the speech signal, whereas SBERT embeddings better capture semantic content, and that their integration may improve prediction of BOLD signal patterns in the Dorsal Attention (DA), Ventral Attention (VA), and Default Mode (DMN) Networks as defined by Yeo that are involved in narrative comprehension and semantic transitions.

Methods

We used the publicly available narrative fMRI dataset4 (OpenNeuro :https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds002345/versions/1.1.4). Four stories were selected from the this collection: "I Knew You Were Black" (black; 534 TRs), "The Man Who Forgot Ray Bradbury" (forgot; 558 TRs), "Pie Man (PNI)" (piemanpni; 267 TRs), and "Running from the Bronx" (bronx; 358 TRs). All four stories were originally acquired from 46 participants; however, one participant was excluded due to missing data, resulting in a final sample of 45 participants (32 females, mean age 23.33 ± 7.55 years). Functional data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma with a 1.5 s TR and 2.5 mm isotropic voxels. Further details of the dataset can be found in the original paper. The functional data was preprocessed using fmriprep (v.25.1.4:https://fmriprep.org/en/latest/index.html). 

To reduce computational demand, we parcellated the data using Schaefer parcellation with 1000 parcels (1-mm resolution), and focused on 3 of Yeo's 7 intrinsic connectivity networks: the Dorsal Attention Network (DA), Ventral Attention Network (VA), and Default Mode Network (DMN), these networks were chosen for their known involvement in attentional allocation and semantic processing during narrative comprehension. Additionally, we included the Early Auditory Cortex (EAC) available with the dataset4 to examine low-level auditory processing. fMRI BOLD signals were averaged across subjects to yield a one-dimensional time series for each parcel.

As shown in Figure 1, for each story, we extracted three features from Whisper (whisper-tiny: https://huggingface.co/openai/whisper-tiny): (1) acoustic embeddings from the input layer of the encoder, (2) speech embeddings from the final layer of the encoder (layer 3), and (3) language embeddings from the decoder. Layer 3 was selected based on prior evidence that intermediate encoder layers capture robust acoustic-phonetic representations.3 All Whisper embeddings had a dimensionality of 384. We compared two audio segmentation strategies for Whisper processing: (1) a 30-second sliding window approach where cumulative word sequences from sentence onsets (e.g., "The" → "The cat" → "The cat sat") slid forward when exceeding 30 seconds, and (2) sentence-boundary segmentation where each sentence-length audio segment was processed independently. In addition, we extracted 384-dimensional sentence embeddings (all-MiniLM-L6-v2: https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2) using cumulative word sequences. All embeddings were aligned to BOLD images by averaging within the TR windows (1.5 s).

We evaluated five encoding models using MultipleKernelRidgeCV5 to predict BOLD time series averaged across subjects: (Model 1) Whisper language model (base model) using language embeddings with 30-second sliding windows, (Model 2) Sentence-segmented whisper: language embeddings using sentence-boundary audio segmentation, (Model 3) SBERT using sentence embeddings with cumulative word sequences, (Model 4) SBERT_add, using sentence embeddings prepended with the complete previous sentence, and (Model 5) Multimodal: SBERT jointly modeling all three Whisper feature types (acoustic, speech, language) with SBERT cumulative embeddings using MultipleKernelRidgeCV. The predicted results were correlated to the actual BOLD signal and shown in Figure 2a.

MultipleKernelRidgeCV from the himalaya library (https://gallantlab.org/himalaya/) was used for model fitting with kernel-specific regularization, enabling automatic estimation of the relative importance of each embedding type in predicting brain activity. Finally, to test cross-narrative prediction accuracy, models were trained on one story and tested on all four stories including the training story itself.

Results

Prediction performance revealed distinct neural representations captured by Whisper and SBERT embeddings across brain networks (Figure 2). 

Models trained and tested on the Black dataset showed divergent patterns (Figure 2a, upper panel). Model 1 exhibited moderate correlations, with approximately 90% of ROIs showing r > 0.2 (p<0.001), while Model 2 had fewer ROIs exceeding this threshold. Models 3 and 4 exhibited high correlations (r > 0.2, p<0.001) across all plotted ROIs, suggesting potential overfitting. Model 5 displayed training patterns similar to Model 2.

We first tested on the Forgot dataset (Figure 2a, middle panel), Model 1 exhibited correlations exceeding 0.2 (p<0.001) spanning DMN, DA, and VA. Model 2 showed some DA correlations absent in Model 1 but overall inferior performance, likely due to sentence-level audio segmentation providing insufficient contextual information for robust brain activity prediction. Model 3 demonstrated similar spatial patterns to Model 1 but with lower prediction accuracy. Model 4, which incorporated preceding sentence context, showed marginal improvement over Model 3, though differences were minimal for single-narrative prediction. Model 5 was outperformed by Model 1 in most regions, suggesting that MultipleKernelRidgeCV identified only limited ROIs where multimodal integration provided substantial benefit, possibly due to overlapping linguistic representations creating feature conflicts rather than complementarity. Predictably, only Model 1 predicted EAC activity; Models 2-5 showed no EAC correlations, consistent with absence of acoustic-phonetic encoding capacity in SBERT.

Averaged across test narratives (Figure 2a, bottom panel), Model 1 maintained consistent DMN correlations, while Model 3 showed modest DA correlations (r > 0.1, p<0.001) in specific regions where Model 1 showed no correlation. Model 4 showed marginal improvements over Model 3, clearer than in single-narrative prediction, suggesting that explicit contextual information enhances prediction performance across narratives. Model 5 showed fewer overall correlations but demonstrated SBERT-based superiority in specific DA and VA areas.

Network-averaged correlations (Figures 2b,c) quantified these patterns. For the Forgot dataset (Figure 2b), Model 1 showed higher overall performance but was comparable to Models 3 and 4 in DA, revealing network-specific advantages of SBERT-based semantic embeddings. Averaged across test narratives (Figure 2c), SBERT demonstrated superior performance in DA and VA. For EAC, SBERT-based models showed negative correlations while Model 1 maintained positive correlations, providing clear evidence that Whisper captures acoustic-phonetic features processed in EAC, whereas SBERT captures higher-order semantic content.

Conclusion

This study systematically compared Whisper and SBERT embeddings for predicting cortical responses during narrative comprehension, the results revealed complementary network-specific strengths supporting distinct acoustic-phonetic versus semantic representations.

The Whisper-based model using 30-second sliding windows demonstrated robust performance in DMN and EAC, effectively capturing both acoustic-phonetic features and continuous narrative flow. In contrast, sentence-segmented Whisper yielded consistently low correlations, suggesting that narrative processing depends on cross-sentence contextual integration—information from preceding sentences continues to shape subsequent processing, requiring temporal windows that extend beyond individual sentence boundaries. 

Interestingly, in EAC, while Whisper embeddings showed positive correlations as expected from an ASR model, SBERT-based models showed negative correlations with brain activity. This negative relationship is theoretically significant: SBERT embeddings exhibit large variations for content words (nouns, verbs) but minimal change for function words and phonological features. The negative correlation suggests that EAC preferentially responds to acoustic-phonetic properties of words and rhythmic elements rather than lexical-semantic content. This dissociation indicates that finer-grained analysis at the word or sub-sentence level may reveal more nuanced encoding principles in early auditory regions. 

Contrary to our initial hypothesis regarding semantic comprehension, SBERT-based models revealed distinct advantages specifically in DA and VA networks. Rather than capturing general semantic understanding, SBERT appears to encode narrative transitions and attentional shifts,processes that engage attentional mechanisms during story comprehension. The addition of preceding sentence context showed minimal effect for single-narrative prediction but clearer benefits when generalizing across narratives, suggesting that explicit inter-sentence context becomes valuable for capturing invariant narrative transition patterns. Future work incorporating multiple preceding sentences could clarify optimal context window sizes for modeling these attentional dynamics. 

However, multimodal integration combining Whisper and SBERT embeddings unexpectedly underperformed the Whisper baseline. MultipleKernelRidgeCV using features with overlapping linguistic characteristics may introduce feature conflicts, when embeddings capture similar linguistic information, their combination creates redundancy rather than synergy. This suggests straightforward feature combination is insufficient for leveraging distinct representational strengths. We propose that future approaches should employ region-specific model selection as the primary strategy. Using BandedRidgeCV to identify regions where each model excels, then aggregating region-specific predictions may better capitalize on complementary strengths, such as Whisper for acoustic-phonetic processing in EAC and narrative comprehension in DMN, SBERT for narrative transitions in DA and VA. Additionally, employing higher-performance models (e.g., larger Whisper models, higher-performance embedding models) could further clarify network-specific representational preferences and improve prediction accuracy.
