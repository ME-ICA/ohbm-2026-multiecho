Introduction

Autism spectrum disorders (ASD) are a collection of development disorders characterized by communication issues, repetitive movements and often mood symptoms and affect nearly 3% of the population1. After over a century of investigating ASD no single underlying mechanism or reliable treatment has been identified, with current medications only providing effective relief to less than half of patients1. Increased inflammation and glymphatic system dysregulation, potential actionable treatment targets, have been a proposed component of ASD pathology2. The glymphatic system facilitates brain waste clearance via perivascular pathways and its functionality can be indirectly assessed non-invasively using diffusion tensor imaging along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS)3. Separately, free water (FW) imaging quantifies extracellular water fraction, with elevations potentially reflecting neuroinflammation4. Studies examining the underlying glymphatic and neuroinflammation dysfunction in ASD, and its link to symptoms and age remains limited. Quantifying these interrelated biological systems in ASD may reveal distinct pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the disorder. Here, we apply both DTI-ALPS and FW imaging to the large-scale Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) dataset to examine: 1) differences in ALPS and FW metrics in ASD versus typically developing (TD), 2) the ALPS-FW relationship; 3) age-dependent changes from 5 to 64 years of age; and 4) FW and ALPS associations with symptoms.

Methods

Diffusion and structural T1-weighted (T1w) MRI, clinical and cognitive data from 146 ASD and age and sex matched 128 typically developing (TD) participants (ages 5-64 years) were obtained from ABIDE II5. Clinical measures included the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS), Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Autism Diagnostic Interview (ADI), Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ) and Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scale. Age at exam, Full-Scale IQ (FIQ), sex, handedness and multi-site location data were used as covariates. Participants were additionally stratified into age tertiles (Young: 5-10y,43 ASD,23 TD; Middle: 10-19y, n=52 ASD, 54 TD; Old: 20-64y, 51 ASD, 51 TD). Diffusion data contained a b=0 along with a single shell and preprocessing included noise, Gibbs ringing and eddy-current correction using FSL and Mrtrix toolboxes, distortion correction using Synb0-DISCO and the T1-w image and registration to MNI space using ANTS software for voxel-wise analyses. The DTI-ALPS index was calculated bilaterally using an automated pipeline which created FA maps and placed 5 mm-diameter ROIs for X and Y on the FA fiber bundles at the lateral ventricle body level. Subsequently, the ALPS index was calculated from the values of X, Y and Z inside the ROIs of the association and projection ROIs: ALPS index= (mean (Dxxproj,Dxxassoci))/(mean (Dyyproj,Dzzassoci)) for each hemisphere and whole brain. FW imaging was performed using DIPY software and a bi-tensor model, extracting FW fraction across 116 Harvard-Oxford atlas regions for ROI analyses and transforming FW maps to MNI space for voxel-wise analyses. FSL’s randomize tool was used with 5,000 permutations to conduct voxel-wise analyses and python was used to conduct ROI analyses which included ANCOVA’s to test group differences and interactions, and regressions of ALPS-FW coupling and FW/ALPS clinical relationships controlling for covariates. A p < 0.05 and p < 0.05 FDR-corrected significance level was used for analyses.

Results

Compared to TD, ASD showed increased FW in widespread regions through voxel-wise analyses (p <0.05 but not p < 0.05 FDR-corrected), and in the left cingulate, left superior temporal gyrus, left pallidum and right superior frontal gray matter at p < .05 FDR corrected-level via ROI analyses (Figure 1a). The ASD group had significantly lower ALPS indices (p = 0.005-0.025) consistent with other studies, but this went away after controlling for age, sex, FIQ, handedness and site (driven by age). Age was significantly positively related to the ALPS indices in all groups (p < 0.01). The ALPS indices were significantly positively associated with FW, most notably in frontal, hippocampal and cerebellar regions via the voxel-wise analyses and ROIs analyses at the p < 0.05 FDR-corrected level (Figure panel 1b & 2a-b). In the ASD group, increased FW, particularly in subcortical hippocampus, pallidum, thalamus, amygdala, caudate and putamen areas at p < 0.05 FDR-correction-level, was associated with an increase in all ADOS, BDI and ADI scores, where increase scores indicate increase symptoms, and decreased Vineland scores, where decreased scores indicate poorer functioning (Figure panel 1C and Figure panel 2D-E).

When the groups were broken down by age, the ALPS-FW correlation was strongly positive in young TD (r=0.86, p<0.001) but significantly weaker in young ASD (r=0.41, p=0.02; Fisher's z=-2.61, p=0.009), suggesting disrupted coordination between glymphatic function and extracellular water homeostasis early in development (Figure panel 2a). This correlation difference normalized in older age groups (Figure Panel 2a). In the Adolescent group, FW was the most significantly increased compared to TD, and had the strongest positive correlation to symptoms, correlating positively with ADOS communication deficits across 39/116 regions surviving FDR correction (Figure Panel 2b-e). Strongest effects appeared in limbic structures: left hippocampus (r=0.32, p=0.004), right amygdala (r=0.31), and left frontal pole (r=0.32). Finally, ASD showed reduced ALPS versus TD in the oldest tertile only (d=-0.52, p=0.02), with trajectories diverging by symptom severity, which was established by SRS > 76 severe (N=99), and < 76 less severe (N=123) and a significant age by severity interaction (p = 0.022*)  (Figure Panel 2f).

Conclusion

We show increased FW in ASD in widespread regions, which is related to an increased DTI-ALPS metric of glymphatic function and increased symptoms. Higher FW is indicative of increased extracellular space and fluid, where there is more room for perivascular flow and more need for drainage, and thereby should co-occur with an increased ALPS index4. There may be a unique compromised coordination between perivascular clearance and extracellular water regulation early in development in ASD. It is clear in the young TD group this relationship is very strong and may underlie critical developmental reactive immune responses. The strong FW increase in ASD in adolescence and its association with communication correlations in limbic regions implicate extracellular pathology in core autism symptoms, and points to a critical intervention period before this point. Finally, other research has found ALPS index to increase steadily and peak around middle-age. Here we see a significant positive relationship between age and ALPS across ages, except for those in the severe symptoms ASD group which have a dramatic decrease compared to the TD or low symptoms ASD starting in adolescence. A decreased ALPS is thought to be related to impaired metabolic waste clearance and accumulation of neurotoxic proteins, further pointing to the need for intervention in the severe symptom group before this change. Our findings add to the growing literature on the important role of the glymphatic system in ASD development and posits ALPS and FW as promising biomarkers of symptoms and development in ASD.
