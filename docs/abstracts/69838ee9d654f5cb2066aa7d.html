Introduction

Early-life stress (ELS) is a major risk factor for later emotional and behavioral problems and has been consistently linked to altered brain structure. However, far less is known about the neural correlates of resilience, the capacity to maintain adaptive functioning despite adversity.1 Prior neuroimaging studies have been limited by small or selective samples and by resilience definitions that do not account for differences in stressor exposure.2 Using the stressor reactivity (SR) residual approach, resilience can be quantified as the extent to which individuals function better or worse than expected given their level of ELS.3 This exposure-sensitive method enables identification of brain correlates specifically linked to stress adaptation rather than psychopathology alone. Here, using a large population-based cohort, we examined whether individual differences in SR to postnatal ELS (birth-10 years) are reflected in cortical and subcortical brain structure in early adolescence and validated the SR score against established resilience-promoting characteristics.

Methods

All analyses were preregistered (https://osf.io/hrj8q). Participants were 3268 children from the Generation R population-based cohort who completed structural MRI at age 10 and/or 14 years.4 Postnatal ELS (birth–10 years) was assessed across multiple domains using extensive multi-informant data.5 Resilience was quantified using a stressor reactivity score derived from regressing the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) 6–18 Total Problems scale (mother-reported at age 10) on cumulative ELS; the standardized residuals served as an exposure-sensitive index of resilience, with lower SR scores reflecting higher resilience. To evaluate construct validity, SR scores were correlated with established resilience-promoting characteristics, including temperament (effortful control, negative affectivity, surgency), prosocial behavior, friendship quality, IQ, and self-esteem. Structural MRI data (n = 4293 scans) were processed using the FreeSurfer v6.0 pipeline to derive vertex-wise estimates of cortical thickness and cortical surface area, as well as volumetric measures of subcortical structures. Whole-brain vertex-wise linear mixed models were used to examine associations between SR scores and cortical morphology. Complementary region-of-interest analyses focused on regions implicated in resilience, including the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), PFC/ACC, hippocampus, amygdala, and ventral striatum. All models were adjusted for relevant sociodemographic covariates (child age at MRI, sex, maternal national origin, maternal age at intake, maternal education, and household income). Region-of-interest (ROI) analyses were additionally controlled for intracranial volume. Multiple-comparison correction was implemented using a Monte Carlo cluster-wise correction for vertex-wise analyses and an FDR correction for ROI analyses.

Results

Higher SR scores were correlated with higher negative affectivity (r = 0.30) and lower effortful control (r = -0.09), prosociality (r = -0.27), friendship quality (r = -0.13), and self-esteem (r = -0.31), supporting its validity as an exposure-sensitive index of stressor reactivity. Higher SR scores were associated with smaller cortical surface area across multiple cortical regions (Figure 1). Vertex-wise analyses identified significant clusters in posterior regions, bilateral lateral occipital gyrus (e.g., left cluster = 1134.9 mm²; right cluster = 1082.5 mm²), cuneus (left = 410.6 mm²; right = 1405.2 mm²), and lingual gyrus (left = 1060.9 mm²). Additional clusters were observed in temporal regions (superior temporal gyrus, banks of the superior temporal sulcus, fusiform gyrus) and frontal regions, including the rostral middle frontal gyrus and lateral orbitofrontal cortex. No associations were found for cortical thickness. In region-of-interest analyses, higher SR scores were also associated with smaller right vmPFC surface area (β = -0.04, 95% CI = -0.07 to -0.01, pFDR = .040) and reduced right ventral striatum volume (β = -0.05, 95% CI = -0.08 to -0.01, pFDR = .040). No associations were found for the hippocampus, amygdala, or PFC/ACC, and no age or sex interactions were observed. Follow-up analyses replacing the SR score with the CBCL Total Problems score revealed a different spatial pattern: CBCL scores were negatively associated with cortical surface area mainly in frontal regions, with no effects in posterior or lateral temporal cortices. ROI models using CBCL showed broader associations, including bilateral vmPFC, bilateral ventral striatum, PFC/ACC, hippocampus, and amygdala. 

Conclusion

In a large population-based birth cohort, higher SR was associated with smaller cortical surface area in posterior visual-sensory regions, temporal areas involved in social perception, and frontal regions supporting regulatory functions, as well as reduced volume in reward-related structures, including the right ventral striatum and right vmPFC. These patterns suggest that higher SR is reflected in structural reductions across multiple functional systems, and that posterior visual–sensory pathways, in addition to prefrontal and reward circuits, may play an important, previously underrecognized role in ELS vulnerability. Importantly, when analyses were repeated using psychopathology scores alone, the neuroanatomical pattern shifted toward frontal and limbic regions, with no involvement of posterior or lateral temporal cortices. These differences indicate that models based solely on psychopathology capture a distinct neuroanatomical pattern compared to models that account for stressor exposure via the SR score. Together, these highlight the importance of exposure-sensitive approaches for identifying neural markers linked to resilience rather than general symptom severity.
