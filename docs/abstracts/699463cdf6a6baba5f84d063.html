Introduction

Precision functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) depends on our ability to accurately localize functional regions of interest (ROI) in individual brains. Traditionally, researchers use single-contrast localizers to identify functional ROIs. For example, to identify the language network, reading sentences is contrasted with reading nonwords in each individual and the resulting maps are thresholded so that all voxels above that threshold are a part of the ROI.1 The threshold is defined either by a fixed statistical criterion across individuals (e.g., a t-value cutoff) or a percentage of activated voxels within each individual (e.g., top 10% voxels). Both approaches have clear disadvantages: fixed statistical thresholds make the size of the ROI signal-to-noise dependent and percentile thresholds ignore any true inter-individual differences in ROI size.

Here, we propose to use a wider battery of tasks to localize functional ROIs in the brain. In this approach, a diverse set of tasks is designed to sample a broad range of brain functions. The multi-dimensional nature of these batteries allows functional localization of multiple regions including the functional ROI. We evaluate this approach using both simulations and empirical fMRI data. In simulations, we compare the two approaches and show the costs of using specific thresholding methods in single-contrast localizers. We then extend this comparison to real fMRI data, where we apply the two approaches to identifying the language network in the human cerebellum.

Methods

To compare single-contrast functional localizers to multi-task localizers, we acquired a dataset with tasks covering multiple domains, but with a focus on language-comprehension tasks. The dataset included 16 subjects and 16 tasks including rest. We included traditional language localizer tasks: sentences reading, nonwords reading, intact passage listening and degraded passage listening. Each task was present once in each run for a total of 8 runs. Tasks were presented in 30-second blocks with a 5-second instruction period before each task. Beta weights were modeled using a general linear model (GLM) and cerebellar data were extracted into the SUIT group space. From these data, we then constructed different localization scans of 8 min length. For example, in a battery containing four 30-second tasks, each task would appear four times – therefore we averaged the empirical beta estimates from 4 runs for each task. Following this approach, all localizers used in this study were matched for the total amount of scanning time (5 minutes for simulations and 8 minutes for fMRI localizers).

First, to compare single-contrast and multi-task approaches to functional localization, we simulated data for 1000 individuals for five tasks across a five-region pixel grid, with the central yellow region serving as the target region to be localized (Fig. 1c). We defined functional signal-to-noise (fSNR) as the ratio of the task-evoked signal variance to noise variance, and varied it across individuals using a distribution estimated from an empirical fMRI dataset (Fig. 1d). We also varied the size of the target region across individuals. We implemented 3 types of localizers: (1) a single-contrast localizer with a fixed absolute threshold (“contrast-fixed”), (2) a single-contrast localizer with an adaptive percentile threshold (“contrast-adaptive”), and (3) a multi-task localizer that assigns each pixel to the parcel with the most similar multi-task response profile. For the single contrast localizers, the threshold was calibrated such that the average estimated ROI size matched the average true region size across individuals. Localization accuracy was quantified as the Dice overlap between the true target region and the estimated ROI for each individual and localizer type.

Second, to compare the three localizers in a real fMRI application, we addressed the question of localizing the language network in the human cerebellum. We assembled a battery of five tasks: sentence reading, theory of mind, tongue movement, spatial navigation and a working memory task. We then used this battery to parcellate the right cerebellar hemisphere of each individual into 5 regions based on a modified version of the NettekovenSym32 cerebellar atlas.2 We combined all motor (M1-M4), all action-observation (A1-A3) regions and all demand regions (D1-D4) into a single motor, action and demand region. We grouped socio-linguistic regions S1 and S2 into a single language region. The remaining sociolinguistic regions (S3-S5) defined the last and fifth region. To generate single-contrast localizers, we used t-value maps for the sentence reading vs nonwords reading contrast. These maps were thresholded using either a fixed or an adaptive threshold. We calibrated the threshold such that the average estimated ROI size per subject was the same as average ROI size for the multi-task localizer. This resulted in a uncorrected t-threshold of 1.47 for the fixed threshold, or the selection of the 10.6% most active voxels in the right cerebellar hemisphere. To evaluate the selectivity and specificity of the three localizers, we used two independent contrasts: (1) target contrast (Intact passage vs degraded passage listening), representing a language comprehension contrast, and (2) control contrast (n-back vs rest), representing a working memory contrast. For each contrast, mean activation across voxels was extracted inside and outside the language ROI for each subject and localizer type.

Results

The main difference between the two approaches can be seen in Fig 1a-b. In the single-contrast approach, the contrast between the two tasks defines a single dimension, along which researchers then need to define a threshold - all voxels above this threshold are considered part of the ROI. One option for choosing this threshold is to use a fixed statistical cutoff for the activation values (contrast-fixed). This approach, however, has the problem that the estimated size of the selected region will depend on the fSNR (Fig. 1e). To remedy this situation, often researchers use an adaptable threshold that selects a certain percentage of the highest activity voxels in a specific brain structure (contrast-adaptive). This approach is fSNR independent (Fig 1e), but is blind to true differences in the size of the functional region (Fig. 1f).

To address these issues, we suggest characterizing each voxel using multiple dimensions instead of one (Fig. 1b). In this framework, each voxel is characterized by its response to multiple tasks. A voxel at the origin of the space would show the same value for all tasks (i.e. would show no response). The distance from the origin would therefore indicate the strength of the response, and the direction of the vector characterizes the response profile across tasks. Each region then also has a specific response profile (green arrows). Instead of thresholding, each voxel is then assigned to the single region with the most similar response profile. This has the distinct advantage that the voxel assignment is not sensitive to the fSNR, which only influences the distance of the voxel from the origin and not its direction, but is sensitive to differences in ROI size (Fig 1e-f). Across all individuals, the multi-task localizer showed the highest accuracy when compared to the single-contrast localizers (Fig. 1g).

Comparing the two approaches using real fMRI data, we found that both approaches defined ROIs that included the medial part of Crus I and II (Fig. 1h-i). However, the overlap between individuals was much stronger for the multi-task (average dice = 0.26,SEM = 0.007, t(15) = 29.18, p <0.001) than for contrast-fixed (average dice =0.12, SEM = 0.006, t(15) = 17.59, p < 0.001) and contrast-adaptive (average dice = 0.12, SEM= 0.003, t(15) = 39.35, p < 0.001). The main difference between the localization methods was that the single-contrast localizers also selected regions in lobule VI, VIIb and VIIIa, previously described as being as parts of the multi-demand network.2

To assess the selectivity and specificity of the language ROI for each localizer type, we extracted the mean activation within and outside the language ROI for a target and control contrast (Fig. 1j). For the target contrast (dashed), we found that voxels inside of the putative language ROI indeed showed the expected higher activity for intact passages compared to degraded passages. This contrast did not show any difference for multi-task vs contrast-fixed, t(15) = −1.09, p = 0.29, and for multi-task vs contrast adaptive, t(15) = −1.13, p = 0.28. The difference between the two approaches became visible when we looked at the control contrast. The multi-task localizer showed substantially lower activity when compared to the single-contrast localizers for the control contrast inside the language ROI (purple solid bars) t(15) = −4.76, p < 0.001 for multi-task vs contrast-fixed and t(15) = −4.53 , p < 0.001 for multi-task vs contrast-adaptive.

Conclusion

In this work, we systematically compared traditional single-contrast localizers with a novel multi-task approach to functional localization. By constructing localizers using each approach, we directly assessed how thresholding choices and task-dimensionality shape the accuracy, selectivity and specificity, and inter-subject consistency of the estimated functional ROIs. Our simulations revealed that single-contrast localizers are either dependent on fSNR or insensitive to true differences in region size, while multitask localizers are not dependent on fSNR and are sensitive to true differences in region size. In cerebellar language mapping, both approaches identify a similar language region in the medial part of Crus I and II previously described as a core language region,3 but they differed in the extent of additional areas selected. Single-contrast localizers additionally included regions previously described as a part of the multiple demand network. Additionally, the multitask localizer showed higher spatial consistency across subjects.

Overall, our results highlight how the choice of localization approach -whether single-contrast or multi-task- fundamentally shapes the resulting ROI definitions, emphasizing the importance of understanding the trade-offs of each approach.
