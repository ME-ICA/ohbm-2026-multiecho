Introduction

Two major advances have reshaped human neuroimaging over the past decade: large-scale multi-site consortia (e.g., Human Connectome Project, ABCD Study) and dense-sampling studies involving intensive, repeated scanning of individuals over weeks or months. Consortium studies enable population-level inference through standardized data collection across thousands of participants, while dense-sampling studies maximize temporal precision by collecting high-frequency data from single individuals. Both approaches address the limitations of traditional small, underpowered neuroimaging samples. However, each approach lacks the strengths of the other.

Here we introduce the Neurobiographical Imaging Consortium (NIC), a novel framework that combines the benefits of both approaches through a uniquely positioned population: neuroimaging researchers who collect intensive multimodal data on themselves. This self-imaging model offers several advantages. First, researchers have unparalleled access to imaging infrastructure and analytic expertise. Second, self-experimentation eliminates participant recruitment burden and maximizes compliance with demanding protocols. Third, annual data collection bursts enable longitudinal tracking of within-person brain-body dynamics across years—capturing processes like hormonal fluctuations, stress responses, aging, and life events at a resolution unachievable in traditional studies. Fourth, data sharing among consortium members will allow the consortium to leverage the combined intellectual and analytic capacity of participating labs.

We present the consortium framework, standardized protocols for 30-day intensive multimodal data collection, and plans for preliminary analyses from the first pilot study.

Methods

Consortium Structure

Our goal is for the NIC to ultimately comprise 10-50 neuroimaging investigators who collect 30 consecutive days of multimodal data on themselves annually. Each investigator serves as both researcher and participant, collecting daily neuroimaging, biomarker, physiological, and behavioral data using standardized protocols. Data are shared through secure infrastructure, enabling cross-investigator analyses while preserving the within-person precision of dense-sampling designs. Repeated annual bursts allow longitudinal modeling of intra-individual change unachievable in even the most intensive traditional consortium studies.

30-Day Multimodal Protocol

We have developed a comprehensive daily data collection protocol encompassing neuroimaging, biomarkers, ecological momentary assessment, and wearable physiological monitoring.

Daily MRI (~60 min): Each session includes a field map for distortion correction, resting-state fMRI to capture intrinsic network variability, and cognitive tasks probing multiple domains including working memory, executive function, emotion processing, language, social cognition, and motor function. Structural scans (T1, T2), diffusion MRI, and arterial spin labeling perfusion imaging are collected at regular intervals throughout the month. MRI parameters follow Human Connectome Project standards to facilitate harmonization across sites and scanners.

Biomarker Sampling: Daily first-morning urine and saliva samples capture hormonal and stress-related biomarkers (cortisol, α-amylase, reproductive hormones). Weekly venous blood draws provide comprehensive panels including hormones (reproductive, thyroid, metabolic), inflammatory markers (hsCRP, cytokines), and hematology. Stool samples collected twice weekly enable microbiome characterization. This multimodal biomarker approach captures dynamics across the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, immune system, and gut-brain axis.

Ecological Momentary Assessment: Participants complete app-based surveys multiple times daily assessing sleep quality, mood, stress, pain, fatigue, social interactions, and health behaviors (caffeine, alcohol, exercise, medications). These measures contextualize brain and biomarker data within daily lived experience.

Wearable Monitoring: Continuous smartwatch monitoring captures heart rate, heart rate variability, physical activity, and sleep architecture, providing objective physiological context without additional participant burden.

Pilot Study

We are conducting an initial 30-day pilot on the first author to evaluate protocol feasibility, refine procedures, and generate preliminary data. If available by the time of the meeting, we will present initial analyses examining test-retest reliability of brain measures across days and relationships between daily biomarkers, brain function, and behavioral states.

Data Management

MRI data are organized in BIDS format. Biomarker samples are tracked via laboratory information management system with standardized labeling. All data streams are time-locked and integrated for multivariate time-series analysis of dynamic brain-body-environment relationships.

Results

Protocol Development

We have developed comprehensive protocols and supporting materials for 30-day intensive multimodal data collection. The protocol balances scientific depth—capturing brain structure, function, connectivity, perfusion, and multiple biomarker systems—with practical feasibility for extended daily participation. Implementation materials include daily operator checklists, participant instruction sheets, standardized sample collection and labeling procedures, and quality control workflows. These materials are designed for adaptation across institutions with varying resources and infrastructure.

Consortium Infrastructure

We have established data management infrastructure using BIDS-compatible organization for neuroimaging data and integrated tracking systems for biomarker and behavioral data. Cross-institutional standardization guidelines address MRI acquisition harmonization (fixed parameters, phantom-based calibration), containerized cognitive task software ensuring identical stimuli and timing across sites, and centralized biomarker assay protocols conducted at Penn State to minimize inter-site variability. An implementation manual with site checklists, example data, and training materials will support onboarding of new consortium members.

Scientific Applications

The NIC framework enables investigation of questions inaccessible to traditional neuroimaging designs. Examples include: (1) How do brain network configurations fluctuate with hormonal cycles, stress, sleep, and daily experiences? (2) What is the temporal relationship between peripheral inflammatory markers and brain functional connectivity? (3) How do major life events (job changes, births, losses) alter brain-body dynamics, and over what timescale do effects emerge and resolve? (4) How do relationships between brain, physiology, and behavior change across the lifespan when tracked longitudinally within individuals? As one specific example, our pilot study will examine relationships between ovarian hormones and pain-related brain activity—a question where prior research has produced inconsistent findings partly due to imprecise hormone measurement.

Preliminary Pilot Findings

At the time of the meeting, we anticipate presenting preliminary analyses from the 30-day pilot. Planned analyses include test-retest reliability of resting-state functional connectivity across days, within-person covariation of biomarkers and brain measures, and initial time-series modeling of brain-body dynamics. These analyses will demonstrate the feasibility of intensive self-imaging and provide initial insights into the temporal structure of within-person variability.

Conclusion

The Neurobiographical Imaging Consortium represents a novel approach to human neuroimaging that leverages the unique position of imaging researchers to generate unprecedented datasets for understanding dynamic brain-body-environment relationships. By combining the temporal precision of dense-sampling studies with the cross-participant scope of consortium approaches, this framework can address fundamental questions about within-person variability that remain inaccessible to traditional research designs.

The self-imaging model offers distinctive advantages that make this approach uniquely feasible. Investigators bring domain expertise to protocol optimization, data collection, and quality control. Self-experimentation eliminates the substantial challenges of recruiting participants for month-long daily studies and ensures high compliance with demanding protocols. There is also potential for an initial distributed funding model where investigators initially leverage local resources for the initial wave of data collection before we seek external funding. This approach creates a sustainable, scalable path to consortium development without requiring large upfront investment.

Perhaps most importantly, annual data collection bursts enable true longitudinal tracking across years. While even the most intensive traditional longitudinal studies sample participants only a handful of times, consortium members collecting 30 days of data each year will accumulate rich longitudinal trajectories. This opens possibilities for studying transitions (menopause, retirement, parenthood), recovery from events (illness, stress, loss), and aging trajectories with exceptional temporal and biological resolution.

Although the consortium population is unlikely to be representative of the general population on all demographic axes, the resulting datasets will offer unprecedented depth of multimodal integration and temporal resolution. Furthermore, standardized data collection across multiple investigators of different ages, genders, and demographics will add some of the benefits of generalizability achieved in multi-participant studies. These data will complement larger, more representative consortium studies by revealing dynamic processes that can then be tested with targeted designs in broader populations.

We invite interested neuroimaging researchers to join the consortium as we expand beyond the pilot phase. The NIC offers an opportunity to contribute to—and benefit from—a shared resource while generating novel insights into brain-body dynamics at a level of detail not achievable through any existing research paradigm.
