Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) disrupts the functional architecture of the central nervous system by reshaping the coordination of large-scale brain networks. Resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) captures how the brain reorganization unfolds in real time, thanks to its high temporal resolution. Within this framework, EEG microstate analysis summarizes ongoing brain activity as a sequence of brief, quasi-stable topographical maps that have been linked to canonical resting-state networks (RSN), including auditory, visual, salience, and attentional systems.

Recent high-density EEG studies in relapsing–remitting MS without overt cognitive impairment have shown altered temporal dynamics of several microstate classes. These changes correlate with disease duration and information processing speed, suggesting that microstates could capture an early signature of network reorganization in MS1,2.

However, most studies on microstates have focused on sensor-level temporal parameters. The underlying cortical generators, their connectivity architecture, or their potential for patient classification are largely unexplored. Here, we developed an EEG microstate analysis pipeline that combines (i) scalp-level microstate segmentation, (ii) distributed electrical source imaging, (iii) source-level functional connectivity, (iv) graph-based metrics, and (v) microstate-specific classification using a support vector machine (SVM). The framework aims to characterize the microstate-specific networks in MS and evaluate their ability to discriminate MS at the single-subject level.

Methods

EEG data and preprocessing. Eyes-closed resting-state EEG data from 51 MS patients and 24 controls were analyzed. Data were band-pass filtered (1-40 Hz), noisy channels were removed, and independent component analysis (ICA) was applied to identify and remove ocular and cardiac artifacts. Rejected channels were interpolated using spherical splines, signals were downsampled to 125 Hz, and re-referenced to the common average. For each subject, five minutes of artifact-free EEG were visually selected, checked to rule out epileptiform discharges or pathological slowing.

Scalp-level microstate analysis. Microstate analysis was performed at the scalp level using the MICROSTATELAB plugin integrated in EEGLAB. Global field power (GFP) was computed, and GFP peaks were extracted to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. Microstate segmentation was then performed on the GFP peak maps using a k-means clustering approach to identify 7 group-level microstate scalp maps. Topographical ANOVA (TANOVA, permutation-based) assessed between group differences (MS vs. controls) in microstate topographies. The resulting group-level templates were then back-fitted to each subject’s continuous EEG. This procedure labelled the EEG as a timeseries of microstates. For each microstate class and subject, we computed temporal parameters: mean duration, occurrence, time coverage, and global explained variance (GEV). Group differences in these parameters were assessed via non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U-test).

Source imaging of microstates. To bridge scalp patterns to their cortical generators, we estimated distributed source activity using the eLORETA algorithm. For each subject and microstate class, source activity was estimated from the EEG segments labelled with the corresponding microstate, obtaining microstate-specific source activation maps. TANOVA was applied at the source level to assess group differences in the spatial distribution of microstate generators.

Microstate functional connectivity. Functional connectivity was calculated separately for each microstate in source space. All corresponding epochs were concatenated, and the weighted phase lag index (wPLI) was computed between all pairs of sources. Connectivity matrices were parcellated in regions-of-interest (ROIs) using a mixed atlas that combines cortical parcels from the Schaefer atlas with subcortical anatomical regions. The outcome is an ROI-to-ROI connectivity matrix per microstate and subject.

Graph-theoretical analysis. Graph-theoretical metrics that reflect node centrality and global network organization were extracted for each ROI-level connectivity matrix. For each graph feature, we computed descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis). Group comparisons (controls vs. MS) were performed on these features individually using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (Mann-Whitney U-test) and the classification power was assessed using the area under the ROC curve (AUC). 

Classification. Finally, for each microstate independently, feature ranking was performed with recursive feature elimination (RFE), and an SVM classifier was trained on the selected features under 5-fold cross-validation. Balanced accuracy, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and F1-Score were compared across microstates to determine which microstate-specific networks carried the strongest discriminative information.

Results

Scalp-level temporal results. At the scalp level, group comparisons of temporal parameters did not reveal robust differences between MS and controls. In our preliminary analysis, no statistically significant group effects were found for microstate duration or time coverage for any of the seven microstate classes (Mann–Whitney U-test, all p > 0.05). Global explained variance showed a similar pattern, with no clear separation between groups.

Classification performance of microstate networks. In contrast, the machine-learning analysis of microstate-specific network features showed clear discriminative power. When training SVM classifiers separately for each microstate, feature selection with RFE identified informative subsets for microstates A, C, and E, while the remaining microstates performed close to chance. For these three microstates, balanced accuracies reached 68%, 74%, and 73%, with corresponding MCC of 0.49, 0.52, and 0.52, respectively, whereas the other microstates showed balanced accuracies around ≈50–60% and negligible MCC, indicating limited discriminative value.

Discriminant network features of microstates. Inspection of the graph features selected by RFE showed that, for microstate A, the most informative predictors were centrality measures in broadband and low-gamma networks. The strongest feature was broadband betweenness kurtosis, higher in MS than controls (p = 0.0053, AUC = 0.701), indicating a more heavy-tailed distribution of betweenness and thus a more hub-dominated network. Low-gamma eigenvector skewness (AUC = 0.596) went in the same direction (first row in Fig. 2). For microstate C, the main discriminator was low-gamma characteristic path length (p = 3.9×10⁻⁴, AUC = 0.756), with higher values in MS, consistent with reduced global integration and less efficient communication. Beta-band betweenness kurtosis showed a trend (p = 0.0666, AUC = 0.632), again suggesting a less evenly distribution of relay nodes (second row in Fig. 2). In microstate E, low-gamma global efficiency (p = 0.0021, AUC = 0.721) together with skewness of low-gamma closeness (p = 0.0098, AUC = 0.686) and degree (p = 0.0249, AUC = 0.662) were most informative. This pattern indicates that, in MS, communication becomes more concentrated in a restricted set of highly central nodes (third row in Fig. 2).

Conclusion

We developed an EEG microstate pipeline that links scalp segmentation to source microstates connectivity, graph metrics, and subject classification in MS. Despite the absence of significant group differences in temporal parameters (duration, coverage, and global explained variance), microstate-specific network features showed clear discriminative power. SVM classifiers based on graph features from microstates A, C, and E achieved balanced accuracies up to 74% with substantial MCC values.

These findings suggest that alterations in MS are better captured from microstate-specific network organization than by temporal dynamics alone. The classification power of microstate E is in line with previous work reporting alterations of this map in MS1, reinforcing its relevance as an electrophysiological marker of disease-related network changes, typically associated with the Default Mode Network3. On the other hand, the low discriminative value of microstate G aligns with studies suggesting that this topological pattern can be disregarded, supporting a six-microstate model2. Moreover, the mean microstate duration of ~41 ms observed here is consistent with recent reports that revised microstate time scales, lowering the typical lower bound to approximately 30 ms4.

Some limitations of our work should be acknowledged. The relatively short microstate durations, combined with the use of wPLI on concatenated microstate epochs, could introduce discontinuities that could bias connectivity estimates. wPLI requires a temporally continuous signal to obtain reliable estimates of phase relationships. When short epochs are put together, the transition between segments does not accurately reflect the real physiological continuity. At the boundaries, the phase can jump and generate fluctuations that wPLI could misinterpret as changes in coupling. Therefore, the edge effects might distort the connectivity patterns in some cases (when many short segments are combined). Alternative approaches, such as amplitude envelope correlation, could also be considered in future work to test the robustness of our findings.

Overall, our results show that microstate source-level connectomes can reveal network reorganization and provide clinically meaningful biomarkers for MS diagnosis, even when conventional microstate temporal metrics are uninformative. Other studies have already investigated microstate-related source activation at rest in healthy subjects3 and during sleep and dreaming5, but they did not analyze the underlying brain connections. Significantly, this work opens a different perspective for microstate analysis, shifting the focus from scalp level temporal properties to source brain connectivity.
