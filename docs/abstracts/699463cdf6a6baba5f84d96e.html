Introduction

Remembering and imagining episodic events engage similar patterns of neural activation that largely overlap with the default mode network (DMN). According to the constructive episodic simulation hypothesis, simulating hypothetical events – including episodic future events, counterfactual past events, and atemporal events – relies on the reactivation and recombination of details from episodic autobiographical memory (AM).1 In other words, episodic memory and episodic imagination leverage a shared neurocognitive system to construct mental event simulations, irrespective of temporal direction and veracity. Benoit and Schacter (2015) previously conducted a neuroimaging meta-analysis using Activation Likelihood Estimation (ALE) to investigate this core simulation network, finding several DMN and frontoparietal control network (FPCN) regions of both joint and differential activation between AM and hypothetical event simulation.2 However, this study is now a decade old and is limited by its analytic approach (ALE) and the scope of its examined contrasts in that it did not assess hypothetical event simulation alone vs. control tasks. In this updated meta-analysis, we utilized a new meta-analytic technique, seed-based d-mapping (SDM), that not only accounts for spatial location of activations across studies but also effect sizes, resulting in more robust and informative meta-analytic maps. We assessed joint and differential activation patterns during episodic AM and hypothetical event simulation, as well as during hypothetical event simulation compared to control tasks as part of a meta-regression that examined the effects of control task heterogeneity on activation patterns.

Methods

We searched the literature for neuroimaging studies that assessed brain activity during hypothetical event simulation that compared activity to AM and/or a control task. The initial search yielded 741 results, with an additional 21 studies identified from other sources (citation tracking, prior literature searches, etc.), resulting in a total of 762 studies screened for eligibility. Selected studies had to report whole brain peak coordinates in standardized space (MNI or Talairach) for at least one of the following contrasts: Hypothetical simulation + AM > control (15 studies, N=316); hypothetical simulation > AM (14 studies, N=322; note that very few studies reported results for AM > hypothetical simulation and so a meta-analysis of this contrast was not computed); hypothetical simulation > control (25 studies; N=661). Studies reporting only ROI analyses were excluded. See Figure 1 for the PRISMA flowchart.


SDM is a coordinate-based neuroimaging meta-analysis (CBMA) method that combines the reported peak coordinates of activation from multiple studies to create meta-analytic activation maps for a given condition.3 Whereas other CBMA methods such as ALE consider only the spatial location of peak coordinates, SDM incorporates the magnitude of effects at each peak (t/z- statistics). This allows for imputations of effect-size study maps that utilize all available peak information, rather than treating all peaks within a study as equivalent. For included studies, peak coordinates and their corresponding statistics (z/t-scores) were extracted from the tables reporting on relevant contrasts and entered into the SDM software (www.sdmproject.com). A random-effects model was then applied to generate a 3D meta-analytic map, which was subsequently submitted to permutation testing at the imputed subject image level for FWE correction. Significance was determined by threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE), an inference method that takes into account both peak height and cluster extent to calculate a TFCE statistic which was then be converted to a pTFCE value. SDM also allows for the investigation of covariates in the data via meta-regression. We conducted a meta-regression on the hypothetical simulation > control contrast data to assess how simulation-related activation from these contrasts is influenced by the type of control task against which simulation is compared. Control tasks were classified into two broad categories: Simple (e.g., control tasks not involving event simulation, such as semantic and imagery tasks, 13 studies) and complex (e.g., control tasks involving types of event simulation other than episodic AMs/simulations including general or routine events, or repeated instances of constructing the same event simulation, 13 studies).

Results

The results showed robust activation of a common network underlying both hypothetical event simulation and AM retrieval (pTFCE < 0.001). The strongest cluster exhibiting joint activation peaked in the posterior cingulate cortex (SDM-Z=8) and extended throughout the medial prefrontal and parietal cortices and into the medial temporal lobes (MTL) bilaterally. Other clusters in the DMN included bilateral lateral temporal cortex (LTC), inferior parietal lobes (IPL), and posterior cerebellum (crus I, lobules VI and IX). There was also overlapping activity in a few extra-DMN regions, namely right rolandic operculum, right caudate nucleus and left superior occipital gyrus. Hypothetical simulation was reliably associated with greater activity than AM retrieval (pTFCE < 0.05) in some DMN regions (precuneus, posterior cingulate, IPL, cerebellar subregions crus I/II), as well as some extra-DMN regions including bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), right postcentral gyrus, and bilateral cerebellum lobule VI (with the strongest peak for this contrast evident in right lobule VI, SDM-Z=6). Although hypothetical simulation engaged widespread DMN activity when compared to control conditions (pTFCE < 0.001), the meta-regression indicated that activation in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and the bilateral cerebellar lobule IX were modulated by the type of control task used, with less activity evident when more complex control tasks were used (pTFCE < 0.05).

Conclusion

Consistent with the constructive episodic simulation hypothesis, the present meta-analysis found widespread and overlapping DMN activity during AM and hypothetical event simulation, highlighting the importance of this network during the mental simulation of autobiographical events, regardless of temporality. Extending on previous meta-analytic findings showing AM-related activity in cerebellar regions comprising the DMN such as Crus I/II,4 we found reliable activity in Crus I/II and Lobule IX during both AM retrieval and hypothetical event simulation. Despite the high degree of neural overlap, we found differential DMN activity between the two types of event simulation, with hypothetical events engaging precuneus, posterior cingulate and IPL and Crus I/II to a greater level than AM. Previous explanations have linked increased DMN engagement during the construction of hypothetical events to heighted demands on associative processes such as the recombination and integration of details into coherent narratives. Findings of greater activation for hypothetical simulations outside the DMN, in dlPFC, IFG and cerebellar lobule IV, are consistent with increased executive demands during the construction of novel events, including working memory and strategic semantic processes involved in selecting, holding, and integrating disparate details. Interestingly, the previous meta-analysis by Benoit and Schacter (2015) also reported differential activity in the MTL, which we did not find evidence of despite having more studies in our meta-analysis. This disparity could reflect the different meta-analytic techniques used (ALE vs. SDM) and possibly also that effect sizes reported in the literature are consistently smaller in the MTL than other DMN regions. Lastly, the meta-regression found that activity within two DMN regions – cerebellar lobule IX and vmPFC – were indeed modulated by the type of control task used, with greater activity evident when more simple control tasks are used. Given that the complex control tasks were always some form of autobiographical event task, it is unsurprising that contrasts against these control tasks resulted in less DMN activity than when contrasted against simpler control tasks. In particular, the vmPFC is a critical node underpinning self-schema,5 and its common involvement in both the experimental tasks and the complex autobiographical control tasks likely attenuated effects versus when comparing experimental tasks to simple, non-autobiographical tasks. The present study further characterizes the neural network underpinning the mental simulation of autobiographical events, including the identification of specific cerebellar subregions involved in this ability. This work also highlights the importance of considering the nature of the control tasks when designing and interpreting the findings of fMRI studies investigating autobiographical event simulation.
