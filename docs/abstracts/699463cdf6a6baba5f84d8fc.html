Introduction

Individual’s brain development is shaped by both genetic and environmental influences, at large following a typical developmental trajectory. A better understanding of normal brain development trajectory can provide insights into atypical brain structural patterns associated with various neurological and psychiatric disorders along the life span [1]. Existing studies leveraging multimodal MRI have investigated and established normative brain gray matter, white matter developmental templates [1, 2]. Yet, the key genetic factors fundamentally underlie normal brain development is largely under studied. Previous studies focused on genetic variants and gene expression temporal dynamics but did not leverage the whole brain spatial patterns of gene expression. With the transcriptomic data from postmortem brains provided by Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA, https://abagen.readthedocs.io/en/stable/usage.html), several studies have examined spatial patterns of transcriptomic gradients of the human cerebral cortical and the findings support their robustness, even present in other mammalian species, and relevance to capture biological features and normative brain organization [3, 4]. In this study, we evaluated the biological validity of the population-derived typical brain neuroimaging template in late childhood and identified genes underlying the normative brain based on whole-brain transcriptomic spatial pattern. As the proof of concept, we investigated normative brain structural template of 9-10 years old, aiming at revealing the relation of the normative brain pattens with cognition and behavior using a large population cohort, and identifying the specific neuro-developmental genes.

Methods

We analyzed T1-weighted MRI data from a large, cohort of children aged 9–10 years (n = 11842) recruited across multiple sites in USA from the ABCD study (Data Release 5.1). Structural MRI images were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 for spatial registration, segmentation and normalization. The segmented gray matter (GM) images were normalized into the MNI space, smoothed using a Gaussian kernel (6mm). After applying rigorous quality control, a final sample of 11,176 participants’ GM images were analyzed, among which 3,475 participants reported zero scores in Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) problems. A typical brain GM template was generated by averaging across all participants with zero problem scores. Then, for each child, similarity to the typical brain template was quantified using three metrics: Cosine similarity, Euclidean distance similarity, and Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), which provide assessment of how closely each child’s brain is conformed to the normative pattern.

Cognitive ability was assessed using the NIH Toolbox Cognitive Battery, including list sorting, card sort flanker, reading recognition, pattern comparison, picture vocabulary, picture sequence memory, measuring cognitive domain of executive control, response speed, language, working memory and episodic memory. Two composite scores of fluid intelligence and crystallized intelligence gauge the learned skills and innate ability. Behavioral problems were assessed by CBCL extended version, including eight domains, Anxiety, depression, social problem, thought problem, attention problem, rule-breaking, aggressive and somatic complaints, with additional internalizing, externalizing, and total problems. Association analyses between template similarity and these measures were tested using Pearson correlation and Bonferroni multiple test correction. 

The gene expression data were obtained from the AHBA, including gene expression measurements 3702 tissue samples from 6 post-mortem human brains. Following the processing steps suggested in Abagen package[5], we derived gene expression data for 15,632 genes and 486 brain regions using a whole brain atlas including Schaefer 400 cortical regions, and 54 subcortical regions and 32 cerebellar regions. To extract transcriptional features associated with the normative brain GM template, we applied partial least squares (PLS) regression using GM template partitioned into 486 brain regions as the target variable and the gene expression as predictors. Spatial autocorrelation preserved spin permutation (1000 iterations) by BrainSpace package (https://brainspace.readthedocs.io/) was used to evaluate the significance of PLS components, followed by gene-set enrichment analyses by FUMA package (https://fuma.ctglab.nl/) to identify biological pathways of significant genes in the PLS components.

Results

A typical GM template of 9-11 year old children (Figure 1a ) was derived and the similarity to the normative brain template showed significant associations with multiple cognitive and behavioral measures (examples are shown in Figure 1b). Higher Cosine and SSIM similarity values were significantly related to better overall cognitive performance, including improvements in working memory, executive functioning, language, processing speed, and composite cognitive scores from the NIH Toolbox. In parallel, higher similarity values were associated with reduced behavioral problem scores, including both lower internalizing and externalizing problem scores, with the strongest effect on rule-breaking and aggressive problems. These patterns indicate that children whose brain structure more closely resembles the normative template demonstrate more optimal behavioral and cognitive outcomes.

PLS analysis revealed four significant transcriptomic components linking to normative brain structure with empirical permutation p values of 0.001,0.009,0.003,0.008. Component 1 (Figure 1c) showed the strongest (correlation  = 0.42) and significant spatial correspondence with the normative brain, capturing a set of genes (167 genes, threshold |Z|>2.5). The 3 significantly enriched gene sets correspond to midbrain neuronal markers, particularly GABAergic neuron subtypes (HNBGABA: human neuroblast GABA-producing neuron cells and HGABA: human GABAergic neuron cells), indicating that the differentially expressed genes are strongly associated with midbrain neuron GABAergic neurons (Figure 1d). Component 2 also implicated genes involved in neuronal processes, including gene sets of MANNO_MIDBRAIN_NEUROTYPES_HDA2 (Dopamine neuron subtypes, p-value=5.25E-10), GOCC_SYNAPTIC_ MEMBRANE (p-value=2.36E-08), and GOCC_SYNAPSE (p-value=2.73E-07). Together, these findings demonstrate that normative structural patterns align with biologically interpretable transcriptomic features.

Conclusion

Our study of the typical brain template demonstrates broad associations with cognitive ability and behavior problems, consistent with recent large-scale normative modeling studies. Thus, the normative templates have the potential to be utilized as a biologically meaningful reference for assessing normal structural variation. As a proof of concept we only investigated the brain template of  9-11 year old, future in-depth studies along the development and life span such as brain chart [1] are warranted. Integrating spatial maps of gene expression in brain further reveals that normative brain structure is tightly linked to fundamental neurodevelopmental genes. These results highlight the value of normative modeling both for characterizing healthy brain development and for uncovering the genetic architecture that shapes these trajectories.  One main limitation of this study is that our gene expression data of the brain are from adult postmortem brains, presenting adult gene expression pattern. The transcriptomic patterns may reflect genes with relatively stable temporal expression dynamics from late childhood to adulthood. Future work incorporates temporal dynamics of gene expression and age matched associations between brain templates and transcriptomics should be conducted to provide more precise linking between brain structural and molecular features during the development.  Future work will extend these approaches to longitudinal prediction and clinical applications.
