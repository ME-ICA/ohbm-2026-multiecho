Introduction

Autism is primarily characterized by social-communicative difficulties, and atypical face processing has been a core focus of many neuroimaging studies1,2. The fusiform gyrus (FFG), a central region of the face processing network, shows different activation and connectivity patterns in autistic individuals1,3. However, most prior work has largely focused on static functional properties across the whole FFG, leaving it unclear how distinct FFG subdivisions dynamically reconfigure with large-scale brain networks in autism compared to non-autistic individuals (NAI). Furthermore, typical sex differences in face processing exist with females outperforming males on emotional face recognition tasks4. These differences appear diminished in autism6, but it remains unclear whether this attenuation extends to the neurobiological level, particularly at the level of dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) between FFG subregions and the rest of the brain. Using a recent extension of a dFC approach, micro-co-activation patterns (μCAPs) analysis7,8, we aimed to a) identify distinct FFG co-activation networks and their functional subdivisions; and b) characterise their spatial and temporal dynamics as a function of diagnosis and sex. We specifically examined which μCAP networks differ in how often they dynamically couple with the FFG in autistic compared to non-autistic males and females.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data was analyzed from 286 autistic individuals (208 males) and 228 NAI (146 males), aged 6–30 years, from the EU-AIMS Longitudinal European Autism Project. μCAPs were derived from k-means-clustering of dynamic co-activation states using bilateral FFG as the seed region. Unlike conventional dFC methods, μCAPs extract dynamic spatial configurations that reveal functional subdivisions within the seed region (i.e., FFG) (Figure 1). We additionally applied connectopic mapping11 to characterize each μCAP along the principal functional connectivity gradient derived within the FFG capturing the dominant axis of intrinsic functional organization. To temporally characterize the μCAPs, we modelled each μCAP’s occurrence using regression with diagnosis, sex, and their interaction, controlling for age, meanFD, and site. For μCAPs with significant sex-by-diagnosis effects, we further examined dwell time and transition probabilities. To examine spatial characteristics for the μCAP with an occurrence interaction, we thresholded individual μCAP maps at the 90th percentile of the group-level template and quantified normalized suprathreshold voxel counts. Spatial-extent measures were then tested for main and interaction effects of diagnosis and sex, adjusting for age, motion, and site.

Results

We identified six μCAPs co-activating with major networks (ventral/dorsal attention, visual central/peripheral, and two default mode network [DMN] subdivisions), each mapping to distinct FFG subregions (Figure 2). The spatial arrangement of the six μCAPs followed an established posterior-to-anterior gradient along the FFG, with lower-order visual networks mapping posteriorly and higher-order networks mapping more anteriorlysupported by connectopic mapping analyses. There was a significant sex-by-diagnosis interaction for the occurrences of the DMN-C μCAP 3 (β=0.003, Standard Error [SE]=0.001, t=2.88, p=0.004, pFDR=0.025), with NAI females showing more frequent occurrences of this μCAP than NAI males (p<0.001), while no sex difference was observed among autistic individuals (p=0.89). More fine-grained temporal dynamics of μCAP 3 also showed sex-by-diagnosis interactions: NAI females had longer dwell time (β=0.027, SE=0.011, t=2,43 p=0.015), more frequent transitions from baseline state to μCAP 3 (β=0.24, SE= 0.08, Z=2.87, p=0.0041,pFDR=0.029) and a higher probability of remaining in μCAP 3 across consecutive time points  (β=0.41, SE=0.15, Z=2.84, p=0.0045, pFDR=0.029). These sex differences were absent in autistic individuals (p=0.23, p=0.78 and p=0.07, respectively). Finally, spatial extent of μCAP 3 also showed a sex-by-diagnosis interaction (β = −0.001, SE < 0.001, t = −2.03, p = 0.043). NAI males showed greater spatial extent than NAI females (p=0.019), whereas the opposite pattern was observed among autistic individuals (p<0.001).

Conclusion

In this study, we used the novel micro-co-activation patterns to reveal how the FFG dynamically reconfigures with large-scale brain networks in autistic and NAI males and females. Our findings extend prior work by showing that the FFG contains multiple subdivisions linked to both perceptual and socio-cognitive processes that are not static, but dynamically functionally couple with distinct large-scale systems. Notably, only the DMN-C μCAP 3 linked to higher-order anterior FFG regions, showed sex-by-diagnosis differences across occurrences, dwell time and transition probabilities. These findings suggest that dynamic interactions between higher-order FFG subdivisions and the DMN are important for understanding sex-related variability in autism. Results further imply that both autistic males and females may be characterized by a different engagement of networks involved in socio-cognitive aspects of face processing rather than early perceptual processes and is consistent with behavioural evidence of reduced sex differences in social-emotional domains6. Furthermore, there was a reversal of sex-typical patterns in spatial extent potentially indicating that autism is associated with a reorganization of the spatial architecture through which anterior FFG–DMN coupling is achieved in NAI. These findings align with the notion that autism affects the processes that typically contribute to sexual differentiation of social-cognitive functions and highlight the importance of considering sex-linked pathways when examining heterogeneity in autism.
