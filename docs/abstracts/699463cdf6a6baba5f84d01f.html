Introduction

Prenatal brain maturation is a highly dynamic process, marked by rapid cortical growth and folding beginning in the second trimester. Disruptions in sulcal folding are linked to developmental disorders and long-term functional impairments. The spatial organization of early sulcal folds, shaped by functional specialization, cortical connectivity, and genetic influences, remains stable with age1. Thus, it provides a robust substrate for modeling early neurodevelopment and detecting deviations.

            Previous studies have used quantitative graph-matching methods to characterize sulcal pattern alterations by representing sulcal pits as graph nodes and comparing individual graphs to predefined reference sets2. This framework has revealed sulcal abnormalities across diverse conditions, yet it faces limitations: manually assigned feature-importance weights, high computational demand, and dependence on inconsistent reference sets. These limitations restrict sensitivity to individual deviations, particularly subtle and spatially localized abnormalities. 

            Recent advances in unsupervised AI have enabled data-driven modeling of typical anatomical variability by learning normative patterns from large cohorts of healthy individuals. Such models can detect abnormalities in unseen cases while overcoming key limitations of the graph-matching approach. In this study, we introduce a diffusion-based graph anomaly detector for normative sulcal pattern analysis3. Diffusion models learn complex high-dimensional distributions and offers a principled mechanism for capturing normative variability. To evaluate this framework, we apply it to congenital heart disease (CHD), the most common birth defect and associated with disrupted neurogenesis and functional disabilities4. To our knowledge, this is the first application of diffusion-based anomaly detection to sulcal patterns.

Methods

To train and validate the diffusion-based sulcal pattern normative modeling, we used a retrospective dataset of T1-weighted brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) from three publicly available healthy datasets (total n=2,100 age: 5-35 years): Human Connectome Project (male/female: 500/500/0), Child Mind Institute (500/500), and Beijing Normal University (50/50). To validate the trained models, we used a retrospective dataset of T1-weighted MRIs (age: 10-19 years) from 248 CHD patients (164/84) and 94 healthy controls (46/48) from studies previously published and collected at Boston children’s hospital (BCH): single ventricle (SV; 67/48), transposition of the great arteries (TGA; 70/22), and tetralogy of Fallot (ToF; 27/14). These studies were approved by the BCH institutional review board and written informed consent was obtained for all MRIs. The MRIs were processed to extract left and right white matter surfaces using the FreeSurfer. The sulcal patterns were represented using a graph structure with sulcal pits and surrounding catchment basins as nodes. The sulcal pits were connected to an edge in the graph if their sulcal basins coincided. Each node had geometric features, comprising the sulcal area, sulcal depth, and 3D position of the sulcal pit. 

            We propose a graph-based sulcal pattern anomalous diffusion, namely SPA-Diff (Fig. 1) that perturbs graph features to generate pseudo-abnormal (PA) graphs closely resembling the original ones. An anomaly classifier was then trained to distinguish between original and PA graphs, enabling the model to learn the decision boundary of normative sulcal patterns without relying on labeled abnormal dataset. The proposed model consists of four components: a graph encoder-decoder, a latent diffusion model, a conditioning module, and an anomaly classifier. (1) The graph neural network (GNN) encoder extracts latent features from input graphs and the decoder recovers edge and node information. We first pre-trained them, aiming at learning a mapping from graph space to latent space. (2) The latent diffusion model gradually adds noise to latent embeddings. After T steps of the forward diffusion, the reverse process with neural network denoises back toward the original distribution. (3) The conditioning module integrates the initial embedding with noise to introduce controlled and diverse perturbations to the diffusion network, thereby generating diverse PA embeddings. (4) Based on that, the decoder reconstructs the PA graphs, and a GNN anomaly classifier discriminates them from original graphs.

            We trained the model using the 2,100 MRIs with 95% used for training, and 5% for validation. Firstly, we pre-trained the GNN encoder-decoder using variational inference with a reconstruction error. We jointly trained the diffusion network and the anomaly classifier using a score-matching loss, and a binary cross-entropy loss. We applied the trained model to 248 and 94 MRIs from subjects with and without CHD. Anomaly scores were defined by the output logits from the GNN anomaly classifier.

            To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed framework, we evaluated the anomaly scores using area under receiver operator characteristic curves (AUROC). We then compared its performance against well-established unsupervised baselines. These baselines included (1) graph-agnostic algorithms such as Local Outlier Factor (LOF), Isolation Forest (iForest), and one-class support vector machine (OCSVM), and (2) reconstruction-based AI models such as the graph autoencoder (GAE) and variational graph autoencoder (VGAE). For the graph-agnostic algorithms, each graph was represented using either the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) graph kernel or the mean-pooled node embeddings extracted using our pretrained VGAE encoder (Deep). For the reconstruction-based methods, we computed reconstruction errors for node, edges, and a combined metric, and used the average error as the anomaly score.

Results

The comparison results across CHD subtypes (SV, TGA, and ToF) as well as the full CHD cohort are summarized in Fig. 2. Across CHD subtypes, SPA-Diff demonstrated clear advantages in both hemispheres. For the left hemisphere (LH), SPA-Diff achieved the best AUROC for all subtypes (0.644-0.733) and for the overall CHD cohort (mean AUROC of 0.692), consistently surpassing the other baselines. For the right hemisphere (RH), SPA-Diff achieved the highest performance for TGA, ToF (second-best) and the full CHD group (mean AUROC of 0.752, 0.729, and 0.652, respectively), while performing competitively but not optimally for SV. These findings indicate robust cross-hemispheric generalization, with subtype-specific and hemisphere-specific factors influencing anomaly-detection sensitivity. Among the baselines, graph-kernel approaches, particularly WL-OCSVM, showed comparatively strong performance. Their high mean AUROC in TGA (0.670 and 0.689 for LH and RH) and ToF (0.714 and 0.763 for LH and RH) suggest that these subtypes might involve distinctive topology-driven sulcal abnormalities, which likely reflect large-scale reorganizations in early cortical development. The fact that SPA-Diff outperformed WL-OCSVM in most settings indicates that diffusion-based modeling effectively captures these topological deviations while providing additional sensitivity to more complex sulcal-pattern variations.

            SV presented a distinct pattern. SPA-Diff showed relatively weaker performance for SV, especially in the RH. Interestingly, node-feature reconstruction errors from GAE-based models achieved higher AUROC (0.561-0.630) in SV, suggesting that SV may contain more localized geometric abnormalities, such as alterations in sulcal position, area, or depth, rather than broad topological changes. Consistent with this interpretation, SPA-Diff detected these abnormalities well in the LH (AUROC of 0.644), where its performance remained competitive, but showed reduced sensitivity in the RH, implying possible hemispheric asymmetry in SV-related sulcal alterations. Reconstruction-based baselines were also examined in more detail. The improved edge decoder used in heterophily-aware VGAE (HAVGAE) enabled more anomaly-sensitive reconstructions compared with GAE and VGAE. HAVGAE-edge achieved higher AUROC in LH for all subtypes (0.618-0.647) except ToF (0.589). These findings indicate that enhanced modeling of edge structure increases sensitivity to structural perturbations in sulcal pattern graphs, by capturing fine-grained deviations from a learned decision boundary of the normative distribution. 

Conclusion

In this study, we developed an unsupervised diffusion-based graph anomaly detection framework for sulcal patterns. Diffusion generative models have recently emerged as strong alternatives for unsupervised anomaly detection due to their stable training and high-fidelity reconstructions. However, their medical applications have focused primarily on focal, high-contrast anomalies such as lesions. To our knowledge, no prior work has evaluated diffusion-based models for sulcal pattern anomalies, which are far more subtle and distributed and exhibit substantial inter-individual variability. Although quantitative graph-matching methods have shown utility in identifying sulcal abnormalities, they are also limited by high computational cost, dependence on inconsistent reference sets, and the need for manually defined feature importance. The proposed framework addresses these issues by leveraging diffusion-based generative modeling to synthesize abnormal patterns that closely resemble the original graphs. The model learns complex perturbations in a data-driven manner within the latent space, enabling detection of a wide spectrum of deviations, from subtle geometric distortions to large-scale topological alterations of varying severity. Once trained, the method produces anomaly scores within seconds, representing a major improvement over graph-matching procedures that require several days for the same task.

Sulcal patterns are highly complex, and their abnormalities are often imperceptible on visual inspection. This complexity underscores the need for computational approaches capable of characterizing deviations across different spatial scales and intensities. The proposed framework provides such capability, offering a sensitive and scalable solution for detecting heterogeneous sulcal abnormalities. Despite these advantages, the method still faces limitations in discriminative performance. The approximately 2,000 healthy samples used for training may not fully capture the diversity of normal sulcal morphology. Future work should incorporate substantially larger normative datasets, ideally exceeding 10,000 samples, to improve robustness. Extending the framework to additional abnormal conditions, including congenital brain malformations and developmental disorders, will also be important for evaluating its broader utility. Overall, the proposed method offers a generalizable and efficient AI-based tool for sulcal pattern analysis and has strong potential to support future investigations in early neurodevelopmental research and risk stratification.
