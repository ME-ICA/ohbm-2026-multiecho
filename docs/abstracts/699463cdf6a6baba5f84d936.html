Introduction

Olfactory identification is a core higher-level olfactory function reflecting the integrity of distributed cognitive–sensory networks. Recent studies have shown that olfactory function is impaired early along the Alzheimer’s disease continuum, 1 with odor identification and recognition being among the most affected functions. Unlike basic sensory olfactory abilities (e.g., olfactory threshold), these higher-level olfactory processes place substantial demands on cognition. In particular, odor identification requires the integration of bottom-up perceptual processing and top-down semantic elaboration, executive control, and memory-related processes. Consistently with this view, olfactory identification has often been conceptualized in cognitive science as a naming task, in which access to the semantic system is mediated by olfactory input. However, while language-based models have provided valuable insights, they offer only a partial account of the cognitive mechanisms supporting successful odor identification. 2

Beyond semantic labeling, higher-order olfactory abilities likely rely on additional cognitive and metacognitive processes, including perceptual awareness and evaluative monitoring. 3 To date, these aspects remain insufficiently captured by existing theoretical frameworks, and explicit models addressing the role of metacognition in olfactory processing are still lacking. 

In this context, the present study aims to replicate and deepen previous findings on the relationship between higher-level olfactory abilities and cognition, with a specific focus on the neural changes underlying odor identification training. By combining behavioral assessment and functional neuroimaging in a longitudinal training design, we seek to contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of how olfactory identification engages higher-order cognitive and metacognitive mechanisms.

Methods

Participants

Eight healthy participants (mean age = 35.8 years; 3 females) have been enrolled so far in this ongoing study. Exclusion criteria included anosmia or hyposmia, history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, smoking habits, and any contraindications to MRI. The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Olfactory training

Participants underwent a two-month olfactory training protocol, conducted twice a week. Training sessions consisted of structured olfactory tasks involving repeated exposure to odors from different semantic categories, requiring classification and identification. In addition, to engage episodic memory mechanisms, participants were encouraged—when possible—to relate the odor to a specific personal episodic experience. Odor identification was performed without verbal labels or response cues, relying solely on internally generated semantic and episodic representations. Training was supervised and monitored, with participants being regularly followed throughout the intervention to ensure adherence and correct execution of the tasks. Olfactory abilities were assessed before and after training using the Sniffin’ Sticks (https://www.burghart-mt.de/medizintechnik/sniffin_sticks_und_taste_strips/sniffin_sticks/), while cognitive functioning was evaluated with a comprehensive neuropsychological battery (Figure 1)

fMRI experiment

Functional MRI was acquired during an odor identification task, administered both before and after the olfactory training. Olfactory stimuli were delivered via an MRI-compatible olfactometer (https://www.cynexo.com/portfolio/sniff-0-olfactometer/) and belonged to four odor families: floral, fruity, spicy, and herbal. Odors were selected in a preliminary phase to ensure they were culturally familiar within the Italian population.

The experimental task comprised four functional runs. In each run, four odor presentations per family were administered (16 trials per run), with odor families presented in a pseudo-randomized order. Participants were instructed to identify or classify the odor by assigning it to one of the four odor families. When unable to classify the odor, they were instructed to explicitly report this outcome by saying: “I don’t know”. 

Each trial had a total duration of 24 s, comprising a preparation phase (1 s), an odor stimulation phase (6 s), a response phase (6 s), and a final rest period (11 s). To reduce olfactory habituation, the stimulation phase consisted of two 2-second odor pulses, each followed by 1 second of clean air. MRI data were acquired on a 3T GE Signa Architect system. Functional imaging acquisition parameters were: TR = 1200 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel size =3.5×3.5×3.5 mm, no gap, and field of view (FOV) = 22 cm. A high-resolution T1-weighted 3D anatomical image was acquired for anatomical localization and spatial normalization (TR = 2184.5 ms, TE = 3 ms, 46 slices, voxel size = 1×1×1 mm).

fMRI data analysis

All preprocessing and statistical analyses were performed using SPM25 running in MATLAB. For each participant, event-related responses were modeled within the General Linear Model (GLM). Trials were modeled based on the behavioral outcome of odor identification and classified into three conditions: Correctly Identified, Uncorrectly Identified, and “I don’t know” responses. The fixation period and the response window were included as regressors of no interest, and the six realignment parameters were entered as nuisance covariates. Training-related effects were assessed using within-subject paired t-tests  comparing pre- and post-training activation maps. Group-level analyses were conducted using a random-effects model. A double statistical threshold (single-voxel statistics and spatial extent) was used to achieve a combined (i.e., corrected for multiple comparisons) significance level of α < 0.05, as computed by the 3dClustSim AFNI routine with the “-acf ” option. 

Results

Behavioural results

A paired t-test revealed a significant increase in semantic fluency test (FS) scores from pre- (T0) to post-training (T1) (t(7) = 3.75, p = .007), accompanied by a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.32). Although the effect did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, the consistent direction of change and the large effect size suggest a robust trend toward improved semantic fluency following training. No other significant differences emerged in the analysis across the remaining cognitive tests.

fMRI results

At the whole-brain level, the contrast “post- vs. pre-training identified odors” revealed a significant activity increase in the right posterior insula (pINS; peak MNI coordinates: x 36 y -4 z -7; T = 12.08; k = 144; Figure 2).

Conclusion

The present study is part of an ongoing investigation examining the effects of a two-month olfactory training on behavioral performance and neural responses during odor identification. The results reported here should therefore be considered preliminary, pending confirmation in a larger sample.

At the behavioral level, participants showed an improvement in semantic fluency. Although indirect, this behavioral change is consistent with the notion that olfactory training can influence higher-order cognitive processes, possibly including semantic organization and  specific vocabulary development, which are also relevant for successful odor identification.

The main fMRI finding was a training-related increase in activation of the right posterior insula, observed in the post- versus pre-training comparison for successfully identified odors. Given the exploratory nature of the present analysis, this result should be interpreted with caution, but it suggests that olfactory training modulates neural mechanisms supporting conscious odor recognition. In the human and other mammalian brain, the insula is the primary cortical substrate involved in the flavor perception in the taste domain and in the representation of an odor-flavor complex percept. In line with this view, several lesion studies in humans have reported the emergence of a wide range of olfactory alterations following insular damage, ranging from olfactory hallucinations to the alterations in odor-induced taste perceptions. 4 Despite this evidence, the specific role of the insula in higher-order cognitive-olfactory functions remains poorly understood. 

Importantly, while the fMRI task required participants to identify odors, the training protocol primarily focused on repeated odor exposure and discrimination, while also including elements that encouraged associations with autobiographical episodic experiences. The emergence of posterior insular activation after training may therefore reflect changes beyond low-level sensory processing, pointing instead to enhanced perceptual awareness, evaluative processing, or metacognitive access to olfactory information. Increased recruitment of this region following training may indicate a strengthened capacity to monitor and evaluate olfactory percepts, potentially supporting improved confidence or insight during odor identification. In this context, the posterior insula may act as a hub linking olfactory percepts to conscious access and subjective experience, rather than coding odor identity or quality per se. An alternative explanation for this preliminary finding is that training facilitated the emergence of category-level odor representations, supported by the integration of multimodal sensory information across higher-order associative regions. In line with this view, previous evidence suggests that insular cortices may contribute to the formation of category-specific representations for novel odors, potentially supporting the organization of semantic knowledge within the olfactory domain. 5

In conclusion, our preliminary results suggest that  olfactory training was associated with changes in semantic verbal production, indicating that language-related mechanisms play an important role in accurate odor identification. Concurrently, the observed training-related modulation of posterior insular activity suggests that additional processes may be involved. Specifically, a comprehensive understanding of odor identification and classification likely requires models that extend beyond semantic processing alone, incorporating metacognitive capacities, perceptual awareness, and decision-making mechanisms. Despite the limited sample size, the within-subject design and the anatomical consistency of the findings suggest that the observed effects may reflect experience-dependent neural plasticity. Further studies combining neuroimaging with explicit assessments of metacognitive confidence are needed to better characterize these processes.
