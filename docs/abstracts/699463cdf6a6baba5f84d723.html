Introduction

Ketamine exerts rapid antidepressant effects, yet its neurobiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Network-level alterations have frequently been investigated using EEG and fMRI functional connectivity; however, fMRI connectivity reflects a mixture of neurovascular and metabolic processes, complicating interpretation. Functional PET (fPET) with [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) offers a complementary framework to functional connectivity measures by capturing fluctuations in glucose metabolism, thereby providing a molecular connectivity (MC) measure that is temporally dynamic yet directly linked to neuronal energy demand. 1 Here, we characterize how an acute ketamine challenge alters MC across large-scale brain networks in healthy individuals, aiming to provide mechanistic insight into ketamine’s distributed metabolic effects.

Methods

Twenty-three healthy adults (14 male; age 25.48 ± 4.08 years) underwent two fPET sessions on a Siemens mMR PET/MR scanner in a double-blind, cross-over design. The radioligand [18F]FDG was administered in a bolus + constant infusion protocol (ratio 20:80%, 5.1 MBq/kg body weight) for the entire scan time of 75 min. After 20min baseline fPET acquisition participants received either (S)-ketamine of 0.25 mg / kg or placebo (0.9% NaCl solution) via infusion blocks of 3 x 5min, each separated by 10min (Fig. 1). fPET data were reconstructed into 2-second frames and preprocessed as previously described. 2 MC was estimated in the fPET toolbox 3 using the Euclidean distance option 4 (eMC). The approach computes pairwise Euclidean distances between time-activity curves (TACs) and has proven effective in resolving biologically meaningful kinetic structure in dynamic PET data. TACs were extracted region-wise using the 100-parcel Schaefer atlas. 5 Paired t-tests assessed ketamine-induced differences relative to placebo in eMC, with false discovery rate correction (p < 0.025).

Results

Ketamine produced widespread and spatially heterogeneous alterations in MC. Reductions emerged within and between the default mode (DMN), limbic, and control networks (Fig. 2A). The most pronounced decreases occurred within the DMN and in its interactions with the control and salience networks, as well as between the dorsal attention network (DAN) and both control and somatomotor networks. In contrast, ketamine induced MC increases within the visual network and between visual regions and most other cortical networks. Among within-network effects, the largest magnitude changes were observed in the visual and DMN systems. Between-network increases were most prominent for limbic–visual and DMN-visual interactions. Together, these results reveal a pattern characterized by reduced metabolic coupling in higher-order associative circuits and enhanced coupling in sensory-visual pathways.

Conclusion

Acute ketamine administration leads to significant reorganization of molecular connectivity across large-scale human brain networks. The observed dissociation of decreased MC in transmodal and control systems alongside increased MC in visual and sensory-related networks, suggests a shift in metabolic coordination that may underlie ketamine’s rapid neuropsychological effects. fPET-based MC provides a sensitive molecular lens for characterizing pharmacological modulation of brain networks and may contribute to a mechanistic framework for understanding ketamine’s therapeutic action.
