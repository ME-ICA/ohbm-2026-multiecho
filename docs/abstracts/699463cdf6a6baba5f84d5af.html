Introduction

The sense of body ownership relies on the integration of multisensory inputs, particularly visual and tactile signals, and has been extensively studied at the cortical level using paradigms such as the Rubber Hand Illusion (RHI). In contrast, the contribution of the spinal cord remains underexplored, despite evidence that it serves as a dynamic hub for sensorimotor processing rather than a passive relay [1]. Spinal cord fMRI has validated canonical organizational principles and demonstrated spinal involvement in higher-order functions such as movement perception, learning, and observed pain, consistent with top-down cognitive–affective modulation at this level [1,2]. Recent advances in simultaneous corticospinal fMRI now enable whole-neuraxis characterization of brain–spinal interactions in complex sensorimotor and cognitive processes [2], yet these tools have not been applied to body ownership.

Furthermore, current neuroimaging studies of body ownership suffer from low ecological validity, as standard RHI-like protocols in MRI environments often rely on complicated setups that do not induce a lifelike sense of presence and may therefore miss the whole experience of embodied cognition [3,4]. Addressing this gap requires experimental frameworks that combine naturalistic phenomenology with comprehensive imaging of the entire neuraxis. Virtual reality (VR) has emerged as a powerful solution, providing immersive, controllable multisensory environments that enhance ecological validity in fMRI studies [3]. In this work, a novel experimental platform is presented that integrates real-time motion-tracked VR with corticospinal fMRI, adapted from recent VR–fMRI developments [3]. This immersive setup enables precise manipulation of visuo-tactile congruence under naturalistic conditions to test whether illusory ownership over a virtual limb engages both cortical multisensory hubs and spinal modulation pathways via distinct top-down and bottom-up mechanisms.

Methods

Nineteen healthy, right-handed participants (3 females; age 27.8 ± 2.2) were included in this study. The experiment was approved by the local ethics committee (2024-00992), and all participants gave their written consent in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. Corticospinal fMRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner using a 64-channel head-neck coil with a specialized acquisition protocol proposed and utilized in [4]. 

We developed a custom MRI-compatible VR framework adapted from the setup described in [3] for a visuo-tactile experiment paradigm specific to our study. Participants lay supine in the scanner, wearing MRI-compatible VR goggles (Visual System HD, NordicNeuroLab) with dual-display optics, thereby immersing them in a photorealistic virtual replica of the MRI room. A virtual body was rendered from a first-person perspective, with a virtual right hand spatially aligned and anatomically matched with the participant's real hand position. To ensure high ecological validity and enable precise manipulation of visuo-tactile congruence, we implemented a real-time motion tracking system integrated with the virtual environment using Qualisys MRI-compatible motion capture system composed of six Qualisys Oqus 500+m cameras (Fig. 1A). Custom made handplate, body markers, and brush objects were built with support for motion capture trackers attached to them. Two rigid bodies handplate and brush) were used to track the participants’ hand position and the brush used by the experimenter (Fig. 1A). The calibration, reference alignment, and object mapping followed guidelines from [3]. A TV screen was placed in the VR environment to display the experiment cues. In the MRI control room, the stimulation computer was connected to the visual, motion tracking, and trigger systems. A researcher applied tactile strokes to the participant's real right index finger with a standard paintbrush; these movements were captured using the motion-tracking system positioned outside the scanner bore. The tracking data were transmitted and streamed in real-time to the VR rendering engine. The virtual brush was rendered with sub-100 ms latency, ensuring temporally synchronized visuo-tactile feedback.  

Prior to fMRI acquisition, participants completed an immersion phase within the scanner environment (no scanning) to validate sensorimotor contingencies. This included a sensory task (observing synchronous brush strokes on the right index finger) and a motor task (reaching toward virtual targets). Participants then completed a questionnaire adapted from [3], to rate items related to body ownership, agency, embodiment, and self-location. This procedure verified that the VR setup successfully induced a strong sense of embodiment and presence prior to the functional imaging session. The experimental design employed a structured block paradigm (Fig. 2A) comprising Embodiment, Illusion, Rest, and Questionnaire repeated across two separate fMRI runs. The Embodiment phase (30s, 15 synchronous visuo-tactile strokes of 2s each) was designed to induce body ownership delivered on the palmar side of the right-hand index finger. The Illusion block alternated between congruent trials (real and virtual brush synchronized) and incongruent trials (~25%; virtual brush touches finger while real brush passes over without contact). This was followed by a Questionnaire phase  after each block to assess their subjective embodiment on a 7-point scale. This selective decoupling, enabled by our VR system, created a "Visual Touch / No Tactile" condition to isolate the neural response to multisensory conflict from actual somatosensory processing across the corticospinal axis. 

Results

Functional images underwent slice timing correction, followed by cropping of anatomical and functional images to isolate brain and spinal cord volumes. Brain and spinal cord data were then processed separately using optimized pipelines previously utilized in [5]. For both regions, preprocessing included motion correction using rigid-body spatial realignment, tissue segmentation (gray matter, white matter, CSF), physiological noise correction (cardiac and respiratory signals), and spatial smoothing (anisotropic for spinal cord along the rostro-caudal axis, isotropic for brain). Functional images were registered to high-resolution structural scans and normalized to standard templates (MNI152 for brain; PAM50 for spinal cord). 

A General Linear Model (GLM) was applied at the first level (session-specific) to model discrete brush stroke events in the illusion block, with the contrast Incongruent > Congruent computed for each participant in each session. Subject-level maps were then generated by averaging the two session-level contrasts. Finally, group-level (third-level) analysis averaged across all the participants to identify significant activation clusters. Statistical thresholds were set at Z-stat > 3.1 (p < 0.05, FWE-corrected) for brain regions, computed using fixed-effect modelling, and at T-stat > 1.65 (p < 0.05, uncorrected), computed using non-parametric TFCE approach for spinal cord.

Pre-scanning immersion testing confirmed successful embodiment across all participants. Questionnaire responses revealed high scores for hand ownership (feeling that the virtual hand was their own hand), agency (perceived control over the virtual hand), self-location (spatial alignment with the virtual hand), and overall embodiment (Fig. 1C), consistent with prior findings [3]. These findings validated that the VR-MRI setup effectively induced a naturalistic, lifelike sense of presence, fulfilling the prerequisite for investigating body ownership illusions during subsequent fMRI acquisition.

The group-level analysis revealed distinct activation patterns across the neuraxis when participants viewed a virtual brush touching their finger, without actual tactile contact (Fig. 2C). At the brain level, significant bilateral activation was observed in the precentral gyrus, consistent with motor system engagement during visuo-tactile conflict. The precuneus showed robust activation, consistent with its established role in self-referential processing, spatial updating, and bodily self-consciousness, whereas additional clusters in visual area V5/hMT+ and the cuneal gyrus indicated heightened visual motion processing in the absence of tactile feedback, hinting at cross-modal compensation and the multimodal nature of V5. At the spinal level, activation was detected in the cervical cord despite the absence of physical touch. The lateral corticospinal tract (CST) at segments C5–C7 showed significant activation, indicating top-down descending modulation from cortical motor and sensory areas to spinal sensory circuits. Crucially, ascending pathways were also engaged: significant clusters emerged in the dorsal column-medial lemniscus (DCML) at C6 and the left dorsal horn at C7, suggesting bottom-up anticipatory gating and preparatory sensory modulation in response to the expected but absent tactile stimulus.

Conclusion

This study introduces an innovative multimodal framework that combines an MRI-compatible, motion-tracked VR environment with simultaneous brain–spinal cord fMRI to probe body ownership under ecologically valid conditions. Building on this novel imaging approach, the Incongruent > Congruent contrast reveals, to our knowledge, the first corticospinal signature of a body ownership illusion: cortical activations in precentral cortex, precuneus, and V5/cuneal regions capture multisensory conflict resolution and visually driven predictions of touch, while concurrent engagement of lateral CST, DCML, and dorsal horn circuitry demonstrates tightly coupled top-down descending modulation and bottom-up anticipatory gating at the spinal level. Together, these findings position the spinal cord as a computationally active partner in constructing the bodily self and show that high-level perceptual illusions reverberate across the entire neuraxis. This VR-based corticospinal imaging framework also opens new avenues for mechanistic studies of body representation and may ultimately inform VR-driven diagnostic and rehabilitative interventions in conditions involving pain, disrupted body ownership, or motor dysfunction.
