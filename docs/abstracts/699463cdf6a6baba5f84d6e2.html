Introduction

Substance use disorder (SUD) is linked to stronger delay discounting (DD), a preference for smaller sooner (SS, e.g. 100 Euros now) over larger later rewards (LL; e.g. 200 Euros in 365 days).1 Neuroscientific dual system models propose that this is driven by a hyperactive reward system that overvalues immediate rewards which cannot be sufficiently regulated by a hypoactive control system.2,3 Stronger control network activation during LL compared to SS choices was observed in SUD compared to healthy controls, reflecting increased neural effort in light of dispositional hypoactivation1. Because these groups differ in both substance use and substance-related problems, altered brain activation in reward and control networks may reflect either a specific link to SUD, or result from an underlying association with the degree of substance use. To test this, we examined reward- and control-related neural activation in preregistered regions of interest, where we expected significant associations with substance-related problems when controlling for the degree of use (osf.io/6ptje). 

Methods

Substance use and DSM-5 SUD diagnostic criteria for all used substances were assessed at baseline (N = 119, 49 female) and 12-months follow-up (N = 107, 46 female) in short-term abstinent (poly-)substance users.4 The baseline assessment included an individualized DD task during fMRI demanding hypothetical choices between an SS and an LL reward with six different delays. In hard trials, SS and LL had similar subjective values based on the individually determined discounting rate. FMRI data were acquired on a 3T Trio Siemens scanner and underwent standard preprocessing in SPM12. Control-related neural activation was operationalized as the contrast between LL and SS choices in hard trials (more likely to require control activation to achieve an LL choice). Neural delay discounting was operationalized as decreased responsiveness to amount (parametric modulation) in trials with larger delays. Mean a priori region of interest (ROI) activation was extracted for each contrast (control: right inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and middle frontal gyrus (MFG), left IFG; neural delay discounting: bilateral ventral striatum (VS) and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC)). We performed linear regressions of substance-related problems on ROI activation with and without controlling for the degree of use and controlling for sex. Resulting p-values were corrected for the number of ROIs.

Results

Control-related brain activation was unrelated to substance-related problems at baseline (all ps >.05), but prospectively predicted substance-related problems at follow-up (right IFG: b=.21, p=.042; right MFG: b=.21, p=.042; left IFG: b=.17, p=.042) and when controlling for the degree of use, it explained substance-related problems at baseline (left IFG: b=.18, p=.01; not significant in any other ROIs, ps>.05), and at follow-up (right IFG: b=.18, p=.012; right MFG b=.13, p=.04; left IFG: b=.18, p=.01). Neural delay discounting was unrelated to current or future substance-related problems, with or without controlling for the degree of use (all ps > .05). An exploratory analysis comparing SS vs. LL choices in easy trials, indicative of immediate reward valuation, revealed that reward network hyperactivation predicted substance-related problems at baseline (vmPFC: b=.28, p=.002, VS: n.s.) and follow-up (vmPFC: b=.31, p<.001, VS: b=.15, p=.054) and effects remained significant when controlling for the degree of use at baseline (vmPFC: b=.22, p<.001, VS: b=.14, p=.015) and follow-up (vmPFC: b=.21, p=.003, VS: b=.20, p=.003).

Conclusion

Dysregulation in brain reward and control networks during DD was associated with substance-related problems. Effect sizes remained stable or even increased (VS) when controlling for the degree of use or predicting prospective outcomes. This highlights that hyperreactivity to immediate rewards along with putatively compensatory hyperactivation during successful controlled choice may reflect specific SUD risk. Our results rather point towards an overvaluation of immediate rewards than to an increased neural delay discounting, putatively creating strong and rigid motivational drives to consume.5 Our results substantiate the role of a dimensional approach controlling for the degree of substance use for studying specific links to substance-related problems.
