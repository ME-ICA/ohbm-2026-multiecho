Introduction

Connectomics, which studies the brain’s structural (SC) and functional connectivity (FC), describes the brain as a network, with different regions of interests (ROIs) acting as nodes, their SC through white matter fiber bundles extracted from diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI), or FC through statistical temporal dependencies using functional MRI (fMRI) as edges. Understanding how multimodal brain connectivity supports cognitive development is a central question in developmental neuroscience, yet it requires analytic frameworks that can integrate information from both structural and functional networks while addressing the challenges of high-dimensional data. Although multimodal prediction approaches are increasingly used, standard strategies such as early fusion (feature concatenation) or late fusion (averaging model outputs) often ignore modality-specific structure and do not explicitly evaluate whether joint modeling improves prediction. Here, we investigate how best to combine FC and SC to predict cognition in youth using the Human Connectome Project in Development (HCP-D) dataset. Specifically, we apply a cooperative learning framework, [1] a supervised multimodal approach that encourages agreement between modality-specific predictors through a tunable penalty parameter. This work aims to clarify when and how combining structural and functional connectivity enhances cognitive prediction, and to provide insight into the complementary roles of brain structure and function during development.

Methods

2.1 Dataset and Preprocessing

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), diffusion MRI (dMRI), and cognitive measures from the Human Connectome Project in Development (HCP-D) dataset were used. HCP-D is a large-scale study designed to examine the brain connectome in approximately 1,300 healthy children and adolescents aged 5–21 years. In the current data release, imaging and behavioral data are available for 652 participants.

Minimally preprocessed rs-fMRI data provided by HCP were used, and dMRI preprocessing was performed using MRtrix3; details of the preprocessing steps are described in our previous work [5]. SC and FCwere derived based on the Glasser atlas, [2] which parcellated the brain into 360 cortical regions of interest (ROIs). Functional connectivity matrices were constructed based on statistical associations between ROI time-series, while structural connectivity matrices were generated from weighted streamline counts between ROIs.

Cognitive performance was assessed using the NIH Toolbox, and the age-adjusted total cognitive composite score was used in all analyses. After applying quality control procedures across modalities, the final sample included 372 participants with complete data.

2.2 Cooperative Learning and Implementation

To evaluate multimodal integration strategies, we employed cooperative learning, a supervised multiview framework that jointly models multiple data modalities while controlling the agreement between their predictions [1]. Let

X(FC) ∈ Rn x pFC and X(SC) ∈ Rn x pSC

denote the functional and structural connectivity feature matrices for n participants, and let

y ∈ Rn

represent the cognitive outcome.

Cooperative learning fits modality-specific prediction functions fFC(·) and fSC (·) by minimizing

minfFC, fSC L(y, fFC(X(FC))) + L(y, fSC(X(SC))) + α || fFC(X(FC)) - fSC(XSC) || 22,

where L(·) denotes squared error loss and α ≥ 0 controls the strength of the agreement penalty. In the special case of linear models where f (X) = Xβ, this reduces to the original cooperative learning formulation of Ding et al. [1].

The parameter α governs the multimodal integration regime. When α = 0, the framework reduces to early fusion,in which features from both modalities are combined additively into a single predictor. As α increases, agreement between modality-specific predictions is increasingly enforced, approximating a late fusion regime in which FC and SC are modeled independently and combined only at the prediction stage.

Parameter Selection. Five-fold cross-validation was used to select α by minimizing mean root mean squared error (RMSE) across folds. This procedure identified α = 0 as optimal, motivating subsequent analyses focused on early fusion.

Feature Selection. Given the high dimensionality of FC and SC features (p ≫ n), univariate screening was performed separately within each modality using Pearson correlation with the cognitive score. Following a common heuristic (n/5), the top 74 features per modality were retained within each cross-validation fold to prevent information leakage

2.3 Multimodal and Baseline Model Training

To evaluate the added value of multimodal integration, four baseline models were trained using identical gradient boosting machine (GBM) settings: (1) an FC-only model, (2) an SC-only model, (3) a naive late-fusion model formed by averaging predictions from the FC-only and SC-only models, and (4) an early-fusion model trained on concatenated FC and SC features. GBMs were trained using 1,000 trees, interaction depth of 3, learning rate of 0.01, and bag fraction of 0.7. These parameters were fixed across all models to ensure comparability.

Results

Cross-validated model selection across integration strategies identified early fusion (feature concatenation) as the optimal integration strategy, achieving a cross-validated root mean squared error (RMSE) of 15.09. Early fusion significantly outperformed single-modality models in training performance (RMSE = 6.21, R² = 0.86) relative to FC-only (RMSE = 8.09, R² = 0.75) and SC-only (RMSE = 7.21, R² = 0.80) models, representing improvements of 23% and 14%, respectively. The multimodal model also outperformed naive late-fusion averaging (RMSE = 6.93, R² = 0.82), demonstrating the advantage of joint multimodal feature integration.

Feature importance analysis from the gradient boosting model indicated that FC accounted for 88% of total predictive influence, with SC contributing the remaining 12%, suggesting that functional network dynamics are the primary drivers of individual differences in cognitive performance in this developmental sample, while structural connectivity provides complementary information.

At the network level, the cingulo-opercular network (CON) emerged as the most predictive across modalities (FC: 29%; SC: 24% of within-modality importance), followed by the dorsal attention network (DA; FC: 21%) and the visual network (SC: 24%). The default mode network (DMN) also showed substantial contributions (FC: 14%; SC: 17%), indicating that its organization supports cognition through both functional interactions and structural scaffolding. These network-level effects were driven by a subset of highly predictive regions within each network. For FC, key contributing regions were primarily located within the CON and DA networks, while for SC, influential regions were concentrated within the visual and DMN systems (see Figure for regional detail).

All cortical regions of interest were defined using the Glasser atlas, and functional network assignments were based on the large-scale cortical–subcortical network parcellation described by Ji et al. [3]. Cross-validated performance demonstrates good generalization despite strong training performance, with the observed gap reflecting expected optimism in gradient boosting models. Five-fold cross-validation provided a robust estimate of out-of-sample prediction accuracy.

Conclusion

This study shows that early fusion of multimodal neuroimaging data provides an effective and interpretable framework for predicting cognitive performance in late childhood. By combining feature-selected functional and structural connectivity, we achieved strong generalization performance (CV RMSE = 15.09) and substantially improved prediction relative to single-modality models. These results indicate that FC and SC carry complementary information, and that jointly modeling the two modalities captures relationships that neither modality alone can fully represent.

Functional connectivity accounted for the majority of predictive influence (88% vs. 12% for SC), suggesting that dynamic network interactions are more closely tied to cognitive variability in youth. Nonetheless, the inclusion of SC improved performance beyond FC alone, highlighting the value of incorporating structural constraints when modeling brain–behavior associations. The most predictive networks – Cingulo-Opercular, Dorsal-Attention, Default Mode, and Visual systems – align with established roles in cognitive control, attention, and integrative processing, while region-level analyses highlight interpretable contributors such as FEF, MIP, and POS1.

Methodologically, univariate feature screening effectively addressed the p ≫ n challenge by reducing each modality from 65,160 to 74 features while preserving predictive signal. Gradient boosting enabled nonlinear multimodal integration and provided clear measures of feature importance, supporting both performance and interpretability.

Several limitations should be noted. The use of a single, composite cognitive measure may mask domain-specific effects; the relatively modest sample size required aggressive feature selection; and the cross-sectional design limits developmental interpretation. Resting-state FC may also differ from task-evoked connectivity in its relevance to specific cognitive processes.

Future work should validate these findings in independent samples, extend the approach to domain-specific cognitive measures, and incorporate longitudinal data to capture developmental trajectories. Additional imaging modalities or advanced feature-selection approaches may further improve performance, while emerging multimodal deep learning frameworks could model more complex structure–function interactions.

Overall, early fusion offers a principled and scalable strategy for multimodal connectome analysis, revealing that functional connectivity is the primary driver of cognitive prediction in youth while structural connectivity provides meaningful complementary information. This framework can be readily applied to other cognitive domains, developmental periods, and clinical populations to advance our understanding of brain–behavior relationships.
