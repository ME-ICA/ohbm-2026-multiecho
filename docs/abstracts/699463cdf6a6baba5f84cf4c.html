Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly common psychiatric condition with a lifetime prevalence ranging from 5% to 17%. Approximately 30% of MDD patients exhibit poor responses to two adequate trials of antidepressant drugs at therapeutic doses and durations, subsequently developing treatment-resistant depression (TRD). These individuals undergo increased frequency of depressive episodes and are prone to cognitive impairment and suicidal behavior.1 Previous research has demonstrated that TRD patients reveal abnormalities in specific white matter (WM) structures, including the corpus callosum and internal capsule.2 However, studies investigating WM functional abnormalities in TRD remain limited. Building on recent advances in functional correlation tensor (FCT) techniques—which enable the quantification of blood oxygenation level dependent signals within WM tracts and provide micro-functional activation insights beyond conventional structural imaging—this study utilized FCT to investigate WM functional abnormalities in TRD patients and to examine associations with clinical scales, aiming to elucidate potential disruptions in WM transmission underlying TRD.

Methods

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were collected from 62 patients with TRD (female/male: 39/23; mean age: 41.5 ± 20.5 years) using a 3.0T GE SIGNA PET/MR scanner. The imaging protocol included T1-weighted structural images and resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) scans (TR/TE = 2500/30 ms, field of view = 222 × 222 mm, slice thickness = 3.6 mm, 200 volumes). 

Preprocessing of the rs-fMRI data included head motion correction, slice timing correction, spatial smoothing, and band-pass filtering within the frequency range of 0.01 to 0.1 Hz to retain low-frequency fluctuations. In addition, nuisance signals from gray matter and cerebrospinal fluid were regressed out to reduce physiological noise. FCT values were subsequently computed by assessing the temporal correlation between each voxel's rs-fMRI time series and those of its 26 neighboring voxels. The resulting correlation matrices were normalized to create voxel-wise FCT maps. Fractional anisotropy (FA) of FCT, FAFCT as then calculated from the FCT maps to characterize the directionality of functional connectivity within WM regions.

The WM regions were segmented by JHU white-matter atlas (NeuroVault ID: 1408, https://neurovault.org/images/1408/). The atlas was registered to each subject’s native fMRI space, and the FAFCT maps were extracted according on the corresponding anatomical labels from the atlas. Finally, partial correlation analyses were performed between the mean FAFCT values of each WM region and clinical scale scores, including the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) and Maudsley Staging Parameters and Suggested Scoring Conventions Scale (MSM), to explore potential associations between WM functional activity and clinical symptoms.

Results

In the HAMD-17 analysis, four JHU WM regions demonstrated significant negative correlations between FAFCT values and clinical scores (p < 0.05), including the splenium of the corpus callosum (SCC), left anterior limb of the internal capsule (ALIC-L), and bilateral anterior corona radiata (ACR-L and ACR-R), as illustrated in Figure 1(a) and (b). These regions have been previously implicated in emotional and cognitive regulation. The SCC facilitates interhemispheric information transfer and often exhibits structural and functional disruption in MDD, which may contribute to impairments in attention and cognitive processing. The ALIC-L, a common target for deep brain stimulation, has been shown to modulate functional connectivity between the amygdala and insula, leading to a normalization of emotional responses to salient stimuli. In line with our findings, the reduced functional activation in this region among TRD patients may reflect the neural basis of emotional dysregulation. The ACR has been associated with emotional control, social cognition, and behavioral inhibition. Previous studies have reported significantly reduced structural FA values in this region among MDD patients, supporting our observation that functional abnormalities in the ACR may be linked to impaired socio-emotional functioning. In the MSM analysis, three JHU WM regions exhibited significant correlations with total scores (p < 0.05), including the bilateral cerebral peduncle (CP-L and CP-R) and the right posterior thalamic radiation (PTR-R), as illustrated in Figure 1(c) and (d). Previous studies have associated the CP with motor function and decreased connectivity within the sensorimotor network in MDD, and evidence suggests that default mode network imbalance during depressive episodes may further disrupt sensorimotor and visual networks.3 In conjunction with our findings, these findings indicate that CP dysfunction may reflect distributed network alterations associated with TRD, while reduced functional activation in the PTR may affect cortical signal transmission, influencing emotional processing.4

Conclusion

Traditional investigations of MDD and TRD have predominantly examined WM structural alterations through metrics such as fractional anisotropy derived from diffusion tensor imaging or fiber density measurements. The present study extends this approach by applying FCT to characterize directional patterns of functional activation within WM, thereby providing a more comprehensive and dynamic perspective on the neural mechanisms underlying TRD.

Using FCT methodology, we investigated associations between functional changes in WM regions and depression-related clinical measures in TRD patients. Our findings revealed that multiple WM regions involved in emotion regulation, cognitive processing, and cortical signal transmission—including the SCC, ALIC-L, bilateral ACR, bilateral CP, and PTR-R—showed significant correlations between mean FAFCT values and clinical symptom severity. These results clarify the specific WM regions contributing to emotional and cognitive dysfunction in TRD, further supporting the critical role of WM functional alterations in TRD pathophysiology.

Notably, although regions such as the CP and PTR are typically associated with motor and visual processing, our findings indicate significant correlations between mean FAFCT values in these regions and depressive symptoms. This suggests that WM pathways traditionally considered to primarily serve motor control may also participate in affective dysfunction. These results demonstrate that neural dysfunction in TRD extends beyond gray matter structures, and that functional alterations within WM networks warrant greater attention in future neurobiological investigations of TRD.
