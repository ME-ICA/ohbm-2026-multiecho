Introduction

Questionable replicability of brain–psychopathology associations has become a central concern in mental health neuroimaging. Numerous studies report neural correlates of mental disorders and associated risk factors. However, the combination of limited small effect sizes, underpowered samples, exceptionally high analytic flexibility, and publication bias has raised doubts about the credibility of these findings.1–3

Marek et al. demonstrated that for typical brain–behavior associations effect sizes are small, and thousands of study participants are required to obtain stable and reproducible effect estimates, whereas smaller samples produce highly inflated effect sizes and yield effects that fail to replicate in independent data.4 These findings imply that nominally significant voxel-wise results in typical sample sizes, even when applying rigorous statistical thresholds, may substantially overestimate true effects.

Direct and conceptual replication studies are a diagnostic tool to evaluate the replicability of specific findings or within a research field. Despite the arguably high relevance of this for mental health neuroimaging, dedicated replications are extremely rare within this domain and thus the state of replicability is empirically unknown. In addition, the multidimensional characteristics of MRI data and subsequent findings (i.e., spatially distributed statistical effects) pose specific challenges to conduct replications in this domain. No consensus framework exists to date for conducting voxel-wise replications in MRI research or for evaluating replication success of spatially distributed effect patterns.

Our goal was to develop such a replication framework and investigate the replicability of voxel-based gray matter findings associated with depression and depression-related predictors, using cross-cohort conceptual replications, multiverse analysis and a series of direct replications of published findings.

Methods

We used data from over 6000 participants drawn from three independent harmonized neuroimaging cohorts from Germany: the Marburg-Münster Affective Disorders Cohort Study (MACS), the Münster Neuroimaging Cohort (MNC) and the BiDirect cohort study. All cohorts include healthy control (HC) and major depressive disorder (MDD) subsamples. Psychiatric diagnoses, or the lack thereof, was assessed using structured clinical interviews (SCID or MINI). Depression severity was assessed using standardized clinician-rated and self-report instruments, including the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D). Key environmental risk factors were measured using validated questionnaires, most prominently the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) for retrospective assessment of childhood maltreatment. Detailed information on illness course (e.g., age of onset, number of episodes, hospitalizations) was obtained via clinical interviews, and current and past psychotropic medication use was assessed using standardized questionnaires. Overall, the datasets include assessments with more than 80 questionnaires and rating instruments, covering approximately 50 psychological and clinical constructs, including affective symptoms, comorbidities, symptom trajectories, environmental risk factors, lifestyle variables, and related domains. Structural MRI data were acquired on 3T scanners using harmonized T1-weighted sequences with 1 mm isotropic voxel resolution. Scanning sequence parameters and preprocessing steps were harmonized across cohorts. Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) was used to operationalize gray matter density. 

General linear models were used at liberal uncorrected (punc <.001) and conservative (voxel-wise pFWE<.05) thresholds, controlling for age, sex and intracranial volume to investigate the respective predictors of interest. 

The cross-cohort conceptual replicability of MDD (case-control design) and of CM was investigated by inspecting the spatial overlap of brain-wide significance between the three cohorts. The number of replicable voxels was counted per analysis, and significance of replicability was assessed using a permutation-based approach (i.e., is overlap in significance across cohorts higher than chance). For the investigation of CM a multiverse analysis was conducted including over 100 model specifications, varying in the operationalization of CM (investigating extreme forms of CM or subtypes) and of gray matter, as well as varying in statistical control for MDD, and in the inclusion of different subsamples (male/female, non-clinical/clinial). 

In addition, we conducted a series of direct replications of original published findings using the available datasets. Replication targets were selected from the literature based on predefined eligibility criteria, including (i) voxel-based mass-univariate, whole-brain MRI findings associated with depression-related variables, ii) sufficient neuroimaging quality (e.g., at least a field strength of 3T and a resolution of 2 mm) and (iii) sufficient comparability between the original study design and available data in the participating cohorts. For each direct replication, original study sample characteristics, variable operationalizations and analysis specifications were recreated as closely as possible based on the available datasets. 

For the series of direct replications replication success was rated based on a set of complementary predefined criteria, including significance at the peak voxel localization of the original study, 10 and 15 mm spherical ROIs around this peak, and predefined anatomically defined ROIs. All protocols for the direct replications were preregistered including the concrete selection of the effects to replicate, the creation of the replication sample and the ROIs, as well as the hypotheses and analysis plans.

Results

For the conceptual replications N=4021 were included to investigate MDD case-control effects and N=3225 were included to investigate CM effects. MDD participants had reduced gray matter as compared to HC participants in widespread clusters with a maximum effect size of partial R²=.01. Cross-cohort replicability analysis revealed substantial spatial overlap in significance between all pairwise cohort combinations (up to k=10217 voxels at analysis-wise punc<.001 and up to k=441 voxels at pFWE<.05) and some overlap converging across all three cohorts combined (at analysis-wise punc <.001 k=1149, no overlap at pFWE<.05). MDD-related effects showing the highest replicability across cohorts were located in bilateral clusters covering insula, thalamus, and a parahippocampal-fusiform-lingual complex. Of note, no effects were found implicating the hippocampus.

In contrast, CM-related gray matter associations did not show robust cross-cohort replicability. While individual cohorts yielded nominally significant effects under most model specifications, no consistent spatial overlap of significant voxels was observed across cohorts. Marginal descriptive overlap was observed for some analyses (largest pairwise cohort overlap at analysis-wise punc<.001, k=3 voxels; no overlap at analysis-wise pFWE<.05). However, permutation-testing indicated that none of the observed cross-cohort overlap was significantly higher than expected by chance (all pFDR>.611). This pattern of non-replicability was consistent across all multiverse model specifications, including models with and without controlling for diagnosis, investigating HC and MDD separately, testing all CTQ subscales separately as predictors, comparing severe forms of CM with groups with none-to-mild CM, testing interaction terms with age, and when stratifying by sex. Non-replicability was also observed when investigating segmentation-based subcortical volume, and cortical thickness and surface as an alternate measure of gray matter structure.

A total of n=7 direct replications have yet been conducted of which five were on CM X gene interactions (including 5HTTLPR, BDNF Val66Met, OXTR and FKBP5), one was on the longitudinal effect of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT; pre-post) and one was on the effect of physical activity on gray matter in the context of depression. All direct replication attempts had substantially larger sample sizes as compared to the original studies. Results indicated replication success only in one of the replications (the pre-post ECT effect), where several of the replication success criteria were met. All other replications showed only marginal evidence for replicability. Effect sizes were considerably smaller in all replications as compared to the original studies and effect localizations were often far from the original effect localization.

Conclusion

By combining large-scale multi-cohort datasets with conceptual and direct replication approaches and multiverse analysis, this work provides an empirical assessment of voxel-wise replicability of previous findings, in the context of depression neuroimaging. Across well-powered harmonized datasets, we show that replicability is highly construct-dependent and suggest concrete strategies for addressing spatial complexity of MRI-based replications. 

MDD-related reductions in gray matter were small in magnitude but showed robust replicability across all three cohorts in regions involved in emotion regulation and sensory processes. No evidence was found for replicability of hippocampal effects, as opposed to previous studies. These findings indicate that structural brain alterations in depression are detectable and replicable, albeit subtle and spatially distributed. 

In contrast, gray matter associations with CM, one of the most prominent risk factors of depression,  showed no evidence for cross-cohort replicability. Despite extensive multiverse analyses spanning a wide range of model specifications, operationalizations, and subsamples, no spatially converging CM-related gray matter pattern emerged. This suggests that frequently reported voxel-wise CM effects may not reflect robust structural correlates in adult samples.

Results from a series of direct replications further expand the evidence for low repliability. Across seven preregistered replications of published voxel-wise MRI findings, most effects could not be replicated, despite substantially larger sample sizes than in the original studies. When effects were observed, they were markedly attenuated and often spatially displaced relative to original reports. Together, this evidence indicates that findings that are supposedly established in the literature may vary considerably with regard to their credibility potentially due to biases in the interpretation and publication of research data. 

Our findings have several limitations. All analyses were conducted using harmonized preprocessing and analysis pipelines within a single research group, and the included cohorts were demographically and geographically relatively non-divers. While this design leads to optimal conditions to identify replicability between the utilized datasets, it also limits the generalizability of our findings to other labs, samples, demographic characteristics and methodological approaches. Replicability estimates derived here should therefore be interpreted with caution. 

Taken together, these findings indicate that while replicability may pertain to some neurobiological effects when samples are large, a substantial part of effects may not be replicable. This highlights the relevance of replication as a necessary refinement step in mental health neuroimaging, enabling the separation of robust, generalizable signals from effects driven by analytic flexibility and known biases.
