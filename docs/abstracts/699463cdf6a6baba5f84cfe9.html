Introduction

Traditional cognitive EEG research has predominantly relied on highly controlled, trial-based laboratory paradigms. While such designs ensure high internal validity and reproducibility, they are limited in capturing the neural underpinnings of how humans naturally engage with complex, dynamic environments. Recent perspectives in the literature1-3 emphasize the need for more ecologically valid experimental frameworks. However, truly real-world settings introduce considerable noise for reliable EEG measurements, and passive stimuli such as narratives or movies lack the interactive components essential to naturalistic behavior. Thus, video games have emerged as a promising approach to bridge this gap.1-2,4 Video games are intuitive, engaging, and intrinsically motivating, potentially enhancing participant involvement.1 Crucially, video games can simulate realistic scenarios while in principle allowing precise control over experimental variables. Despite growing interest in this field, no comprehensive overview exists of how effectively video game-based paradigms have been employed in event-related EEG research. In this systematic scoping review, we aim to address this gap by examining the current state of research regarding (1) the types of video games utilized and (2) the extent to which established cognitive EEG findings could be replicated using video game designs. Specifically, we compare video game-based findings with canonical markers of cognitive events, such as the P3 following the onset of unexpected stimuli, the N2/P3 complex in go/nogo tasks, movement-related (lateralized) readiness potentials (L/RP), or, in the context of performance monitoring, response-locked error-related negativity (ERN) and outcome-locked feedback-related negativity/reward positivity (FRN/RewP).

Methods

To conduct our review, we followed the PRISMA-ScR guideline5 and adopted the video game definition by Allen et al.1 Studies were deemed eligible for review if they reported original research with healthy human participants, employed video game paradigms to investigate event-related EEG measures, and were published as peer-reviewed journal articles in English. Two electronic databases (PubMed and Web of Science) were searched in February 2025 using search strings that combined EEG-related terms (EEG, electroencephalography, ERP) with video game-related terms (video game, computer game, virtual reality game, and variants). Reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were also screened. Only studies reporting EEG recorded during gameplay with event-related analyses time-locked to video game events were included. Studies involving exergames, brain-computer interfaces, neurofeedback, brain stimulation, intracranial or mobile EEG, or focusing on interbrain connectivity analyses or machine learning-based emotion recognition were not included. Screening proceeded in three phases: title, abstract, and full-text examination to confirm EEG recording during gameplay and event-related analysis. Data were extracted using a standardized charting procedure that captured study objectives, sample characteristics, task details (game genre, visual format, player perspective, core mechanics), EEG parameters, and key findings. Given the heterogeneity of paradigms and methods, data were synthesized qualitatively and mapped onto canonical event-related EEG findings.

Results

Of 1,098 identified records, 24 studies met our inclusion criteria. Publication dates ranged from 1984 to 2024, with a gradual upward trend in recent years. Of 22 distinct video games employed, seven (32%) were commercial games and 15 (68%) were custom-designed for research purposes. Arcade-style games predominated (n=9, 41%), followed by 3D action games (n=5), simulations (n=3), platformers (n=2), puzzle games (n=2), and sports games (n=1). Visual formats included 2D (n=10), 3D (n=7), and VR (n=6). Fifteen studies (63%) reported ERPs, six reported event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/ERS), three reported event-related spectral perturbations (ERSP), and six reported other event-related spectral changes, including band power or asymmetry indices. Below, we summarize the canonical event-related EEG findings observed in video game-based paradigms, organized by EEG activity following stimulus onset, response onset, and feedback-related events.

Eight studies examined the EEG signatures of stimulus-onset events. Of these, six studies analyzed EEG time-courses following unexpected events, while two explicitly examined the EEG time-courses following go/nogo stimuli. With respect to unexpected events, a number of studies reported a P3 following suddenly appearing or changing task-relevant stimuli, observed in the purpose-built research game Space Fortress, an arcade game, and a flight simulation game. In Space Fortress, stimulus onset was also accompanied by delta power increases. Furthermore, in a taxi simulation game, frontal theta modulation distinguished target from non-target stimuli that were otherwise visually identical. With respect to go/nogo events, two studies using a custom 3D shooting game and a VR casual action game reported characteristic N2/P3 complexes, with larger N2 amplitudes for nogo than go targets, consistent with common findings in laboratory go/nogo paradigms.

Seven studies examined the EEG correlates of response-related events, such as button presses. Several studies reported canonical motor preparation-related activity, including slowly increasing RPs prior to voluntary movements, LRPs characterized by enhanced contralateral motor cortex negativity, and pre-movement alpha/beta ERD followed by post-movement ERS. These findings were observed in a Space Fortress variant, a gamified decision-making task, and an arcade game. Notably, the gamified decision-making study showed that the neural patterns did not differ from those observed in a simplified laboratory version of the task, suggesting that adding ecological context does not alter motor preparation-related activity. One study used a custom VR military shooting game and reported a frontocentral ERN following erroneous responses (missed shots), with amplitudes exceeding those after correct responses.

Finally, the EEG signatures of feedback-related events constituted the largest category of video game events among the studies assessed, comprising 13 studies. Here, all commonly assessed feedback-related EEG components could be identified. Specifically, an FRN was observed after missed shots in a VR target shooting game and was associated with negative outcomes in a Space Fortress variant. The same target shooting game also reported a RewP following successful hits. A feedback-locked P3 was consistently observed across Space Fortress variants and a commercial baseball game. Several studies reported the typical sensitivity of outcome-related components to magnitude and probability. One dual-task study combining Space Fortress with a standard oddball task replicated the characteristic P3 resource reciprocity pattern observed in laboratory settings.

In addition to replicating canonical EEG components, several studies provided convergent evidence that video game events evoke EEG signatures similar to those observed in laboratory paradigms, allowing for approaches such as cross-paradigm pattern classification and cross-task performance prediction.

Conclusion

Our systematic scoping review demonstrates that a variety of video game-based paradigms have been employed in event-related EEG research, with the majority using custom-designed games. The types of video games include arcade, action/adventure, simulation games, platformers, puzzle games, and one sports game across all visual formats and player perspectives, with 2D custom-designed arcade-style games predominating. Across 24 studies spanning attention, response preparation, error processing, and feedback evaluation, canonical ERP components, such as P3, N2, L/RP, ERN, RewP/FRN, and oscillatory patterns, were observed during interactive gameplay. 

Our findings provide an overview of current research and may serve as a reference for researchers seeking to enhance ecological validity through video game-based experimental designs. In addition, our review identified several methodological considerations for video game-based paradigms. First, the relatively small number of eligible studies (24 of 1,098 records) suggests that this approach is still emerging. Additionally, the close temporal coupling between actions and outcomes in real-time gaming can complicate distinctions between response-locked and feedback-locked components, while the visual and auditory richness of video games introduces potential confounds. Commercial games present additional challenges, as precise variable control and access to exact event onset times are often limited. Finally, the diversity of video game genres and characteristics further presents challenges for direct comparability across studies. Future research should prioritize the systematic replication of classical EEG paradigms within video game contexts, incrementally adding perceptual complexity while carefully controlling potential confounds, with particular attention to event timing precision and stimulus control. Custom research video games that balance ecological validity with experimental control appear particularly promising. As the field evolves, video game-based paradigms may offer unique opportunities to investigate decision-making, reward processing, and cognitive control in interactive environments that better approximate the complexity of human cognition.
