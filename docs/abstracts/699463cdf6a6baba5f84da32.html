Introduction

Gliomas account for 26.3% of all primary brain and other central nervous system tumors, representing a significant challenge in neuro-oncology.^1 Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is widely used to support presurgical planning and functional mapping; however, the interpretation of BOLD-based activation in patients with glioma is complicated by tumor-induced alterations of the neurovascular unit. Recent work from our group demonstrated marked variability in task-evoked hemodynamic response function (HRF) characteristics in these patients, underscoring the need to better characterize the vascular mechanisms affecting BOLD signal interpretation.^2 

A critical factor influencing the BOLD signal is cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR), which reflects the capacity of cerebral vessels to modulate blood flow in response to physiological stimuli. While CVR is traditionally assessed using breath-hold or CO₂ challenges, resting-state fMRI approaches allow for the estimation of CVR by Amplitude of Low-Frequency Fluctuations.^3,4 CVR and HRF are thought to provide distinct but complementary information, CVR primarily indexes vascular reserve, whereas HRF parameters integrate neurovascular coupling efficiency and metabolic demands. 

The objective of this study is to characterize the relationship between CVR and HRF metrics across  task-based activated clusters in patients with glioma.  

Methods

This study involved the analysis of a group of 22 patients with low- and high-grade gliomas (WHO grades II–IV), treated at Hospital Carlos Van Buren (Valparaíso, Chile) between 2018 and 2022. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (CEC–SSVSA #72/2017), and all participants provided written informed consent. Tumor segmentations were manually performed by experienced neuroradiologists on anatomical MRI scans.  

Functional MRI data included both resting-state and task-based acquisitions. MRI data were acquired on a 1.5 T General Electric Signa HDxt scanner (Waukesha, WI, USA) using an 8-channel head coil. Anatomical images were obtained using a T1-weighted fast spoiled gradient echo (FSPGR) sequence, TE/TR of 1.9/6.1 ms, with a 256 × 256 matrix, 24 cm field of view, and 1.2 mm slice thickness, yielding high-resolution 3D T1-weighted volumes.^2 Task-fMRI data were acquired considering motor and/or 3 language paradigms. Motor tasks consisted in finger tapping or foot motion, according to the tumor location  and to the surgeons evaluation. Language paradigms consisted of Boston naming task, or of the reading of pseudo-words vs. a full sentence, or of verb  generation task. 

Task-fMRI data were previously analyzed using a Fuzzy General Linear Model, designed to accommodate tumor-related variability in BOLD responses. Task-evoked activation clusters were identified and hemodynamic response function (HRF) descriptors (including Height, Time-to-Peak (TTP), Width, Onset and Area Under the Curve (AUC)) were estimated using a double-gamma model. Cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) maps were obtained from resting-state fMRI data. After standard preprocessing steps (including motion correction and nuisance regression), voxel-wise Amplitude of Low-Frequency Fluctuations (ALFF) maps were computed. ALFF was calculated after temporal band-pass filtering in the 0.01–0.023 Hz frequency range, selected to emphasize low-frequency BOLD fluctuations associated with vascular reactivity while minimizing contamination from physiological noise and higher-frequency nonvascular components. This frequency band has been previously validated for resting-state CVR estimation without explicit vasoactive challenges^3,4. To enable inter-subject comparability, individual ALFF maps were normalized by the global mean ALFF value, computed across all non-zero voxels within each subject’s brain volume. This normalization step reduces inter-individual variability related to global signal scaling and yields relative CVR maps suitable for regional comparison across patients. 

For each patient and task, masks were reconstructed to identify HRF activation cluster locations and spatially resampled to match the geometry of the corresponding CVR maps. To ensure spatial integrity and data consistency, a multi-step verification process was performed: (a) visual inspection of HRF clusters overlaid on CVR maps to confirm anatomical correspondence; (b) cluster counting to verify consistency with the original HRF source files; and (c) preservation of original patient, task and cluster identifiers to ensure traceability throughout the pipeline. Exploratory analyses were performed by computing Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (ρ) between the median CVR of each cluster and selected HRF parameters (Height, TTP, Width, and Onset), allowing assessment of monotonic relationships without assuming linearity. 

Results

Across the full dataset, a total of 849 task-evoked HRF activation clusters were spatially matched to resting-state CVR maps across all patients. Cluster-level HRF descriptors showed substantial variability, particularly in temporal parameters. Time-to-peak (TTP) values ranged from approximately 4.7 to 8.1 s (10th-90th percentiles), response width from 3.7 to 7.3 s, and onset time from 1.6 to 3.4 s, whereas HRF height exhibited comparatively lower dispersion. 

Spearman correlation analysis revealed weak between cluster-level median CVR and selected temporal HRF descriptors. Median CVR was positively correlated with HRF width (ρ = 0.136, p < 10-4) and time-to-peak (ρ = 0.116, p < 10-3). A null association was observed between CVR and HRF onset (ρ = 0.062), which did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.071). No significant association was found between CVR and HRF height (ρ = -0.022, p = 0.52). 

Scatter plots of median CVR versus HRF descriptors illustrated a high degree of inter-cluster dispersion, with overlapping distributions across patients and tasks. 

Conclusion

In this study, we integrated resting-state CVR estimates with task-evoked HRF activation clusters across a group of patients with glioma, resulting in a unified dataset comprising 849 spatially matched clusters. HRF descriptors exhibited marked inter-cluster variability, particularly in temporal parameters, highlighting the heterogeneous nature of neurovascular responses in this population. 

Cluster-level analyses revealed null associations between CVR and temporal HRF descriptors. The substantial dispersion observed in CVR–HRF scatter plots indicates that clusters with similar vascular reactivity can exhibit markedly different HRF profiles. 

Together, these findings suggest that CVR alone is insufficient to explain HRF variability in glioma, supporting the notion that multiple vascular and neurovascular mechanisms contribute to BOLD signal alterations. Beyond these insights, this work establishes and validates a robust data-integration framework that enables region-specific CVR–HRF comparisons and provides a foundation for subsequent statistical analyses aimed at improving the interpretation of fMRI signals in the presence of brain tumors.
