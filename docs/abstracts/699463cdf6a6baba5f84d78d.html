Introduction

Understanding how brain regions causally interact to generate cognition and behavior remains a central challenge in neuroimaging. Traditional approaches—Granger causality, structural equation modeling (SEM), Bayesian networks, and dynamic causal modeling (DCM) - are constrained by acyclic, pairwise assumptions that preclude feedback, recurrence, and higher-order dependencies. Yet such recurrent and cyclic motifs are fundamental to neural computation, leaving essential aspects of causal architecture undetected.

These limitations are particularly evident in resting-state fMRI, where intrinsic, metastable coordination emerges without external input. Even at rest, the brain exhibits large-scale hemispheric symmetry, with persistent interhemispheric loops linking homologous sensorimotor and visual regions—patterns incompatible with acyclic, feedforward models. While existing methods can estimate directional effects among small sets of regions, they fail to capture the distributed, cyclic communication that dominates spontaneous neural activity. 

We propose a fundamentally different perspective that interprets causality through the lens of the minimum energy principle, thereby eliminating the acyclic assumptions inherent in regression-based causal models. Rooted in physics, this principle views the brain as a dynamical system that continually evolves toward minimal energy configurations, with causal flow proceeding naturally from regions of high potential energy (causes) to regions of low energy (effects). By embedding this principle within Hodge theory [1,3,6], we decompose network flows into dissipative and persistent modes, revealing recurrent cyclic causal patterns that remain invisible to conventional approaches. This formulation transcends the limitations of traditional causal models.

Methods

Spatial Scaffold. Resting-state fMRI data from 400 Human Connectome Project participants were used, with additional preprocessing steps as described in our previous study [4]. Brain regions were defined by the AAL parcellation, yielding 116 regional resting-state fMRI time series. A dynamic spatial scaffold was then constructed using a simplicial complex representation of the functional brain network (Fig. 1A). Directed edge flows were estimated via time-lagged Pearson correlations within sliding windows (20 s), producing a time-varying edge-flow matrix X(t). Higher-order simplices (triangles and tetrahedra) were retained only when all constituent edges exceeded a correlation threshold of 0.6, resulting in a sparse, hierarchical complex that captures meaningful higher-order interactions. The scaffold was encoded algebraically using boundary (incidence) matrices: B1 represents node–edge incidence and B2 represents edge–face incidence.

Dirichlet energy of an edge flow X(t) is defined through the 1-Hodge Laplacian

L1 = B1ᵀ B1 + B2 B2ᵀ [1,3], where B1ᵀ B1 captures divergence (source–sink activity) and B2 B2ᵀ captures cyclic circulation. This operator acts directly on edges and jointly characterizes acyclic and cyclic interactions in the network. The Dirichlet energy is given by

E(X) = Xᵀ L1 X = ||B1 X||² + ||B2ᵀ X||², which measures the total potential energy of the flow and defines the energy landscape governing causal dynamics. Edge flows are interpreted as fluid-like transport of information. Causal evolution follows the steepest descent of this energy (Fig. 1B), yielding a diffusion process in which energy decreases monotonically over time. Transient components decay, while the steady-state solution—the harmonic flow—persists as the non-dissipative backbone of network communication, encoding stable recurrent causal patterns.

Results

As a nontrivial demonstration of the proposed framework, we conducted two complementary analyses.

Emergence of Stable Cyclic Connectivity. We applied the minimum-energy framework to time-varying rs-fMRI edge flows to examine their long-term behavior. Conventional temporal averaging of connectivity does not yield a meaningful dynamical pattern—much like averaging white noise (Fig. 2A-left). In contrast, our approach evolves directed edge flows toward an extremely stable minimum-energy configuration, selectively suppressing transient, noise-driven fluctuations while preserving non-dissipative components. As a result, the system converges to an exceptionally stable metastate—the harmonic flow—that captures persistent, recurrent communication patterns (Fig. 2A-right). This stability arises because it is confined to recurrent cycles, whose number (the first Betti number) [1] is much smaller than the number of edges, thereby reducing variance in proportion to the cycle-to-edge ratio. This intrinsic stability enables principled averaging across time and subjects without loss of structure. The resulting population-level harmonic flow reveals robust cyclic interactions across functional systems that remain invisible to standard network methods.

Counterfactual modeling. We evaluated the stability of the inferred causal structure under counterfactual perturbations by selectively modifying or removing subsets of edges and recomputing the minimum-energy solution (Fig. 2B). This allowed us to assess how causal flows reorganize in response to hypothetical network disruptions and interventions, such as disease-related alterations (e.g., temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) [2]) or surgical procedures (e.g., anterior temporal lobectomy (ATL) [5]). Theoretically, the harmonic flow—defined as the minimum-energy fixed point—remains invariant under such perturbations, demonstrating exceptional metastability to counterfactual changes. This invariance highlights harmonic flow as a stable causal backbone of brain organization, capturing directed, recurrent structure that is not represented by conventional undirected constructs such as the default mode network (DMN).

Conclusion

We introduced a minimum-energy framework for dynamic causality that overcomes the acyclic and pairwise limitations of existing causal models. By modeling causality as directed edge flows governed by Dirichlet energy, the framework identifies harmonic flows as stable, non-dissipative fixed points encoding recurrent cyclic communication. Applied to large-scale resting-state fMRI, harmonic flow reveals robust population-level causal structure that persists across time, subjects, and counterfactual perturbations. Unlike undirected constructs such as DMN, harmonic flow provides a directional and topologically grounded alternative for characterizing intrinsic brain organization and causal invariants.
