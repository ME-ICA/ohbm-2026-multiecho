Introduction

One of the major outstanding challenges in neuroscience is the lack of a technique that measures brain activity with both high spatial and high temporal resolution. To address this, our group previously proposed an MR line‑scanning approach, “Direct Imaging of Neuronal Activity” (DIANA), designed to achieve millisecond temporal resolution (~5 ms) while preserving the spatial specificity achieved with MRI. Although initial rodent studies yielded promising results, they could not be robustly reproduced and were later shown to be partly confounded by a delay time used for stimulus triggering in the DIANA pulse sequence that generated artefactual peaks1. Furthermore, we recently reported a physical mechanism that significantly contribute to and may confound DIANA signals in some sense, called pseudo-steady state (PSS) of magnetization induced by RF spoiling2. While there remains a significant need for methods that combines high temporal and spatial resolution, it is not yet clear whether DIANA signals primarily reflect direct neuronal changes, indirect vascular or other physiological processes, or simply doesn’t capture a physiologically relevant signal. In this study we developed a new version of the DIANA line‑scanning sequence without a trigger delay time and with improved control of RF spoiling‑related artefacts and asked whether the resulting signal in humans behaves like a physiologically plausible visual response. If the DIANA signal captures a specific physiological response to visual stimulation (whether reflecting direct or indirect neural activity), we would expect stimulus‑locked activity peaking within the ~100 -150 ms time window3 and spatially localized to primary visual cortex (V1), with no such consistent spatial or temporal pattern in the control condition.

Methods

MRI sequence and data acquisition: We employed a gradient-echo-based line‑scanning method similar to that described in2, in order to achieve a high temporal resolution of 5 ms. Scan parameters were as follows: TR/TE = 5/2 ms, 38 encoding lines using a GRAPPA acceleration factor of 2, 100 measurements, in-plane resolution = 3x3 mm2, slice thickness = 3 mm, dummy scan = 2000 TRs. We acquired 8 slices per participant resulting in a total scan duration of 3 min 52 s per trial.  Since it has been shown that the original DIANA signals can be confounded by PSS oscillations at specific frequencies determined by the RF spoiling phase increment2, we developed a method to effectively suppress PSS oscillations. Based on equation (1) to (3) in Figure 1A, we found that the time series of the signal at normalized time t reduces to S[0]/36 ( Eq. (4), Figure 1A), which is constant across time frame index n (i.e., steady in time), thereby efficiently suppressing PSS oscillations when 36 trials are acquired per participants. However, performing 36 trials in human studies generally exceeds an acceptable scan time (with our parameters, experimental time > 1h 30m). Thus, a trade-off between scan time and data quality can be achieved by using a number of trials that is an integer divisor of 36. In this study, we therefore performed 9 trials with an RF spoiling phase increment of 50°, advancing the phase cycling table by four steps in each trial to suppress PSS oscillations to a considerable extent (Figure 1B).   

Experimental design: We scanned 14 participants on a 7T Siemens Magnetom Terra system and excluded 1 participant due to the presence of a brain cyst. To restrict stimulation to a single eye and reduce inter‑subject variability, participants wore an eye patch over the left eye. Each trial began with a 150 ms baseline period during which participants observed a fixation cross (1m distance, screen size 35x22 cm), followed by the flash of a dartboard pattern displayed on the half right side of the screen and ended with a 345 ms post‑stimulus baseline with the same fixation cross. Each participant completed 9 trials with visual stimulation and 9 trials with eyes closed (control condition) (Figure 2A).

Whole-brain T1-weighted MPRAGE and partial-FOV GRE images were acquired prior to the DIANA runs and used to normalize DIANA images to MNI space (nonlinear registration with FSL FNIRT estimated from the MPRAGE to MNI transformation). To further mitigate residual RF spoiling‑related artefacts2, we applied band‑stop filters at 6, 28, and 56 Hz, followed by 3 mm FWHM Gaussian spatial smoothing. Data were then temporally smoothed with a 5-point Hamming window, converted to percent signal change (PSC), linearly detrended, and the first 10 time points of each time series were discarded. After preprocessing, the mean tSNR per participant was 576 ± 47, and the cumulated tSNR across 13 participants was 1377.

For each participant, the 9 trials per condition were averaged to obtain a single mean time series per voxel.  For each voxel and each time point, we performed two one‑tailed paired t‑tests to assess (1) whether the signal at that time point was significantly higher than mean baseline (time before stimulus display), and (2) whether it was significantly higher than the other conditions. We considered a time point significant only if both tests yielded p < 0.01 and the corresponding 99% confidence intervals were consistent with that direction of effect. The same analysis was applied to the stimulus and control condition, and a shuffle condition in which each participant’s time series was temporally permuted (Npermutations = 100) to define a cluster‐size threshold (cluster size > 4 voxels, p < 0.05). Because we acquired 8 slices per participant, to maximize the brain volume covered in our analysis, we perform the analysis on the voxels that were overlapping with a minimum of 10 subjects (tSNR of 10 subjects = 1301).

Results

Because the physiological origin of the DIANA signal is not yet well established, we restricted our analyses to the visual cortex (V1-V4, derived from Schaefer atlas) and lateral geniculate nucleus (from Juelich atlas) for which we had specific a priori hypothesis.  In the stimulus condition, we observed that 6.75% of V1 voxels included in the acquisition volume (see Figure 2B) were activated in the left hemisphere and 6.9% in the right hemisphere, whereas in the control condition 4% of voxels in left V1 and 4.7% of voxels in right V1 were activated across time points (Figure 2C). This suggests that the DIANA sequence measures a different response in the stimulus condition compared to the control condition. Importantly, more voxels were activated in the stimulus condition, with the majority being significant after stimulus presentation (6.4% in both left and right V1 and 6.4% in right V1 voxel significant after stimulus display compared to  0.45% and 0.42% before stimulus display, respectively), whereas in the control condition the activated voxels were sparser and did not show any clear difference between baseline (1.36% in left V1 and 1.37% in right V1) and post‑stimulus period (2.6% in left v1 and 3.3% in right V1). Considering our criterion that a voxel must pass two paired t‑tests at α = 0.01, the expected chance‑level false‑positive rate is around 1% of voxels within a region of interest (0.01 × 0.01 per measurement × 100 measurements). Thus, the observed effects are unlikely to be explained by chance alone and suggest that the DIANA sequence is sensitive to differences between the stimulus and control conditions, and to the timing of stimulus presentation.

Figure 2D depicts the remaining significant voxels in V1 after cluster‑size correction. Most voxels in the control condition disappear, and the one remaining are not time‑locked to the stimulus. In contrast, in the stimulus condition, the remaining significant voxels are found after stimulus presentation, mainly in the right V1 at 130 ms (mean peak = 0.06 PSC, t = 5.9, df = 12, p < 0.0001) and 220 ms (mean peak = 0.06 PSC, t =4.6, df = 12, p < 0.0001). These latencies correspond to the expected timing of visual responses after stimulus onset, as reported in3. Within the 100–150 ms time window, we observed (Figure 2E) a single significant cluster at 130 ms in the stimulus condition (MNI coordinates: x = 18, y = −67, z = 9). The control condition also shows one significant cluster, but these are more sparsely distributed outside of V1, such as the cluster at 100 ms (peak = 0.059 PSC, t = 5.28, df = 11, p < 0.0001, MNI coordinates: x = −25, y = −90, z = 11). We note that when averaging the signal within the whole ROI (V1/V2/V3), the signal obtained varies around 0.02% PSC, as previously reported in4, suggesting that an ROI average based approach does not seem the most suitable for the DIANA fMRI signal (at least with the current preprocessing parameters).

Conclusion

The larger number of stimulus‑locked activated voxels compared to the control strongly suggests that the DIANA peaks are not solely due to sequence artifacts. Thus, the core question remains, what kind of signal do we measure with the DIANA line-scanning sequence? Once the RF spoiling-related artifacts with specific frequencies are accounted for and effectively minimized, DIANA fMRI signals may predominantly reflect changes in the effective spin-spin relaxation time (T2*), driven by microscopic magnetic field inhomogeneity and the associated broadening of the resonance spectrum. Based on our computation, we speculate that a transient narrowing of the Lorentzian spectral peak (i.e., a reduction in HWHM) could increase the signal in the corresponding time frame (around 0.2 % signal change for a 0.159 Hz reduction in HWHM at TE = 2 ms). This suggests that, if physiological processes linked to brain activity can induce millisecond-scale reductions in the effective linewidth, such events could manifest as positive peaks in the DIANA time course.

Our analysis reveals a difference of pattern observed between the stimulus and control condition, with a signal time-locked to the stimulus display in the stimulus condition compared to the control condition. Although we initially expected a more prominent signal in the left hemisphere, the stimulus covered half of the screen, making bilateral activation plausible if participants did not maintain precise fixation on the cross. Our current analysis seems thus sensitive enough to detect spatially and temporally consistent cluster of activation that could resemble (direct or indirect) neural signal. However, we also found significant cluster in the control condition, (although not time locked to the stimulus and not in V1 within the expected time window). Thus, the specificity of our analysis (the ability to discriminate between stimulus and control) should be improved. Our next planned experiment is to use a stimulus presented further into the right visual field, to test whether the line‑scanning DIANA sequence can detect hemispheric differences.

Although encouraging, those results should be interpreted in light of several important limitations, such as the lack of quantitative measure for head motion and eyes blinking. Additionally, given that the visual stimulus used is known to elicit one of the strongest responses in conventional fMRI, our effect size is small. Finally, our statistical approach does not include a full correction for multiple comparisons across time points and voxels, which inflates the likelihood of false positives despite the shuffle‑based cluster procedure. However, our primary goal here was to provide empirical, quantitative evidence showing that DIANA signal can capture a difference in signal between stimulus and control conditions which cannot be attributed solely to MR physics artefacts. Future work should thus focus on determining the number of participants and trials required to reliably discriminate between conditions and refining the analysis pipeline to improve the specificity of our results. Finally, these findings require replication in larger samples and under a broader range of experimental designs before strong conclusions can be drawn.

Despite these limitations, the present results provide an encouraging first step toward establishing the physiological relevance of line‑scanning DIANA fMRI in humans. They highlight the potential of this technique to combine high temporal resolution with meaningful spatial specificity in early visual cortex, thereby opening new avenues for studying fast neural dynamics that are difficult to access with conventional fMRI. We hope these findings will serve as a useful proof of concept for the neuroscience community and underscore the need for continued methodological refinement, quantitative validation, and systematic evaluation of both the promise and limitations of DIANA line‑scanning for studying human brain function.
