Introduction

Adolescents often fall short of established sleep guidelines, which puts them at increased risk for depression and anxiety. This study used arterial spin labelling (ASL) cerebral blood flow (CBF) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to investigate baseline brain  correlates of observed sleep changes associated with our innovative, whole-person health focused, fully remotely delivered mind-body intervention: Training for Awareness, Resilience, and Action (TARA). The current study investigated the neural mechanisms associated with mindfulness-based sleep improvement using ASL CBF. While most of the neuroimaging work done on sleep and mindfulness has relied on structural or functional MRI, relatively few studies have investigated how sleep changes may relate to CBF. ASL offers a non-invasive, safe method for quantifying CBF. The first aim was to test the efficacy of our 12-week TARA intervention on participants’ sleep quality, as compared to a wait-list control group, among a cohort of healthy adolescent in a randomized controlled trial. Based on past trials of TARA, we hypothesized that individuals in the TARA group would experience significantly greater improvements in self-reported sleep quality, as compared to those in the control group. The second aim was to investigate the ASL CBF correlates of sleep improvements in brain regions that are important to both mindfulness and sleep. Based on the prior literature, we chose to investigate the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), the middle frontal gyri (MFG), the amygdala, the putamen, the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and the medial orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). Because of the importance of these regions in both mindfulness and sleep, we hypothesized that baseline ASL CBF could serve as a predictor of sleep improvements from TARA, and that ASl CBF changes in the aforementioned regions would be associated with changes in sleep, as measured by the insomnia severity index, in the TARA group compared to the control group.

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of California, San Francisco. To be included in the study, adolescents were required to be (1) between 14-18 years, (2) English-speaking, and (3) without major medical or psychiatric diagnosis. Participants who reported current use of psychotropic medications, presence of MRI-incompatible metallic implants, recent mindfulness practice (within three months), or a lack of access to a device with a camera and microphone were excluded from participation. The recruited sample included 100 adolescents (57 female, 43 male; mean age =15.93 (1.33) years). This study used a wait-list control randomized controlled trial design. Participants were randomly assigned to either the 12-week TARA intervention group (N=50) or a waitlist control group (N=50). To acquire neuroimaging data, all participants underwent an MRI scan prior to participating in the study, and at 12-weeks post-baseline. TARA is a 12-week MBI that integrates elements of mindfulness and yoga. It is designed to target neural systems commonly disrupted in adolescents experiencing depression, including emotional dysregulation, autonomic dysfunction, default mode network dominance, and amygdala hyperactivity. All TARA sessions were delivered remotely via Zoom. Sleep disturbance and insomnia were measured using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), which is a 7-item scale assessing the characteristics of participants’ difficulties sleeping over the past two weeks, and how they may interfere with quality of life. Across items such as “difficulty falling asleep,” “difficulty staying asleep,” and “to what extent do you consider your sleep problem to interfere with your daily functioning?”, participants rated the severity of each symptom on a Likert scale from 0-4, where a higher number represented increased severity. Total ISI scores range from 0 to 28. The ISI has been validated in adolescents 14-19 years old with excellent internal consistency (α = .90). In this study, the ISI was collected remotely and securely through online surveys at baseline and 12-weeks after baseline (pre- and post-training in the TARA group). All participants underwent structural and perfusion imaging on a 3T GE Discovery MR750 (General Electric Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) with a Nova 32-channel head coil (Nova Medical, Wilmington, MA, USA). The scan included a standard high-resolution T1-weighted sagittal MPRAGE (TE/TR/TI=3/1060/7ms, flip angle=8º, FOV=25.6x25.6cm, matrix=256x256, slice thinness=1mm) sequence and a pseudocontinuous ASL  sequence (3D multi-shot fast spin echo with 8 arms of spiral readout, TE/TR=10.52/4846 ms, slice thickness=4mm). Data processing was conducted using the fMRIB Software Library (FSL v5.0.8) and custom Matlab scripts. First, the perfusion-weighted images were converted into quantitative ASL CBF maps. Per the ISMRM Perfusion Study Group Recommendation, we used the single-compartment model with the assumed efficiency of 0.8 for conversion of raw ASL signal into quantitative CBF units. Visual inspection was performed on the original ASL images and CBF maps to exclude images with motion artifacts. ASL CBF Maps were aligned to the T1-weighted structural image using FSL FLIRT. The T1-weighted images were registered to MNI152 2mm template space with FLIRT, using 12 degrees of freedom and the correlation ratio cost function. The transformation matrix was inverted and applied to the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas, which was then resampled into native ASL space using nearest-neighbor interpolation. Mean ASL CBF was quantified in the following AAL a priori regions of interest bilaterally, selected based on theoretical relevance to the TARA intervention, mindfulness, and sleep: Putamen, Caudate, Thalamus, Amygdala, Hippocampus, Insula, ACC, PCC, MFG, and medial OFC

Results

Participants

Out of the 100 participants originally enrolled, 45 participants in the TARA group and 49 participants in the control group completed the study. 3 participants (2 controls and 1 TARA participant) did not fill out post-intervention ISI and were excluded from analysis.  All of the remaining participants completed pre- and post-intervention MRIs. The ASL MRI data from 3 participants (1 control and 3 TARA participants) was excluded due to inadequate MRI quality. Following these exclusions, the final sample for analysis included 90 participants, 42 participants in the TARA group and 48 in the waitlist control group. 

The Effect of TARA on Changes in ISI

There was a significant group effect on change in ISI scores, controlling for age and sex (F(1,86) = 5.98, p = 0.0166). Adolescents in the TARA group showed a significantly larger improvement in ISI scores compared to the waitlist control group. This effect remained significant in a sensitivity analysis using a linear mixed model that included baseline ISI scores (F = 4.9, p = 0.037).

The Effects of TARA on the Relationship Between Baseline ASL CBF and ISI Change

There were significant group by baseline ASL CBF interaction effects on change in ISI for the Right ACC ROI (F =4.76, p = .032), the Left Putamen ROI (F =4.38, p = .039), and the Right Medial OFC ROI (F =9.1721, p = .0033). See Table 2.  For each ROI, in the TARA group, there was a positive relationship between baseline ASL CBF and change in ISI. Lower baseline ASL CBF was associated with improvements in ISI. In the waitlist control group, there was no relationship between baseline ASL CBF and change in ISI. There were no significant interactions for the other ROIs.  

Significant main effects of baseline ASL CBF were observed for the left amygdala (F = 8.555, p = 0.0045) and left middle frontal gyrus (MFG) (F = 4.132, p = 0.0452). For each ROI, in both groups, there was a positive relationship between baseline ASL CBF and change in ISI. Lower baseline ASL CBF was associated with worsening ISI. There were no significant main effects for the other ROIs. 

The Effects of TARA on the Relationship Between Change in ASL CBF and Change in ISI

There were significant main effects of change in ASL CBF on change in ISI for the Right PCC ROI (F = 5.563, p=.0208), the Left MFG ROI (F = 7.53, p=.0075), the Right MFG ROI ( F = 5.668, p=.0197), the Left Medial OFC ROI (F =4.599, p=.035), the Left Caudate ROI (F =8.385, p = .0049) and the Right Caudate ROI (F =6.3405, p = .0138). See Table 3,  Across all these ROIs, increases in ASL CBF were significantly associated with a decrease in ISI scores. These associations were consistent across both groups, with no significant group-by-ASL CBF change interactions detected. There were no significant effects for other ROIs.

Conclusion

This study identified three key findings related to changes in insomnia symptoms and associated brain perfusion patterns among adolescents undergoing the TARA intervention. First, adolescents in the TARA group who completed 12-weeks of mindfulness-based training showed a significantly greater reduction in ISI scores compared to those in the waitlist control group, indicating improved sleep quality. Second, we observed significant group-by-baseline ASL CBF interaction effects on ISI change for the Right ACC, Left Putamen, and Right Medial OFC. Specifically, within the TARA group, lower baseline ASL CBF in these regions was predictive of greater improvements in ISI. Finally, significant main effects of ASL CBF change on ISI change were identified across multiple regions, including the Right PCC, bilateral MFG, Left Medial OFC, and bilateral Caudate. In each of these regions, increases in perfusion were significantly associated with reductions in ISI scores in both groups, suggesting a potential common neurobiological mechanism underlying sleep improvement across groups.

Clinical Implications

Our results show that the TARA group had significant decreases in insomnia symptoms, supporting previous findings that MBIs can positively impact sleep quality in adolescents.  TARA shows strong potential to serve as a preventative, whole-person health-focused intervention for both physical health and emotional wellbeing, given sleep’s well-established associations with anxiety and depression. In the TARA group, we also observed a significant association between improved ISI scores and decreased baseline right ACC, left putamen, and right medial OFC ASl CBF. If replicated, ASL CBF in these brain regions may serve as a marker to identify adolescents with sleep disruption for whom TARA is especially indicated. We also found that increases in right PCC, bilateral MFG, left medial OFC, and caudate ASL CBF were associated with ISI improvements, irrespective of group status. The association of ASL CBF changes in these regions and insomnia symptom improvement suggest a potential mechanistic link with sleep pathology that future studies could investigate. The clinical utility of TARA is also greatly enhanced by the fact that no advanced degree is required to lead the program, and remote administration is feasible, both of which allow for increased scalability and accessibility to adolescents living in areas with limited or no access to adequate mental healthcare. 

Conclusion. Our study supported our prior findings that the TARA intervention has efficacy in improving sleep among healthy adolescents. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to find a significant predictive effect of decreased baseline regional ASL CBF and sleep improvement in a mindfulness-based intervention group, compared to the control group. We also found increases in ASl CBF coinciding with sleep improvement in both groups, suggesting common underlying neural processes. We emphasize the significance of these findings within the growing literature at the intersection of sleep and mindfulness and highlight the clinical utility of TARA as a remotely delivered, neuroscience-based intervention to improve sleep in adolescents.
