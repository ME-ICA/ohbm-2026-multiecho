Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) is one of the most affordable and portable neuroimaging techniques, allowing the measurement of fast-changing brain processes linked to cognition and fluctuations in mental state. Despite its widespread clinical use, EEG research is often constrained by modest sample sizes, limited multi-site coverage, and insufficient harmonization. Most studies still rely on a simple case-control paradigm, juxtaposing a “healthy” and a pathological cohort from which to extract features or train and test algorithms. This practice is vulnerable to sampling bias in the healthy (training) cohort and offers limited generalizability. When sample size is low, investigators typically resort to covariate stratification (e.g., sex splits, age bins), which only partially accounts for developmental and demographic effects and cannot recover true population variability. Normative modeling offers a principled alternative by mapping individual EEG features onto, for instance, age- and sex-specific reference distributions1, yielding subject-level deviation scores rather than group averages. Here we present EEGNorms, a full-lifespan normative atlas of resting-state EEG spectral features, trained on more than 1400 reference individuals and validated on 200+ healthy individuals, and apply it to individuals with different illnesses, spanning neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative, and psychiatric conditions.

Methods

We modeled resting-state EEG (eyes-open and eyes-closed) from a large (N = 1434, 1-85 years, 64% female) multi-site dataset (10 sites) (Figure 1a). Raw signals were cleaned with an automatic standard denoising pipeline. PREP algorithm was applied to re-reference (RANSAC), notch (50 or 60 Hz) and bandpass filter (0.5-50 Hz) each channel autonomously via automatic filtering. Each clean EEG recording, per channel, was divided in 8 seconds epochs and epochs sent to subsequent feature extraction step. We performed additional independent component analysis to reduce eye-blinking and spurious peak artifacts; results did not substantially differ from the base pipeline.

Power spectra and relative narrow-bands ratios were calculated via Welch’s method (2-s Hamming window, 50% overlap). Power spectral density estimates were then averaged over the epochs to reduce bias as per Bartlett’s method. Average power spectra were then normalized to the total power and narrow-bands relative powers were extracted as the integral of the spectrum within fixed frequency ranges. The frequency bands were delta [0.5,4] Hz, theta [4,8] Hz, alpha [8,12] Hz, beta [12,32] Hz and gamma [32,50] Hz. To include further post-hoc evaluations, we modeled both relative band powers as   and as  after proper log-transform and scaling. Power ratios were calculated as ratios between channel-wise narrow-bands powers and log-scaled (natural logarithm) to facilitate modeling. Aperiodic component 1/f slope and intercept were estimated using FOOOF fitting algorithm. Peak alpha frequency (PAF) was estimated using a derivative-based approach as the local maximum within the alpha band. Finally, we exploited Koessler atlas 3D channels mapping to the MNI space to aggregate channels from different standards. The atlas maps canonical channels to a set of (x, y, z) coordinates that refer then to a total of 11 lobes. Regions were Frontal right, median, left; Parietal right, median, left; Temporal right, left; Occipital right, median, left. All 231 features were normalized at the individual level, and data quality was quantified as the percentage of retained fixed-length epochs after automatic rejection. Normative trajectories were estimated with a distributional regression algorithm, generalized additive models for location, scale and shape (GAMLSS) with a 4-parameter SHASH family distribution2. We included a sex-specific spline for μ(age), a mild spline for σ(age), random site intercepts, and fixed effects for resting-state paradigm (eyes open vs. eyes closed) and data quality. Models were validated with an independent subset of more than 200 non-diagnosed subjects (sex-, age-matched) to test against overfitting.

Results

We used the 462 models to: (i) confirm established EEG developmental trends; (ii) quantify effects of sex and topography in the reference population. (iii) characterize heterogeneity, overlap and diagnosis-specific abnormalities in pathological cohorts; (iv) describe diagnostic-specific abnormal trajectories.

Reference-to-reference (lifespan norms) Normative models yielded biologically plausible lifespan trajectories for all spectral features (Figure 2a). Delta and theta relative power decreased steadily from childhood into early adulthood, with roughly constant variability across regions. This is consistent with a developmental shift of EEG power from slower to faster frequencies 3. In contrast, beta and gamma power increased monotonically, often showing plateaus from early to mid-adulthood and age-related increases in variability. Alpha relative power and peak alpha frequency (PAF) followed non-linear, inverted-U trajectories. Both increased rapidly up to around 20 years, then remained relatively stable between 20–40 years, followed by subtle declines at older ages. Median PAF values stabilized around 9.5–10 Hz across regions, with largely constant variance. Aperiodic 1/f slope and intercept showed steep decreases in early life, flattened during adulthood, and then exhibited a mild acceleration of decline at older ages. Band ratios captured additional age-specific and region-specific patterns that reflected these underlying band trends. Across 1,354 left–right region–band comparisons, permutation tests yielded a mean p-value of 0.94 (SD 0.13). After false discovery rate (FDR) correction, no significant hemispheric asymmetries were detected at the lobar scale. Likewise, for 231 region–band features, none of the sex effects survived FDR correction. Validation set z-score distributions closely approximated the standard normal, indicating good calibration of the models while still capturing intrinsic inter-individual EEG variability.

Overall, the lifespan models are in line with previous reports on developmental changes in EEG spectral properties. This convergence strengthens the representativeness of our models and supports the use of EEGNorms as a tool to aid individualized medicine.

Pathological-to-reference (multi-diagnostic deviations) In Alzheimer’s disease (N = 36, 49–80 years, 66% female), z-scores frequently deviated from the reference norms, with medium to large effect sizes (r ∈ [−0.55, 0.74]). Key findings included increased delta and theta power, reduced alpha power and PAF, with direct effects on band ratios. Deviations from the norm were more pronounced in early-onset patients and converged toward zero with age, suggesting that physiological aging reduces the gap between pathological and expected trajectories. In frontotemporal dementia (N = 22, 45–79 years, 41% female), deviations followed similar patterns but with smaller effect sizes. This overlap highlights the limited discriminative power of standard spectral quantitative EEG for fine-grained dementia subtyping. Our results are consistent with the well-characterized EEG spectral phenotype of dementia 4 (Figure 2b,c,e).

For attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), we analyzed a large cohort (N = 1,024, 4–22 years, 60% female) to compare diagnostic z-scores with age- and sex-matched norms. EEGNorms enabled replication of classic ADHD findings based on band ratios5, and highlighted marked within-group variability, in line with spectral heterogeneity of ADHD subtypes Effect sizes of significant results were small (r ∈ [−0.19, 0.18]). Group-level z-scores clustered around zero and mirrored those in autism spectrum disorder (ASD; N = 174, 5–21 years, 63% female). 

This proves substantial within-group variability (Figure 2d) and diagnostic overlap among neurodevelopmental conditions6. ADHD and ASD share similar trajectories and deviations, which markedly limits the diagnostic specificity of the spectral EEG biomarkers examined here.

Conclusion

EEGNorms provides a full-lifespan, multi-site normative framework for resting-state EEG spectral organization. The models are harmonized across sites, paradigms and key covariates, and are expressed as age- and sex-specific conditional distributions rather than single average curves. They reproduce known developmental and aging trends in oscillatory and aperiodic activity, show minimal sex and hemispheric effects at the lobar scale, and generate well-calibrated z-scores for unseen reference cohorts. When applied to multiple diagnostic groups, EEGNorms quantifies both group-level deviations and within-group heterogeneity. It reveals substantial spectral overlap between Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal dementia, and between ADHD and ASD, underscoring the limited diagnostic specificity of group-level spectral markers alone. Taken together, these findings position normative EEG spectral modeling as a scalable first-line functional tool for early abnormality detection, lifespan tracking of brain dynamics, and harmonized comparison of heterogeneous clinical cohorts within a common deviation space.
