Introduction

Structural–functional coupling (SFC), defined as the degree to which structural (i.e. white matter) connectivity is aligned with functional connectivity, has been proposed to reflect aspects of brain organization and maturation1. Adolescence is one of the key developmental periods characterized by neuroplasticity, leading to extensive reorganization and maturation that may support ongoing cognitive and socioemotional development. Ongoing white matter maturation during this period strengthens communication efficiency but also reduces plasticity. Importantly, these developmental changes unfold differently across the brain, shaping how structural and functional connectivity relate to one another1. 

Within this developmental context, the present study characterized brain-wide SFC patterns during adolescence, a period when structural–functional alignment becomes increasingly relevant for higher-order cognitive and socioemotional developmental processes. We first examined the degree of coupling in unimodal (e.g., vision, sensory) and transmodal (e.g., frontal, parietal, salience) regions. Unimodal regions are typically marked by stronger within-network structural–functional alignment, whereas transmodal regions often exhibit lower within-network alignment1. In addition, we examined between-network SFC to capture the extent to which structural connectivity supports functional connectivity across large-scale brain networks. By examining brain-wide SFC, we aimed to capture how coupling differs across networks that follow the hierarchical organization of the brain, from unimodal regions to transmodal regions, during the developmental period of heightened neuroplasticity. Moreover, we examined whether SFC differs based on sociodemographic factors documented to have implications on brain development during adolescence, including sex, puberty, and early life household poverty.

Methods

Data from 161 adolescents in the Study of Adolescent to Adult Neural Development (75% African American; 58% female; mean age = 15.88 years, range = 15.03–17.60) were used. To calculate SFC, structural connectivity matrices were derived from diffusion MRI using probabilistic tractography, while functional connectivity matrices were computed from resting-state fMRI. SFC measures were estimated as Spearman’s correlation between a region’s structural and functional connectivity vectors. For each network, within-network SFC was derived by subsetting regions to only those in the same network, while between-network coupling was calculated using connectivity from all regions within a network to all regions outside of the network. Regional SFC values were then averaged to generate network-level between- and within- coupling metrics. We compared within- and between-network SFC across seven networks in the Schaefer (2018) 7-network atlas: visual, somatomotor, dorsal attention, salience, frontoparietal, limbic, and default mode. We used linear mixed-effects models to test within- and between-network SFC differences, adjusting for in-scanner head motion during resting scan, with one model for within-networks and another for between-networks metrics. Pairwise comparisons were adjusted with a Tukey correction. In terms of the associations between SFC and sociodemographic factors, we examined sex (female/male), pubertal status (sum score using self-report on the Pubertal Development Scale; mean (sd): 3.58 (0.47) girls, 2.87 (0.46) boys), and household poverty (income-to-needs determined by the U.S. Census Bureau; median: little over 100% of poverty threshold), all in one model using multiple regression. These variables were selected given their well-documented roles in shaping neurodevelopment during adolescence. All analyses were adjusted for head motion. As an exploratory analysis, for sociodemographic variables that were significant, we examined whether within- and between-network SFC patterns were more homogeneous or heterogeneous across the brain by computing Pearson correlations of SFC values across brain networks within each demographic subgroup, followed by Fisher’s r-to-z tests to compare correlation coefficients between subgroups.

Results

Overall, within-network SFC (rs ≈ 0.24–0.29) were consistently higher than between-network SFC (rs ≈ 0.03–0.12) across all seven networks. For within-network SFC, unimodal networks, such as the visual and somatomotor networks (rs ≈ 0.27–0.29), exhibited comparable or higher SFC than transmodal networks (rs ≈ 0.24–0.29) (p ≈ < 0.0001–0.94). However, for between-network SFC, transmodal networks (rs ≈ 0.05–0.12) exhibited higher SFC than unimodal networks (rs ≈ 0.03–0.04) (p ≈ < 0.0001). Within-network SFC was highest for the somatomotor network (r = 0.292), followed by the dorsal attention network (r = 0.290), and lowest for the default mode (r = 0.25) and frontoparietal (r = 0.24) networks. Between-network SFC was highest for the salience network (r = 0.12), followed by the dorsal attention network (r = 0.11), and lowest for the visual (r = 0.03) and somatomotor (r = 0.04) networks. After accounting for pubertal status, boys consistently had higher SFC than girls across all within- and between-network SFC measures (rs ≈ 0.03–0.30; rs ≈ 0.03–0.29). Specifically, statistically significant differences were observed in the somatomotor (p < .01), salience (p < .01), frontoparietal (p < .001), and default mode (p < .001) networks for within-network SFC, and in the salience (p < .001) and frontoparietal (p < .01) networks for between-network SFC. These differences emerged even after accounting for household poverty threshold (Figure 1). While mean between-network SFC was lower for girls, girls generally showed a more homogeneous between-network SFC pattern across the seven networks. Pairwise correlations ranged from −0.04 to 0.61 in girls and −0.12 to 0.59 in boys, with Fisher’s r-to-z tests indicating significantly higher pattern-level correlations in girls for many of the network pairs (p < .05).

Conclusion

Within-network and between-network patterns showed diverging trends: unimodal networks generally had higher within-network SFC than transmodal networks, whereas transmodal networks generally exhibited higher between-network SFC than unimodal networks. While girls showed more advanced pubertal status, boys demonstrated higher SFC coupling than girls. In addition, girls showed more homogeneous between-network SFC patterns, whereas boys exhibited more heterogeneous SFC patterns. Taken together, these findings suggest brain-wide neurodevelopmental variations in SFC during adolescence, reflecting both potential hierarchical differences across neural systems and sex-based variability in SFC patterns.
