Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) disproportionately affects women, yet the neural mechanisms underlying this sex difference remain poorly understood (Lopez-Lee et al 2024). Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)–based connectomics provide a promising framework for identifying early network-level biomarkers of AD. Prior studies using functional connectomes (FC) have reported early hyperconnectivity followed by network degradation, particularly within the default mode network (DMN) and salience network (Schultz et al. 2017). Some work has shown FC measures correlate with positron emission tomography (PET) markers of tau and amyloid-β pathology, two core hallmarks of AD (Abuwarda et al. 2025), but a comprehensive picture of the relationship between FC and misfolded protein status is not complete.

We also know females have a higher risk of developing Alzheimer's - and thus may have a different pathological or physiological set of biomarkers of the disease. Further investigation into the sex-specific differences in dementia conversion holds the potential to elucidate the brain networks that are vulnerable to AD pathology in males and females. Here, we collect and analyze resting-state fMRI connectomes and both amyloid‑β and tau PET status to compare how the functional connectome differs between the sexes, and within the sexes across positive or negative PET status. Having knowledge of sex-specific early changes in FC would allow both a more precise early warning system and could shed light on potential therapeutics that work better for both sexes.

Methods

The quantitative neuroimaging laboratory (QNL) dataset, collected at Weill Cornell Medicine, includes older adults (N = 233, aged 60-80 years, 115 female) with PET tau and PET amyloid-β imaging as well as rs‑fMRI and DTI data. Thirty-two out of 115 females were tau+ and 23 were amyloid‑β+, while 34 males were tau+ and 21 males were amyloid‑β+. MRI data were processed using previously reported pipelines (Jamison et al., 2025). Resting-state fMRI scans were acquired with 2 mm isotropic voxels and 800 ms TRs. T1-weighted anatomical images were used for registration and parcellation. Preprocessing included motion correction, slice timing correction, spatial normalization to MNI space, and regression of nuisance signals (e.g., white matter, CSF, motion parameters). Functional connectomes were generated by parcellating the brain into a 264 region cortical, subcortical and cerebellar atlas (Power et al. 2011) and computing pairwise Pearson correlations of voxel-wise average rs-fMRI time series. PET Imaging was collected using [18F]Flourobetaben and [18F]MK6240 tracers for amyloid‑β and tau, respectively.

We performed statistical testing to compare FCs across 1) sexes (independent of PET status), 2) PET status categories (independent of sex), 3) within-sex, across PET status categories, and 4) within PET status, across sex. This allows us to analyze sex differences and PET status differences, as well as determine the sex-specific and sex independent effect of PET status on FC. Edge‑wise comparisons of FC entries were made using Welch’s unpaired t-test, and p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction with significance set at p < 0.05 after multiple comparisons. To visualize the separation of our FC space, we applied UMAP to subject‑wise functional connectivity vectors using 15 neighbors , a minimum distance of 0.1, and Euclidean distance as the metric. Finally, support vector machine (SVM) classifiers were trained to predict PET‑derived amyloid‑β and tau status (positive vs. negative) from subject‑wise functional connectivity features. To reduce dimensionality and mitigate overfitting, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied prior to classification. Model performance was evaluated using nested 4‑fold cross‑validation.

Results

Figure 2A shows the t-statistics (unthresholded) for the various comparisons; while few edges reached statistical significance, group‑level trends emerged. Overall, females had lower FC than males, particularly within and between somatomotor/sensory (SMN), Cingulo-opercular Task Control (CON) and auditory network (AN). Males and females demonstrated comparable connectivities within the default mode network (DMN). Females had markedly increased connectivity between subcortical networks (SCN) and SMN and CON networks. 

Across sexes, AD pathology as measured by PET tau and PET amyloid‑β positivity increases connectivity between SMN, CON, and AN, while decreasing within network connectivity for those same regions. There is also an increase in connectivity within the visual network (VN) and between VN and all other networks with the exception of the DMN. Within-sex comparisons demonstrate females drive most of the SMN-CON-AN effect between network hyperconnectivity and within network hypoconnectivity, while males drive hyperconnectivity within VN and between VN and non-DMN regions and drive the DMN-VN hypoconnectivity. Generally, these within sex patterns hold between PET tau and PET amyloid‑β, but are more pronounced in PET tau.

Figure 2B shows UMAP projections of FC space. These projections fail to capture demographic differences as well as PET status between subjects. Figure 2C is a table with SVM models classification accuracies, which show that amyloid‑β and tau status (+ or -) had accuracies of 64% and 61%, respectively.  

Conclusion

Our preliminary results highlight that there are differences in the FC of individuals with and without amyloid‑β or tau pathologies, even before AD diagnoses, and, moreover, those differences vary by sex. When performing FC edge-wise comparisons, we find hyperconnectivity present in response to AD pathological proteins. We also note sex-specific differences, with more exaggerated elevations in functional connectivity emerging for females with pathological proteins versus males with pathological proteins overall. This suggests that there are sex-specific differential vulnerabilities to AD proteins that may underpin differing outcomes between the two groups. Understanding sex differences in dementia pathology and vulnerability is critical if we are to better treat and prevent AD in both sexes.

Future work will include application of the Krakencoder on the QNL’s connectome data to create a multi-modal (structure-function) connectome latent space that can be crafted to reflect outcomes - for example, AD pathological protein status - in more sensitive ways. We also plan to validate our findings in a second dataset - namely ADNI - to verify that our model is generalizable. We anticipate that once the base Krakencoder model is finetuned on QNL data, held-out data will show differences between sex, age, and PET amyloid-β and tau status, unlike when observing FC projections alone. Latent embedding loadings will be utilized on the ADNI data for predictive modeling of diagnostic labels and neurocognitive scores. Because we anticipate biologically meaningful separation between PET amyloid-β and tau status within the latent space, we hypothesize that embedding loadings will be predictive of conversion between CN to MCI and MCI to AD on longitudinal ADNI data. We also anticipate a linear relationship between latent embeddings and neurocognitive scores.
