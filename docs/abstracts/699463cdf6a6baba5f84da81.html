Introduction

Precise delineation of amygdala sub-nuclei is critical for neuroimaging research but extremely challenging, as internal boundaries are largely invisible at standard resolutions (1mm). Consequently, current atlases rely on priors derived from histology1, which introduces non-linear geometric distortions when registering 2D cellular maps back to 3D MRI space due to tissue shrinkage and tearing. Alternatively, ex vivo 7T MRI2 provides a distortion-free reference but lacks the ultra-high resolution and SNR required to resolve microscopic interfaces, thereby leaving fine boundaries between adjacent nuclei obscured by partial volume effects. These limitations result in a lack of high-fidelity, distortion-free ground truth labels, creating a critical bottleneck for deep learning segmentation. Unlike traditional atlas-based warping, which biases results toward the target atlas, deep learning offers the potential for unbiased segmentation of diverse cohorts such as neonates or pathological brains, but only if trained on robust, high-resolution anatomical definitions. To address this gap, we constructed a probabilistic atlas based on ultra-high-field 9.4T ex vivo MRI T2-weighted images, establishing a reliable reference standard to underpin these unbiased automated frameworks.

Methods

Data & Preprocessing: We utilized 10 post-mortem human-brain specimens (ages 71.7 ± 5.2 years). Each underwent a multi-scale imaging protocol: whole-brain scanning at 7T (1mm) using a T2-weighted 3D SPACE sequence, followed by dissected amygdala scanning at 9.4T (0.1x0.1x0.2mm) using a TurboRARE sequence. We developed a robust registration pipeline to bridge these scales: 1) 7T whole-brain scans were manually registered to MNI space to align the final atlas to this standard coordinate system (Figure 1A); 2) left and right hemispheric ROIs were cropped from the 7T scans and resampled to 0.1mm isotropic resolution (Figure 1B); 3) 9.4T hemispheric scans were manually co-registered to these corresponding 7T ROIs. Finally, volumes were cropped to a 200x200x200 voxel ROI (20x20x20mm) centered on the amygdala (Figure 1C).

Protocol & Atlas Construction: We established a manual segmentation protocol for 10 sub-nuclei on T2-weighted images, accelerated by nnInteractive3 and based on architectonic definitions from The Human Nervous System and cross-referenced with the Allen Human Brain Reference Atlas. Two expert raters segmented all 9.4T hemispheres, and a population-specific T2-weighted template was constructed using an iterative shape-averaging pipeline implemented in Greedy. The process began with rigid initialization, followed by multi-resolution deformable registration using the Sum of Squared Differences (SSD) metric (200x100x50x25 iterations). The template shape was updated via averaged inverse warps to minimize bias (Figure 1D).

Quantitative Profiling: To investigate signal properties within these anatomical boundaries, we constructed quantitative MRI maps (R2*, T2*, and SWI) using QSMxT4 from multi-echo gradient echo magnitude and phase images. These maps were propagated to the template space to assess the spatial correlation between our manual geometric boundaries and intrinsic tissue magnetic susceptibility profiles.

Automation Feasibility: To demonstrate the utility of the atlas for unbiased automation, we trained SynthSeg5, a generative framework that synthesizes diverse, contrast-agnostic training scans from input label maps. We utilized our specimen-specific ground truth label maps as these inputs, employing a label fusion strategy to embed the high-resolution 9.4T amygdala labels within global 7T brain segmentations. This provided the necessary anatomical context to constrain the random non-linear spatial deformations applied by SynthSeg's generator, ensuring the synthesis of anatomically realistic training data (Figure 1D).

Results

Manual Reliability: The proposed protocol yielded consistent reproducibility between raters, with an average Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 0.62 across all nuclei. Performance was highest in the Lateral (0.88), Basomedial (0.74), Basolateral (0.72), and CATA (0.71) nuclei. Moderate agreement was observed in the Paralaminar (0.57) nucleus. Smaller, low-contrast nuclei showed greater variability: Medial (0.48), AAA (0.43), and Central (0.42).

Quantitative Microstructural Characterization: Quantitative profiling confirmed distinct, hemispherically consistent microstructural signatures. Averaged R2* values distinguished the Lateral and Basolateral nuclei (~53.6 Hz) from the CATA (~49.0 Hz). A stable ~1.6x heterogeneity ratio (Lateral StdDev ~6.2 vs. Basolateral ~3.9) across hemispheres validated the Lateral Nucleus's complex vascular architecture as a reproducible biological feature. Additionally, SWI uniquely highlighted the Medial Nucleus (intensity ≈ 0.67) as the most distinct susceptibility source. Collectively, these findings demonstrate robust relative contrast gradients across all sub-nuclei, preserved despite a systematic inter-hemispheric offset (Figure 2).

Feasibility of Automation: The SynthSeg model, trained on the fused labels from a single rater, achieved a Dice score of 0.698 on the held-out test set. This performance exceeds human inter-rater reliability, demonstrating that a generative deep learning approach can effectively learn high-resolution features using a framework designed to avoid the population bias inherent in traditional atlas warping.

Conclusion

We present a high-fidelity 9.4T probabilistic atlas of the human amygdala, developed through a combined manual registration and segmentation pipeline. By integrating ultra-high-resolution ex-vivo data into standard MNI space and characterizing sub-nuclei with distinct susceptibility signatures (R2* and SWI), we resolve persistent ambiguities in anatomical definition. Leveraging this reliable reference standard, our results demonstrate that generative deep learning (SynthSeg) can surpass human reliability in automating these segmentations. This framework provides a critical technical foundation for robust neuroimaging, allowing future automated tools to improve segmentation generalizability across diverse clinical, neonatal, and pathological populations.
