Introduction

Psychosis emerges along a continuum from genetic predisposition through prodromal symptoms to full syndrome manifestation. Understanding how brain network organization changes across this trajectory may illuminate mechanisms underlying symptom emergence and identify targets for early intervention. The triple network model comprising salience, default mode, and executive control networks has proven valuable for characterizing cortical dysfunction in psychosis [1]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that cerebellum and thalamus show parallel functional organization and may contribute critically to network level pathology [2-4]. Thus, we examined cerebellar-thalamic and cerebellar-cortical connectivity across multiple states of psychosis risk to test whether network disruptions differsystematically from genetic vulnerability through clinical high risk (CHR) states to first episode psychosis (FEP).

Methods

Resting state functional MRI was acquired from 241 individuals including 100 healthy controls (HCs), 41 unaffected relatives (URs) with multiple affected family members, 63 CHR individuals meeting prodromal criteria, and 37 FEP patients. Data were collected using a 3 Tesla scanner and preprocessed using standard pipelines including motion correction, normalization to standard space, and bandpass filtering. We applied a winner-take-all parcellation method to identify cerebellar and thalamic network subdivisions. This approach assigned each thalamic and cerebellar voxel to whichever cortical network demonstrated the strongest functional coupling, effectively mapping cortical network representations throughout the cerebellum and thalamus. Between-network connectivity was computed using correlation-based methods.

Results

URs showed no detectable differences in cerebellar, thalamic, or cortical network connectivity despite their genetic loading for schizophrenia, compared to the HCs. CHRindividuals showed a pattern of localized network strengthening, in which connectivity differed within each cerebellar, thalamic, and cortical networks. FEP patients exhibited a fundamentally different pattern characterized by large scale dysconnectivity compared to the HCs. Cerebellar connectivity with cortical networks was substantially increased across all three network systems. However, this strengthening was accompanied by marked reductions in cerebellar-thalamic connectivity. Direct comparison between CHR and FEP groups confirmed these differences, with FEP showing greater cerebello-cortical hyperconnectivity but lower cerebellar-thalamic hypoconnectivity

Conclusion

These findings deepen our understanding of how cerebellar and thalamic systems contribute to large-scale network organization across the psychosis spectrum. By distinguishing alterations that appear state-related from those that may reflect underlying vulnerability, this work underscores the value of examining psychosis through a whole-brain, circuit-level perspective rather than focusing on isolated regions. Clarifying the role of cerebellar–thalamo–cortical interactions may support efforts to refine neurobiological models of psychosis and guide the development of early identification and intervention strategies grounded in systems-level dysfunction.
