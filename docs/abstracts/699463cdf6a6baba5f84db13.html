Introduction

Neuropsychiatric disorders have risen in recent decades, as highlighted by the Global Burden of Disease Study and the WHO. In response, neuroimaging relies on international collaborations and individual patient data to enable large-scale, personalised insights. EEG has therefore emerged, owing to its speed, affordability, and high temporal resolution. EEG oscillations reflect functional brain connectivity, and frequency-specific power spectral features are associated with cognitive abilities. ^1 Characterising how these metrics change across the lifespan in healthy individuals can provide normative benchmarks and guide personalised medicine by identifying deviations from expected trajectories. Large-scale collaborations such as ENIGMA-EEG allow pooling of EEG data across countries, acquisition systems, and montages, increasing statistical power. ^2 Such initiatives require harmonisation to remove site-specific effects. ComBat—originally developed for genomics and adopted in MRI—is promising, yet its performance in EEG has not been systematically evaluated. ^3 In this project, we assess the performance of ComBat in harmonising resting-state EEG alpha and theta power derived from ~5,000 participants aged 5–82 across 5 cohorts. Harmonisation quality is evaluated through site-mean comparisons, variance reduction, PCA, kNN mixing, RF site classification, and preservation of age effects. This work represents the first step toward constructing normative EEG trajectories across the lifespan (target N ≈ 30,000) and extracting site-specific parameters that can be applied to new data. Such trajectories constitute the largest international benchmark for EEG-based neurodevelopment and ageing, supporting the future development of a website where patient data can be uploaded and compared with normative trajectories, enabling biomarker detection. The platform will enable the inclusion of portable EEG devices to increase accessibility for researchers and users.

Methods

Healthy participants from five cohorts were used (N: total number of participants/recordings, M: male, F: female); 

* Publicly available data  

1. DZPG (Deutsche Zentrum für Psychische Gesundheit; Germany, N=608, M=232 & F=376, ages: 20-70), 

2. SRM (Stimulus-Selective Response Modulation project; Norway, N=111, M=42, F=69, ages: 18-72, age distribution skewed toward younger adults), and 

3. TDBrain (Two Decades of Brain Research; Netherlands, N=104, M=58 & F=46, ages: 8-70, mainly younger participants).  

* Restricted access data 

1. LIFE (Leipzig LIFE adult study; Germany, N=3484, M=1766 & F=1718, ages: 42-82, skewed toward older adults), 

2. NTR (Netherlands Twin Registry, N=1586 recordings, M=769 & F=817, ages: 8-18).  

Note: N refers to the number of unique participants across all cohorts except NTR, where it reflects the number of recordings. Unique subject IDs are 844 for NTR, adding to a total of 5,151 subjects.

The datasets were pre-processed using an automated in-house EEG workflow (EEGWorkflowApp) available as a MATLAB app or as a command-line executable. The app was developed using EEGLAB and executed in batch mode with identical settings across cohorts. After import, flatline channels (standard deviation < 1 µV) were removed, followed by channels exhibiting excessive noise (Z-score > 5). Second, channel locations were matched to each cohort-specific montage to ensure consistent topographic mapping across datasets, and all recordings were resampled to 250 Hz to standardise sampling resolution and reduce computational load. All data were re-referenced to the common average reference, and band-pass filtered between 1 and 45 Hz to remove slow drifts and high-frequency muscle noise. Lastly, eye and muscle artefacts were removed using ICA with IClabel classification. Identified components were removed using a source-subtraction procedure, maintaining the original data dimensionality.

After obtaining clean EEG wave traces for all cohorts, alpha (8–13 Hz) and theta (4–8 Hz) power were extracted using a second MATLAB workflow. Continuous EEG was divided into 4-second windows with 50% overlap and a Hanning taper. Each window underwent a Fast Fourier transformation to generate the power spectral density. Power values within the alpha and theta ranges were summed per channel and converted to decibels. This resulted in one feature matrix per cohort and frequency band containing subject identifiers and channel-wise power estimates.

To prepare the data for ComBat harmonisation, feature matrices were aligned across cohorts based on overlapping channels. Demographic covariates (age, sex, and site/apparatus) were merged by subject ID, yielding a unified dataset containing one row per participant with all covariates and features. ComBat harmonisation was performed using the neuroCombat package, which corrects for both mean (location) and variance (scale) batch effects under a linear model for covariates. ^3 Standard parametric settings were applied, and several diagnostics were run to evaluate harmonisation quality: (i) comparison of feature means before and after harmonisation, (ii) PCA using all channel-level features to inspect site clustering, (iii) k-nearest neighbour (kNN) mixing metrics, and (iv) a random forest classifier predicting site of origin to quantify residual site-identifiability. Lastly, normative age trajectories for alpha and theta power were visualised before and after ComBat. For each channel, cohort-specific cubic polynomial curves (chosen for interpretability) were fitted by regressing power on age within each cohort, and plotted separately for the pre-ComBat and post-ComBat datasets, to assess whether age-related trends aligned more closely across cohorts following harmonisation.

Results

Across all five cohorts and for both power bands, substantial site-related variability was observed before harmonisation. Mean power values before ComBat ranged from 3.06 to 17.93 dB for alpha and from -1.73 to 9.20 dB for theta, and dropped to narrower ranges after ComBat with 7.20–11.72 dB for alpha, and 1.55–7.84 dB for theta. The decreases in both mean and variability, together with the drop in site-explained variance (median R²) from 0.35 to 0.13 for alpha and from 0.40 to 0.29 for theta, indicate effective removal of location and scale effects and a marked reduction in scanner- and site-driven variability.

Multivariate assessments further supported improved cross-site alignment. PCA revealed strong cohort stratification before harmonisation for both bands, with clear site-specific clustering in the first two components. Post-ComBat, site-distinctive clusters largely disappeared, with observations mixing around a shared centroid (for each band). Pairwise Mahalanobis distances between sites decreased from 1.82 to 0.61 for alpha and from 1.91 to 0.73 for theta, and kNN increased from 0.28 to 0.39 for alpha and from 0.27 to 0.32 for theta, confirming improved inter-cohort integration. RF classification performance dropped from a balanced accuracy of 0.45 (SD = 0.01) to 0.31 (SD = 0.01) for alpha and from 0.49 (SD=0.02) to 0.39 (SD=0.01). Given that chance-level accuracy for five cohorts is approximately 0.20, these findings show substantial—but not complete—removal of site information.

Importantly, biological relevance was preserved or enhanced, with the median absolute correlation between alpha power and age remaining stable (|r|=0.40 pre, 0.39 post) and increasing for theta (|r|=0.43 pre, 0.58 post). Similarly, age-related regression coefficients were highly consistent (r = 0.99 for alpha and 0.95 for theta).

Polynomial (cubic) age-trajectory modelling showed that cohort-specific lifespan curves, which were highly divergent at baseline, became tightly aligned after harmonisation for both power bands. The fitted trajectories overlapped almost entirely across childhood, adulthood, and ageing, indicating effective attenuation of site-driven distortions while maintaining underlying age-related structure. 

For alpha, pre-ComBat curves differed substantially in both intercept and shape: SRM showed a decrease in alpha after the age of 40, DZPG showed an overall decrease across adulthood, TDBrain and LIFE displayed a downward-sloping pattern with reduced power in mid-life, and NTR exhibited lower values in adolescence compared to early childhood. These cohort-specific curves diverged most strongly in mid-to-late adulthood, where site effects produced inconsistent declines or plateaus across cohorts. After ComBat, all cohorts converged onto a shared trajectory that captured the expected gradual decline in alpha across adulthood, with highly overlapping curves and no residual site-specific distortions.

For theta, pre-ComBat curves displayed considerable heterogeneity across cohorts. SRM and DZPG showed relatively flat or slightly declining adulthood trajectories. TDBrain exhibited a marked decrease from ages 8 to 40, followed by a slight increase in mid-adulthood. LIFE cohort values increased between 40 and 50 years, then decreased from 50 to 70, and rose again from 70 to 82. NTR showed sharply elevated theta in childhood, followed by a steep age-related drop into adolescence. After ComBat, cohort-specific theta trends were preserved but tightly aligned across sites, with early-life declines, mid-adulthood fluctuations, and late-life increases maintained.

Overall, ComBat substantially reduced site-driven artefacts across both alpha and theta power while preserving and even sharpening biologically meaningful age associations. Polynomial modelling confirmed that harmonisation aligned the underlying lifespan curves for both alpha and theta, enabling consistent interpretation of developmental and ageing effects across cohorts.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that ComBat harmonisation can effectively reduce site-related variability in multi-site EEG datasets while preserving biologically meaningful age-related trends. Across five cohorts spanning ages 5–82 years, alpha and theta power exhibited substantial baseline differences between sites, with site-specific means and variance diverging. Post-ComBat harmonisation, both univariate and multivariate assessments showed strong evidence for improved cross-cohort alignment. Mean power ranges narrowed considerably, site-explained variance decreased, PCA clusters dissipated, Mahalanobis distances between cohorts declined, and kNN and random forest metrics indicated reduced residual site-identifiability. Together, these findings highlight ComBat as a way to mitigate acquisition- and cohort-specific artefacts while retaining the fundamental signal structure necessary for lifespan analyses.

Crucially, the biological relevance of EEG power trajectories was maintained and, in some instances, enhanced with age correlations for theta power increasing post-harmonisation. For alpha, the observed gradual decline aligns with previous literature showing that resting-state alpha power tends to decrease with advancing age. ^4 For theta, age-related patterns were more complex: early-life reductions were followed by subtle mid-life fluctuations and later-life increases in some cohorts, reflecting known developmental and ageing dynamics where theta may decrease with experience and reduced cognitive effort but increase again in older age. ^5 Post-ComBat, these patterns were harmonised into interpretable trajectories, retaining biologically plausible cohort-specific variations while eliminating site-driven distortions.

Despite these encouraging findings, limitations remain. The dataset is disproportionately weighted toward older adults due to the LIFE cohort (ages 42–82), whereas younger participants are underrepresented. This imbalance may influence the fit of age trajectories, particularly in mid-childhood and early adulthood. The project is in an early phase (~4 months), and several extensions are planned to address this limitation. The immediate next steps are to include an additional cohort providing a broader representation of younger individuals, or to downsample LIFE to achieve a more even distribution across age groups, ensuring harmonised age trajectories are representative of the full lifespan. These steps will also enable a more robust evaluation of ComBat’s performance across a balanced age range. Beyond age distribution, further extensions are planned to refine the harmonisation workflow. These include exploring non-parametric ComBat approaches, such as ComBat-GAM, testing harmonisation of additional EEG features (e.g., beta and gamma power, coherence), and evaluating longitudinal stability of harmonised measures. The newly harmonised datasets will form the basis for constructing normative lifespan trajectories and a publicly accessible EEG benchmarking platform. This platform could enable researchers to harmonise their EEG recordings and receive comparisons with age-appropriate normative ranges, supporting early detection of deviations linked to neurodevelopmental or neuropsychiatric disorders.

In conclusion, ComBat harmonisation provides a robust framework for reducing site-specific variance in multi-site EEG datasets while preserving meaningful age-related changes. Initial results indicate that harmonisation can reconcile divergent cohort-specific trajectories into biologically interpretable lifespan curves. By extending the dataset to include younger participants and further optimising the workflow, this approach has the potential to establish the largest international EEG benchmark to date, enabling more reliable comparisons across studies and laying the groundwork for translational applications in neuroscience and personalised medicine.
