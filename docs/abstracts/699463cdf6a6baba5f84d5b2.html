Introduction

Previous research has linked incentive-related neural activity to momentary self-reported affect [1], but the separability by valence, temporal specificity, and generalizability of these associations remains unclear. Here, we examined whether spatially separable neural circuits support the inference of affect on a second-to-second basis, how this activity evolves over time, and whether neural signatures generalize across different affective probes? 

Methods

Across two studies, participants completed a Monetary Incentive Delay (MID) task with affective probes (Figure 1). After either the anticipatory fixation or the outcome presentation phase of the trial, they rated their momentary experienced valence and arousal. Study 1 (N = 30) used separate valence and arousal ratings, while Study 2 (N = 31) used a two-dimensional affect grid [2]. Valence and arousal ratings were then mean-deviated and transformed into positive arousal (PA = valence + arousal) and negative arousal (NA = valence - arousal) ratings [3]. 

Data were acquired on a 3T GE scanner using a single-band, whole-brain EPI sequence optimized for subcortical sensitivity (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 25 ms, flip angle 70°, 2.9 mm isotropic). After standard preprocessing, we estimated parametric GLMs in which trial-wise PA and NA modulated neural activity 2 sec prior to rating onset (i.e., after anticipation or outcome). We additionally estimated three images during the same volume acquisition for each participant corresponding to low, medium and high levels of ratings, and performed multivariate decoding using a SpaceNet classifier with Graph-Net and TV-L1 priors [4]. Finally, we quantified temporal specificity by shifting the parametric modulator across multiple lags relative to the rating period. 

Results

Despite some differences in participants’ rating behavior across the two self-report schemes, parametric GLMs confirmed spatially dissociable neural patterns of PA and NA across studies (Study 1: Spearman’s rho = 0.05; Study 2: rho = -0.12). As predicted, PA was associated with activity in the Nucleus Accumbens (NAcc), and NA with activity in the Anterior Insula (AIns) (Figure 2A, 2B). In contrast, the neural correlates of self-reported arousal and valence were spatially inseparable (Study 1: Spearman’s rho = 0.27; Study 2: rho = 0.36). GraphNet decoding yielded above-chance classification of PA levels (cross-validated accuracy = 0.589 ± 0.158) and NA (0.422 ± 0.204). The most informative voxels recapitulated GLM-identified regions.

Additionally, neural activity associated with PA and NA showed different temporal profiles. While NAcc activity 2s before the rating onset best predicted PA, AIns activity at the time of rating onset best predicted NA (Figure 2C). ROI-based time-course analyses confirmed these dissociable temporal associations.

Conclusion

Together, these findings confirm that PA and NA are supported by spatially and temporally distinct neural correlates, and demonstrate the added value of transforming valence and arousal ratings into composite dimensions of positive and negative arousal when modeling affective experience in the brain. These findings have implications for improving the prediction of choice and self-reported affective experience from dynamic brain activity.
