Introduction

White matter pathways support the integration of information from distributed brain regions essential for motor control, and their microstructural properties can adapt in response to experience and training. Motor learning engages these pathways through repeated activation, leading to plastic changes that enhance communication efficiency within motor networks. To investigate such training-induced adaptations, we implemented a long-term motor learning intervention to induce neuroplastic changes.

Methods

Sixty-two healthy adult volunteers (25 male, 37 female; age mean±SD = 25.9±4.0 years; all right-handed) practiced either a complex or simple version of the bimanual tracking task (BTT) for four weeks. Weekly standardized progress tests were used to calculate the learning slope, defined as the increase in % performance per minute spent on the BTT. The complex training group completed a performance-based level system with increasing task difficulty and semi-random task exposure to varying inter-hand frequencies, directions and speeds. The simple training group practiced only the easiest subtask, in which both hands move at the same speed.

T1-weighted (T1w) and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) was collected using a 3 T Philips Achieva dStream at baseline (PRE), 2 (MID) and 4 weeks (POST) of motor training. T1w images were acquired using a three-dimensional turbo field echo (3DTFE) sequence: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 9.7/4.6 ms, flip angle = 8°, field of view = 256 × 242 × 182 mm3, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, and acquisition time ~ 6 min. High angular resolution diffusion imaging (HARDI) was acquired using a spin-echo EPI sequence with the following parameters: DWI volumes with b-values = 700 s/mm2 (16 gradient directions), 1200 s/mm2 (30 gradient directions), and 2800 s/mm2 (50 gradient directions); 6 interspersed volumes without diffusion weighting (b = 0 s/mm2); TR/TE: 5000/74 ms; multiband factor = 2; sensitivity encoding (SENSE) = 2; flip angle = 90°; phase-encoding direction = posterior to anterior (PA); field of view = 240 × 240 × 140 mm3; voxel size = 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm3; matrix size = 96 × 96; 56 transverse slices. We also acquired five volumes with b = 0 s/mm2, and two of each, b = 700, 1200 and 2800 s/mm2 with reversed phase encoding (AP) for the purpose of susceptibility-induced distortion correction. The total acquisition time for DWI was ~  10 min.

Using the Fun-with-Tracts toolbox1 including MRTrix3 (v. 3.0.4)-based probabilistic multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) tractography, motor-related pyramidal and callosal (CC) fiber bundles were defined in the sample’s template space, modelling connections involving the primary (M1), supplementary (SMA), and premotor cortices (PMC) have been modelled, i.e., the CC segments connecting bilateral M1 (CC_M1), bilateral PMC and SMA (CC_PMC/SMA), contralateral SMA–M1 (CC_L2R_SMA-M1, CC_R2L_SMA-M1), as well as the corticospinal tract (L-/R-CST), and the pyramidal tracts emerging from PMC (L-/R-PyT_PMC) and SMA (L-/R-PyT_SMA). Using a fixel mask for each fiber bundle, the mean log-transformed fiber cross-section (logFC), fiber density (FD), and a combination (FDC) were extracted for each participant and time point.

Longitudinal changes were assessed via linear mixed models (DWI_outcome ~ GROUP + TIMING + GROUP×TIMING + (1|SUBJECT)), with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc testing using paired t-tests. Associations between DWI-derived outcomes and motor learning progress (for simple and complex subtasks apart) were assessed using multiple linear models (BTT_slope ~ DWI_outcome + GROUP + DWI_outcome×GROUP), with post hoc simple linear modelling per GROUP where applicable.

Results

There were several longitudinal changes in FD and FDC (all, p < 0.045 for F-test of GROUP×TIMING), but none in logFC. Post hoc testing revealed that overall, only for the complex but not the simple training group, FD and FDC transiently changed in several motor-related subparts of the CC and in the right CST with an overall increase from PRE to MID and a subsequent decrease from MID to POST, but no significant changes from PRE to POST or for the simple training group. More specifically, for the complex training group, FD in CC_PMC/SMA, and both FD and FDC in CC_L2R_SMA-M1 increased from PRE to MID and subsequently decreased again from MID to POST (all, p[adjusted] < 0.034). Furthermore, FD in CC_R2L_SMA-M1 and FDC in CC_ PMC/SMA increased from PRE to MID (all, p[adjusted] < 0.039), and FDC in CC_M1 and R-CST decreased from MID to POST (all, p[adjusted] < 0.038).

Regarding associations between the learning slopes and DWI outcomes, baseline DWI outcomes in CC_PMC/SMA, and PRE-MID changes in the DWI outcomes of CC_R2L_SMA-M1 were linked to training progress (all model F-tests, p < 0.047). More specifically, baseline logFC (p = 0.040) and FDC (p = 0.043) values in CC_PMC/SMA were negatively associated with progress on the simplest BTT subtask, independent from group assignment. This indicates that individuals with lower fiber cross-section (and a potentially linked lower FDC) showed a steeper learning slope, which might be connected to either an initially worse performance, and/or an overall steeper learning progress, on the simplest subtask. Post hoc modelling of the DWI_outcome×GROUP interactions (all, p < 0.040), i.e., for the association between the learning slope on the complex subtasks and the baseline FD value in CC_PMC/SMA, as well as the change in FD and FDC in CC_L2R_SMA-M1 from PRE to MID did not reveal any further significant associations (all, p > 0.063 for effect of DWI_outcome when modelling per group).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that short-term motor training can induce transient microstructural changes in specific callosal and corticospinal pathways. These changes were detectable only in participants who practiced the complex version of the bimanual task, suggesting that training demands and variability in task exposure modulate white-matter plasticity. The observed increase from baseline to the mid-training interval, followed by a return toward baseline after four weeks, indicates a transient rather than cumulative pattern of structural adaptation. Baseline properties of callosal SMA/PMC connections were associated with individual learning progress. This highlights the relevance of pre-existing structural features for subsequent skill acquisition. These findings support the idea that the structural architecture of the motor system forms a neural substrate that shapes training responsiveness and may help to explain why individuals differ in their learning trajectories.
