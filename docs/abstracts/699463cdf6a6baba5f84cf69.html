Introduction

Suicide is a leading cause of global death, with approximately 10 per 100,000 individuals dying by suicide each year. Although non-suicidal self-directed violence (NSSDV) is an established risk factor for suicide attempt (SA), the neural distinctions between these behaviors remain insufficiently understood. Variability in blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals has been applied to differentiate individuals with various physiological states and disorders.^1,2 Previous studies suggest that the neural complexity of BOLD signals may serve as potential biomarker for distinguishing psychological symptoms.^2 In this study, we aimed to stratify self-directed violence (SDV) using BOLD signal complexity quantified by multiscale entropy (MSE).

Methods

A total of 1,070 participants with a history of SDV were classified into SA (n = 579) and NSSDV (n = 491) groups based on self-reported questionnaires in the UK Biobank. Both cross-sectional data and resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data were obtained from the UK Biobank. Imaging data were preprocessed with DPABI toolbox and parcellated into 90 regions based on the Anatomical Automatic Labeling (AAL) atlas. Sample entropy analysis with a pattern length of m = 2 and a similarity factor of r = 0.15 was used to quantify BOLD signal complexity. Two-sample t-tests were conducted to identify brain regions showing significant between-group differences. MSE analysis up to scale 4 was applied to examine the pathological differences in signal complexity, indicating trends toward regularity or toward uncorrelated randomness.

Results

Individuals were on average age of 51.82 (S.D. = 7.08) years old, with 72.71% of them being female. Compared with the NSSDV group, SA subjects had significantly decreased sample entropy in bilateral angular gyrus, postcentral gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, as well as in the left caudate, precentral gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, temporal pole, and in the right orbital part of middle frontal gyrus, precuneus. Although the MSE analysis did not reveal any brain regions with significant between-group differences, the SA group demonstrated a consistent trend of decreased complexity toward regularity in the aforementioned brain regions.

Conclusion

This research identified distinct patterns of BOLD signal dynamics between SA and NSSDV groups, implicating that neural correlations may serve as potential biomarker for self-harmed behavior. The findings support the notion that a loss of brain signal complexity could help stratify individuals along the SDV spectrum. Future research integrating functional connectivity analyses with clinical status, such as frequency and recency, may offer deeper insights into the neural underpinnings of SDV-related complexity changes.
