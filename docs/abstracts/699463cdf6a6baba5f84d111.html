Introduction

The human cervical spinal cord transmits motor signals from the brain to the muscles, for moving the head, neck, shoulders, upper back and upper limbs. The muscles are innervated by motoneurons organized into specific groups within lamina 9 of the ventral grey matter of eight cervical spine segments (C1-C8). A previous cytoarchitectonic mapping has identified 11 laterally and medially located motor neuron groups in the human cervical spine1. For example, the phrenic motor neuron group (Ph9) is present from C4 to C6 and regulates diaphragm movement. In addition to intersegmental differences, the organization of motor neuron groups can vary within a single segment2. Such intrasegmental differences have not been described in more recent spinal cord maps1. To date, a detailed high-resolution cytoarchitectonic human spinal cord atlas is still lacking.

Analyzing the deep neuroanatomical organization of spinal motor neuron groups can help to identify disorder-related alterations, enabling more precise diagnosis and targeted therapeutic interventions. In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), distinct functional impairments, such as diaphragm weakness, are associated with differential atrophic foci in cervical segments, including degeneration of Ph9 motoneurons. In vivo MRI has been used to detect changes in the cervical spine cross-sectional area in patients with ALS3. However, the lower resolution of in vivo MRI does not enable the assignment of neurodegeneration to individual cervical segments or to specific motor neuron groups.

In this study, we cytoarchitectonically mapped motor neuron groups in the cervical human spinal cord using high-resolution images of cell body-stained sections. Our detailed inter- and intrasegmental topographic characterization facilitates the identification of cervical motor neuron groups in in vivo neuroimaging and comparison of disorder-related alterations such as motoneuron degeneration within specific motor neuron groups.

Methods

A cervical human spinal cord (female; age 86 years; postmortem delay 10h) with no history of neurological disorders was obtained through the body donor program of the Department of Anatomy I at the University of Düsseldorf, Germany. The spinal cord was prepared in accordance with the guidelines of the local ethics committee (study number 2023-2632). The histological processing has been described previously4. The cervical spine was fixed in 4% formalin, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Serial axial sections (20 µm thickness) were mounted on glass slides and stained for cell bodies using a modified silver staining method. In total, 2,670 cervical spinal cord sections were processed. The stained sections were digitized using a brightfield microscope (TissueScope LE120, Huron Digital Pathology) resulting in images with an isotropic resolution of 1 µm/pixel. Images were stored as grey-level images with a fixed pixel size. Motor neuron groups in lamina 9 of the cervical grey matter were mapped in every fifth section. The borders of the motor neuron groups were delineated in the high-resolution histological images using the in-house developed ‘Online Section Tracer Tool’. To obtain quantitative values such as cell size and cell density, we used the Contour Proposal Network for automatic cell segmentation5. Figure 1 shows an exemplary histological spinal cord section illustrating the principle of cell segmentation. These segmented images can be used to calculate quantitative values, including cell size and regional cell densities.

Results

Cytoarchitectonic analysis revealed the delineation of 14 motor neuron groups within lamina 9 of the ventral horn of the human cervical spinal cord segments C1-C7. The spinal cord, particularly the cervical segments, can be divided based on the butterfly-shaped grey matter structure and the distinct organization of motor neuron groups. Interestingly, in addition to intersegmental differences, our analyses identified distinct intrasegmental organizations of motor neuron groups within segments C4, C5 and C6, which we segregated into more cranial (upper) and more caudal (lower) parts.

The medially located axial muscle motor neuron group (Ax9) was present throughout all cervical segments. From C1 to C3, the medial infrahyoid muscle (IH9) and the lateral trapezius and sternomastoid (TzSM9) motor neuron groups were observed. The TzSM9 group decreases in size from C1 to C3. Segment C4 differs from C3 by a flattened shape of the grey matter in C3 and by the presence of medial Ax9, central Ph9, and the deltoid (De9) and rhomboid (Rh9) motor neuron groups in upper C4. Within C4, intrasegmental differences were observed, such as a more flattened ventral grey matter in the upper part than in the lower part, which contained the retrodorsolaterally located biceps motor neuron group (Bi9). Segment C5 was characterized by the presence of the ventrolateral pectoral muscle (Pec9) and centrally located supraspinatus and infraspinatus (SI9) motor neuron groups. In the upper part of C5, the angles of the ventral grey matter are generally sharper, and Pec9 is more prominent than in the lower part of C5, where Bi9 is larger. Segment C6 showed rounder angles of the ventral grey matter and lacked Ph9. The lower part of C6 showed no SI9 or Rh9 groups, but two retrodorsolateral motor neuron groups were identified: the forearm extensor (FEx9) and forearm flexor (FFl9). In C7, the FEx9 and FFl9 groups are more prominent and the Tr9 group is present dorsolaterally. Additionally, the centrally located latissimus dorsi (LD9) and ventrolateral serratus anterior (Sr9) motor neuron groups were observed.

Conclusion

Our cytoarchitectonic analyses provide detailed mapping of 14 lamina 9 motor neuron groups of the human cervical spinal cord and we described their topography and specific organization. In addition to intersegmental differences, we also found intrasegmental changes within the C4, C5 and C6 segments. The precise mapping of motor neuron groups - for example, the presence of Bi9 from the lower C4 to the upper C5 - improves the understanding of both the neuroanatomical and functional organization of the human cervical spinal cord.  

We provide the densest (i.e., every fifth section) mapping of the motor neuron groups in the human cervical spinal cord currently available. This high-resolution mapping is fundamental for reconstructing motor neuron groups into longitudinal columns and, further, for developing a three-dimensional human spinal cord atlas. Such an atlas may serve as a reference for the precise localization of motor neuron groups in in vivo neuroimaging studies and for comparisons with disorder-related alterations. In the future, our cytoarchitectonic mapping will be expanded using computational models, such as the Contour Proposal Network5, enabling the calculation of quantitative morphological values (e.g., cell size and regional cell density) for statistical analyses of spinal cord cytoarchitecture.
