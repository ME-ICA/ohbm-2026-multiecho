Introduction

Chronic pain has reached epidemic proportions in children. Pain across multiple body sites with functional impairment, absent peripheral injury, is indicative of central nervous system (CNS) involvement and central sensitization. Children with chronic pain conditions commonly present with comorbid symptoms: anxiety and stress-related disorders, sleep disturbances, and cognitive fatigue. Shared neurobiological mechanisms involving stress-response sensitivity, sensory over-responsivity, brain network reorganization, and central sensitization warrant investigation to identify predictive biomarkers.1

We hypothesized that the genetic architecture of chronic widespread pain would have overlap and enrichment across pathways highly expressed in the CNS with commonly observed traits and comorbidities, including anxiety, neuroticism, intelligence, insomnia, myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME/CFS), and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). Following construction of a polygenic risk score (PRS) of chronic overlapping pain conditions (COPC), we hypothesized that brain imaging measures would serve as endophenotypes linking genetic risk to  widespread pain, and that pubertal maturation would modulate these genetic–brain–pain relationships - as adolescence represents a period of hormonally driven neuroplasticity that could unmask latent vulnerability. We also hypothesized  sex-specific effects reflecting distinct gonadal and adrenal hormone influences on pain-related circuits. To test these hypotheses, we integrated longitudinal multimodal neuroimaging, genetic, pubertal development, and clinical pain data from the ABCD Study, creating a pain-spreading score that quantifies spatial expansion of pain over 2 years while controlling for baseline distribution.

Methods

To establish shared genetic liability for chronic widespread pain and associated traits, we obtained genome-wide summary statistics for COPC, anxiety, ADHD, depression, neuroticism, intelligence, insomnia, ME/CFS, and IBS. Multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) jointly analyzed these summary statistics, accounting for sample overlap via bivariate LD Score regression to leverage genetic correlations and boost power. Genes mapped to significant loci via FUMA/MAGMA underwent multi-gene-list meta-analysis in Metascape to identify commonly- and selectively-enriched pathways. A functionally-informed PRS was calculated using sBayesRC with COPC MTAG statistics in European-ancestry ABCD participants.2

The ABCD Pain Questionnaire assessed pain intensity, duration, impairment, and location across 74 body sites. We consolidated the body map into nine anatomical regions to avoid misclassifying localized pain as multisite.3 Participants were categorized by pain in 0-4+ regions, using a previously validated approach. A Pain Spreading Score (PSS) quantified longitudinal changes over 2 years in pain sites (-4 to +4), adjusted for baseline sites, age, and follow-up duration, serving as the clinical endpoint for pain chronification.

Multimodal brain imaging endophenotypes included structural metrics (FreeSurfer/Destrieux atlas and subcortical regions), resting-state fMRI connectivity (RSFC) of childhood-specific networks (Gordon atlas and subcortical regions), and diffusion tensor imaging (AtlasTrack fiber tracts). Brain imaging phenotypes were harmonized across scanning sites using LongCombat - a longitudinal mixed-effects approach that removes site-related variance while preserving biological variation associated with age and income (fixed effects) and family structure (random effect). Pubertal status was assessed using the youth-reported Puberty Development Scale (PDS). Following established protocols,4 sex-specific adrenal (body hair growth, skin changes) and gonadal scores (males: growth spurt, voice deepening, facial hair; females: growth spurt, breast development, menarche) were computed.

We applied sex-specific linear mixed-effects models with FDR correction to examine multiple pathways linking genetic risk, brain development, and pubertal maturation to pain vulnerability in European-ancestry participants with complete data (N males = 2,255, N females = 1,842). All models controlled for age, household income, and a random effect for family ID. First, we established the total effect of genetic risk on pain outcomes by testing associations between COPC PRS and PSS (path c). Second, we identified neural endophenotypes by testing whether COPC PRS predicted baseline brain imaging features and longitudinal changes in connectivity, structure, and white matter integrity over two years (path a). Third, we characterized puberty-related mechanisms by examining associations between gonadal and adrenal pubertal maturation and PSS. Finally, we conducted moderated mediation analyses (PROCESS Model 58)5 to test whether gonadal puberty moderated the indirect pathway from genetic risk to pain spreading via brain endophenotypes (paths a and b), determining whether brain-mediated genetic vulnerability varied across pubertal development stages.

Results

Significant global genetic correlations were observed between COPC and all other traits (p < 0.001). MAGMA tissue expression analyses showed in the brain and pituitary gland across all MTAG traits (all q < 0.05). Gene set enrichment analyses revealed significant enrichment across multiple biological processes (all q < 0.05), with the strongest effects in "postsynapse," "cell morphogenesis," and "GABA-ergic synapse." Cell type enrichment was highest in midbrain neuroblasts that are GABAergic precursors (all q < 0.05). PRS predicted greater PSS in girls (p = 0.0002) but not boys (p = 0.54). Gonadal (but not adrenal) PDS scores also predicted PSS in girls (p = 0.03). At baseline in girls, higher PRS predicted lower thalamus, caudate, and ventral diencephalon (VDC) connectivity to sensorimotor, auditory, and visual networks, but higher connectivity to frontoparietal and default-mode networks. PRS also predicted lower connectivity between the amygdala and default mode network, but higher to the cingulo-opercular network (all q < 0.05).

Longitudinally over two years, higher PRS predicted greater volume decreases in the hippocampus and thalamus (all q < 0.05). PRS also predicted significant changes in resting-state functional connectivity over time: increased connectivity between subcortical structures (thalamus, caudate) and higher-order networks (default-mode, salience, cingulo-parietal), alongside decreased connectivity to sensorimotor and auditory networks (all q < 0.05).

Gonadal PDS scores moderated the degree to which significant associations between PRS and RSFC mediated the PSS. Higher PRS predicted lower amygdala–default mode connectivity; this protected against pain spreading only at high gonadal PDS scores (conditional indirect effect = 0.015, 95% CI [0.001, 0.034]). Lower auditory–caudate connectivity (conditional indirect effect = −0.015, 95% CI [−0.036, −0.001]) and greater VDC–frontoparietal connectivity (conditional indirect effect = −0.015, 95% CI [−0.035, −0.001]) increased vulnerability to pain spreading at high gonadal PDS scores. Direct genetic effects remained significant across all models (p < 0.001), indicating mediation through puberty-moderated brain mechanisms.

Conclusion

This is the first study to demonstrate that genetic vulnerability to widespread pain in children is expressed through sex-specific, puberty-gated neural mechanisms. The genetic liability of COPC and comorbidities is highly enriched in the brain and pituitary prenatally, and converges on synaptic plasticity and GABAergic precursors in the midbrain. This implicates descending inhibitory circuitry in early development. This genetic risk is mediated by subcortical-cortical connectivity, but only predicts pain spreading during gonadal maturation in girls. 

The absence of the PRS predictive power of PSS in boys underscores fundamental sex differences in pain neurobiology. This aligns with epidemiological evidence that females have greater prevalence, and puberty being a setting off point and pain persisting throughout the lifespan. The specificity of gonadal development suggests gonadal hormones (primarily estrogen) interact with genetic risk to shape pain-relevant circuitry during adolescent brain development. 

PRS-associated connectivity alterations revealed a coherent neurobiological signature characterized by reduced “bottom-up” sensory integration - reflected by lower subcortical connectivity with sensorimotor, auditory, and visual networks - alongside enhanced “top-down” modulatory engagement, including increased connectivity with default mode and frontoparietal networks. This pattern may reflect altered sensory gating that contributes to sensory over-responsivity, accompanied by compensatory recruitment of self-referential and attentional control systems commonly observed in chronic pain populations. Across both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, the thalamus emerged as a central hub, consistent with its role as the primary relay for ascending nociceptive signals and a key regulator of cortical sensory gating.

The moderated mediation findings give insight as to how genetic risk becomes developmentally expressed. PRS-associated brain signatures predicted PSS only in girls with advanced gonadal development at baseline - suggesting that earlier or faster pubertal maturation unmasks genetic vulnerability. Reduced amygdala-DMN connectivity was protective, potentially reflecting adaptive disengagement of threat-processing from self-referential rumination. Conversely, enhanced VDC-frontoparietal connectivity increased vulnerability. The VDC contains the hypothalamus - the central regulator of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis. Increased connectivity to frontoparietal executive networks in early-developing girls may reflect premature integration of neuroendocrine signaling with top-down attentional systems before regulatory circuits have fully matured.

These findings identify early pubertal maturation as a sensitive period when genetic vulnerability to chronic pain becomes expressed through brain network reorganization, providing integrated biomarkers for precision prevention in genetically susceptible, early-developing girls.
