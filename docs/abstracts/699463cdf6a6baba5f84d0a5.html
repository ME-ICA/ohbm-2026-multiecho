Introduction

Traditional 1 mm isotropic MPRAGE scans take 4-8 minutes to acquire. Not only is this time costly, and an increased burden on subjects, but these acquisitions can be highly susceptible to motion artifacts. Alternatively, rapid scanning techniques have been shown to yield comparable morphometric results1 with additional benefits of: (i) reduced patient burden, (ii) less susceptibility to motion, and (iii) repeat scanning within the same time it takes to acquire one conventional scan. We have recently explored the use of incoherent under-sampling with compressed sensing (CS) reconstruction2 in standard MPRAGE acquisitions3,4, showing the ability to acquire MPRAGE scans in as little as 75 seconds with comparable morphometric results to those obtained from longer scans1,4, and to reduce the uncertainty in morphometric estimates to below common rates of change in neurodegeneration by averaging estimates from multiple scans3. However, to avoid over-smoothing of the resultant images with excessive regularization in the CS reconstruction, the SNR is noticeable poorer than in conventional scans, especially in the central brain region. Here, we explore for the first time, results from a new Deep-Learning (DL) reconstruction method using the same incoherently under-sampled data acquisition approach; with the goal to improve image SNR while not detracting from brain morphometry.

Methods

Participants were six healthy young adults (4 female, average age 25.7 years, range 22 - 29 years) and with no MRI contraindications. Written informed consent was obtained as reviewed by the Harvard University Internal Review Board. Participants were compensated for their time. Subjects completed two scan sessions within a two-week period.

T1w images were acquired using a 3T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma-fit, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany), and the vendor’s standard 32-channel head coil. All scan protocols were based on the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) protocols, including FOV = 256x240 mm, 208 slices, 1 mm isotropic spatial resolution, BW 240 Hz/Px, TE = 2.98ms, TI/TR = 900/2300ms. The conventional protocol used GRAPPA = 2 acceleration for a scan time of 5:12 min – a single scan was acquired in each session. Four rapid MPRAGE scans were acquired in each session, using a research application sequence that employs incoherent under-sampling in k-space based on a cartesian phyllotaxis pattern with jittered trajectories for conventional CS reconstruction. The scans used 6-fold under-sampling, with a turbo-factor (i.e., samples per TR) of 207, for a scan time of 1:09 min. The images were reconstructed on the scanner console using a CS method (nonlinear iterative optimization algorithm using a wavelet transform as sparse representation) with a default regularization factor 0.0003. Each scan was then retrospectively reconstructed using the DL method on the same k-space data. This approach employs multiple iterations of a standard SENSE reconstruction of the anatomical information with a learned network trained to remove noise and other image artifacts. In total, we obtained 4 CS, 4 DL and 1 conventional scan in each session. Example images are shown in Figure 1.

Images were processed using MRIQC for QC metrics, yielding White Matter (WM) and Gray Matter (GM) SNR, WM/GM CNR and the degree of image spatial smoothness (spatial correlation of voxels) as determined from the calculated full-width-half-maximum (FWHM) of the spatial distribution of image intensity values. Structural Similarity Indices (SSIM) were calculated using TensorFlowMRI. SSIM was calculated between pairs of CS and DL images reconstructed from the same k-space data, as well as with the conventional ADNI scan in each session. Brain morphometrics were calculated using FreeSurfer5 v7.4.1 after registering all nine images from each session to a common base space. Volumetric and regional cortical thickness, as parcellated by the Desikan-Killiany atlas, were compared across scan types. Volumetric estimates included estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV), hippocampus volume, and amygdala volume. Test-retest reliability of each scan type was calculated by comparing estimates from across scans in each session. Percent Error was calculated as the percent difference in estimates between scans.

Results

Figure 1 shows example 1.0 mm MPRAGE images acquired with the conventional (ADNI) protocol, as well as the incoherent 6-fold under-sampled sequence with both CS and DL reconstructions. Axial and coronal views of the same image are displayed. As we have noted previously, the images are generally comparable in quality, remarkable given the reduction in scan time. However, the CS images are notably noisier in the center of the brain, although sub-cortical structures remain clearly visible. By contrast, the DL images show much improved SNR, matching or even slightly exceeding the ADNI image. However, contrast of some sub-cortical structures is reduced. The SNR gain is highlighted in the plots below the images. Figure 1g) shows mean SNR from the eight rapid scans plus two ADNI scans averaged across both sessions, for each subject. While there is subject-to-subject variation, the WM SNR from the ADNI and DL images track very similarly, while the WM SNR from the CS images is much lower. There is less of a variation in the GM SNR between scans, but the CS image still has a lower GM SNR in all subjects. The box-and-whisker plot in Figure 1h) highlights the SNR deficit in WM and GM from the CS images, indicating the difference is significant in the WM. The SNR gain in the DL reconstruction was achieved without an impact on the image smoothness – the image FWHM for the DL images generally matched those from the ADNI scans; while it was lower in the CS images. SSIM values calculated between each CS image and the ADNI image acquired in each session ranged from ~ 0.75 – 0.84 (mean 0.815±0.004), while those between the DL image and the ADNI were ~ 0.84 – 0.88 (mean 0.856±0.002), again indicating a higher degree of similarity between the DL image and the conventional scan. SSIM between the CS and DL images reconstructed from the same k-space data were ~ 0.92 – 0.97 (mean 0.939±0.002).

Figure 2 shows example FreeSurfer surface tracings and summaries of morphometric results from a single subject. Figure 2 a-c) show single slices of the extracted brain from each of the three image types, again showing the SNR challenge in the central brain in the CS images. In a) surface tracing from the ADNI image (white and red) overlay those from the CS image (yellow and aqua); in b) surfaces from the DL image (green and purple) overlay those from the CS image; and in c) the surfaces from the ADNI image overlay those from the DL image. Very high agreement is observed, as noted by the fact the overlaying surfaces almost totally obscure the underlaid surfaces in each case. Figure 2 d-e) show the correlation between the volumetric measures and cortical thickness measures obtained from the ADNI image with those from the CS and DL images. Volumes of sub-cortical structures show very high agreement, although there are individual differences – the left and right thalamus is noted. Regional cortical thickness measures are also highly correlated. Figure 2 f-g) show percent differences in morphometric estimates from CS or DL images and the ADNI image, plotted against the volume or thickness from the ADNI image, in Bland-Altman style. While many volumetric differences are within 5%, some notable outliers are visible – generally the DL images produce larger differences than the CS images in this case. The Right Accumbens Area is highlighted in f). In cortical thickness, the DL images also often produce larger differences than the CS images; and we also observed a specific bias in cortical thickness whereby most differences from CS and DL images were positive compared to ADNI, except at the very thickest regions (temporal pole and entorhinal cortex) which are located in areas of high magnetic susceptibility difference.

Conclusion

This work provides a preliminary assessment of the use of DL reconstruction approaches to improve image quality in highly-accelerated scans, in this case, a 1.0 mm MPRAGE protocol acquired in under 70 seconds. In terms of visual image quality, the DL images are remarkably similar to those acquired with a conventional 5+ min MPRAGE protocol. Quantitative image quality metrics such as SNR, CNR and image smoothness indicate the DL images match very well with the conventional longer scan. This is a notable advance over CS reconstruction approaches where noise is amplified especially in the center of the field-of-view, which corresponds to the central brain regions in standard neuroimaging protocols. While the SNR of CS images can be improved with judicious use of the regularization term in the reconstruction, this does inevitably result in additional smoothing to the image4 – we sought to avoid that in this case by using a lower regularization value which did result in significantly lower SNR in the WM. The problem of reconstructing an image without overly impacting the central brain region remains a challenge – we did notice visually in a few cases that deep-brain sub-cortical structures had lower contrast or were harder to see by eye in the DL images than in the CS or conventional image.

Estimates of brain morphometry from DL images were, for the most part, widely comparable with those from the CS and ADNI images. Cortical surface tracings were mostly extremely well-matched, perfectly overlaying in many cases. Correlation of values between the ADNI and CS or DL images were also very high – indeed the correlation plots for cortical thickness were better than many seen in comparisons of other rapid-scan methods4. However, some interesting points were observed which will require further investigation. While volume estimates of sub-cortical structures such as the hippocampus and amygdala – which traditionally show large variation – were highly consistent here, volume estimates for the thalamus showed a noticeable inconsistency across multiple subjects. The thickest parts of the cortex, in the temporal lobe, also showed considerable variation between CS and DL images, although large variations in this susceptibility-prone region have been observed previously4. While generally promising for morphometry, we caution that more investigation of this novel method is required to determine whether the variations noted here continue to be observed in larger groups of subjects, especially among subjects who are not healthy young adults.

We note that the rapid imaging protocol used here was in no way optimized for the DL reconstruction process. Our imaging protocol was a simple repeat of previously optimized protocols, where DL was not a reconstruction option. Varying the de-noising strength of the DL network was not attempted; nor did we consider protocol optimizations to scan faster without further under-sampling; as may eventually prove to be an optimal use of the DL reconstruction approach.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that quality MPRAGE images can be obtained from highly-under-sampled data reconstructed with a DL method, and those images exhibit similar SNR, CNR and smoothness to a much longer conventional scan. Morphometrics are mostly comparable to those obtained with a conventional scan; although a few notable variations were observed which will require further study.
