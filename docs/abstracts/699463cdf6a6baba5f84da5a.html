Introduction

Vocal familiarity plays a central role in primate social communication, supporting affiliative behavior, group cohesion, and recognition of social partners. The common marmoset is a highly vocal species with rich social dynamics, engaging in near-constant vocal exchanges with group members,1 making it an excellent model to examine how the brain differentiates familiar from unfamiliar conspecific calls, and whether such familiarity encoding resembles mechanisms described in humans.2 We have previously mapped the whole-brain network for conspecific vocalization processing, including voice-selective regions in temporal and cingulate cortex.3,4 However, it remains unknown whether these regions encode familiarity information or whether familiarity relies on other structures. Here, we used ultra-high-field fMRI in awake marmosets to examine brain responses to familiar versus unfamiliar vocalizations in a passive listening paradigm.

Methods

Six adult common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus) participated in a passive block-design experiment presenting conspecific calls from two social categories: familiar individuals (partners or animals housed in the same room) and unfamiliar individuals (from a separate colony room). Stimuli consisted exclusively of natural phee calls recorded from different animals; for each subject, familiar and unfamiliar sets were carefully matched.

Functional data were acquired at 9.4T using whole-brain EPI in awake marmosets. Standard preprocessing and individual GLMs modeled familiar and unfamiliar blocks, and group-level analyses (ANOVA; familiar > unfamiliar) identified regions modulated by vocal familiarity. 

All procedures followed guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care, were approved by the University of Western Ontario Animal Care Committee, and we have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Results

Both familiar and unfamiliar calls robustly recruited the previously identified network for conspecific vocalization processing.3,4 Direct comparison revealed widespread modulation by familiarity: frontal and temporal regions, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC, area 32), hippocampus, and amygdala showed stronger responses to familiar than unfamiliar calls, consistent with the involvement of social memory and affective circuits. Several face-selective patches along the occipitotemporal axis, which we previously identified in marmosets,5 also exhibited enhanced activation for familiar voices, indicating sensitivity to socially relevant identity information beyond the visual domain.

However, auditory cortex did not show familiarity differences. ROI analyses using our recently identified marmoset voice patches 2 further demonstrated a functional dissociation within the voice-processing system. Only the ACC voice patch showed familiarity-related modulation, whereas anterior, middle, and posterior temporal voice patches did not differ between familiar and unfamiliar calls. This indicates that temporal voice patches are sensitive to conspecific vocalizations but not to social identity, whereas ACC additionally encodes familiarity.

Conclusion

These findings provide the first evidence that vocal familiarity recruits a distributed network spanning frontal, temporal, cingulate, and limbic regions in the marmoset brain. While temporal voice patches selectively respond to conspecific vocalizations, they do not encode familiarity information, which instead emerges in temporal face-selective regions and limbic–cingulate circuits. This study offers a foundation for future comparative investigations of familiarity processing across primate species.
