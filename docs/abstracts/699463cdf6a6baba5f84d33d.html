Introduction

Psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia, depression, or anxiety are characterized by high heterogeneity in symptoms and co-morbidities, challenging diagnosis and treatment. Current diagnostic practices are largely based on interviews and questionnaires aligned with DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria. Objective biomarkers derived from neurophysiological data, such as electroencephalography (EEG), offer promising opportunities for improving diagnostic precision before symptoms show up and monitoring treatment outcomes. Additionally, there is a trend towards integrating social, cultural, and environmental factors of health alongside measures of functioning and quality of life.

To enable robust and reproducible findings across diverse populations and studies, a prerequisite for clinical application, large and rich data sets as well as standardized data structures and processing pipelines are essential.1 The complexity of the data and the high number of degrees of freedom in data acquisition and processing across research groups hamper this endeavour.2 We propose a comprehensive data schema that incorporates EEG data as well as non-EEG information such as demographics, clinical assessments, and cognitive tests. Combined with a ready-to-use processing pipeline, including common steps like artifact removal and quality control, this framework supports multiple research goals: standardized data integration and processing, creating a foundation for cross-study comparability, multi-site validation, scalable computational analyses, and detailed evaluation of methodological choices.

This approach provides a foundation for scalable, data-driven psychiatric research, and the development of objective diagnostic and prognostic tools. The study presented here includes the requirement analysis with use case definitions, obtaining functional and non-functional requirements, which lead to the concept and basic design.

Methods

The requirement analysis contained workshops, semi-structured and unstructured interviews with stakeholders of the system: clinicians, neuroscientists, signal processing researchers, and data scientists. Key use cases include: 1) Transdiagnostic investigations relying on symptoms rather than diagnostic labels. 2) Research on raw data about artifact detection and their removal. 3) Performing deep learning analyses on quality ensured time series data. 4) Studies on replicability across data sets. 5) Multiverse analyses on quality-ensured (spectral) biomarkers. 6) Enable informed decisions on suitable processing steps for specific analyses.

The functional requirements that different data sources need to be harmonized and that the data needs to be stored analysis ready and therefore organized in an analysis-centered structure, led to the design decision for the database architecture. The requirement for quality assessed data prompted a structured review of current research on data cleaning and quality metrics and a systematic comparison of existing methods. Requirements for the processing pipeline were that it needs to be extensible, can be parametrized, and the code needs to be quality-assessed. Those requirements formed the basis for the architecture of the toolbox. 

The database should be initialized with a variety of comprehensive publicly available transdiagnostic data sets in order to inform the specific initial data scheme design and already include the transformation processes for this data in the processing toolbox. This requirement triggered extensive research on available data sets and an analysis of their structure and content. The minimal requirement for a data set to be included is: resting state EEG data, demographic data with at least age and gender, neuropsychological testing quantifying the severity of at least three major psychiatric disorders. 

Finally, compatibility with common standards needs to be ensured and current tools and frameworks need to be considered. This prompted another extensive systematic research on these topics.

Results

Concept: data schema & data

For data organization, we decided on a star-schema-based structure to support flexible and scalable analysis (Fig. 1). This dimensional model consists of central fact tables that store quantitative measurements at different processing stages and multiple dimension tables that provide descriptive context. Each fact references its dimension, enabling flexible aggregation and analysis across subjects, sessions, and sites. This structure supports reproducibility and scalability for large, heterogeneous datasets.

Only two public data sets fulfilling our requirements can be initially included: 1) Two Decades-Brainclinics Research Archive for Insights in Neurophysiology (TDBRAIN); 2) Healthy Brain Network (HBN). The two NIH datasets Establishing Moderators/Biosignatures of Antidepressant Response - Clinical Care (EMBARC) and Healthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) were not accessible for us. Therefore, we additionally incorporated the Leipzig Study for Mind-Body-Emotion Interactions (LEMON) to include more healthy control subjects, and our own clinical data set comprising 426 subjects with resting state as well as task EEG data, neuropsychological and cognitive testing.

To harmonize multiple EEG datasets into a unified data scheme, we defined a common technical setup covering data formats, target sampling frequency, electrode configurations, and strategies for handling incompatible or missing information. 

As we do not anticipate analysis of EEG data over the frequency of 100Hz and following recommendations to include a safety margin beyond the Nyquist–Shannon theorem, we decided on a standard sampling frequency of 256Hz. For electrode placement, we adopted the extended 10-10 system, an internationally recognized standard widely supported by major frameworks and toolboxes. Missing channels will be interpolated when appropriate or marked as NA otherwise. For referencing, we selected the Reference Electrode Standardization Technique (REST), endorsed by IFCN guidelines, recommended in OHBM best practices for MEEG, and integrated into widely used software environments. REST was chosen for its ability to approximate an ideal neutral reference and reduce reference-related bias across datasets. 3,4

Concept: pipeline

The important architectural decision for the pipeline is for it to be modular and parametrizable as well as extensible. The end user can choose which processing steps in which form they want to include but also extend those steps with their own implementation. Quality must be assessed in each step of the processing pipeline.

Most commonly used toolboxes are implemented in MATLAB or Python. We chose Python because it is completely open source and it excels for machine learning use cases which will likely increase. 

While researchers will have to write their own ETL scripts to load their data into our schema, common standards like EEG-BIDS will be considered concerning the file structure as well as the data standards common for BIDS. That also includes the extraction of filtered data from the database into raw EEG files.

Basic Design

Four categories of fact tables were defined (Fig. 1): 1) Raw continuous EEG data 2) Preprocessed continuous time-series data 2) Extracted EEG features 4) Non-EEG data. Dimension tables include participant demographics, database metadata, EEG recording parameters, task descriptions, and absolute and relative time markers.

Conclusion

Our study provides a design for a transdiagnostic Data Warehouse containing initially continuous EEG-data (resting-state or task) with a single recording per subject and accompanying neuropsychological testing. However, the schema is designed to be extensible and is explicitly designed to cover further use cases such as longitudinal studies or other experimental paradigms such as evoked potential data. The same concept applies to the technical aspects of the EEG data: Although we decided on a technical standard setup, methods to transform the data into other commonly used electrode setup and referencing spaces will be provided in the processing pipeline.

One open question to date is how to handle substantial differences in number of electrodes across datasets. Interpolating data in the 10-20 system to match a full 128-channel configuration would substantially increase space requirements without increasing information content. Furthermore, such interpolation may lead to overinterpretation of spatial patterns. A more robust approach is to restrict analyses to the 19 overlapping electrodes. Finally, the decision on this question might depend on the specific use case. Nevertheless, harmonization of other methodological aspects remains essential to ensure comparability.[RA1] [FC2]  Other aspects of the harmonization process also need close monitoring. It is important to note that the original sampling frequency has to be documented for each dataset, as upsampling does not introduce new information and should not be used for spectral analyses beyond the original recorded bandwidth under consideration of the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem.5

In summary, this concept provides a scalable and extensible framework for harmonizing EEG data across heterogeneous sources, enabling reproducible research and advanced modelling in psychiatry.
