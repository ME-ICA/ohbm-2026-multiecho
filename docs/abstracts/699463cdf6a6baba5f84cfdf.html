Introduction

Schizophrenia (SCZ) is characterized by substantial clinical and biological heterogeneity, which complicates diagnosis and individualized treatment. Resting-state fMRI has provided valuable tools for characterizing this heterogeneity, with ALFF, fALFF, ReHo, and functional connectivity each capturing distinct aspects of spontaneous neural activity. However, as these metrics emphasize different signal properties, they also present methodological constraints—such as sensitivity to physiological noise, limited spatial specificity, or restricted ability to characterize regionally anchored disruptions—which may influence their utility in patient stratification. These considerations highlight the need for complementary functional measures that can further illuminate the neurobiological variation underlying SCZ.

Alterations in excitation–inhibition balance have been increasingly recognized as a core feature of SCZ, suggesting that neural excitability may provide a relevant dimension for investigating its heterogeneity. Building on analytical developments in detecting task-evoked activity without predefined predictors, we applied a functional structure–informed synchrony approach to estimate cortical excitability (CE) from resting-state fMRI. By quantifying signal synchrony within connectivity-derived spatial windows, this method offers a way to index local excitability within neurobiologically meaningful functional structures.

To minimize confounding influences of chronic illness and medication, we examined drug-naïve first-episode SCZ (FES) patients. We tested whether CE spatial profiles could support data-driven subtyping, relate to clinical characteristics and longitudinal treatment changes, and reflect underlying transcriptional and molecular signatures. Through the integration of machine learning, clustering, follow-up imaging, and transcriptomic mapping, this study aims to advance a biologically informed framework for understanding heterogeneity in SCZ.

Methods

Participants, Imaging Acquisition, and Preprocessing

We included drug-naïve FES patients and demographically matched healthy controls recruited from a single clinical site. All participants completed standardized clinical assessments and underwent identical MRI scanning on a 3T system, including high-resolution T1-weighted imaging and resting-state fMRI. Data were preprocessed using standard pipelines with motion correction, nuisance regression, and surface-based alignment.

Clinical and Cognitive Assessment

Symptom severity was assessed using PANSS; mood by HAMD/HAMA; functioning by GAF. Cognitive performance was measured using the Chinese BACS, yielding Z-scored composite scores.

CE Mapping

CE mapping was based on our model-free approach for quantifying neural activity without task regressors.^1 For each voxel, a functional-structure–informed spatial window was defined using a group-averaged voxelwise functional connectivity matrix derived from 812 HCP resting-state datasets.^2 This window captured the voxel’s functional neighborhood. A modified PCA was then applied to the time series within each window to estimate local synchrony, producing a whole-brain CE map. This CE index reflects excitability-related fluctuations in spontaneous activity while incorporating functional topology.

SVM Classification

Voxelwise CE values were averaged within 246 Brainnetome regions and standardized. Support Vector Machine models were trained to classify FES and HCs (70/30 split; five-fold cross-validation in training). Model performance was evaluated using test-set predictions.

FES Subtyping via Hierarchical Clustering

Brain regions showing significant CE differences (covarying age and sex) were used as features. Ward linkage hierarchical clustering was performed. The Calinski-Harabasz index (K = 2–10) was used to determine the optimal cluster number. Cluster stability was assessed with 5000-iteration bootstrapped Adjusted Rand Index.

Gene Expression Associations

Allen Human Brain Atlas microarray data were processed using abagen, including probe filtering, mapping to Brainnetome regions (left hemisphere only), and robust-sigmoid normalization.
Partial least squares (PLS) regression related regional CE alterations to whole-genome expression across 123 regions. The significance of PLS1 was tested using 5000 spatially constrained permutations. Bootstrapping (5000 iterations) generated gene Z-scores; genes with |Z| > 6 were retained as PLS1+ or PLS1–.

Enrichment Analysis

PLS1 genes (|Z| > 6) were submitted to Metascape for Gene Ontology and KEGG enrichment (significance threshold 1%).

Neurotransmitter System Associations

Spatial correlations between CE alterations and PET-derived neurotransmitter maps were examined using JuSpace v2.0, covering serotonin (5-HT1a, 5-HT1b, 5-HT2a), dopamine (D1, D2, DAT, FDOPA), GABAa5, muscarinic M1, NMDA, and mGluR5 receptors. Spearman correlations (Fisher-Z transformed) were tested via 5000 permutations with FDR correction.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic characteristics were examined using t-tests or χ² tests. CE differences between groups were assessed using voxelwise and regionwise two-sample t-tests (covarying age and sex), with FWE (voxelwise) or FDR (regionwise) correction. Longitudinal changes were tested with paired t-tests. Correlations used Pearson or Spearman tests depending on data distribution.

Results

Demographics

After excluding subjects with excessive head motion (>2.5 mm/°; 7 FES, 4 HCs), the final cohort included 77 drug-naïve FES patients and 76 HCs. Groups were sex-matched, but FES patients were slightly older and exhibited lower cognitive scores. Nineteen FES patients completed 12-month follow-up after antipsychotic treatment, with no additional medications.

CE Differences Between FES and HCs

Voxel-wise CE analysis revealed six clusters with reduced CE in FES, primarily in frontal regions (bilateral superior/middle frontal gyri, medial superior frontal gyrus, left anterior cingulate), sensorimotor cortex, and right cuneus. Region-wise CE averaged over 246 Brainnetome parcels enabled SVM classification of FES vs HCs, achieving 86.7% accuracy (sensitivity 85.7%, specificity 87.5%).

FES Subtyping

Hierarchical clustering of regions showing significant CE reductions (pFDR < 0.01) identified two stable subtypes (K = 2; ARI = 0.797): FES1 (n = 35) with widespread CE reductions, and FES2 (n = 42) with preserved CE. FES1 exhibited more severe depression, negative symptoms, and cognitive deficits than FES2. CE in left A9l_L negatively correlated with depression, right A6vl_R with negative symptoms, and CE in A8vl_L/A6vl_R positively correlated with cognition.

Longitudinal Changes

At 12-month follow-up, FES1 showed improvements in global functioning, mood, and symptom severity, while FES2 improved only in global functioning and overall symptoms. Cognitive performance remained largely unchanged. CE trajectories differed: FES2 displayed significant reductions in multiple regions, whereas FES1 showed trending CE increases.

Transcriptional Associations

Left-hemisphere AHBA data (123 regions × 15,633 genes) were analyzed using PLS regression. PLS1 explained 15.6% (FES1) and 14.85% (FES2) of regional CE variance. PLS1 gene expression maps exhibited opposing spatial patterns between subtypes, significantly correlating with CE t-maps (Spearman r = 0.543, 0.430). Subtype-specific PLS1+/- gene sets were identified (FES1: 504+/795−; FES2: 1333+/1030−). Intersecting PLS1 genes with 120 SCZ-associated GWAS genes highlighted 8 genes in FES1 and 19 in FES2, including NLGN4X and KLF6 (FES1) and R3HDM2 and LRRC4B (FES2).

Functional Enrichment

GO/KEGG analyses revealed PLS1− genes in FES1 and PLS1+ genes in FES2 were enriched for synaptic transmission, neuron projection development, and membrane localization, whereas FES2 PLS1− genes were linked to receptor signaling, hydrolase regulation, hemopoiesis, and Notch signaling.

Neurotransmitter Associations

FES1 regional CE changes negatively correlated with 5‑HT1b, GABAa5, and mGluR5, and positively with DAT. FES2 CE changes correlated positively with 5‑HT2a and mGluR5, and negatively with D2, DAT, and FDOPA. Across both subtypes, regions with high receptor density generally exhibited greater CE reductions, indicating subtype-specific neurochemical associations.

Conclusion

In this study, we mapped CE in FES patients and HCs, revealing spatially heterogeneous CE reductions involving frontal, sensorimotor, and occipital regions. These alterations aligned with large-scale networks implicated in executive control, salience processing, and sensory integration, suggesting widespread but functionally distinct disruptions in  SCZ. Using CE alterations as features, hierarchical clustering identified two neurobiological FES subtypes with clear spatial and clinical divergence. FES1 showed pronounced CE reductions and higher depressive and negative symptoms, along with poorer cognitive performance, resembling a more impaired phenotype. FES2 displayed relatively preserved CE patterns and milder symptoms, potentially reflecting partial compensatory mechanisms. Treatment trajectories further distinguished the subtypes: FES2 showed improved global functioning, whereas FES1 exhibited broader symptom remission accompanied by partial CE recovery, indicating differential functional plasticity.

Molecular analyses suggested distinct biological mechanisms underlying these CE patterns. FES1-related CE reductions were linked to gene sets associated with synaptic structure and neurodevelopmental processes, consistent with a hypoexcitability-driven trajectory. In contrast, FES2-associated genes implicated signal transduction and immune pathways, pointing to a more multisystem mechanism. Neurotransmitter receptor mapping further differentiated the subtypes: FES1 showed negative associations with 5-HT1b, GABAa5, and mGluR5 distributions, suggesting altered excitatory–inhibitory balance, while FES2 exhibited associations involving dopaminergic and serotonergic systems, including positive correlations with 5-HT2a receptor density, consistent with distinct neuromodulatory signatures.

Together, these findings demonstrate that CE-based profiling can reveal biologically informative heterogeneity in FES, integrating neural, clinical, and molecular dimensions. CE mapping provides a promising framework for precision subtyping by capturing individualized patterns of cortical dysfunction and their underlying mechanisms. Larger cohorts, broader cognitive assessments, and bilateral transcriptomic data will be important for validating and extending these results.
