Introduction

Humans have the ability to understand and speak multiple languages, switching between them by accessing distinct sets of words and meanings. While we use the term "bilingual" to describe someone who speaks two languages, there is no single prototype of bilingualism. With more than 7,000 languages spoken worldwide, there are countless possible language pairs, each learned at different ages, developed to different proficiency levels, and used with varying frequency in daily life. Because of this diversity in bilingual profiles, studying how two language representations coexist within the same brain is challenging, especially for unbalanced bilinguals who are clearly stronger in one language than the other, as they often perceive their second language (L2) through their native language (L1).1

Prior research has studied the bilingual brain using language-switching paradigms, confirming the cognitive effort required to manage two languages.2 Studies using local analysis methods such as multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) searchlight and univariate analyses have identified specific regions, including middle occipital cortices, fusiform gyri, and lateral temporal, temporoparietal, and prefrontal cortices, that respond differently to language pairs,3 suggesting functional independence of neural representations for different languages in bilinguals.

In this study, we decode which language Chinese L1-English L2 bilinguals are speaking during rapid picture naming using whole-brain functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) activity with a linear model, and identify the most informative brain regions for language discrimination. While previous work has decoded language identity in bilinguals3, to our knowledge, this is the first study to (1) train individual-specific models for each bilingual participant, and to (2) determine the minimal network sufficient for accurate language decoding by comparing multiple feature selection methods.

Methods

We analyzed fMRI data from 77 bilinguals who named pictures in either Chinese or English based on a color cue, completing 160 trials across two runs². Participants were native Chinese speakers who began learning English at age 9.4 years (SD = 2.6). We averaged single-trial estimates separately for all Chinese trials and all English trials per participant and extracted activation patterns from 400 regions of interest (ROIs) defined by the Schaefer functional parcellation⁴. We trained 77 participant-specific ridge-regularized logistic regression models to decode language from brain activity.

To understand which brain regions are most critical for bilingual language control and whether decoding performance could be preserved with fewer features, we tested four feature selection methods across multiple feature set sizes (k=10, 30, 50, 75, 100 regions): (1) statistical contrast: ranking regions by t-statistics between Chinese and English trials to identify regions showing the strongest language-specific responses, (2) Language Atlas (LanA)⁵: selecting 30 regions with at least 20% overlap with the established universal language network to test whether known language comprehension regions are sufficient for language control, (3) genetic algorithm: evolutionary optimization using a population-based search (population size=32, 80 generations) to identify optimal feature combinations, with stable features defined as those appearing in at least 50% of 8 independent runs with different random seeds, and (4) control methods: randomly selected features (averaged across 20 repetitions) and inverse models that excluded top-performing features to validate that our feature selection captured meaningful neural signals rather than overfitting. Our method allows us to determine the minimal distributed network needed for accurate language decoding and whether bilingual control relies on the same regions as language comprehension.

Results

We show that language identity can be decoded from whole-brain fMRI signals during bilingual picture naming, with a mean accuracy of 0.63 ± 0.01 across participants (n=77), with high inter-individual variability (range: 0.52-0.73). Accuracy ranged from a mean of 0.54 ± 0.01 in the bottom 15% of performers (n = 12) to 0.72 ± 0.00 in the top 15% (n = 12) using all 400 features.

For top performers, the genetic algorithm feature selection achieved accuracy of 0.72 at 75 features, outperforming t-statistic selection by +3.59% (Figure 1A), though both methods performed similarly to the 400-feature baseline (GA: +0.16%, t-statistic: -3.44%). For bottom performers, both methods improved accuracy at 100 features compared to baseline, with the genetic algorithm showing greater improvement (+2.24%) compared to t-statistic selection (+1.67%) (Figure 1B). Overall, the genetic algorithm consistently outperformed t-statistic selection for both high and low performers.

We observed that the top performers maintained above-chance decoding even with randomly selected features as few as 10 regions, whereas bottom performers required optimized feature selection (genetic algorithm or t-statistic) to achieve above-chance performance (Figure 1A, 1B). This suggests that top performers' language-specific signals are detectable even with suboptimal feature sets, while bottom performers require a more careful selection of discriminative regions.

We observed little improvement in performance when using only language comprehension regions from the LanA atlas (Figure 1C), suggesting that the neural mechanisms for language selection and control in bilinguals require more regions than those supporting general language comprehension alone.

Overall, models using 75–100 selected regions performed just as well as the full 400-region whole-brain model, while models with fewer than 50 regions performed worse.

Conclusion

Our results show that language identity can be decoded from whole-brain fMRI activity using individual-specific models, with a high inter-individual variability. Decoding performance relies on a distributed, large-scale brain network; however, good accuracy can be achieved using only a limited subset of informative regions when these are identified through effective feature selection methods.
