Introduction

Isolated Rapid-Eye-Movement (REM) sleep behaviour disorder (iRBD) is a parasomnia characterized by unpleasant dreams and the loss of skeletal muscle atonia in REM sleep, resulting in dream-enacting vigorous behaviours. Together with other non-motor symptoms, such as hyposmia, depression and cognitive deficits, iRBD is currently recognized as a prodromal symptom of α-synucleinopathies, with up to 90% of patients converting to Parkinson’s disease (PD), dementia with Lewy bodies or multiple system atrophy in 5 to 15 years. In this line, α-synuclein  brain pathology has been confirmed in iRBD, supporting its consideration as an early stage of alpha-synucleinopathies1. 

The glymphatic system is a complex waste clearance mechanism of the brain, which removes metabolic waste products through transporting of interstitial and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) through perivascular spaces, mainly during sleep. Recent evidence suggests that α-synuclein accumulation may be linked to impaired glymphatic clearance2. Few studies have investigated Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) proxies of perivascular glymphatic activity in iRBD showing its association with clinical risk factors of progression3.   

Using resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI), significant coupling between slow signals (<1Hz) of global blood-oxygen-level-dependent (gBOLD) and CSF signals has been shown to occur during sleep4. This gBOLD-CSF coupling has been interpreted as a coordination between hemodynamic activity and CSF flow and has been proposed as a potential index to measure impairment in glymphatic clearance. In this line, a reduced gBOLD-CSF coupling has been reported in α-synucleinopathies like PD5 during wakefulness, showing its associations with longitudinal motor impairment and sleep disturbances. No prior study has assessed the gBOLD-CSF coupling in prodromal stages of α-synucleinopathies. We hypothesize that patients with iRBD will show decreased coupling and that this will be associated with clinical symptoms.  

Methods

iRBD patients and healthy controls (HC) were recruited from the Sleep Unit, Hospital Clínic de Barcelona, with diagnosis confirmed by video-polysomnography. Total sample comprised 71 HC and 87 iRBD. Exclusion criteria included a score below 25 in the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and MRI artifacts or pathological findings. HC were excluded if they self-reported RBD symptoms, or other major sleep disturbances disclosed in a sleep interview or anosmia.  

Sociodemographic information was collected for all subjects. Clinical assessments included neuropsychiatric symptoms obtained from Cummings Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), Beck Depression Inventory 2nd edition (BDI-II), Starkstein’s Apathy Scale (AS), and related non-motor symptoms were also assessed through the Non-Motor Symptoms Questionnaire (NMS). For iRBD patients, disease duration, age of onset and motor symptoms with part III of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS-III) were collected. 

MRI data were acquired with a 3T scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens, Germany), at the Hospital Clínic. The scanning protocol included high‐resolution three‐dimensional T1‐weighted images acquired in the sagittal plane (TR = 2400 ms, TE = 2.22 ms, TI = 1000 ms, 208 slices, FOV = 256 mm; 1 mm isotropic voxel) and a 10‐min rs-fMRI gradient‐echo echo‐planar (750 T2*‐weighted volumes, TR = 800 ms, TE = 37 ms, flip angle = 52°, slice thickness = 2 mm, FOV = 208 mm). 

All rs-fMRI data were corrected for time differences in slice acquisitions and realigned correcting for head motion. Finally, linear and quadratic trends were removed, along with temporal filtering applying a bandpass filter from 0.01-0.1 Hz. For posterior steps, realigned data was then registered to T1-weighted (T1w)5.  Finally, group summary motion was computed and subjects with mean framewise displacement (FD)>0.5mm, maximum translation>1.5mm or rotation>1.5º were excluded. 

To quantify gBOLD-CSF coupling, we first obtained the signals of gBOLD and CSF from the preprocessed rs-fMRI data. The gBOLD signal was computed averaging BOLD signal across all GM voxels. GM masks were computed from SPM segmentation results, selecting voxels classified as GM with probability > 0.5.  

CSF signal was averaged within a mask computed with an in-house script. First, the rs-fMRI reference scan was normalized from 0 to 1. A dilated brainstem mask was calculated from Freesurfer (v7.1) automatic segmentation to restrict the calculation area of the CSF mask. Next, the brightest voxels from the last three slices were selected based on an adaptive threshold, starting by selecting voxels with an intensity greater than 0.6 and checking that the mask size was greater than 80 voxels. If the size was not adequate, the threshold was reduced by 0.05 until the subject's mask exceeded 80 voxels. All masks were visually inspected, and subjects with the last slices at the level of the middle cerebellum were excluded.  

The first and last 10 volumes were removed from both signals to minimize the influence of magnetization stabilization and edge artifacts introduced by bandpass filtering. Then, both signals were z-scored normalized. Following Fultz et al. methodology4, we computed the cross-correlation function between both signals over a range from -10s to 10s to quantify the coupling between gBOLD and CSF signals in different time delays. To obtain the gBOLD-CSF coupling value, we saved the maximum anticorrelation value per subject and the time at which it occurred.  

Sociodemographic analysis was conducted with student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney tests according to normality of data. Categorical data were analyzed with chi-squared test. ANCOVA was used to perform group analysis, accounting for confounding variables. Lately, partial correlational analysis was done with spearman or pearson tests, accounting for normality. Statistical significance threshold was set at FDR corrected p<0.05. All analyses were done with RStudio. 

Results

After exclusion by excessive head motion and a quality control of GM and CSF masks, the final sample consisted of 51 HC and 63 iRBD. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics are reported in Figure 1A. There were group differences in age and sex. Thus, all subsequent group analysis will be controlled by these variables. Also, mean FD was included as a confounder in posterior imaging group analyses to account for the possible influence of head motion in gBOLD-CSF coupling results. 

We successfully calculated the gBOLD-CSF coupling for all the sample and obtained values which were in alignment with previous literature and with our prior results with pilot data and with an external sample of HC (data not shown). 

Patients with iRBD scored lower in global cognition and showed significantly more depressive symptoms, apathy, non-motor and neuropsychiatric symptoms compared to HC (Figure 1 A).  

A significant group difference was observed in gBOLD-CSF coupling, characterized by a lower anticorrelation peak in iRBD group (mean ±SD: -0.33±0.16) than in HC group (mean ±SD: -0.45±0.17), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d=-0.773, U=987.0, p<0.001), After controlling for age, sex and mean FD, the difference remained highly significant (t=-3.774, p<0.001) see Figure 1B.. No group differences were found in the temporal delay where the anticorrelation peak occurs (time lag variable), (mean±SD, HC: 3.50±1.54, iRBD: 3.99±1.94, t=-1.506, p=0.135). 

Within the iRBD group, weaker coupling was associated with AS (r=0.361, p=0.005), NMS (r=0.316, p=0.014) and NPI (r=0.331, p=0.010) after adjusting for age and sex (Figure 2C). Also, we found a correlation with BDI (r=0.264, p=0.039), but it was not maintained after FDR correction. No correlations were seen in HC group and other  clinical or cognitive variables. 

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a significatively reduced gBOLD-CSF coupling in iRBD, which is associated with worse neuropsychiatric and non-motor symptoms. This suggests that glymphatic impairment is related to pathology and clinical symptoms.  

These findings highlight the potential of gBOLD-CSF coupling as a marker of glymphatic impairment in prodromal stages of α-synucleinopathies. Further longitudinal studies are needed to address the associations between gBOLD-CSF coupling, phenoconversion trajectories and neurodegeneration biomarkers to support its use as a disease progression marker.
