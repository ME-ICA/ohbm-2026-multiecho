Introduction

Comparative studies between humans and macaque monkeys offer valuable insights into cortical evolution in primates. Previous work has identified putative homologous regions involved in sensory and higher-order functions across species¹.  However, most cross-species comparisons have typically focused on isolated cognitive  or sensory processes, without providing a comprehensive map of functional homology. Naturalistic stimuli, such as movies, offer a unique advantage over traditional task designs by engaging multiple sensory modalities simultaneously. These rich, ecologically valid inputs more closely approximate real-world experiences, making them particularly valuable for understanding functional homology between primate brains .

In this study, we applied a unified brain encoding framework to fMRI data collected from both humans and rhesus macaques viewing the same naturalistic movie (Monkey Kingdom). This movie featured dynamic scenes with natural and man-made objects, various actions performed by macaques, humans, and other species, and synchronized audio. We constructed vertex-wise encoding models² using multiple feature spaces spanning objects, actions, visual motion, and audio information. By combining variance partition analyses with group-level PCA of encoding weights, we aimed to (i) dissociate the unique contributions of distinct feature types and (ii) create a functional homology map linking human and macaque brains in multimodal information processing.

Methods

Datasets and preprocessing:

MRI data were collected from 24 human participants (each viewing the stimuli once) and 12 rhesus macaques (each viewing the stimuli 7–11 times), all of whom watched five clips from the documentary Monkey Kingdom (MK). Human fMRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens scanner at Dartmouth College (2.5 mm isotropic, TR = 1 s) and preprocessed using fMRIPrep20.2.7. The data were then resampled to the onavg-ico32 cortical surface template³. Macaque fMRI data were collected on a 3T Siemens PRISMA scanner at KU Leuven, with an isotropic spatial resolution of 1.25 mm (n = 8, TR = 1 s) or 0.8 mm (n = 4, TR = 2 s). To enhance the contrast-to-noise ratio, contrast agents (MION) were used in the macaques. Macaque data were preprocessed using in-house scripts, and were then mapped to the mkavg-ico32 cortical surface template, which was generated from the cortical surfaces of all 12 macaques.

In addition to the MK dataset, we analyzed data from 100 Human Connectome Project (HCP) movie subjects, who passively viewed a series of audiovisual movie clips across four runs. These clips were drawn from various films, mostly depicting the human world.

Features:

We extracted multiple feature types from the movie clips, including objects, actions, visual motion, and audio features. For MK, objects and actions were annotated at 1-s temporal resolution by 10 independent raters, followed by manual quality-checks. For the HCP movie, we used pre-existing annotations available at https://github.com/esfinn/movie_cpm/tree/master/data/7T_movie_resources. These annotations were transformed into semantic embeddings using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model, which organizes semantically related concepts in closer proximity in the embedding space. To capture dynamic, low-level visual features,  we applied a motion-energy model implemented in PyMoten⁴. Audio features were represented using log-Mel spectrograms.

Vertex-wise brain encoding models:

For each subject, vertex-wise brain encoding models were trained to predict fMRI responses from the extracted stimulus features. To prevent overfitting we used a leave-one-movie-clip-out cross-validation scheme. Model performance was assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients and the coefficient of determination (R²) between predicted and observed time series. Given the inclusion of four feature types in the encoding models, we employed banded ridge regression, which estimates a separate regularization parameter for each feature space and each vertex. Optimal hyperparameters were selected via nested leave-one-movie-clip-out cross-validation, in which inner-loop validation was conducted within the training data of each outer fold.

Variance partition analysis:

To estimate the unique contribution of each feature type, as well as specific categories of objects (e.g., animate vs. inanimate) or actions (e.g., social vs. nonsocial), we constructed partial models in which features corresponding to a given category were excluded from the full model (the model including all features). The contribution of a specific feature or category was assessed by performing paired t-tests between the prediction accuracies of the full and partial models (r_full vs. r_partial).

Group PCA analysis:

To identify the visual and audio dimensions shared between humans and macaques, we concatenated the feature weight matrices derived from the human and macaque brain encoding models along the vertex dimension. We then performed group PCA on the concatenated weight matrices. Each principal component was interpreted by correlating its scores with behavioral ratings of objects and actions along several well-established semantic dimensions, including mobility, animacy, biological relevance, man-made, social, real-world size, and emotional valence.

Results

High prediction performance in visual and auditory cortex for the vertex-wise brain encoding models:

We first constructed vertex-wise brain encoding models using all four feature types—objects, actions, visual motion, and audio—as predictors. Figure 1A shows the average prediction performance across subjects in each dataset. In both humans and macaques, regions with higher prediction accuracy were observed in the early and higher-level visual, auditory, parietal, and prefrontal cortex. Overall, prediction performance was higher in macaques than in humans, possibly because the macaque fMRI data were averaged across multiple runs, which increased SNR and captured stimulus-driven brain responses more effectively. The spatial pattern observed in the HCP dataset closely resembled that in the MK human dataset. However, prediction performance was lower in the HCP data likely due to the between-movie prediction setting. Notably, in contrast to the humans, the macaques showed strong prediction performance in ventral lateral prefrontal regions.

Similar and different patterns for processing visual information in human and monkey brains:

To identify brain regions uniquely explained by each feature type, we performed variance partition analyses (Figure 1B). Overall, we observed largely similar patterns in humans and macaques. For both species, animate object features enhanced prediction performance in face- and body-selective regions, while inanimate object features improved predictions in scene-selective regions such as the parahippocampal place area (PPA).

Notably, both humans and macaques showed strong responses to social-related actions involving conspecifics in the anterior superior temporal sulcus (STS). In contrast, social-related actions performed by macaques did not activate the human anterior STS. We also observed species-specific differences: in macaques, area MT showed the strongest unique contribution from low-level visual motion features, whereas in humans, MT exhibited the strongest unique contribution from action-related features.

Functional homology maps of visual and auditory information processing in human and monkey brains:

To intuitively examine potential homologous brain organization in visual and auditory information processing, we performed joint PCA on the brain encoding weight matrices from both species and extracted the first three principal components. These three components, which explained 39.5% of the total variance, were mapped to RGB values⁵, with similar colors indicating functional correspondence in multimodal information processing.

As shown in Figure 1C, primary visual cortices in both species appear light purple, while auditory cortices are yellow–green. Additionally, higher-level visual cortices in both humans and macaques exhibit a shared (purple) visual processing pathway. Consistent with the variance partition analyses, area MT shows distinct color profiles between species.

We also performed separate PCA analyses on the weight matrices corresponding to object and action features (Figure 1D). For object features, the first two principal components corresponded to the animate–inanimate and artificial–natural dimensions, respectively. For action features, the first two principal components were associated with social–nonsocial and mobility-related dimensions.

Conclusion

In summary, by applying a unified brain encoding framework to humans and macaques viewing the same naturalistic movie stimuli, we uncovered both shared and species-specific principles of multimodal cortical organization. Across species, visual and auditory information is represented within largely homologous cortical networks, reflecting conserved processing architectures for naturalistic perception. However, we also observed some functional divergences within structurally homologous regions, such as area MT, highlighting species-specific specializations in information processing. Together, these findings demonstrate the value of naturalistic brain encoding models for cross-species comparisons and provide a general framework for exploring the evolutionary origins of complex sensory processing and social cognition.
