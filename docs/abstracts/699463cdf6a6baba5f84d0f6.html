Introduction

Delirium tremens (DT) represents the most severe and life-threatening expression of alcohol withdrawal, yet its neurophysiological antecedents remain poorly defined. Early detection is crucial, as DT emerges abruptly and carries substantial mortality if prophylactic treatment is not initiated in time. Progress has been hindered by the inherent difficulty of acquiring high-quality electroencephalogram (EEG) from acutely ill, agitated patients, resulting in a literature dominated by case reports and underpowered analyses. Moreover, virtually no studies have examined DT-related neural dynamics in Asian populations—despite well-documented ethnic differences in alcohol metabolism and withdrawal expression—leaving a major cross-cultural gap in the field.

To address these limitations, we assembled one of the largest rigorously phenotyped Asian cohorts in Taiwan of patients with severe Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD), based on resting-state EEG acquired during the pre-DT baseline. This uniquely large dataset offers a rare opportunity to define the electrophysiological signatures that precede the emergence of DT. Our aim was to identify quantitative EEG (qEEG) markers capable of differentiating patients who later developed DT from those undergoing uncomplicated withdrawal, thereby establishing early neurophysiological risk indicators and informing precision prophylactic management.

Methods

Participants and Study Design

We conducted a retrospective study in the alcohol detoxification ward of the Taipei City Psychiatric Center. To ensure that neurophysiological findings reflected alcohol withdrawal specifically, rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied.
Inclusion Criteria: (1) Age 20–65 years; (2) DSM-5 diagnosis of severe Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD); and (3) active alcohol consumption within 24 hours prior to admission.
Exclusion Criteria: Patients were excluded if they had (1) comorbid major psychiatric disorders (schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or major depressive disorder) or were currently receiving antipsychotics or mood stabilizers; (2) concurrent substance use disorders, confirmed by urine toxicology screening (amphetamines, opioids, etc.); (3) severe systemic illnesses (e.g., decompensated liver cirrhosis or acute infection); or (4) major neurological conditions (e.g., seizure disorder, traumatic brain injury, or dementia).

Clinical Management and Cohort Formation

All participants received a standardized detoxification regimen consisting of fixed-dose lorazepam and thiamine supplementation. EEG recordings were obtained only after detoxification and clinical stabilization, ensuring a valid neurophysiological baseline free from acute intoxication effects. From an initial pool of 468 inpatients (431 without DT and 37 with DT), we identified 79 patients who met all criteria and had valid, artifact-free EEG recordings. The final cohort comprised 25 patients who later developed delirium tremens (DT) during hospitalization and 54 demographically matched non-DT controls who experienced uncomplicated withdrawal.

EEG Acquisition and Preprocessing

Resting-state EEG was recorded using a Cadwell Arc system with standard 10–20 electrode placement. Continuous EEG data were band-pass filtered between 0.5–50 Hz. To minimize contamination from motion and muscle artifacts—given the agitated state typical of this population—all data underwent strict visual inspection and artifact rejection by trained raters prior to analysis.

Quantitative Spectral Analysis

Quantitative EEG (qEEG) spectral analysis was conducted using Welch’s method to compute absolute power for each artifact-free epoch. Power spectra were averaged across four canonical frequency bands: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), and beta (13–30 Hz). Group-level comparisons of demographic characteristics and spectral power were performed between the DT and non-DT groups, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics
 The final resting-state EEG analysis included 79 inpatients with severe AUD. The DT group (n = 25) had a mean age of 50.8 ± 7.2 years with a sex distribution of 23 males and 1 female. The non-DT group (n = 54) had a mean age of 44.4 ± 10.3 years with 33 males and 21 females. All patients fulfilled criteria for severe AUD and were managed under a standardized withdrawal protocol on the same specialized ward.

Quantitative EEG Spectral and Connectivity Analysis
 Topographic maps of relative power (1–50 Hz) revealed a distinct resting-state pattern in patients who later developed DT (Figure 1). Compared with the non-DT group, the DT group showed marked reductions in alpha (8–13 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) relative power over frontal and central regions, together with relative increases in theta (4–8 Hz) and delta (1–4 Hz) power over temporal and occipital areas. This combination resulted in elevated frontal theta/beta ratios (TBR) in the DT group, consistent with cortical hypoarousal and disrupted regulatory balance rather than simple global hyperexcitability.

Regional spectral profiles averaged within canonical ROIs (frontal, central, parietal, temporal, occipital) corroborated this pattern. Across temporal and parietal regions in particular, DT patients exhibited lower alpha and beta power than non-DT patients, while slow-frequency power tended to be relatively higher, suggesting a shift from fast, synchronized activity toward slower, less efficient oscillations.

Functional connectivity analyses using the phase-lag index (PLI) further highlighted network-level alterations. At the group level, DT patients showed widespread reductions in alpha- and beta-band connectivity, especially among fronto-temporal and fronto-parietal links, with more subtle changes in theta and delta bands. Many of these effects were apparent at the uncorrected level, but only a small number of connections—predominantly in the delta band—remained significant after FDR correction, pointing to a pattern of global desynchronization with limited focal disconnection rather than a few highly localized lesions in the network. These EEG alterations were not explained by differences in baseline withdrawal severity or benzodiazepine dosing, suggesting a DT‐specific neurophysiological phenotype.

Conclusion

This study represents one of the largest quantitative resting-state EEG investigations specifically targeting patients who subsequently develop delirium tremens. Integrating spectral and connectivity findings, our data indicate that DT is characterized not by generalized adrenergic “overdrive”, but by a signature of cortical slowing and desynchronization—reduced frontal and central alpha/beta power, relatively increased slow-frequency activity over posterior regions, elevated frontal TBR, and weakened alpha/beta connectivity across fronto-temporal and fronto-parietal networks. This multi-modal qEEG profile supports the view of DT as an acute encephalopathic state reflecting profound cortical network dysfunction. The identified combination of spectral slowing and connectivity loss holds promise as an objective biomarker to stratify DT risk and to inform early, prophylactic interventions in hospitalized patients with severe AUD. The identified spectral and connectivity markers provide a principled foundation for future machine-learning models aimed at early DT prediction and precision withdrawal management.
