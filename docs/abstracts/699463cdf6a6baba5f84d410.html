Introduction

The architecture of connections in the human brain spans multiple spatial scales, from individual axons to small fasciculi and major long-range tracts, establishing a clear structural hierarchy. Existing imaging modalities cannot cover this entire range simultaneously due to inherent trade-offs between resolution, field of view and contrast. Especially, diffusion MRI cannot accurately resolve complex patterns such as fanning, branching and forking within a voxel1. Here, we introduce a multiscale workflow applied to an ex vivo human hemisphere that leverages Hierarchical Phase-Contrast Tomography (HiP-CT) to obtain whole-organ imaging at 20 µm and sub-micron voxels in targeted regions2. Combined with diffusion MRI and micro-CT, the resulting dataset connects fine-scale microstructural features to macroscale patterns of connectivity.

Methods

A fixed human brain hemisphere from a 63-year-old male donor was acquired from Massachussetts General Hospital. The sample was initially examined with mesoscopic diffusion MRI at high angular resolution3 (cf. figure). This was followed by a multi-scale HiP-CT workflow that included a whole-hemisphere scan at 20 µm to generate a global reference volume, as well as higher-resolution scans of selected areas (ROIs) down to the sub-micron range to image fine fiber structure. After these imaging steps, small biopsies were taken, osmium-stained, and imaged with micro-CT to visualise detailed cytoarchitecture. HiP-CT served as the spatial reference supporting consistent multiscale analysis.

Results

HiP-CT generated a high-fidelity hemisphere volume with preserved geometry, clear delineation of cortical layers and major white-matter bundles. Higher-resolution ROIs revealed distinct fascicles and fiber groupings, enabling visualization of local curvature, dispersion, and fine-scale branching patterns. Diffusion MRI tractography showed broad correspondence with large-scale HiP-CT pathways, with observable discrepancies in regions containing complex crossings, reflecting known limitations of dMRI crossing-fiber models1. Micro-CT biopsies closely matched HiP-CT at comparable resolutions, supporting the validity of HiP-CT contrast mechanisms. Collectively, the integrated modalities established a continuous multiscale framework connecting microscopic axonal features with mesoscopic pathways and whole-brain organization, allowing direct examination of how local structure influences global connectivity.

Conclusion

By establishing a unified cross-scale workflow, this approach shows that HiP-CT can link cellular features with macroscale brain organization in an intact hemisphere. When combined with diffusion MRI and micro-CT, it delivers a coherent, distortion-free basis for evaluating connectivity across spatial scales. The results indicate that HiP-CT offers a practical, high-throughput option for comprehensive whole-brain connectomic mapping.
