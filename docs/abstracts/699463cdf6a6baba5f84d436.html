Introduction

While functional brain connectomes are highly stable within individuals, they can simultaneously vary with cognitive states such as what task a person is performing and how attentive they are. But what about other biological processes occurring in the body? In individuals with menstrual cycles, female sex hormones (estradiol, progesterone, FSH and LH) vary across the approximately 28-day menstrual cycle and correspond with changes in functional connectivity.1 Beyond naturally produced hormones, exogenous hormones can also be introduced through oral contraceptives. Previous work has shown that within a single individual, functional connectivity predicts oral contraceptive (OC) use and the predictive model generalizes to predict OC use in three other individuals.2 However, it is unknown if these models generalize to predict use of OCs with different formulations in large samples. In this work, we analyze resting-state fMRI data collected from the same participant while using OC and naturally cycling (NC) (nOC=30, nNC=30)1. We investigate what brain networks differ while naturally cycling compared to when taking an OC, and how these networks generalize to predict OC use in other individuals. 

Methods

Datasets: There are three publicly available data sets used in this work. The first is the 28andMe dataset1 which includes data from a single individual scanned every day for 30 days while naturally cycling, and then again a year later for 30 days while taking oral contraceptives. The data used here is 10 minutes of resting-state fMRI (TR=720ms, voxel size=2mm3). Each scan was accompanied by a blood draw to test sex hormone levels. The second data set is the Human Connectome Project3 Young Adult sample. After excluding anyone who did not report gender as female, reported an endocrine disorder or had missing endocrine data, we used a sample of 353 subjects (average age = 29.5, 102 OC users, 251 not). Here we used two 15-minute resting-state scans (one in each encoding direction, see functional connectivity description below) (TR = 3200ms, voxel size = 0.7mm3). Each participant self-reported OC use and days since cycle start. The third data set is from Wang et al.4 53 women with regular menstrual cycles (average age= 22.7 years, all naturally cycling), completed 8-minute resting-state scans (TR=2000ms, voxel size=3mm3). Each subject gave a saliva sample before each scan to test estradiol and progesterone levels.  

Preprocessing and Functional Connectivity: The minimally preprocessed HCP data were used in this analysis. The 28andMe and Wang et al. data were preprocessed to match the steps in the HCP minimal preprocessing pipeline. Data were initially preprocessed using fmriprep, followed by registration to MNI space, regressing 6 motion parameters, white matter, CSF, and global signal, and all their squares, derivatives, and derivatives squared, and high-pass filtered above 0.0005Hz. All preprocessed data were parcellated using the Shen 268-node atlas, and functional connectivity was calculated using 3dNetCorr in AFNI. For the HCP data where there were scans in both encoding directions, the RL and LR functional connectivity matrices were averaged together to produce the final matrix. 

Oral contraceptive use network calculation: Networks were identified using two methods: Connectome-Based Predictive Modeling (CPM) and Network Based Statistics (NBS).5 Connectome-based predictive models were built to predict OC use in the 28andMe dataset. We performed 1000 iterations of stratified 10-fold cross-validation to identify edges that were significantly (p < 0.01) related to OC use using point-biserial correlation. Edges stronger during the OC use month become the ‘Oral Contraceptive (OC) Network’ and edges stronger during the naturally cycling month become the ‘Naturally Cycling (NC) Network’.

Predicting oral contraceptive use within subject: For each scan we take the difference in network strength (mean functional connectivity) between the OC Network and the NC Network. This is converted to a prediction by thresholding at 0.5. Accuracy is measured as percent correct. Significance was determined by comparing to 10,000 phase randomized null permutations. This is important because autocorrelation in the prediction target can artificially increase the prediction accuracy. 

Generalization across individuals: For each scan in the external datasets we take the difference in network strength between the OC Network and the NC Network. Only edges that were significant in all iterations and folds during model training in the 28andMe dataset were included in the final networks used for cross-dataset validation. 

Results

Both CPM and NBS identify widely distributed sets of functional connections that reflect oral contraceptive use within an individual (Figure 1a,b). Additionally, the two methods identify very similar networks (Figure 1c) whose strength is correlated with one another (Figure 1d). Importantly, these networks significantly predict oral contraceptive use within an individual (Figure 1e) with an average of 98% correct classification across 1000 iterations. 

While these within-subject findings demonstrate the brain-wide relationship between hormones and functional brain organization, they do not give insight into whether these effects are shared across people or idiosyncratic to individuals. To test this, we applied the models defined in 28andMe to two independent datasets. We found that the model identified from within-subject 28andMe data did not generalize to predict OC use in the Human Connectome Project dataset. We see no significant difference in OC, NC or OC - NC network strength between individuals who self-report taking oral contraceptives and those who do not (Figure 1f). 

We assessed generalizability to a second dataset, shared by Wang et al., that only included naturally cycling individuals. To do so, we tested whether the distribution of OC - NC network strength values in this sample was more similar to the NC or OC scans from 28andMe. If the model generalized, we would expect to see those network strengths closer to 28andMe’s average naturally cycling value. We see a small but statistically significant effect such that network strength is more similar to the NC than OC patterns in 28andMe (CPM: T=4.203 p<0.001; NBS: T=8.802, p<0.001), providing tentative evidence for generalization. (Figure 1g). 

Conclusion

In this work we identified networks in the brain that reflect oral contraceptive use within an individual using CPM and NBS. CPM and NBS are both widely used to identify networks that vary widely across people and conditions, but include different methods to control for false-positives (CPM relies on cross-validation, while NBS uses a cluster-based approach). Despite these differences, the networks identified by both methods are very similar (97% of edges are shared), which supports their robustness. Overall, in line with previous work2, we find that oral contraceptive use is reflected in edges across all networks in the brain, reflecting the widespread relevance of hormones in brain function. 

Despite the success of the network models within subject, the models fail to generalize to predict OC use in other individuals. In HCP, we do not see any significant differences in predictive network strength between OC and non-OC users, for either the CPM or NBS model. While this null result may suggest a lack of generalizability and idiosyncratic effects of female sex hormones on brain organization, it may also reflect differences in effects of OCs with different formulations and/or the limitations of self-report. In HCP, participants were asked ‘Do you take oral contraceptives’ with almost all participants only answering with ‘yes’ or ‘no’. This means that there is no further detail on which formulation of oral contraceptives they take, or if they use any other types of hormonal contraceptives. In the Wang et al. dataset, the network strengths show some evidence of generalizability, however without a comparison group of OC users the result is challenging to interpret. 

Overall, these findings motivate the use of precision imaging approaches to understand the relationship between hormones and the brain. More studies focusing on topics related to women's health will improve our understanding of these dynamics, and more broadly, neuroimaging studies should include questions about endocrine state to create more relevant datasets to approach these questions. Continuing to develop the data and tools to ask complex questions is crucial to our understanding of the human brain.
