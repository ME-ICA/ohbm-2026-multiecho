Introduction

Decision-making (DM) and inhibition deficits have been consistently reported among individuals with a history of suicide attempt(s) (SA)1, 2. While suicidal behavior can be understood as an outcome of altered decision-making processes, deficient inhibition may play a crucial role in the transition from suicidal ideations to behavior. These cognitive deficits may reflect dysfunction in neural networks, particularly disruptions in structural and functional connectivity among brain regions supporting decision-making and inhibitory subprocesses. However, our understanding of the underlying neurobiological mechanisms remains limited. Findings from previous DTI studies in the SA have been largely inconsistent3. The variability in the results might be due to the heterogenity and small size of the samples as well as the methods applied for preprocessing DTI data and extracting the white matter tracts. We therefore investigated potential alterations in white matter tracts associated with decision-making and inhibition deficits in suicide attempters. To overcome methodological issues, we employed TractSeg4, a deep-learning-based tool that performs fast and accurate subject-specific white matter tract segmentation in individual native space. Following tract segmentation, we applied tractometry5, which samples diffusion metrics (e.g., FA) along the length of each tract—typically divided into 100 equidistant segments, enabling the detection and localization of microstructural alterations at specific tract locations, rather than relying solely on mean values across the entire tract. 

Methods

49 suicide attempters, 34 individuals diagnosed with Major Depression with no previous suicide attempt (PC) and 49 healthy individuals (HC) with no previous suicide attempt or mental illness were examined in the present study at two sites (Jena, Germany and Nimes, France). We examined alterations in structural connectivity along the striato-fronto-orbital (ST-FO) tract, comprising fibers that originate from the caudate nucleus and putamen and project to the orbitofrontal (OFC) and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC); the superior longitudinal fasciculus II (SLF II), which connects parieto-occipital regions with the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC); and the cingulum bundle, which links the cingulate gyrus with the DLPFC, OFC, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, and premotor areas. Neurocognitive performance was assessed using the Iowa Gambling (IGT) and Go/No-Go tasks.

All imaging data were collected on a 3T whole-body system equipped with a 64-element head matrix coil. DTI data were acquired with both AP (anterior‒posterior) and PA (posterior‒anterior) phase-encoding directions to correct for susceptibility artifacts. A total of 96 contiguous transverse slices with 1.5 mm thickness were obtained (TR = 3318 ms, TE = 87.40 ms, flip angle = 78°, multiband factor = 4) that covered the entire brain, including the lower brainstem. The matrix size was 140 × 140 pixels with an in-plane resolution of 1.5 × 1.5 mm2 (FOV (field of view) = 210 × 210 mm2, b value = 2500 s/mm2, and 103 collinear directions with 8 non-weighted b0 images. 

White matter tracts were identified and segmented in subject space through TractSeg’s convolutional neural network–based approach, and corresponding tract orientation maps and endpoint segmentations were generated. The MNI-registered diffusion scalar maps were then projected onto these tract masks to compute the diffusion values along each tract. Then, tractometry was used to resample all streamlines to ensure equal segment numbers across tracts. The FA and RD profiles were then evaluated at 100 equidistant points along each tract's trajectory. The values from the first and last segments were automatically removed. For each tract, we extracted 98 FA and RD values and used them in an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to examine group differences. ANCOVA was conducted separately for each tract segment, with FA or RD as the dependent variable, group (SA vs. PC vs. HC) as the independent variable, and age and sex as covariates. The FDR method was applied to correct for multiple comparisons. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess correlations between FA/RD values and relevant neurocognitive and clinical measures.

Results

SA showed pronounced alterations in the right striato-fronto-orbital (ST-FO) compared to control groups, with elevated fractional anisotropy (FA) in central and reduced FA in anterior segments; similar patterns were observed for radial diffusivity (Figure 1). Bilateral RD and left ST-FO FA values correlated significantly with IGT performance.  A significant group effect was also observed in the left SLF-II tract. FA differences were localized to the posterior and anterior portions of the tract. A post hoc test revealed lower FA in the SA and PC groups than in the HC group, with no significant difference between the SA and PC groups. No significant effects were found in the cingulum bundle. 

Conclusion

Altered decision-making might stem from white matter microstructural changes in SA. This first tractometry study in SA provides novel evidence of orbitofronto-striatal white matter alterations linked to cognitive deficits relevant to suicidal behavior. WM integrity of the ST-FO tract was linked to performance on the IGT task. Such alterations may contribute to deficits in decision-making and motor inhibition in SA.
