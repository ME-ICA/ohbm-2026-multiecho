Introduction

Population receptive field (pRF) mapping is a fundamental approach for quantifying retinotopic organisation in the human visual cortex. As proposed by (Dumoulin & Wandell, 2008), the pRF mapping technique works by identifying the best-fitting pRF parameters (spatial position (µₓ, µᵧ) and size (σ)) for each voxel. It does so by searching for the parameter combination that produces a model-generated time course that best explains the voxel’s recorded fMRI signal.

Despite its wide adoption, commonly used pRF implementations remain constrained by slow, iterative refinement procedures that make large-scale datasets difficult to analyse. Tools such as mrVista (Dumoulin & Wandell, 2008) rely on repeated General Linear Model (GLM) evaluations, resulting in computation times measured in hours or days. Several faster alternative implementations also exist, including deep-learning (Thielen et al., 2019) or hashing-based approaches (Bhat et al., 2021), but these methods generally require extensive training or sacrifice accuracy for speed. 

Here, we introduce GEM-pRF, a highly accurate and computationally efficient implementation for pRF mapping. It is distributed as an open-source Python package (https://pypi.org/project/gemprf/). Our implementation uses a reformulated GLM and follows the same two-stage structure, coarse fitting and refine fitting, as the original approach by (Dumoulin & Wandell, 2008). However, for the coarse-fitting and refine-fitting steps in GEM-pRF, the prediction signals and their derivatives are directly computed and utilised on the GPU side for performance gain. Crucially, we replace iterative refinement with a single-step quadratic approximation, yielding analytic refined estimates that are statistically indistinguishable from iterative optimisation while dramatically reducing runtime. We validate our approach using simulated and empirical data and demonstrate substantial speed gains across datasets of varying sizes on different GPU systems.

Methods

We reformulated the classical GLM approach for pRF mapping by orthogonalising the regressor matrix so that the objective function for coarse fitting reduces to the dot product between a measured fMRI time course and an orthonormalised prediction time course. This transformation allows the entire coarse-fitting stage to be implemented as a matrix multiplication on the GPU. Prediction time courses were generated for dense sampling grids (151×151×16 for simulated data or 187×187×24 for empirical data) while maintaining a spacing of 0.19° between adjacent pRF positions along the spatial axes. Owing to the linearity of convolution, we used a pre-convolved version of the visual stimulus with a canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF) to compute these prediction time courses. All prediction signals were computed and stored on the GPU to avoid CPU–GPU transfer overhead. For each voxel, coarse-fit pRF parameters were obtained by selecting the parameter combination that maximised this dot product.

To eliminate iterative refinement, we computed first-order derivatives of the objective function with respect to the pRF parameters, again on the GPU. For each voxel, we then fitted a quadratic surface to the objective function values in the local neighbourhood around the coarse-fit estimate. Solving this system yielded analytically refined parameters, which were accepted only when they improved the objective function. Because this procedure operates on a small neighbourhood per voxel, it runs efficiently on both GPU and CPU. Figure 1B visualises the agreement between this quadratic refinement and exhaustive iterative optimisation.

To evaluate the accuracy of our methodology using simulated data, we used the prfsynth validation framework (Lerma-Usabiaga et al., 2020) to generate 5000 simulated fMRI time series at three spatial positions (P, Q, R) under low- and high-noise conditions. As the ground-truth pRF parameters were known, this allowed us to assess absolute accuracy. GEM-pRF and mrVista were run using matching HRFs and regressors. Figure 1A shows parameter deviations across noise conditions using boxen-plots.

We next evaluated the software implementation using empirical data. For this, we analysed the V1 region of the visual cortex in the NYU retinotopy dataset (n = 44 participants) (Himmelberg et al., 2021) using mrVista’s prfanalyze-vista docker container (version d9b64480cf1e) and GEM-pRF, with identical sampling spaces, regressors, and HRFs. The pRF estimation results shown in Figure 1B were restricted to plausible ranges for centre, size, and variance explained. 

Lastly, for computational performance analysis, we evaluated runtimes on an RTX 3050 Ti laptop GPU and a DGX V100 HPC system containing four NVIDIA GPUs. Datasets ranged from 5k to 100k voxels, and prediction signals were initialised once and reused. Figure 2 displays the computational times achieved by different configurations across datasets of varying sizes.

Results

Figure 1A shows the comparison of GEM-pRF with mrVista using simulated data. Under low-noise conditions, both tools estimated spatial position and size accurately across all eccentricities. Under high noise, variance increased, and both methods exhibited a peripheral bias, particularly at the most eccentric location (R), consistent with previous benchmarking studies. Both GEM-pRF and mrVista underestimated pRF size (σ) for low-SNR voxels, but the two methods behaved similarly and produced comparable goodness-of-fit. Figure 1A summarises these error distributions.

Figure 1B depicts the comparison of GEM-pRF with a state-of-the-art exhaustive iterative refinement approach. Across V1 voxels in the NYU dataset, the analytic quadratic refinement method produced estimates nearly identical to iterative refinement. Deviations in pRF parameters (µₓ, µᵧ, σ) and variance explained (ρ²) clustered tightly along the identity line. Correlation coefficients were extremely high (r_µₓ = 0.99, r_µᵧ = 1.00, r_σ = 0.97, r_ρ² = 1.00), confirming that a single analytic step can recover essentially the same solution as iterative minimisation. Minor deviations appeared only when the spatial grid spacing was artificially widened, consistent with expectations for a local quadratic approximation.

Finally, our computational performance analysis shows a gain of almost two orders of magnitude compared with the popular mrVista implementation. Figure 2 shows that GEM-pRF scaled nearly linearly with dataset size. For 100k voxels:
 • mrVista required ~3 hours on a multi-GPU HPC system.
 • GEM-pRF required ~1–2 minutes (coarse+refine) on the same system.
 • A consumer laptop with RTX 3050 Ti GPU completed coarse-only fitting in ~4 minutes.

Conclusion

We introduce a new software package, called GEM-pRF, for pRF mapping. It is open-sourced on PyPI and can be easily installed using pip, allowing quick adoption and rapid integration into existing neuroimaging workflows. GEM-pRF uses a novel mathematical reformulation similar to the classical pRF mapping approach to achieve performance gains. By expressing the GLM in a form that allows both predictions and their derivatives to be computed and used directly on the GPU, and by replacing iterative refinement with a closed-form quadratic solution, GEM-pRF achieves substantial speed improvements without sacrificing estimation accuracy. The method replicates the behaviour of state-of-the-art tools such as mrVista on both simulated and empirical datasets, showing extremely high voxel-wise correspondence.

The ability to analyse 100,000 voxels in roughly 1-2 minutes fundamentally changes the practical scale of pRF studies. Large datasets such as HCP or multi-session clinical cohorts become feasible to process repeatedly, explore alternative stimuli or HRFs, and perform large parameter sweeps. Such tasks would be prohibitively slow using iterative CPU-based pipelines. The modular design further supports the integration of alternative pRF models (e.g., DoG) and future CUDA-level optimisations. 

Figure 1 demonstrates the methodological validity of the quadratic refinement and the accuracy of GEM-pRF relative to mrVista, while Figure 2 highlights the substantial runtime gains across hardware configurations. Together, these results position GEM-pRF as the first GPU-accelerated, iteration-free, GLM-based pRF mapping framework capable of delivering high-precision results at modern neuroimaging scales.
