Introduction

According to Carroll’s Three-Stratum Theory of Intelligence, spatial reasoning is the ability to mentally process the structure and orientation of objects by transforming their parts or rotating the whole object to understand relationships, equivalences, or spatial changes in everyday nonverbal tasks. Spatial cognition is a heterogeneous construct composed of several factors, the first two within the domain of visual perception are spatial relations and visualization.1

Fehringer developed the R-Cube-SR Test to assess spatial relations, also known as mental rotation, defined as the ability to perceive spatial patterns and maintain an object’s orientation in space, typically by rotating it mentally as a whole. This is a rapid and simple process, characteristic of speed tests, in which participants judge if one object can be obtained from another through complete 3D rotations.2

In addition, Fehringer created the R-Cube-Vis Test to measure visualization, defined as the ability to mentally manipulate the internal image of a spatial pattern by modifying the configuration of its parts to form a new arrangement. This ability requires sequential and complex transformations and is assessed with power tests because it requires deeper cognitive processing.3

To date, no studies have differentiated which brain areas are specifically involved in visualization and spatial relations. However, a recent meta-analysis identified a crucial network activated during mental rotation, involving the superior parietal lobes, insular cortex, inferior temporal lobes, left primary motor cortex, right supplementary motor areas, and right dorsal premotor cortex.4

The aim of this research is to address this knowledge gap by providing the first fMRI evidence of differential brain activation for spatial relations and visualization. We expect spatial relations to elicit the areas previously linked to mental rotation, and visualization to produce a broader and more complex activation pattern.

Methods

PARTICIPANTS

The sample was conformed by 49 healthy right-handed participants (28 women; mean [SD] age = 21.14 [2.57] years). Referring to their studies, 30.6% of the participants came from Social Sciences bachelor’s degrees, 38.8% from Healthy Sciences, and 30.6% from Science and Technology. The sample was recruited from Universitat Jaume I students through advertisements on the university campus. The experiment was approved by the Universitat Jaume I ethics committee. They all gave their prior informed consent and were economically remunerated.

CUBE-SR-VIS TASK 

During the fMRI session, participants performed the Cube-SR-VIS Task, a task conformed by items from R-Cube-SR Test2 and R-Cube-Vis Test3. Cube-SR-VIS Task consists on present several pairs of cubes and ask the participant whether the cube on the left can be transformed into the cube on the right by rotating the entire cube or rotating one element (side) of the cube. The pair of cubes has six different colors, but the participant can only see three of their faces. The participants were instructed to respond “same cube” by pressing a button with the right index finger and “different cubes” with the left index finger. 

The Cube-SR-VIS Task is formed by 3 conditions: control stimuli (CTRL), spatial relations stimuli (SR), and visualization stimuli (VIS) (Figure 1). Each condition is presented during 12 blocks, making a total of 36 blocks. Each condition was created by two types of cube pairs. CTRL was created by 12 pairs of identical cubes with 0º of rotation and 12 pairs of completely different cubes (a plain cube and a pattern cube). SR was created by 12 pairs of the same cube (one of them rotated 90º on any axis) and 12 pairs of different cubes (because cannot be the same rotated cube). VIS was created by 12 pairs of the same cube (one of them with a 90º rotated side on any direction) and 12 pairs of different cubes (because cannot be the same cube with a rotated side).

Therefore, Cube-SR-VIS Task was formed by 72 trials, every trial was presented during 7700 ms (with 300 ms between each trial). The whole task lasts 576 seconds. The task was programmed and presented using E-Prime 3.0 (Psychological Software Tools Inc). Before the brain acquisition session, participants trained for 3 minutes outside the MRI scan a practice trial. 

fMRI ACQUISITION 

Structural and functional MRI was collected with a 3T scanner (SIGNA Architect, GE Healthcare) and a 24-channel head coil. High-resolution anatomical images were acquired with T1 MP-RAGE sequence (1 mm of slice thickness; TR = 2.5 s; TE = 2 ms; FA = 8°). Functional T2*-weighted data was acquired with multiband gradient echo-planar imaging sequence (50 axial slices collected in ascendent interleaved order; 288 volumes; 2.5 mm of slice thickness; TR = 2 s; TE = 20.4 ms; FA = 90°; multiband acceleration factor = 2).

DATA PREPROCESSING AND fMRI ANALYSES

Neuroimaging preprocessing and statistical analyses were performed using SPM25 in MATLAB. Functional images were aligned to the AC-PC plane using each participant’s anatomical image, followed by standard preprocessing steps: motion correction through realignment and reslicing, coregistration of anatomical to mean functional images, anatomical segmentation, normalization to MNI space using segmentation-derived deformation fields, and spatial smoothing with a 6 mm FWHM isotropic Gaussian kernel.

Statistical analyses included first-level modeling of the SR>CTRL and VIS>CTRL contrasts, with the BOLD response estimated by convolving stimulus onsets with the canonical hemodynamic response function and including six motion parameters as covariates. At the second level, whole-brain one-sample t-tests identified task-related activations for each condition, and a paired t-test compared conditions (SR>VIS; VIS>SR). Significance was set at p<0.05 using cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction, with a voxel-level threshold of p<0.001 with a critical cluster size.

Results

BEHAVIORAL RESULTS

Regarding the behavioral results, descriptive statistics for the Cube-SR-VIS Task were as follows: For the response accuracy, out of 24 stimuli per condition (control, spatial relations, and visualization), participants showed a mean [SD] of 23.53 [0.915] hits for CTRL, 21.51 [2.073] hits for SR, and 22.24 [1.493] hits for VIS. For response times (RT), participants showed a mean [SD] of 1903 [637] ms for CTRL stimuli, 4024 [644] ms for SR, and 3157 [650] ms for VIS.

BRAIN RESULTS

A whole brain one-sample t-test analysis showed that spatial relations effects (SR > CTRL) had larger brain bilateral activation in the middle and inferior occipital gyrus, lingual gyrus, calcarine sulcus, precuneus, inferior and superior parietal lobule, postcentral gyrus, fusiform gyrus, thalamus, insula, lentiform nucleus, caudate, and inferior and middle frontal gyrus (see Figure 2a).

A whole brain one-sample t-test analysis showed that visualization effects (VIS > CTRL) had larger brain bilateral activation covering the middle, inferior and superior occipital gyrus, lingual gyrus, precuneus, inferior and superior parietal lobule, postcentral gyrus, fusiform gyrus, inferior temporal gyrus, middle and inferior frontal gyrus, and thalamus (Figure 2b).

Regarding the paired sample t-test contrast (SR > VIS), we found that spatial relations showed larger activity against visualization covering the bilateral cuneus, superior occipital lobule, anterior cingulate gyrus, superior frontal gyrus, lentiform nucleus, and insula. Additionally, a left-lateralized activation was observed, involving calcarine sulcus, lingual gyrus, inferior parietal lobule, postcentral gyrus, and inferior frontal gyrus (Figure 2c).

In the inverse contrast (VIS > SR), we found that visualization showed larger activity against spatial relations covering the bilateral middle occipital gyrus, angular gyrus, inferior parietal lobule, middle and posterior cingulate gyrus, and middle and superior temporal gyrus. Lateralized activation was observed in the left superior medial frontal gyrus, right postcentral gyrus, and right supramarginal gyrus (Figure 2d).

Conclusion

Although extensive fMRI evidence has identified the neural underpinnings of mental rotation, no study has examined the visualization ability. Our findings provide the first evidence that visualization recruits different neural mechanisms than those involved in spatial relations.

On the one hand, the contrast between rotating the whole cube vs. not rotating it (SR>CTRL) showed increased activation in bilateral occipital lobes (including lingual gyri and calcarine sulci), parietal lobes and postcentral gyri, frontal lobes, precuneus, fusiform gyri, insulas, lentiform nuclei, caudate, and thalamus. When directly comparing the two abilities (SR>VIS), spatial relations elicited greater activation in bilateral superior occipital lobes (including cuneus and left calcarine sulcus), left inferior parietal lobule, postcentral and lingual gyri, bilateral superior frontal gyri and left inferior frontal gyrus, bilateral anterior cingulate gyri, insulas, and lentiform nuclei.

Overall, these results largely supported our hypothesis, although not all predictions were fully met. As expected, robust activation was observed in parietal regions and the insular cortex, consistent with their role in spatial processing during mental rotation. Activation in the superior and middle frontal gyri supports the involvement of premotor and right supplementary motor areas during spatial relations, reflecting motor planning processes, although no clear activation was found in the left primary motor cortex. These findings align with fronto-parietal networks commonly reported in mental rotation, encompassing covert motor rotation, visuospatial processing, and high-level object recognition.4

On the other hand, the contrast between rotating parts of the cube vs. no rotation (VIS>CTRL) revealed a broader activation pattern including bilateral occipital lobes and lingual gyri, parietal lobes, postcentral gyri, precuneus, inferior temporal and fusiform gyri, frontal regions, and the thalamus. When visualization was directly compared to spatial relations (VIS>SR), greater activation emerged in bilateral middle occipital gyri, inferior parietal lobes, angular gyri, posterior cingulate, temporal regions, left superior medial frontal gyrus, and right postcentral and supramarginal gyri.

Consistent with our hypothesis, visualization engaged more complex and distributed neural resources, reflecting its cognitively demanding nature and reliance on sequential and anticipatory transformations. The combined frontal and temporal activations suggest mechanisms supporting the maintenance and transformation of complex visuospatial representations, while thalamic involvement likely reflects coordination between cortical regions. This profile aligns with recent evidence highlighting the role of fronto-temporo-thalamic circuits in demanding visuospatial working memory tasks, where cognitive load modulates both cortical and subcortical activity.5 Moreover, the activation of the supramarginal gyrus and inferior parietal lobe reflects that spatial manipulation in the visualization task is linked to the visuospatial processing network of the parietal lobe.4

Despite the novelty of these findings, some limitations should be noted. The Cube-SR-VIS Task requires a dichotomous response (same/different), which may increase correct responses due to chance. Moreover, although participants were instructed to use a specific strategy, they may have relied on alternative strategies. Future studies could use a sequential presentation format, presenting each cube one at a time instead of simultaneously, to reduce reliance on point-by-point comparison strategies. Additionally, training these abilities with a gamified interactive tool may lead to more efficient brain activation.

In conclusion, spatial relations primarily engaged parietal-frontal networks associated with mental rotation, whereas visualization recruited a more distributed set of regions, consistent with its greater cognitive complexity.
