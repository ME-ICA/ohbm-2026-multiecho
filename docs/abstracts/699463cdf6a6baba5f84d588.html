Introduction

Simultaneous EEG-fMRI at ultra-high field (e.g.,7 T) provides high temporal and spatial resolution for non-invasive investigation of brain function. However, EEG recordings at 7 T are strongly affected by the ballistocardiogram (BCG) artifact, caused by heartbeat-induced motion in the static magnetic field. A common mitigation strategy adds artifact detection loops (ADLs) to the EEG cap to directly record the artifact.1 While effective at lower field strengths, ADLs have not been optimized for 7 T, where shorter RF wavelengths can lead to substantial RF field perturbations that may degrade image quality (Figure 1a) and compromise safety. This study uses electromagnetic field (EM) simulations and experimental measurements to assess the RF effects of ADLs with varying geometries and materials.

Methods

EM Simulations

EM simulations were performed using Sim4Life 8.0 (ZMT, Switzerland). A 16-leg high-pass birdcage coil (diameter=305 mm, length=210 mm) driven in circularly polarized mode was simulated and tuned and matched to 50Ω using Optenni (Optenni Lab, Finland) loaded with the SAM phantom(εr = 45.3 ,σ = 0.87 S/m, ZMT, Switzerland). A model of four ADLs was built based on a 7 T prototype cap (BrainCap-MR7FLEX, Brain Products GmbH), with wire circumferences (21 – 34 cm) corresponding to 50 – 120% of the original loop circumference (28 cm). Two materials were simulated: carbon fibre(σ = 6.3 x 104 S/m) and resistive polymer(5.6 S/m). Figure 1(b) depicts the simulation model. A simulation with no loops (phantom and RF coil only) was run to establish the unperturbed B1+ and SAR10g values.

Simulated B1+ field and SAR10g maps were normalised to 1 W input power, masked to the phantom, and interpolated to 1 mm isotropic resolution. Maximum-intensity projection (MIP) B1+ and SAR10g maps were computed in three planes, and MIP difference maps were computed relative to the phantom-only case to visualise ADL-induced changes.

Empirical Data
For experimental validation, two EEG caps were constructed — one incorporating carbon ADLs (Cap 1) and the other polymer ADLs (Cap 2), each with loop length = 28cm —and tested in vivo. data were acquired in 10 healthy volunteers (N = 6 for Cap 1, N = 4 for Cap 2) on a single-transmit 7 T system (MAGNETOM Terra, Siemens Healthcare). Cap-on/cap-off  maps were coregistered, segmented (SPM12), and compared to assess field homogeneity and potential RF shielding effects.
 
EEG measurements were obtained using a 32-channel 7T prototype cap with four ADLs and 5 kΩ segmenting resistors (BrainCap-MR7FLEX, Brain Products GmbH) in the static field. EEG data quality was evaluated using an alpha-blocking eyes-closed/eyes-open paradigm,2 comparing both caps in the same volunteer. The participant alternated between eyes-closed and eyes-open for 3 x 30 s blocks. Datasets were bandpass filtered between 0.5 – 70 Hz, segmented into 1 s epochs, FFT-transformed, and averaged within each condition.

Results

Figure 2(a) presents the B1+ MIP maps across loop lengths and materials for carbon-wire and resistive-polymer ADLs. In the carbon-wire case, RF interactions with the field increased with loop length, peaking at 70 – 80% of the original loop circumference (23 – 26 cm), before decreasing again. At these lengths, the loop wire approached approximately λ/4, corresponding to the RF wavelength in air at 7 T (∼ 100 cm), matching resonance conditions previously reported for EEG cable bundles.3 In contrast, minimal B1+ perturbations were observed for the higher-resistance polymer ADLs, irrespective of loop length.
 
Figure 2(b) shows the SAR10g MIP-difference maps, which mirrored the B1+trends. In the carbon-wire case, resonance effects at 70 – 80% loop length led to substantial increases in SAR10g, whereas the polymer material showed no such increase due to its resistive damping properties. SAR10g values for resonant carbon-wire lengths increased by approximately 21–26% compared to the unperturbed phantom-only case, while polymer ADLs remained stable across all loop sizes.
 
In vivo maps B1+ (Figure 2(b)) demonstrated similar field distributions for both caps relative to cap-off conditions, though Cap 1 (carbon) exhibited slightly greater RF shielding. EEG recordings (Figure 2(d)) acquired in the scanner showed clear occipital alpha power (8 – 13 Hz) increases during eyes-closed compared to eyes-open blocks, consistent with typical alpha blocking. Comparable results were seen across caps, indicating that both ADL materials provided signals suitable for EEG artefact correction during 7 T EPI acquisition. EEG time-course traces demonstrated characteristic alpha oscillations highly similar to recordings outside the scanner.

Conclusion

Overall, carbon-wire ADLs were more susceptible to RF coupling, with resonance observed at λ/4λ/4 loop lengths, consistent with prior reports.3 The polymer material, with lower conductivity, exhibited minimal RF interaction across all geometries. These findings indicate that moderately resistive ADLs (e.g., carbon) should avoid resonant loop lengths to limit RF interactions, whereas highly resistive polymer ADLs provide improved RF safety without compromising EEG performance. This supports polymer-based ADL designs as a promising strategy for safe, high-quality simultaneous EEG-fMRI at 7T.
