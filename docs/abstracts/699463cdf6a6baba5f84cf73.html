Introduction

During the early postnatal period, the infant brain undergoes rapid development, characterized by increasing size, evolving tissue contrast, dynamic cortical folding patterns, and accelerated myelination1. Infant brain MRI processing is complicated by the isointense stage, during which gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) exhibit overlapping signal intensities, and is further challenged by partial volume effects due to the small brain size. These factors make infant structural MRI processing particularly challenging. While several methods exist for processing infant brain MRI2 (e.g., Infant FreeSurfer, iBEAT, NiBabies, and BIBSnet), these tools typically provide limited functionalities, primarily restricted to tissue segmentation and cortical surface reconstruction. Consequently, they are insufficient for downstream applications requiring regional labels, cortical surface parcellations, and spatially normalized neuroimaging data. To address these limitations, we developed “BabyS2R”, an integrated framework that incorporates brain segmentation into 210 regions, cortical surface reconstruction and parcellation, surface–volume deformable registration, group template construction, and quantitative measurement of regional volumes and vertex-level surface attributes. BabyS2R serves as a comprehensive tool for the research community, facilitating infant brain mapping and enabling the full utilization of emerging large-scale datasets such as the HEALthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) study3.

Methods

Our BabyS2R integrates four modules into a unified framework (Fig. 1): (a) brain MRI segmentation; (b) cortical surface processing; (c) spatial normalization; and (d) feature extraction. We describe each module in detail.

Brain MRI Segmentation: We developed an AI-powered brainwide segmentation method that can accurately parcellate the infant brain into 210 brain regions (Fig. 1a). We designed two-staged human-in-the-loop segmentation networks with nnU-Net4 as the backbone. We trained a total of five segmentation networks dedicated for labeling (i) whole-brain tissues, ventricles, and the subcortex; (ii) GM regions; (iii) WM regions; (iv) cerebellar lobules and vermis subdivisions; and (v) brainstem regions. For the first stage of training, the ground truth labels were generated by manually correcting the noisy automatic labels derived using NiBabies and DrawEM for whole-brain tissues, CerebNet for cerebellar lobules, atlas based label transfer for GM and WM regions, and clustering based on principal diffusion directions for the brainstem regions. Moreover, we manually annotated the subcortex and ventricles. In the second stage, the predicted labels from the trained models were manually refined to retrain the networks. To improve model generalizability, training data were augmented with synthetic images obtained via SynthSeg.

Cortical Surface Processing: Using the WM and GM segmentations, white and pial cortical surfaces of both hemispheres are reconstructed via MIRTK deformable surface model (Fig. 1b). The reconstructed cortical surfaces are registered via Spherical Demons to infant brain atlases1 to establish vertex-wise correspondences. The cortical surfaces are then parcellated into Desikan-Killiany regions and Schaefer's large-scale brain networks by mapping the labels from month-specific infant brain atlases1. The cortical surfaces are mapped to the unit sphere, allowing visualization and manipulation of cortical attributes (including mean curvature, average convexity, and cortical thickness) in the spherical space.

Spatial Normalization: This module performs concurrent spatial normalization of the brain volumes and cortical surfaces using the surface-constrained dynamic elasticity model (SCDEM5; Fig. 1c). Given the poor tissues contrast in infant MRIs, the registration method uses tissue maps instead of intensity images and cortical surfaces as fixed and moving data inputs. The estimated nonlinear deformation is then applied to brain MRIs, tissue maps, and cortical surfaces to consistently map the moving data into fixed space. Another functionality of this module is to construct group average template by registering the population data to a reference space, followed by computing the weighted average of the spatially normalized brain MRIs and cortical surfaces. The generated group template consolidates MRIs and cortical surfaces in a unified coordinate space, facilitating the concurrent analysis of volumetric and surface development.

Feature Extraction: Finally, BabyS2R computes region-specific volumes and vertex-wise cortical surface attributes (Fig. 1d). These imaging phenotypes can be further used for normative growth analyses and identification of atypical development. Thus, our infant-focused computational tool can facilitate comprehensive brain characterization.

Results

We processed 578 T2-weighted (T2w) MRIs acquired between birth and 9 months in the HBCD dataset. BabyS2R accurately and consistently segments brain MRIs into 210 regions, i.e., 9 supratentorial and infratentorial brain tissues, 21 subcortical structures including ventricles, 68 GM regions, 82 WM regions, 27 cerebellar lobules and vermis subdivisions, and 3 brainstem regions (Fig. 2a); reconstructs cortical surfaces without topological defects (Euler number = 2); and precisely parcellates cortical surfaces into Desikan-Killiany regions and Schaefer brain networks (somatomotor, visual, dorsal attention, salience attention, default mode, limbic, and control). These results confirm that our tool is robust to signal intensity changes and can adapt to dynamic morphological development during infancy. We registered infant MRIs and cortical surfaces to a common space and then warped the lables of whole-brain tissues and subcortical structures with the estimated deformation field. We quantified registration performance using Dice ratio computed between the warped and reference labels. The Dice ratio is consistently high for all the brain regions (Fig. 2b), indicating that our method accommodates nonlinear differences across subjects and accurately aligns the data across infancy. The volumetric plots of select brain regions show that they all increase in size, though at different rates (Fig. 2c). The cerebellum grows the fastest with a percentage increase of 292% during the first 9 months, followed by lateral ventricles (174%), cerebral GM (147%), brainstem (135%), and cerebral WM (123%). The cortical thickness increases by 17% and average convexity (representing coarse-scale gyral and sulcal patterns) increases by 44% during infancy while mean curvature (representing fine-scale gyral and sulcal patterns) decreases by 22% during the same time period (Fig. 2d).

Conclusion

Our computational tool provides an integrated, end-to-end solution for processing the infant structural MRIs. This unified workflow will accelerate large-scale infant brain mapping studies, essential for uncovering developmental mechanisms during early years of human life.
