Introduction

The human brain’s cognitive uniqueness is supported by a specialized network architecture that evolved distinctively from nonhuman primates. However, the specific topological principles and evolutionary trade-offs underlying the divergence between the human brain and that of our closest living relative, the chimpanzee, remain incompletely characterized. While humans possess expanded association cortices, it is unclear how this macro-anatomical expansion translates to network topology. We hypothesize that human brain evolution involved a strategic reorganization prioritizing global information integration. Here, we employed graph theory to perform a systematic comparison of structural covariance networks (SCNs) in humans and chimpanzees to elucidate the trade-offs between global integration, local specialization, and systemic robustness.

Methods

We analyzed T1-weighted MRI data from 46 healthy human adults (HCP dataset) and 46 adult chimpanzees (National Chimpanzee Brain Resource). Images were preprocessed using species-specific FreeSurfer pipelines (Figure 1A). We extracted gray matter volume (GMV) using the Brainnetome Atlas (210 human ROIs) and a homologous chimpanzee atlas (200 ROIs). Group-level SCNs were constructed for each species by calculating partial correlations of GMV across subjects, controlling for age, sex, and intracranial volume1. Graph theoretical metrics were computed across a range of network densities (0.17–0.50). We assessed global integration (global efficiency, path length), local segregation (clustering coefficient, modularity), core organization (rich-club, hubs), and robustness (simulated targeted attacks). Network modules were identified using Louvain community detection. Hubs were defined based on betweenness centrality. Statistical significance of between-species differences was determined using non-parametric bootstrap permutation tests with 10,000 repetitions (Figure 1B).

Results

Humans exhibited significantly higher global efficiency and shorter characteristic path lengths than chimpanzees (p < 0.05), reflecting a topology optimized for widespread information integration2. Conversely, the chimpanzee SCN showed significantly higher modularity and local clustering, indicating a prioritization of local segregation (Figure 2). Modular analysis revealed a striking organizational divergence: chimpanzee modules were anatomically coherent, largely respecting lobar boundaries, whereas human modules exhibited a spatially distributed topology, linking distant cortical regions (Figure 2A). Human network hubs were predominantly located in higher-order association cortices, showing strong overlap with the default mode network (DMN), whereas chimpanzee hubs were concentrated in primary sensory and paralimbic regions3 (Figure 2B). Furthermore, robustness analysis revealed an evolutionary trade-off: while the human network is more globally efficient, it demonstrated significantly greater vulnerability to targeted attacks on hub nodes compared to the more resilient chimpanzee network4 (p < 0.05; Figure 2C, Figure 2D).

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that human brain evolution is defined by a fundamental topological trade-off: a shift from anatomically coherent, locally segregated processing (seen in chimpanzees) to a spatially distributed, globally integrated architecture. This reorganization facilitates the supramodal integration required for complex cognition but comes at the cost of reduced local specialization and increased systemic vulnerability to hub dysfunction. These results provide structural evidence that human cognitive uniqueness is supported by a high-efficiency and high-risk network configuration5.
