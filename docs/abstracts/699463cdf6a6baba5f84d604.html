Introduction

Adverse life events (ALEs) are established risk factors for non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), the intentional damage to one’s own body tissue without suicidal intent. It is currently unknown whether the neuroanatomical correlates of ALEs in youths who engage in NSSI differ from those who do not. Early home and school environments play a critical role in youth brain development and psychopathology, and ALEs are associated with subcortical and cortical abnormalities, including in the amygdala, hippocampus, anterior cingulate (ACC), orbitofrontal cortex and inferior frontal gyrus. We aimed to investigate whether gray matter abnormalities associated with ALEs would predict risk for NSSI, and whether school or family environment would have a protective role in these associations. Understanding these mechanisms could inform novel treatments and preventative efforts for NSSI. Longitudinal psychopathology data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive DevelopmentTM (ABCD) study allowed us to identify youth who developed new onset NSSI between baseline (mean age=10, without NSSI) and year 2 follow-up (Y2), which we termed premorbid NSSI (pNSSI). A prominent neurocircuitry model (Auerbach et al., 2021) highlights the importance of the amygdala, ACC and striatum in NSSI. Thus, we hypothesized associations of ALEs with gray matter in these regions would confer risk for pNSSI, and that these associations would be mediated by parental and school environment variables.

Methods

Participants included N=548 (344 females) from the ABCD study (5.1 data release). This included 274 youths with pNSSI (172 female) and 274 controls individually matched on demographic and clinical features but without pNSSI, all with T1weighted MRI at baseline. As per Breslin et al. (2025) we created an ALE variable which included Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) in conjunction with other early life adversity, which included both youth self-report and parent reported incidences of adverse events. Our family variable - the Parental Monitoring Questionnaire – measured rule-setting, supervision, consequences and monitoring. School environment was measured using the School Risk and Protective Factors Survey, which captures school disengagement, involvement and a positive environment. Importantly, these two scales were not included in the ALE variable composite. Given significantly different pubertal stages in males and females at age 10, and our previous work, we chose to examine males and females separately in our analyses. Subcortical morphometry was performed using FreeSurfer initiated Large Deformation Diffeomorphic Metric Mapping. Associations between ALEs and local variation of subcortical regions of interest (amygdala, hippocampus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, nucleus accumbens and thalamus) in surface-based analyses was performed in SurfStat implemented in MATLAB. Morphometric changes were regressed onto ALEs to localise significant regions of local shape variation, covarying pubertal stage, intracranial volume and MRI manufacturer. Separate models were tested for each ROI. To account for multiple comparisons, random field theory was applied using SurfStat. Subcortical results were significant using family wise error rate correction with additional Bonferroni correction for multiple ROIs. Cortical thickness data was obtained from the ABCD-BIDS Community Collection. This involved different preprocessing and quality assurance checks, resulting in a smaller sample of n=470 (290 female) for the cortical thickness analysis. Vertex-wise cortical thickness was regressed onto ALE separate for males and females, covarying pubertal stage, intracranial volume and MRI manufacturer. We averaged the cortical thickness of clusters positively and negatively associated with ALEs to conduct Structural Equation Models (SEMs), which examined whether measures of parental monitoring or school environment mediated the relationship between subcortical and cortical regions associated with ALEs and trajectory into the NSSI v Control group at Y2. 

Results

For the subcortical analysis, in females, ALEs were associated with outward deformation of the left lateral hippocampus and inward deformation of the right amygdala. In males, ALEs were associated with inward deformation of the left inferior caudate head. In the cortex, in females, ALEs were positively associated with cortical thickness in the left precuneus, ventral temporal lobe and orbitofrontal cortices, and the right inferior frontal gyrus, subgenual, anterior and posterior cingulate. ALEs were also negatively associated with cortical thickness in the left frontal pole, bilateral inferior temporal gyrus and the right cuneus. In males, ALEs were positively associated with cortical thickness in the left inferior temporal and right inferior frontal gyri. ALEs were also negatively associated with the left orbitofrontal cortex. See Figure 1 for subcortical and cortical results. 

SEM models were conducted to test the hypothesis that family and school environment would mediate the relationship between amygdala, ACC and striatum and NSSI risk. Females showed a significant association with ALEs in the amygdala and males in the caudate, so these models were tested. Only females showed an association (positive) with ALEs in the ACC, so cortical thickness was only included in the female mediation model. We elected to include the average of the positive association of cortical thickness with ALEs in females, even though this encompassed more than just the ACC, as these regions may form a structural network. In females, SEMs identified that school engagement negatively mediated the relationship between the amygdala and NSSI group attribution. It appears that the presence of more ALEs negatively impacting the amygdala may be linked to poorer school engagement, a protective factor against NSSI in controls. In other words, amygdala structure associated with adversity in early life may have negative consequences on environmental factors, such as school environment, which increase risk toward NSSI engagement. In males, parental monitoring mediated the relationship between caudate morphometry associated with ALEs and NSSI group. It appears that the presence of more ALEs negatively impacting the caudate may be linked to less parental involvement and/or monitoring, a protective factor against NSSI in controls. In other words, caudate structure associated with adversity in early life may be associated with less parental involvement, which increases risk toward NSSI engagement.

Conclusion

Our findings align with existing research reporting gray matter volume and cortical thickness associations with ALEs in adolescents. Here, we demonstrate that ALEs not only influence cortical and subcortical regions, particularly those involved in emotional learning and memory, as well as motivation, planning and reward, but may be associated with altered environments which may ordinarily protect against the presence of NSSI. Results highlight the importance of adaptive rearing and school environments as potential protective factors for brain changes associated with ALEs, which research often interprets as accelerated maturation. Moreover, it highlights that in the presence of ALEs, youth may experience more challenges within their family and school environments and require a higher level of adaptive involvement to buffer against these effects. Finally, our findings highlight the need for early, continuous and adaptive family and school interventions in addressing the detrimental effects of adversity and buffering against potential NSSI risk.
