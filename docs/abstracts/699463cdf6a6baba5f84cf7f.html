Introduction

Huntington’s disease (HD) is characterized by hyperkinetic symptoms with chorea and dystonia. However, bradykinesia may also occur1. These findings align with the basal ganglia pathological neural degeneration associated with HD: the degeneration of striato-pallidal neurons impairs both the direct and indirect pathways, resulting in the co-existence of both hyperkinetic and hypokinetic symptoms. 

In hypokinetic diseases such as Parkinson’s disease (PD), studies have demonstrated pathological pallidal beta oscillations that directly correlate with the severity of motor symptoms. In dystonia, a hyperkinetic movement disorder, pathological theta oscillations have been found in the pallidum and correlate with dystonic hyperkinetic symptom severity. These distinct oscillatory patterns are recognized as symptom-specific electrophysiological signatures that reflect underlying circuit dysfunction. 

Although the genetic pathology of HD is well documented, the pallidal neural oscillatory pattern remains largely unexplored. Given that HD uniquely presents with both hyperkinetic and hypokinetic symptoms, it is plausible that both theta and beta oscillations may coexist in the pallidum and play distinct roles in the complex motor manifestations of HD. Initial evidence from small cases suggest increases in both theta and beta power in the globus pallidus internus (GPi) of HD patients. 

In this study, we conducted systematic, long-term, video-synchronized pallidal activity recordings during symptom fluctuations of HD patients scheduled for GPi-DBS, representing the largest invasive electrophysiological study in HD to date, and compared these with recordings with PD and dystonia. By integrating clinical assessments with electrophysiological recordings and neuroimaging analyses, we sought to identify whether distinct oscillatory patterns might correspond to the different motor manifestations in HD. 

Methods

Fifteen HD patients scheduled to receive bilateral GPi-DBS implantation participated in the study. In addition to HD patients, 11 PD patients and 17 dystonia patients with GPi-DBS implantation were also included for analysis as “mono-symptomatic” controls. Long-term LFP recording was conducted during DBS lead externalization period in 13 HD patients (except sub14 and sub15) with synchronized video. The EMG activity was recorded in 12 HD patients (except for sub04). During recording, patients were free to daily activities, which could mimic the daily life of patients and capture the symptoms fluctuations. Epochs with voluntary movement were removed, resulting in 36±20 recording epochs per patient. The motor signs in each data epoch were rated from the video using UHDRS-maximal chorea (item 12) and UHDRS-maximal dystonia (item 11). We defined the sum score of these 2 items as modified UHDRS (mUHDRS) to represent the severity of patients’ HD signs in each epoch. We extracted epochs with top three ratings as the HD hyperkinetic epochs, and epochs with bottom three ratings as the HD hypokinetc epochs for further analysis. The hypokinetic symptom was rated based on the synchronized video, EMG activity, and mUHDRS in the selected epochs (Figure1). 

All signals from HD, PD and dystonia were notch filtered to reject the 50 Hz line noise, and down-sampled to 1000Hz. Power spectral density (PSD) of pallidal LFP was computed using short-time Fourier transform of a 1s sliding Hanning window with 50% overlap. Percentage normalized band power of theta (2-8Hz), alpha (8-12Hz), low beta (13-20Hz), high beta (20-30Hz) and gamma (45-60Hz) were extracted for analysis. Top 10% PSD was also visualized in the MNI space.  

To assess indirect pathway activity between hyper- and hypokinetic epochs in HD patients, we calculated the absolute imaginary coherence between GPi and globus pallidum externus (GPe) in each hemisphere2. The coherence value in theta and high beta frequency band was averaged for further analysis. The GPi-connected indirect pathway fibers from subthalamic nucleus (STN) of the human Basal Ganglia Pathway Atlas were used to determine the structural connectivity profiles from individual electrode locations in MNI space. Indirect fibers connecting to the GPi recording regions were identified as those fibers in a given radius of each selected GPi bipolar recording contact. The percentage number of fibers connecting a given hemisphere GPi-LFP contact were then correlated to the theta and high beta band coherence, averaged over the hyper- and hypokinetic epochs.

For direct pathway, GSP-100 fMRI connectome was used to investigate the relationship of GPi theta and high beta oscillatory activity with fMRI resting-state connectivity. Whole-brain functional connectivity maps were generated, using the MNI coordinates for all available pallidal bipolar recording contacts as a seed. For each map, the average fMRI connectivity values with caudate and putamen (representing the direct pathway) as defined in the DBS Tractography Atlas were extracted. The caudate/putamen-GPi fMRI connectivity values were compared to the theta and high beta oscillatory activity, averaged over the hyper- and hypokinetic epochs.

Paired t-test or Wilcoxon rank test was performed to compare the oscillation activity between hyper- and hypokinetic epochs. Correlation analysis were performed using repeated-measure correlation. LME was performed for comparing the oscillatory connectivity in each hemisphere between hyper- and hypokinetic epochs: “oscillation connectivity ~ symptom + (1| patient)”. We also used LME to investigate the associations between dMRI/fMRI-based connectivity with the average oscillation connectivity/activity values in each hemisphere: “dMRI/fMRI connectivity ~ oscillation connectivity/activity + (1 | patient)”. Two-tailed P-values < 0.05 were considered significant, and multiple comparisons were controlled using the Bonferroni correction when necessary.

Results

Our intraoperative recording revealed a clear power increase in theta/alpha band (2-13 Hz) and beta frequency band (13-30 Hz), corroborating with previous studies3-5 (Figure1A-C). In long-term, video synchonized LFP recordings, patients’ HD signs showed fluctuation between hyperkinesia and hypokinesia, and we found significant differences in both the mUHDRS (P < 0.0001), EMG activity (P = 0.021) and hypokinetic rating (P < 0.0001) between the hyperkinetic and hypokinetic epochs (Figure1D-G). These clear behavioral differences thus lead us to investigate the pathological pallidal neural activity underlying these distinct clinical manifestations.

We found that theta power (PBonf = 0.003) was significantly increased in hyperkinetic epochs, compared to hypokinetic epochs. High beta power (PBonf = 0.006) showed a marked increase in hypokinetic epochs. Time-frequency analysis further confirmed this symptom-related modulation at the single subject level. Other frequency bands not different, and we thus focus on theta and high beta frequency band in the subsequent analysis. Correlation analysis found a strong positive correlation between theta power and mUHDRS (R = 0.74, PBonf = 0.01), indicating that higher theta power associated with more severe hyperkinetic symptoms. Likewise, we observed a strong positive correlation between high beta power and hypokinetic rating (R = 0.62, PBonf = 0.046), suggesting that high beta power increased as hypokinetic symptoms became dominance. More importantly, theta and high beta was not modulated by active movement, suggesting that they reflected the pathophysiology of HD (Figure2A-H). 

Next, we compared our findings with two control groups: PD and dystonia. Unlike the dual oscillatory pattern in HD, PD patients showed modulation only in low beta power between severe and mild hypokinetic symptoms (P = 0.038), with no changes in theta (P = 0.24) or high beta (P = 0.13). Similarly, dystonia patients exhibited only theta power modulation with symptom severity (P = 0.01), while high beta remained unchanged (P = 0.58). This suggest that single oscillatory patterns were modulated in movement disorders with predominantly hyperkinetic or hypokinetic symptoms, only HD exhibited modulation of both theta and high beta power corresponding to its dual symptom manifestation (Figure2IJ).

Spatial analysis revealed that the top theta power distribution was localized in the posterior of GPi, while sites with top high beta power were found in the central region. Notably, HD theta oscillation hotspots closely resembled those previously reported in dystonia (Neumann et al: x = 21.4 mm, y = -5.9 mm, z = -4.4 mm). Fiber tracking analysis revealed that pathways traversing the theta hotspot projected to motor and sensorimotor cortex, explaining the relationship between theta and hyperkinesia. Building on these spatial findings, we investigated whether these anatomically distinct oscillatory patterns reflect basal ganglia circuits dysfunction. We found that the GPe-GPi connectivity in theta band (LME, P = 0.036), but not high beta band (LME, P = 0.139), was significantly lower in hyperkinetic epochs, suggesting reduced functional connectivity in the indirect pathway during hyperkinesia. We further validated these electrophysiological findings using complementary neuroimaging approaches. For indirect pathway structural connectivity, we identified a significant positive correlation between indirect pathway fiber count and theta oscillatory connectivity (LME, P = 0.007). For direct pathway analysis, we found that high beta oscillatory activity showed a trend of negative association with caudate-GPi connectivity (LME, P = 0.066) (Figure2K-M). 

Conclusion

In this study, leveraging on long term video-synchronized pallidal activity recording, we showcase that pallidal theta power is elevated in hyperkinetic epochs, while high beta power increased in resting epochs in HD patients, and this correlated with symptom severity. We thus propose a hypothesis that the pallidal dual oscillation modulation underlines the co-existence of both hyperkinetic and hypokinetic signs of HD. Our hypothesis is backed by (i) the fact that this observation was not related to voluntary movement and was not observed in movement disorders such as dystonia and PD with only hyperkinetic or hypokinetic signs; (ii) by using neuroimage-based analysis, pallidal theta and high beta activity were respectively mapped to the impairment of HD indirect and direct pathway, which underlines the neural pathology of the hyperkinetic and hypokinetic symptoms of HD. Our findings reveal an electrophysiological basis for HD’s complex symptomatology, with implications for optimizing deep brain stimulation therapy through targeted modulation of these oscillatory signatures. This dual oscillatory framework expands our understanding of HD pathophysiology and offers promising avenues for symptom-specific neuromodulation strategies.
