Introduction

Daily life requires the simultaneous management of motor and cognitive demands. Dual-task (DT) paradigms are widely used to examine this interaction since they involve concurrent execution of two tasks. Findings regarding cognitive-motor dual tasks (CMDT) during walking are contrasting, reporting cognitive decline, motor deterioration, or facilitation1 reflecting how attentional resources are allocated when capacity-limited systems must support two tasks. Three models describe these mechanisms: the bottleneck theory, suggesting sequential processing when tasks rely on shared pathways1; the capacity-sharing model, positing redistribution of finite resources leading to performance costs and/or changes in Event-Related Potentials-ERPs2 and the crosstalk model, suggesting facilitation when overlapping pathways allow parallel processing3. A recent CMDT study from our lab manipulating locomotion and optic flow4, reported that walking enhanced motor and cognitive preparation, improving response time (RT) and accuracy, highlighting the sensitivity of visuo-perceptual and attentional systems to DT demands.

The aim of the present study is to estimate the effects of CMDT paradigms on university students’ cognitive performance and neural mechanisms related to walking modulation (motor effort and motor control). Participants completed a discriminative response task (DRT) during locomotion with varied slope demands while ERPs and behavioural performance (RT and percentage of errors, Err%) were recorded to investigate visuo-perceptual and attentional processing. In addition, motor and cognitive effort were assessed using the Borg Scale and Likert-scale, respectively. Finally, the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-Y5) was administered to test whether increased motor effort influences state anxiety in university students. 

Methods

Thirty healthy, right-handed students (20–36 years old) participated in the study. We used a DT paradigm, combining performing a DRT while walking on a treadmill. 

The DRT consisted of a Go/No-Go paradigm. Participants were instructed to respond to two target stimuli and withhold responses to two non-target stimuli. Stimuli were presented for 250 ms with an inter-stimulus interval of 2200–3200 ms. While performing the DRT, participants walked at a constant speed of 1.2 km/h under three treadmill slope conditions: flat, uphill, and downhill. The visual environment simulated a naturalistic path, created in Unity software, matching treadmill inclinations.  

Concurrently, electroencephalogram (EEG) was recorded using a cap with 64 active scalp electrodes and electrooculogram was also monitored. ERP were obtained averaging separately target and non-target trials, segmenting the EEG in periods from 200 ms before the stimulus onset (baseline) to 1200 ms after it. In the DRT, behavioural performance (RT and percentage of errors, Err% and inverse efficiency score, IES) were also assessed.  

Pre- task questionary assessed the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Pre- and post-task cognitive and physical effort were assessed using the Borg scale for perceived physical exertion, and a Likert scale for cognitive effort. Furthermore, the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) was used to assess whether variations in walking conditions altered participants’ state anxiety.  

The final dataset was analysed using a 2x3 ANOVA with Trial (Target vs. Nontarget) and Slope (flat, uphill, and downhill) as factors, to examine how variations in motor effort and motor control during inclined walking modulated early cognitive processing and behavioural performance in university students, providing insight into the interaction between locomotion, cognitive load, and wellbeing. 

Results

ERP analyses revealed slope-related modulations in neural processing: early attentional processing, indexed by the N1 component, exhibited lower amplitudes during uphill and downhill walking compared to flat walking while early visuo-perceptual processing, as reflected in the P1 component, showed significantly greater amplitudes under both inclined conditions. 

 Behavioural results indicated that both cognitive and motor effort increased in the uphill condition, while RTs and IES significantly decreased, reflecting faster and more effective performance. In contrast, the downhill condition was associated with a decline in cognitive performance, as evidenced by higher RTs and IES. Notably, STAI-Y scores showed a reduction in state anxiety during the uphill condition, supporting a beneficial regulatory effect of moderate physical effort. Importantly, IPAQ scores did not differ significantly across participants, suggesting that the observed effects were not influenced by habitual physical activity levels. 

Conclusion

Together, these findings demonstrate that sloped walking selectively modulates sensory-perceptual processing and cognitive–motor allocation: while increased physical effort alone does not hinder cognitive performance, heightened demands on motor control reduce cognitive efficiency by drawing attentional resources away from secondary cognitive tasks. These results are consistent with the capacity-sharing model (Abernethy, 1988), which posits that attentional and cognitive resources are dynamically reallocated toward motor control when walking becomes more demanding. The compensatory engagement of additional sensory and cognitive processing reflects the brain’s flexibility and neural adaptability in response to motor challenges. Notably, STAI-Y scores revealed that the higher motor effort perceived in the uphill condition was associated with a greater reduction of the state anxiety compared to other conditions and pre-experiment levels. 

Overall, these findings highlight the tight coupling between motor and cognitive domains in young adults, demonstrating that cognitive processing, sensory integration, attentional allocation, and state anxiety are highly sensitive to motor demands. The brain’s ability to flexibly redistribute neural resources during complex motor–cognitive dual tasks underscores both the resilience and adaptability of neural systems in youth.
