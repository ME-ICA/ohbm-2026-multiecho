Introduction

Misophonia is a condition in which ordinary, innocuous sounds elicit a strong emotional reaction and significant distress. Although this condition is relatively frequent (approximately 1 in 22 adults suffers from clinically significant misophonia1), its recognition is relatively recent. Supported by non-for-profit organization, the amount of research being conducted on misophonia has significantly increased in recent years. Yet, the neurobiological mechanisms of this disorder are still very partially understood. Implicated regions (e.g., the anterior insula (aIns), the Supramarginal Gyrus (SMG), the Supplementary Motor Area (SMA), and the Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC) and the Amygdala (Amyg)) have been identified in previous studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), but how the causal interaction between these regions is modified in misophonia is unknown. This work attempts to answer this question.

Methods

We recorded brain activations using 3T functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) in participants with (N=34) and without (N=25) misophonia (age: 18-45 years). Participants listened to sounds, viewed silent videos associated with sounds, or were asked to think about sounds across three sensory modalities (auditory, visual, mental imagery) and three stimulus types (trigger, aversive, non-aversive) in a 3 × 3 factorial design (Figure 1.A). The misophonia group consisted of individuals who experience misophonic reactions triggered by sounds with distinct visual components, but not by specific individuals. We selected trigger sets for each misophonia participant based on the trigger categories identified during the screening interviews. Control group participants were matched with misophonia participants on age, biological sex, handedness, and stimulus presentation. We preprocessed fMRI data using SPM and fMRIPrep.

Using our prior neuroimaging results in misophonia (unpublished results), reports of the involvement of the amygdala in misophonia2, general information about neuroanatomy (e.g., structural connectivity; functional roles of different brain regions), and current knowledge about the role of different brain regions in misophonia3, we defined a model for Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM) analyses (Figure 1.B). Although no evidence supports the role of the primary auditory cortex (A1) in misophonia, this region was included in the model as a sensory input region for auditory information. Using Python and MATLAB SPM, we fitted participant-specific models that included a step function marking stimulus presentation (1 during the stimulus, 0 otherwise) as well as two additional modulatory regressors, each defined as step functions for the trigger and aversive events. To keep the model size manageable, and because we did not have prior hemispheric hypotheses, we modeled all regions as bilateral. For this preliminary analysis, we used only the fMRI recordings in the auditory condition since the model uses A1 as the input region. Further, the stimuli from the different sensory modalities were presented in separate runs, not allowing collapsing across modalities without controlling for systematic offsets in BOLD signals due to scanner drift, inhomogeneity, and session effects across runs. We used null priors for the mean connectivity and modulation. For prior variances, we used 1/64 for intrinsic connectivity (A) and modulatory effect (B) parameters, and a unitary prior variance (uninformative prior) for driving inputs (C).

We computed the percentage of explained variance (R2) for subject-specific DCM models and excluded participants with poorly fit models (R2 < 10%). Then, we used a two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test to determine whether the estimated connectivity was significantly different than zero across participants. We also ran paired (to take advantage of our strong participant-pairing) and independent (to take advantage of all our participants since we have 59 participants but only 24 pairs) to test group effects for the contrast between the two modulatory factors (i.e., trigger vs aversive stimuli). We Bonferroni-corrected for multiple comparisons with α=.05/12=.0042 and α=.05/10=.005 for statistical tests on connections (N=12) and modulatory factors (N=10). 

We further investigated the significance at the individual level using the posterior probability (PP) for a null value. We considered any PP greater than 0.975 (indicating a negative effect, i.e., the posterior distribution is concentrated to the left of zero) or less than 0.025 (indicating a positive effect) as strong evidence for a connection/modulation. We report these results as counts of positive, negative, or null values. Finally, we ran a Parametric Empirical Bayes analysis to determine whether there was a group effect of the trigger vs aversive contrast on the connections identified in red in Figure 1.B.

Results

Four participants were excluded because the R2 of their DCM models was lower than 10%, leaving a total of N=55 participants (miso N=33; control N = 22; pairs N=22). We observed significant effective connectivity (regardless of groups) for Ins → ACC (p=.000017) and Ins → SMG (p=.0014). We observed statistical significance not surviving correction for multiple comparisons for SMG → SMA (.043). 

    The group effect of the contrast trigger vs aversive stimuli was significant for three connections before but not after the Bonferroni correction: ACC → Amygdala (independent p=.0073; paired p=.030), ACC → SMA (independent p=.060; paired p=.017), and Amygdala → ACC (independent p=.019; paired p=.048).

    PP was significant for most connections in most participants, but with little consistency in the directionality across participants. The number of participants with positive effective connectivity for specific connections varied between 12 (Amygdala → ACC) and 42 (Ins → ACC) [M=27.5; SD=8.0]. For negative effective connectivity, this number varied between 7 (Ins → ACC) and 27 (A1 → SMG) [M=18.9; SD=5.8]. Corresponding numbers for null connectivity varied between 2 (SMG → Ins and ACC → Amygdala) and 31 (Amygdala → ACC) [M=8.6; SD=7.9]. The frequency for significant PP values was lower when testing for the trigger vs aversive contrast, but both positive and negative modulations were seen regularly, with positive modulation in 2 to 14 participants [M=9.3; SD=4.0], negative modulation in 4 to 14 participants [M=10.6; SD=3.1], and absence of modulation in 27 to 49 participants [M=35.1; SD=6.2]. The most often modulated connection was SMG → Ins (half of the time with a positive sign), and the least often modulated connection was Amygdala → ACC. Consistent with the large degree of variability in the sign of the modulation between participants, the Parametric Empirical Bayes analysis did not reveal any significant group effect for trigger vs aversive contrast.

Conclusion

Our analysis detected significant effective connectivity within the specified neural circuit, but with high variability between participants. We consequently failed to detect group differences for trigger vs aversive effects. Our prior work looking at physiological responses to triggering stimuli4 clearly showed such a contrast in a subset of participants only. These participants tended to be those who rated the triggering stimuli as very distressing, leading to the hypothesis that the experimental context did not allow for systematically reproducing triggering stimuli. Further, in this previous work, we obtained clear group effects by considering only the subset of trials rated as very distressing by the participants (i.e., trials in which we successfully triggered a misophonic response). Unfortunately, we cannot adopt this strategy here since behavioral responses at the trial level are not available for the fMRI recording. Further, in fMRI, the need to block stimuli would likely make such an approach ineffective. Stronger results may be possible with a protocol that would elicit intense misophonic responses more reliably. However, since triggering is painful, such an approach may not be feasible or ethical.

    The results we reported are from our preliminary implementation of a DCM pipeline to study misophonia, and caution is necessary in their interpretation at this early stage. We plan to continue investigating these data and further confirm them by increasing the number of controls (and misophonia-control pairs), investigating the potential effects of confounders (e.g., motion artifacts) by regressing them out and running the DCM analysis on the residuals, and validating the model we proposed in Figure 1.B through model comparison. Regardless, we believe the outcome of this work is noteworthy as it proposes a clear and detailed framework to investigate effective connectivity within a model-driven approach.
