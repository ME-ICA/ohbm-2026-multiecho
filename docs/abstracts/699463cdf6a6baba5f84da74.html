Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) are among the most common neurodevelopmental disorders in childhood, and the two conditions show substantial overlap in social-communication difficulties, executive dysfunction, and genetic liability.1 This clinical and biological convergence challenges traditional categorical diagnostic frameworks and highlights the need for dimensional and transdiagnostic approaches to identify shared neurobiological mechanisms. Functional connectivity (FC) studies have revealed abnormalities in both ASD and ADHD across networks with emerging evidence of overlapping alterations in comorbid presentations. Our prior work further demonstrated that autism symptom severity is associated with FC of the left middle frontal gyrus across individuals with ASD and ADHD, independent of diagnostic category.2 However, conventional FC analyses rely on zero-lag correlation and provide a static view of brain organization, which may overlook temporal dynamics shaping information integration and interregional communication. To address this limitation, recent work has introduced temporal metrics to capture local and inter-regional dynamics. Although abnormalities in these temporal features have been reported in ASD and ADHD, their relevance to shared, transdiagnostic symptom dimensions remains poorly understood. Moreover, progress has been limited by substantial heterogeneity in pediatric samples, including small cohort sizes, inconsistent behavioral assessments, and motion-related low imaging quality. Here, we address these challenges by leveraging a rigorously phenotyped and high-quality imaging dataset of children with ASD and ADHD.2 We systematically examined whether Intrinsic Neural Timescale (INT), Autocorrelation Function (ACF), and lag FC are associated with ASD- and ADHD-related symptom severity across diagnostic categories and further explored their potential genetic and neurochemical underpinnings.

Methods

A total of 166 children aged 6–12 years were included, with best-estimate DSM-5 diagnoses of ASD or ADHD without ASD. Behavioral phenotyping included clinician-administered ADOS-2, yielding Calibrated Severity Scores (CSS) and its Social Affect (SA) and Restricted/Repetitive Behavior (RRB) subscales, as well as clinician-administered KSADS, assessing inattentive, hyperactive/impulsive, and total ADHD symptoms. For each voxel within the cortical gray-matter mask, we computed the ACF and quantified INT as the sum of consecutive positive ACF values multiplied by repetition time. We extracted ACF values for the first 20 lags to characterize voxelwise temporal profiles (Fig. 1). Voxelwise General Linear Models (GLMs) related INT and ACF values to ADOS-2 and KSADS scores while controlling for age, sex, and median framewise displacement, using 5,000 permutations and TFCE cluster correction (p<0.01, cluster size ≥15 voxels) to identify symptom-associated clusters.

At the interregional level, we first computed static FC (lag 0) and lag FC for lags 1–10. For each lag and each voxel, we derived a whole-brain FC profile for every participant and computed a Euclidean distance matrix across participants based on these profiles. Each lag-specific distance matrix was then entered into voxelwise Multivariate Distance Matrix Regression (MDMR), with ADOS-2 and KSADS symptom scores as predictors. Statistical significance was assessed using 5,000 permutations and the cluster-level threshold of p < 0.01 (cluster size ≥ 15 voxels). Regions consistently significant across multiple lags were combined into cross-lag regions of interest (ROIs), which were used as seeds to compute static and lag FC profiles for each participant. Additional voxelwise GLMs characterized symptom-related coupling patterns originating from these ROIs (Fig. 2).

At the molecular level, symptom-related brain maps were spatially aligned with the Allen Human Brain Atlas3 and a set of 19 neurotransmitter receptor/transporter density maps.4 Gene enrichment and neurotransmitter correlation analyses were used to probe potential genetic and neurochemical mechanisms underlying the observed temporal abnormalities.

Results

In local temporal dynamics, the mean ACF remained positive up to lag 6 (~4.8 seconds). GLMs analyses identified the ventral (vPCu) and dorsal (dPCu) subdivisions of the precuneus as the most robustly ASD symptom related regions: INT and ACF at lags 1–6 were significantly positively associated with total CSS, with vPCu showing stronger associations with total CSS and SA, and dPCu showing stronger associations with RRB (Fig.1). These relationships were directionally consistent across ASD and ADHD subgroups, although a diagnosis × RRB interaction emerged in dPCu. In contrast, no stable associations were found between INT or ACF and ADHD symptoms.

In interregional temporal coupling, MDMR on static FC replicated the well-established association between LMFG connectivity and ASD symptom severity,2 whereas lag FC revealed a temporally structured set of symptom-related pathways. LMFG remained significant across lags 1–6, RIPL and LPCC showed peak pseudo-F values at lag 4, and RMFG emerged only at later lags (5–6). ROI-based GLMs showed that LMFG–PCC connectivity exhibited consistent positive associations with CSS across static FC and early lags, forming a sustained engagement pathway linking frontal control regions and the default mode network. LMFG–IPL coupling emerged only at later lags (4–6), suggesting delayed recruitment of lateral parietal regions. LPCC–cerebellar connectivity remained robust across lags 1–6 (Fig. 2). SA followed patterns similar to CSS, while RRB effects were weaker but increased at intermediate lags. Notably, neither MDMR nor seed-based GLMs analyses revealed any consistent associations with ADHD symptoms.

At the molecular level, INT and ACF related maps showed only sparse overlap with ASD/ADHD genetic risk and no meaningful correspondence with neurotransmitter receptor or transporter densities. In contrast, lag FC maps, particularly those involving LMFG and LPCC, were enriched for neurodevelopmental gene sets related to neurogenesis, neuronal differentiation, and synaptic organization and showed significant negative correlations with Norepinephrine Transporter (NET) density.

Conclusion

Integrating dynamic functional metrics, behavioral symptoms, and multilevel biological annotations, this study demonstrates that ASD-related symptom variation in children is more strongly reflected in intrinsic temporal properties and lag dependent patterns of network interaction than in static FC alone. Prolonged local temporal dynamics in ventral and dorsal precuneus, together with the hierarchical unfolding of LMFG–PCC–IPL and LPCC–cerebellar pathways across lags, delineates an imbalanced relationship between control systems and the default mode network that is linked to ASD symptom severity. These dynamic alterations map onto neurodevelopmental gene programs and NET-dependent neurochemical gradients, suggesting a mechanism that connects molecular architecture to large-scale temporal dysregulation in ASD. Although ADHD symptoms were examined in parallel, no robust dynamic or static FC correlates were identified, suggesting that the temporal neural features captured in this study are more tightly linked to ASD symptom variation than to ADHD symptom expression within this cohort. Overall, the combined use of INT, lag FC, and molecular annotations provides a powerful framework for revealing symptom-relevant dimensions of brain dynamics and may inform future development of transdiagnostic biomarkers for neurodevelopmental disorders.
