Introduction

Myelin is essential for efficient neural conduction and axonal stability, and its alteration is closely associated with demyelinating disorders such as multiple sclerosis (MS). Therefore, reliable evaluation of myelin distribution has been an important objective in neuroimaging research.

Quantitative Susceptibility Mapping (QSM) is a quantitative MRI technique that provides sensitivity to myelin, which has diamagnetic susceptibility.1 However, QSM cannot distinguish between the mixed contributions of paramagnetic and diamagnetic susceptibility sources, such as paramagnetic iron and diamagnetic myelin, that co-exist in a voxel. χ-separation was introduced to address this limitation by separately estimating the paramagnetic susceptibility (χpara) and the diamagnetic susceptibility (χdia) values.2

Recently, a deep learning model to directly synthesize myelin histology from MRI images was developed.3 The model takes multi-contrast MRI images as inputs to synthesize corresponding myelin staining images. Although the synthesized myelin staining images have the potential to offer non-invasive myelin assessment, their consistency with conventional MRI-derived myelin markers remain insufficiently evaluated.

In this study, we quantitatively analyzed the correlation between synthesized myelin staining images and χ-dia maps. Using 24 region-of-interests (ROIs), we evaluate whether the two modalities exhibit comparable patterns of myelin-related contrast. 

Methods

Data acquisition

We acquired MRI data at 3T from 9 healthy subjects, including 3D multi-echo gradient echo (GRE) data with six head orientations, 2D multi-echo spin echo (MESE) data (Siemens Tim Trio and Siemens Cima. X, Erlangen, Germany), and MP2RAGE (Siemens Cima. X, Erlangen, Germany). The acquisition parameters were as follows: TR = 38 ms, TE = 7.7:5.03:32.85 ms (Tim Trio) or 6.7:5.23:32.85 ms (Cima. X), FA = 15°, and voxel size = 1 x 1 x 1 mm3 for multi-echo GRE; TR = 7800 ms (Tim Trio) or 10000 ms (Cima. X), TE = 15:15:90ms, FA = 90°, and voxel size = 1 x 1 x 2 mm3 for MESE;  TR = 5000 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, TIs = 700/2500ms, FAs = 4°/5°, and voxel size = 1 x 1 x 1 mm3 for MP2RAGE. All scans were approved by the institutional review board.

χ-separation processing

χ-separation was performed following the same pipeline proposed in M. Kim et al., using χ-sep-COSMOS to reconstruct χpara and χdia.4 Phase processing for a local field map followed the QSM consensus: phase unwrapping using ROMEO, weighted echo averaging, and background field removal with V-SHARP. From the multi-echo GRE magnitude images, an R2* map was estimated using ARLO. For R2 mapping, the multi-echo spin-echo magnitude images were matched to a simulated extended phase graph dictionary. R2’ map was calculated by R2*-R2 with negative values set to zero. Local field maps and R2’ maps were generated for each head orientation, and then registered to the first head orientation using FSL FLIRT. Finally, χpara and χdia maps were reconstructed using χ-sep-COSMOS with R2’ and local field maps from all orientations.

Myelin Staining Image Synthesis Using MRI2HISTO

The MRI2HISTO model trained using unpatched dataset3 was utilized without re-training. The model takes three MRI contrasts as inputs: T1-weighted image from MP2RAGE, QSM, and R2* map. The T1-weighted image was masked using ANTs brain extraction, and rigidly registered to the GRE magnitude image using FSL FLIRT. The registered T1-weighted image was subsequently masked using the refined mask generated during χ-separation. For the QSM map, the local field map from χ-separation processing was utilized as input into QSMnetME4. The R2* map acquired during the χ-separation pipeline was directly utilized as input to MRI2HISTO network.

The input images were concatenated in the following order:T1-weighted image, R2*, and QSM. Each modality was processed on a slice-wise basis, and z-score normalization was applied independently to each slice.

The original  matrix size was 224 × 256 × 160, and each 2D slice was bilinearly interpolated to 512 × 512 to match the input matrix size required by the MRI2Histo network. These slices were then fed into the model to generate a synthesized myelin map. Subsequently, the model output was resized back to the original in-plane resolution using bilinear interpolation.

ROI-based linear regression

The ROIs used in this study were adapted from the χ-separation atlas,5 including 24 anatomical regions (11 GM and 13 WM ROIs). The ROIs were transformed into each subject’s space using deformation matrices calculated using nonlinear registration (ANTs). For each ROI, the mean χdia and the mean optical density of the synthesized myelin stained image were computed.

The relationship between the synthesized myelin staining and diamagnetic susceptibility was modeled using the linear mixed effect model, with subject-specific random intercepts:

yi,j =β0+β1xi,j+ui+εi,j

where i and j index subject and ROI, respectively; y is the ROI-averaged optical density from synthesized myelin stained image; x is the ROI-averaged diamagnetic susceptibility value; β0 and β1 are the fixed-effect intercept and slope, respectively; ui is the random intercept for subject i; and εi,j is the residual error.

First, the analysis was conducted with all 24 ROIs. Then, separate models were fitted for the GM and WM groups to investigate tissue-specific characteristics. Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

χdia map, synthesized myelin staining map, and the ROI overlay are displayed for two slices from a representative subject along with zoomed in images (Figure 2a). 

When linear mixed-effects model  analysis was conducted (Figure 2b), the results of the whole ROI demonstrated a significant positive correlation between the two measures with fixed-effect slope of β1 = 1.6033 (p < 0.001, R2 = 0.782). When the GM regions were separately analyzed, the regression result also revealed a significant positive linear correlation between χdia and synthesized myelin staining intensity. The fixed-effect slope was estimated at  β1 = 0.6552 (p < 0.001, R2 = 0.723). In the WM regions, a statistically significant positive correlation was also observed (p =<0.001, R2 = 0.869). However, the magnitude of this relationship differed from that of GM. The estimated slope for WM ROIs was β1 = 0.2258, which is flatter compared to the slope estimated from the whole ROI or the GM. The model demonstrated a high conditional R2 of 0.869, despite the weak linear coupling.

Conclusion

In this study, we quantitatively compared GRE-based χdia maps derived from χ-separation with synthesized myelin staining generated by the MRI2Histo network, with the aim of evaluating whether these two modalities capture consistent myelin-related information in the human brain. Our results demonstrate a strong overall agreement between χdia and synthesized myelin maps, as evidenced by high conditional coefficients of determination in linear mixed-effects modeling.

We observed a smaller slope for WM ROIs compared to the GM ROIs (β1 = 0.2258 vs 0.6552, respectively), albeit the high goodness of fit (R2 = 0.869 for WM ROIs). This may result from the lower dynamic range of the synthesized myelin staining image compared to χdia map in highly myelinated areas such as the optic nerve, as can be seen in the zoomed in images in Figure 2a. 

Although we compared the synthesized myelin staining to the χdia map, χdia is also an indirect marker of myelin, and does not directly represent myelin concentration. Therefore, the results do not indicate that either metric provides a direct estimate of myelin concentration, but rather that the two myelin-sensitive contrasts correlate at the ROI level. 

In conclusion, we demonstrate that the two MRI-based indirect measures of myelin, χdia and MRI2HISTO-synthesized myelin staining, reflect overlapping but not identical aspects of myelin-related tissue properties.
