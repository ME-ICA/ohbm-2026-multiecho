Introduction

Generative effective connectivity (GEC) based on the Hopf model 1 has proven to be a powerful tool for capturing differences in functional connectivity using fMRI. Up until now, it had been used mainly in resting state, or when applied in tasks, with long time series (longer than 100 time points). We aim to validate this model for investigating differences in effective connectivity between conditions in typical block design tasks with short blocks (30 time points).
To achieve this, we first examined how well the model replicates the dynamics of the empirical data. Afterwards, we used a Support Vector Machine (SVM) to determine if the resulting GEC matrices from the Hopf model effectively capture information from the connectivity. For this purpose, in a first step, we investigated whether the classifier was able to distinguish between GEC matrices computed from task and resting state. Then, in the core of our study, we proceeded to do the same and classify GEC matrices derived from two conditions from a task with a known difference in connectivity patterns. 2

Methods

Experimental paradigm

The data come from a broader study focusing on Affect labeling in bilingual speakers, following a prior experimental design.3 Subjects, while in resonance, were shown a sample at the top of the screen and two options below, side by side. They had to choose which of the options matched the sample. This was repeated across 6 conditions. We focused on two of them: Affect Match (AM) and Shape Match (SM). In the former, both the sample and the options consisted of human faces expressing an emotional reaction. In the latter, the sample and options were geometrical shapes.
These two conditions showed different effective connectivity patterns when performing DCM with the same design.2

Data acquisition and preprocessing

BOLD signal was acquired using a GE SIGNA Architect 3.0T. Then, standard fMRI preprocessing using the CONN toolbox was performed with the addition of the FIR regression model by Cole.4 After excluding participants due to movement reasons, the sample is composed of 104 healthy subjects. Besides the collected data, a structural matrix derived from DTI from the HCP based on the DK80 parcellation was used.

The model

The Hopf model is a whole-brain design that models the BOLD signal of each region as a Stuart-Landau oscillator. Each brain region oscillates at its own intrinsic frequency and is linearly coupled with other brain regions following anatomical information derived from tractography. When applying the model to study effective connectivity, the strength of the linear couplings is adjusted so that the simulated BOLD signal matches the dynamics of the empirical one. This strength, which indicates how much the signal of a region affects the signal of another region, is interpreted as the effective connectivity from the first to the second region.
In our case, instead of simulating BOLD signal as such, a linear approximation of the model 5 was used due to its computational efficiency. This approach allows for a much faster estimation of the matrices, and hence for the possibility of testing several parameter configurations.

Model fitting

The model accounts not just for the grand average FC, but also tries to fit how the FC fluctuate over time. In the linear approximation, this temporal structure is measured through the lagged covariance.
 For this reason, when measuring the goodness of fit of the linear Hopf, two things are considered: the correlation between empirical and simulated FC and the correlation between lagged covariances.
We explored via a grid search ranging from 0.01 to 0.3 in increments of 0.01 that the maximum connectivity value (Cmax) produced significant fit differences.
 
 

SVM classification

After the fitting phase, two classifications were performed using an SVM in order to verify whether there were differences in GEC resulting from each one of the conditions and resting. We used the Leave One Subject Out technique. First, matrices computed from either AM or SM were labeled as task, and the SVM had to distinguish between task and resting. This classification was performed for all values of Cmax. Next, the same SVM was used to classify the two conditions. This was done twice, following different methods. First, a simple approach without considering subject variability. Here for matrices derived from Cmax=0.02, a simple AM or SM classification was conducted, as well as a permutation test to examine the accuracy values obtained. In the second one, which does take subject variability into account, we built two classes by stacking the GEC matrices in different orders. We defined A = AM|SM and B = SM|AM, and classified these categories. This was studied for all Cmax values. Since the stacked approach is subject-wise, accuracy was observed in the same fashion. For each subject omitted in the training phase, we showed the SVM both A and B and only when both were correctly classified was it counted as right. That is, accuracy is a percentage of correct subjects.

Results

Model fitting

For both conditions, as well as resting state data, we observed a better fitting of FC and COV around Cmax =0.02 as it can be seen in the figure. Moreover, in both conditions and for all values of Cmax, we obtained FC correlations around 0.8 and COV correlations between 0.5 and 0.6.

SVM classification

In the first classification of resting against task, for each of the aforementioned values of Cmax, the SVM could distinguish with complete accuracy between task and resting. Just as a check, this was also done with the stacking matrices technique, giving also an accuracy of 1 for all of the values of Cmax.
Next, in the simple approach of AM or SM, with Cmax =0.02, the resulting accuracy was 0.690±34 (mean,sd), and performing a Label-shuffling permutation test, we obtained that the value of accuracy was not a result of chance with p < 0.001.
 In the stacking approach, we obtained values of accuracy ranging from 0.7 to 0.76. It is important to take into account the fact that, with the subject-wise accuracy used in this case, the chance level stands at 0.25 as opposed to 0.5 in usual accuracy.
Furthermore, the tendency of accuracy by Cmax resembles the one previously seen in fit. In fact, if we take into account the mean of the four fit values by Cmax. That is, the mean fit of FC and COV for both AM and SM, shows a correlation of r = 0.8636 with p < 0.001.

Conclusion

There are two related items we can conclude from this study. On the one hand, GEC analysis based on the linear Hopf model is valid for short task-based time series. In a first level of depth, we proved how, even with a reduced number of time-points, the model was able to fit the data for all values of Cmax. Besides, the SVM classification showed enough information was captured in order to distinguish completely between resting and task.
 In a second level of depth, at what is the core of this investigation, we observed that this difference was also captured with the simple classification between conditions. It is true that the level of accuracy was limited, but it clearly shows that the matrices contain condition-specific information that the classifier can recognize above a chance level.
The value in standard deviation when performing the simple classification indicates there is a between-subject variability, which translates to classification difficulty across subjects for that task. This is reasonable, as we are interested in differences in the connectivity values between AM and SM, but not in the values themselves. That is, we aim to study the changes in connectivity by subject, as it was observed in DCM.2
 On the other hand, we introduced a modification of the usual SVM validation in which subject differences are accounted for. The stacking approach showed an improvement in classification that translates to a more appropriate way of analyzing differences from a subject perspective. Furthermore, this is consistent with the fitting results, as a clear positive correlation between fitting and accuracy was seen. In other words, the better the model fits, the greater the difference seen between matrices, as one would expect. Thus, further giving strength to the linear Hopf model.
 As future work, it is intended to replicate this work using other machine learning models that offer explainability in the differences. This would allow us to go even deeper and analyze where and how these differences are from the machine learning perspective.
