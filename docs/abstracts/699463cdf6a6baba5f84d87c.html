Introduction

Women exhibit a significantly higher lifetime risk of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) than men, with a global female-to-male ratio of approximately 2:1. Accumulating evidence suggests that estrogen decline during the menopausal transition represents a female-specific risk factor for AD. Estradiol plays a critical role in cognition and neuroplasticity via estrogen receptors widely distributed across limbic and cortical regions. Neuroimaging studies have linked menopause with reductions in gray matter volume and other structural alterations associated with early AD pathology.Menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) has been proposed as a potential intervention to mitigate menopause-related brain changes. However, structural MRI studies investigating MHT effects have yielded highly inconsistent findings, with reports of both neuroprotective and adverse outcomes. Emerging evidence suggests that this variability may be driven by differences in the timing of MHT initiation and the route of hormone administration, though findings remain fragmented. To address these discrepancies, we conducted a systematic review of structural MRI studies to evaluate overall patterns of MHT-related brain changes and to identify key modifiers—particularly timing and administration route.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review following PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, Web of Science, and EBSCO were searched for English-language human studies published up to June 2025 using keywords related to MHT, estrogen therapy, structural MRI, and brain structure. Original studies examining the effects of MHT use during or after menopause on brain structure were eligible. Studies involving neurological disorders, non-structural imaging modalities, animal or transgender populations, or MHT use prior to menopause were excluded.

Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Twenty-four studies met inclusion criteria. Data were extracted on sample characteristics, MHT timing and formulation, imaging metrics, and affected brain regions. Study quality and risk of bias were assessed using the FLC 3.0 critical appraisal platform. Findings were synthesized qualitatively, categorized by global brain measures, region-of-interest (ROI) analyses, and specific structural metrics.

Results

Twenty-four studies were included in the qualitative synthesis. Findings on menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) and brain structure were organized by analysis approach: global exploratory analyses (9 studies), region-of-interest (ROI)–based contrasts (11 studies), and contrasts of brain structural metrics (14 studies). Methodological quality was rated as high in 8 studies and average in 16.

Results from global exploratory analyses, predominantly voxel-based morphometry, were heterogeneous. Six studies reported increased regional brain volumes associated with MHT use, whereas two studies drawing on the same WHIMS database reported volume reductions. Regions most frequently implicated across studies included the hippocampus, cerebellum, and frontal and temporal gyri. Studies applying alternative analytic approaches (e.g., tensor-based morphometry or machine learning) further underscored divergence in findings: some reported reduced volumes in medial temporal and prefrontal regions associated with oral conjugated equine estrogens (oCEE), while others found that transdermal estradiol (tE2) attenuated age-related decline or was associated with larger overall brain volumes.

ROI based analyses showed no consistent evidence for an effect of MHT on specific brain regions. Among eight studies examining the hippocampus, one reported a positive association with MHT, one reported a negative association, and six reported no significant differences. For frontal lobe volumes, two WHIMS based studies observed smaller volumes among MHT users, whereas another study reported no effect. The majority of studies examining other ROIs including the temporal lobe, amygdala, and cerebellum—found no significant differences between MHT users and non-users, although isolated positive associations were reported for the amygdala and entorhinal cortex in specific subgroups, such as APOE ε4 carriers.

Analyses contrasting brain structural metrics suggested that MHT effects may be moderated by both route of administration and timing of initiation. Most studies reported no significant association between MHT and intracranial volume, with one study noting a transient negative effect. White matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume increased significantly with oCEE in several analyses from the KEEPS trial, whereas no such increase was observed with tE2. Initiation of MHT more than five years after menopause was also associated with greater WMH progression, while earlier initiation was not. Total gray matter and white matter volumes generally showed no significant associations with MHT use.

Overall synthesis across timing and route provided support for the critical window hypothesis: MHT initiated closer to menopause onset was not associated with adverse structural outcomes and, in some cases, appeared beneficial. Route of administration emerged as a key moderator, with transdermal estradiol demonstrating a more favorable structural profile particularly with respect to WMH burden compared to oral CEE.

Conclusion

This review systematically evaluated MRI evidence on the effects of MHT on brain structure in women, focusing on initiation timing, hormone formulation, and administration route. Negative structural effects were mainly reported in the WHIMS, where oral CEE was initiated a decade or more after menopause, whereas studies accounting for timing and tE2 use generally found no adverse outcomes. These findings support the critical window hypothesis, indicating that MHT is neuroprotective when started near menopause but may be ineffective or harmful if delayed. Administration route further influences outcomes: transdermal estradiol bypasses first-pass metabolism, providing higher bioavailability and favorable neural effects compared to oral CEE. Individual susceptibility, particularly APOE4 genotype, may modulate MHT benefits, with evidence of larger entorhinal and amygdala volumes and improved memory in high-risk carriers.

Methodological limitations likely underlie many null findings, including cross-sectional designs, limited MRI sampling, and predominant focus on the hippocampus. Regions such as the entorhinal cortex may better capture early MHT effects. Future studies should employ longitudinal, densely sampled structural MRI, incorporate cortical thickness and broader ROIs, and use functional MRI to assess network-level consequences. Well-designed RCTs stratified by timing, formulation, dose, and population characteristics are essential for clarifying causal relationships and optimizing individualized, neuroprotective interventions.

In summary, MHT’s neural effects emerge from the interaction of initiation timing, hormone formulation, and individual susceptibility. Transdermal estradiol initiated within the critical window preserves brain structure, whereas late initiation or oral CEE may pose risks. Integrating these factors provides a mechanistic framework for interpreting prior inconsistencies and guiding future research on targeted MHT strategies for brain health in women.
