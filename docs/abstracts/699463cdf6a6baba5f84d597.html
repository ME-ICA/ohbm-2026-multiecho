Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is traditionally conceptualized through reward circuit dysfunction, yet recent evidence suggests depression severity may reflect disruptions in multiple networks.1 Connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM) enables identification of distributed functional connectivity patterns associated with dimensional symptoms across diagnostic categories.2 While reward network alterations have been documented in depression, the relative contribution of other large-scale networks remains unclear. This study examined whether resting-state functional connectivity could predict depression severity dimensionally across healthy and clinical populations, and whether predictive patterns extend beyond reward circuitry. We hypothesized that depression severity would be predicted by distributed connectivity patterns involving multiple functional networks, with contributions extending beyond traditional reward-related circuits. Understanding the neural architecture of dimensional depression severity may inform transdiagnostic approaches to treatment and advance mechanistic models of mood disorders.4

Methods

Participants The study included 335 participants (18-40 years): 202 healthy controls (HC) and 133 individuals with a current or past diagnosis of major depressive disorder (MDD). Depression severity was assessed using the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) as the primary continuous outcome measure. All participants completed resting-state functional MRI scans and clinical assessments.

MRI Data Acquisition Resting-state fMRI data were acquired on a 3T MRI scanner using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence optimized for BOLD sensitivity. One resting-state run was collected per participant. Participants were instructed to remain awake with eyes open during acquisition. 

Data Preprocessing Functional MRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep 25.2.3, which included motion correction, slice-timing correction, spatial normalization to MNI space, and nuisance regression. Standard preprocessing steps addressed motion artifacts, physiological noise, and other confounds.

Connectivity Analysis Functional connectivity matrices were constructed using the Schaefer 400-parcel atlas,5 which parcellates the cortex into 400 regions across seven functional networks (Visual, Somatomotor, Dorsal Attention, Ventral Attention, Limbic, Frontoparietal, Default Mode). For each participant, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the mean time series of all parcel pairs, resulting in a 400×400 connectivity matrix. Each "edge" represents the temporal correlation in resting-state BOLD signal between two brain parcels.

Connectome-Based Predictive Modeling We implemented CPM using 10-fold cross-validation to identify brain connectivity patterns predictive of MADRS scores.2 Within each training fold, edges significantly correlated with MADRS scores (p < 0.01) were identified. Separate positive and negative predictive networks were constructed based on edge directionality. For each test-fold participant, connectivity strength was summed across predictive edges to generate individualized depression severity predictions. Model performance was evaluated using Pearson correlation between observed and predicted MADRS scores, as well as mean absolute error (MAE).

Network-Level Analysis To identify which functional networks contributed most to prediction, we quantified the number of predictive edges within and between each network pair. We specifically examined reward-related connectivity by analyzing edges involving limbic regions and their connections to other networks.

Clinical Classification We assessed the clinical utility of connectivity-based predictions by computing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for classifying high versus low depression severity (MADRS threshold = 7) and for discriminating MDD (current and past) from HC based on predicted scores.

Specificity Analysis To test whether prediction was specific to depression severity independent of anxiety, we examined partial correlations controlling for Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) and Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) scores.

Results

Primary Prediction Performance Resting-state functional connectivity significantly predicted depression severity across the full sample (r = 0.214, p < 0.001, n = 335, MAE = 4.79 MADRS points). Cross-validated predictions demonstrated robust though moderate accuracy, with connectivity patterns capturing meaningful individual variation in symptom severity. The root mean squared error was 6.58 MADRS points.

Clinical Classification Accuracy ROC analysis revealed moderate classification performance for distinguishing high versus low depression severity (AUC = 0.614, MADRS cutoff > 7). When using predicted MADRS scores to classify MDD versus HC, the model achieved an AUC of 0.580, indicating modest diagnostic discrimination based solely on connectivity patterns.

Specificity to Depression Prediction accuracy remained significant when controlling for Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) scores (partial r = 0.207), suggesting the connectivity patterns captured variance specific to clinician-rated depression severity beyond self-reported symptoms and anxiety.

Transdiagnostic Applicability Model performance was comparable within the MDD group and the HC group separately, with no significant difference in prediction accuracy between diagnostic categories. This supports depression severity as a dimensional construct that spans traditional diagnostic boundaries.

Network-Level Contributions The most prominent predictive interactions occurred between Somatomotor and Visual networks (72 edges), Somatomotor and Ventral Attention networks (70 edges), Default Mode and Visual networks (67 edges), Default Mode and Sensory networks (60 edges), and Frontoparietal and Ventral Attention networks (60 edges). These distributed network interactions accounted for the majority of predictive signals.

Limited Reward Network Involvement Despite theoretical emphasis on reward dysfunction in depression, limbic/reward network involvement was limited. Only 19 predictive edges involved limbic regions. Critically, within-reward-network connectivity showed no significant association with depression severity (r = 0.04, p = 0.46), challenging models centered exclusively on reward circuit dysfunction.

Edge Directionality Analysis of positive versus negative predictive edges revealed that most network pairs showed predominantly positive edges (increased connectivity associated with greater depression severity), suggesting hyperconnectivity may be a key feature. The total distribution showed 68 positive edges across Somatomotor-Ventral Attention connections and other major network pairs, compared to fewer negative edges.

Top Contributing Network Pairs Ranking the top 10 network-pair interactions by total predictive edge count identified: (1) Somatomotor–Visual (72 edges), (2) Somatomotor–Ventral Attention (70 edges), (3) Default Mode–Visual (67 edges), (4) Default Mode–Sensory (60 edges), (5) Frontoparietal–Ventral Attention (60 edges), (6) Within-Somatomotor (55 edges), (7) Somatomotor–Dorsal Attention (51 edges), (8) Visual–Ventral Attention (48 edges), (9) Frontoparietal–Default Mode (45 edges), and (10) Within–Visual (42 edges). These findings highlight sensorimotor, attentional, and self-referential networks as core to depression severity.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that resting-state functional connectivity predicts depression severity through distributed brain network interactions that extend well beyond traditional reward circuitry. The moderate but significant prediction accuracy (r = 0.214) aligns with recent neuroimaging literature emphasizing the multifaceted neural basis of psychiatric symptoms. Several key findings warrant emphasis.

First, the transdiagnostic nature of prediction, with comparable accuracy in MDD and HC groups, supports dimensional models of psychopathology and aligns with Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) frameworks.3 Depression severity appears to exist along a continuum reflected in connectivity patterns, rather than as a discrete state separating patients from controls.

Second, the predominance of Somatomotor–Visual, Frontoparietal–Ventral Attention, and Default Mode–Visual/Sensory connections challenges oversimplified reward-centric models of depression. The near-absence of significant within-reward-network connectivity (r = 0.04, p = 0.46) and limited involvement of limbic edges (19 total) suggests depression severity reflects dysfunction in attention allocation, sensory processing, and self-referential cognition as much as hedonic deficits. This finding is consistent with meta-analytic evidence of widespread network dysfunction in MDD.1

The prominence of hyperconnectivity (positive edges predominating) may reflect maladaptive compensatory mechanisms or inefficient neural communication in depression. Frontoparietal and attentional network involvement suggests executive control and sustained attention deficits may be core features of more severe depression. Somatomotor and visual network contributions could relate to psychomotor disturbances and altered sensory processing frequently observed in MDD.

From a translational perspective, these findings have several implications. Connectivity-based biomarkers may eventually complement clinical assessment, particularly if prediction accuracy improves with multimodal data integration or refined parcellation schemes. The distributed nature of predictive patterns suggests that effective interventions may need to target multiple neural systems rather than single circuits. Neuromodulation approaches (e.g., transcranial magnetic stimulation) might benefit from targeting connectivity hubs within these predictive networks.

Limitations include moderate effect sizes, which are likely to reflect the heterogeneity of depression and the inherent noise in resting-state fMRI. Future work should examine whether predictive models can forecast treatment response or identify depression subtypes with distinct connectivity profiles, as suggested by Drysdale et al.⁴ Longitudinal designs would clarify whether these connectivity patterns represent trait vulnerabilities, state markers, or both.

In conclusion, depression severity is embedded in large-scale, distributed functional connectivity patterns that emphasize attentional, sensorimotor, and self-referential networks alongside, but not exclusively within, reward systems. These findings advance mechanistic understanding of mood disorders and support dimensional, network-based conceptualizations of psychopathology.
