Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a widely used non-invasive brain-stimulation technique in both clinical practice and neuroscience research. In particular, TMS is useful in investigations of the motor system where physiological measures such as input–output (I/O) curves, the resting motor threshold (rMT), and the cortical silent period (CSP) are used to quantify corticospinal excitability and intracortical inhibitory processes. Yet, despite the widespread use of these measures, several factors that could introduce methodological confounds remain insufficiently explored.

One such factor is coil orientation (CO), which has received only little systematic investigation. The direction of the induced electric field on the cortical surface plays a crucial role in determining which cortical areas are preferentially stimulated. Therefore, it could potentially alter TMS-derived measures of motor system function. In this study, we aimed to examine how small variations in CO could influence two key motor-system readouts: rMT and CSP.

Methods

We recruited 13 healthy volunteers (5 females, mean age: 24.9 ± 1.3 years). The participants underwent a stimulation protocol consisting of 3 to 5 neuronavigated TMS appointments (separated by at least 5 days). 

First, we identified the M. abductor pollicis brevis (APB) motor hotspot. For 10 subjects, this hotspot, used for both rMT estimation and CSP acquisition, was newly determined in each of their first three sessions. To rule out hotspot re-determination as a potential confound, 6 of these 10 subjects completed two additional sessions in which the hotspot established during session one was reused. We also included three additional subjects who completed three sessions each using the same hotspot throughout. (Fig. 1A)

An optimal CO, that elicited the highest APB motor-evoked potentials (MEPs), was determined at the motor hotspot, and two alternative orientations were defined by rotating the coil ±20° (Fig. 1B). The rMTs were determined for all three orientations according to the Nexstim threshold hunting algorithm. The algorithm defines rMT as the minimal stimulation intensity required to generate an MEP (≥ 50 µV peak to peak) in 50% of stimulations.

For CSP measurement, maximum grip strength was subsequently assessed using a hand force meter. Finally, during each session, 15 CSPs per orientation were recorded while participants maintained 20% of their maximum grip strength, using a stimulation intensity of 120% rMT. The MEPs were recorded via electromyography (EMG) from the APB, M. abductor digiti minimi (ADM), and first dorsal interosseus (FDI) muscles. Measurements of CSP duration were automated with MATLAB and FieldTrip ^1 using a slightly modified version of the detection algorithm described by Garvey et al. (2001) ^2. The CSPs were obtained by first rectifying the EMG signal, i.e. taking its absolute value, and then averaging the 15 EMG recordings per CO. From the rectified averaged EMG, we then calculated the mean consecutive difference (MCD) of the pre-stimulus EMG recordings to derive statistically defined upper and lower variation limits (representing 99.76% of baseline activity). For a more reliable CSP detection, CSP onset was defined at the time of stimulation, while CSP offset was identified when EMG activity first rose above the lower limit and remained above it in at least half of the subsequent 5-ms window.

Statistical analyses for both rMT and CSP were performed using linear mixed-effects regression with parametric bootstrapping. The CO was modeled as a three-level fixed effect. To account for inter-individual and inter-session variability, subject and session were included as random intercepts. Additionally, hotspot determination type was modeled as a random effect to control for potential confounding arising from differences in hotspot identification. The final models were specified as:

● rMT:  rMT ~ CO + (1 | subject) + (1 | session) + (1 | hotspot type)

● CSP: CSP ~ CO + (1 | subject) + (1 | session) + (1 | hotspot type)

Results

Across 140 rMTs from 13 participants, CO significantly modulated rMT. Relative to 0° (i.e., the optimal CO), the +20° orientation increased rMT by 1.78 % maximum stimulator output percentage (% MSO) (p < 0.001), a finding confirmed by 10,000-sample parametric bootstrapping (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.134, 2.417). Further, -20° produced a smaller, but also significant rise of 0.72 % MSO (p = 0.034; bootstrapped 95% CI: 0.085, 1.373). (Fig. 2A) 

Following artifact-related exclusions, 122 examined CSPs from 13 participants also demonstrated a strong CO-dependent effect. Specifically, CSPs were significantly shorter at +20°, showing a 14.6 ms reduction relative to 0° (p = 0.0003) (bootstrapped 95% CI: -21.297, -6.68 ms), whereas -20° orientation yielded a moderate, borderline significant reduction of 7.1 ms (p = 0.067; bootstrapped 95% CI: -13.919, 0.608 ms). The same CSPs measured from the ADM and FDI EMG channels showed an analogous reduction. (Fig. 2B)

Conclusion

Rotating the TMS coil by 20º from the optimal CO for MEP generation significantly increased the rMT estimate and lowered CSP duration. One possible explanation is a shift of the induced e-field away from the APB hotspot. If the e-field peak is displaced, higher % MSO is required to achieve the same effective intensity at the target, which is consistent with the observed increase in rMTs. A similar mechanism may apply to inhibitory circuits mediating the CSP: a reduced local e-field would yield weaker activation of these networks and therefore result in shorter silent periods.

Alternatively, these findings may reflect the physical misalignment of the induced current direction and the predominant neurite orientation of the underlying neurons ^3. A more optimally orientated coil, in our case the 0° CO that yielded the largest MEPs, could induce current more efficiently, lowering activation thresholds. A deviation from this optimal axis may reduce recruitment of both excitatory and late inhibitory circuits. This could elevate rMTs and diminish engagement of the networks that normally sustain the CSP, resulting in shorter durations.

Overall, CO appears to be a meaningful confounding factor in TMS motor effects and should be carefully controlled in both research applications and clinical practice.
