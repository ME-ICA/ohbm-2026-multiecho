Introduction

Many pediatric patients in status epilepticus (SE) do not respond to first line treatment such as benzodiazepine. Recent studies1,2 investigating factors influencing drug responsiveness suggest that brain states evolve dynamically during SE, potentially rendering some patients more likely to respond at certain times. Specifically, these studies observed that decreased band amplitudes after drug administration might indicate a higher likelihood of treatment  responsiveness. Building upon this finding, we classified patients using band power cyclicity with the aim to identify biomarkers for SE treatment responsiveness, which could offer additional guidance for clinical decision making.

Methods

To identify neural features that may explain drug efficacy, we investigated how brain dynamics change in intermittent and continuous SE using quantitative EEG: 26 patients / 117 individual recording segments; age range: 0-13years; EEG recording length: 30min – 20hours, artefacts manually removed, segmented to 4h max., down-sampled to 256Hz; etiologies (n/26): structural (6), genetic (12), auto-immune (4), infectious (2), metabolic (2); focal (14), generalized (12); intermittent SE (6), continuous (20). 

Continuous Morlet wavelet transform was applied to temporal changes (30s windows) in canonical band power (δ, θ, α, β, γ) to identify cyclical patterns and assess whether these patterns distinguish responders from non-responders, and whether differences exist between intermittent and continuous SE and etiologies. To ensure robustness of this biomarker we created surrogate timeseries and only counted positives if their average periodic (cycle) power was significantly above the surrogates. Regression analysis will be performed to identify explanatory variables for cyclicity for different patient groups.

Results

We identified several periodic patterns in canonical frequency band power, typically in the range of 2-4 minutes, in both intermittent and continuous SE patients. These cycles correlated with states of increased neuronal activity in EEG across both patient groups. Additionally, our results indicate that patients with less pronounced cyclic amplitudes were more likely to respond to antiseizure medication. This suggests that patients with smaller fluctuations in neural states may be more responsive to treatment. 

Differences in cycle occurrence in frequency bands and periodicity between etiologies were significant but the underlying causes still need to be thoroughly investigated. 

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that band power can be decomposed into cyclical components and that the amplitude of these components may serve as a useful biomarker for SE treatment responsiveness. Characterizing SE in terms of cyclic patterns and parameter trajectories offers a novel approach for differentiating patient subgroups and could inform clinical decision making by targeting the “right patients” at the “right time” with appropriate treatments.
