Introduction

Decades of research have established that the hippocampus (HPC) and the anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT), well-known components of the Papez circuit1, play a critical role in memory retrieval2. However, emerging evidence suggests that other thalamic nuclei, such as the pulvinar (PLV), may also be key components of the core retrieval network3, interacting directly with the HPC and ANT4. In addition, our recent findings from the HPC suggest the presence of populations of neurons with two distinct profiles of electrophysiological activity during memory recognition5. To further elucidate the interactions between these HPC subpopulations and thalamic nuclei, we examined the electrophysiological activity of the HPC, ANT, and PLV simultaneously within the same subjects during a memory recognition task.

Methods

We recruited 12 neurosurgical patients implanted with stereo-electroencephalography (sEEG) electrodes in both thalamic sites and the HPC. Number of electrode contacts located in each of the three regions of interest (ROIs) was different within and across subjects. Neural activity was simultaneously recorded from the ANT (N = 47 contacts, L = 23), PLV (N = 56 contacts, L = 22), and the HPC (N = 94 contacts, L = 46) while participants performed a verbal old–new memory recognition task involving abstract and concrete words presented earlier in an encoding task followed by a 6-10minutes of distractor task. We analyzed broadband high-frequency (HFA; 50–170 Hz) and low-frequency activity (LFA; 1–8 Hz) within the ROIs, with a particular focus on their temporal dynamics and relationships between these activities. To map causal connectivity between the ROIs, we delivered repeated electrical stimulations (45 trials, biphasic square-wave pulses, 200 µs, 6 mA, 0.5Hz) in a given ROI site while measuring the resulting perturbations in other ROIs (aka, the cerebro-cerebral evoked potential, CCEP approach).

Results

Consistent with our previous findings5, one subset of HPC sites (“red sites”) exhibited stimulus-locked increases in HFA (300–900 ms after word onset), whereas another subset (“blue sites”) showed response-locked decreases in HFA (starting ~600 ms before the response) during the old-new recognition task. Importantly, HFA power in the red sites did not differentiate memory outcomes, increasing similarly for both hit and correct rejection (CR) trials. In contrast, the blue sites exhibited a significant transient increase followed by a delayed decrease of HFA power during hit compared to CR trials. Most importantly, the coupling between LFA and HFA in the red sites was associated with memory outcomes. Within the thalamus, the ANT and PLV exhibited comparable HFA profiles that varied with memory outcome, but displayed opposing patterns in their LFA dynamics, which paralleled the HFA dynamics observed in the HPC. Specifically, LFA in the ANT showed stronger correlations with HFA in the HPC red sites, whereas LFA in the PLV was more strongly correlated with HFA in the blue sites. CCEP results further demonstrated distinct patterns of causal connectivity between HPC subregions and thalamic nuclei, with red HPC sites preferentially interacting with the ANT and blue HPC sites showing stronger associations with the PLV, especially for the hit trials. Finally, we identified a cascade of LFA and HFA responses across these regions, in which activity in the HPC red sites was synchronized with the ANT and preceded the responses observed in the HPC blue sites and the PLV. Based on these findings, we propose a conceptual model illustrating the interactions between the HPC and thalamus during memory recognition.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate coordinated interactions among the HPC, ANT, and PLV during memory recognition. Within the HPC, distinct neuronal subpopulations support different computational subtasks: one subset (red sites) engages with the ANT at an early stage, followed by another subset (blue sites) that is associated with the PLV. Together, these interactions collectively contribute to successful memory recognition.
