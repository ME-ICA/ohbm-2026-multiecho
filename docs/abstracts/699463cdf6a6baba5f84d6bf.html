Introduction

Psychiatric disorders are associated with changes in intrinsic brain functional connectivity, but it remains unclear whether changes are disorder-specific or transdiagnostic. Disorder-specific patterns would point to distinct disease processes, while patterns that overlap between disorders indicate a general vulnerability or response of the brain to pathology. Here we investigate this specificity-overlap spectrum across four major psychiatric disorders: attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), major depressive disorder (MDD) and schizophrenia (SZ).

Methods

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) scans were obtained from 19 datasets for 4,029 patients and 3,564 healthy controls. Functional connectivity was extracted for each subject, harmonized across datasets, and used to conduct a mega-analysis. Disorder-specific effects were estimated by comparing each diagnostic cohort to healthy controls and other cohorts. Transdiagnostic overlap was estimated by taking connections that were consistently altered (same direction and significant) in several disorders. To assess the generality of the observed transdiagnostic pattern, it was compared to an independent second set of three disorders (anxiety, bipolar disorder and schizoaffective disorder) with an additional 958 patients and 529 controls.

Results

ASD-specific hypoconnectivity was observed in the default-mode network (Cohen’s d = -0.43 to -0.12; Figure 1A). MDD-specific hypoconnectivity was observed between the right ventromedial and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (d = -0.13 to -0.11). SZ-specific alterations consisted of hypoconnectivity within the visual network (d = -0.39 to -0.24), altered connectivity around the putamen (d = -0.58 to 0.4) and altered connectivity of the somatomotor network (d = -0.42 to 0.37). ADHD showed no significantly altered connectivity. Cross-disorder overlap was observed between ASD, MDD and SZ (Figure 1B) and was characterized by hypoconnectivity within the somatomotor network (d = -0.26 to -0.16) and thalamo-somatomotor hyperconnectivity (d = 0.23 to 0.34). Assessment within the second disorder set revealed that the observed cross-disorder involvement generalized well, as evidenced by a significant correspondence (r = 0.37, p < 0.0001).

Conclusion

The observed disorder-specific patterns are in line with hypotheses of default mode network dysfunction in ASD,1 of prefrontal cortex dysfunction in MDD,2 and of cortico-striatal-thalamic circuit dysfunction in SZ.3 The transdiagnostic pattern of thalamo-somatomotor hyperconnectivity and somatomotor hypoconnectivity fits into a growing body of evidence implicating somatomotor connectivity in psychopathology,4 and parallels growing attention to psychomotor symptoms within a clinical context.5 Currently, it is not possible to determine whether this pattern represents a causal contributor to psychopathology or whether it arises because of a common response to psychiatric illness, such as heightened stress, reduced physical activity, or medication use. In conclusion, the functional connectome undergoes both disorder-specific and transdiagnostic changes. The presented findings clarify this critical aspect of the architecture of connectivity disruptions across major psychiatric conditions.
