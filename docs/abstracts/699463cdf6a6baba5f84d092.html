Introduction

Prosodic pitch contours are a key component of speech, signaling a speaker’s intentions such as to make a statement or to ask a question. Recent studies implicate the premotor cortex (PMC) in categorical processing of prosodic pitch contours,1 mirroring traditional findings of effector-specific motor involvement in speech phoneme perception.2 Vocal pitch production has been shown to rely on laryngeal movements controlled by sub-regions of the PMC.3 However, a direct link between these regions and categorical prosody perception has not been clearly established. To address this gap, we aim to (i) localize the PMC areas engaged in pitch production using a singing task, and (ii) determine whether these areas are involved in prosodic pitch contour perception using a categorization task.

Methods

We collected MEG (306-channel Vectorview, Elekta Neuromag) data from 25 native German speakers during a melodic pitch production and a prosodic pitch perception task. In the production task (Fig. 1a), participants sang five-tone melodies with rising-falling contours (to engage the larynx) on a schwa sound (to keep articulators engaged as little as possible), alternating with resting blocks, each with a duration of 16 s. In the perception task (Fig. 1b), participants were asked to categorize single words that varied in prosodic pitch contours along a continuum from statement (falling contour) to question (rising contours).

MEG data were analyzed in source space (LCMV beamforming) using neural decoding analysis. First, we analyzed the production data to localize pitch-related motor areas. Specifically, we decoded singing and resting blocks using a linear support vector machine (SVM) with leave-one-out cross-validation, applied to source power in searchlights (radius = 5 mm) across six predefined cortical parcels. These parcels were derived from a multimodal cortical parcellation4; bilateral PMC served as the target region, and bilateral auditory (AC) and superior temporal cortices (STC) as control regions. Group-level decoding accuracy was tested against chance using a one-sample t-test (one-sided), and the top 25% of vertices in T-statistics per parcel (all Bonferroni-corrected p<.001) were selected to define functional regions of interest (fROIs).

Next, the defined fROIs were used in a separate decoding analysis on the perception data to explore where and when perceived prosodic categories were represented. In each fROI, perceived categories were decoded for each trial using linear SVMs with 3-fold cross-validation, based on time-varying source activity patterns (window size/step = 20/5 ms). Decoding performance was quantified using AUC-ROC and tested against baseline (-200-0 ms) using a cluster-based permutation test.

Results

Decoding analysis of the pitch production data identified regions in the middle and ventral PMC bilaterally (Fig. 2a), close to the dorsal and ventral laryngeal motor cortices known to be involved in pitch production.5 Accordingly, we defined the localized regions as pitch-related motor areas. Additionally, singing involved the bilateral Heschl’s gyrus and planum temporale in the AC, as well as the gyral portions of the STC fROI. These results likely reflect neural responses to the perception of the self-produced sounds.

A second decoding analysis on the perception data revealed that pitch-related motor areas in the right hemisphere represented perceived prosodic categories (Fig. 2b). Notably, these areas showed significant decoding performance in two distinct time windows: one (340-380 ms) before and the other (500-550 ms) after stimulus offset (464-500 ms). These results not only replicate the previous findings that the PMC is engaged in categorical prosodic processing but also suggest that it may involve multiple processing stages.1 In the other fROIs, except right STC (845-890 ms), perceived prosodic categories were not successfully decoded.

Conclusion

This study provides a direct link between the motor areas involved in pitch production and categorical prosodic processing, supporting traditional views that the motor system contributes to speech perception in an effector-specific manner.2
