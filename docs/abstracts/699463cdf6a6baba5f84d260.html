Introduction

The cardiovascular system adjusts cardiac parameters to changing internal and external demands via a network of baroreceptors, brainstem, hypothalamus, and the central autonomic network (CAN).

In the predictive brain concept, the CAN operates in hierarchical loops: top-down predictions are compared with interoceptive signals. The anterior cingulate cortex monitors heartbeat regularity and signals prediction errors, which are relayed bottom-up to update the internal model.¹

Despite experimental support for these concepts, their role in heartbeat regulation and consequences of failure remain unclear. Premature heartbeats—early heart contractions—are the most common arrhythmia, affecting 60% of healthy people. Premature contractions (PCs) originate in the ventricles (PVC) or atria (PAC). PVCs may cause abnormal QRS morphology and reduced stroke volume, while PACs usually show fewer hemodynamic changes. Frequent PCs, though often asymptomatic, are linked to increased heart disease risk.

Though clinically relevant, neural events linked to PCs remain poorly understood. An approach to assess neural activity associated with each heartbeat is to simultaneously record EEG and ECG to recover the heartbeat-evoked potentials (HEPs).2 HEPs have been linked to prediction errors associated with exteroceptive stimuli. Here, the brain’s predictive mechanism extended beyond exteroceptive anticipation to include interoceptive input from the regular heartbeat, which it interprets as a predictable event.3 In this study, we aimed to identify neural activity which corresponds to disruptions in the regular heartbeat pattern, as in premature contractions. With access to a unique dataset of simultaneous ECG/EEG recordings from over 3,400 individuals in a population-based study in Leipzig, we identified 103 with a high prevalence of PACs or PVCs.4 In these, we assessed neural activity linked to the premature and subsequent beats, comparing them to regular heartbeats during sinus rhythm.

Methods

Participants
Participants were drawn from the LIFE-Adult population-based study in Leipzig. From 3492 individuals with simultaneous ECG and EEG, we identified 103 participants (51 PAC, 52 PVC) who met the criteria: ≥25 isolated PAC or PVC beats, no pacemaker use, and exclusion of those with frequent arrhythmias or significant cardiac abnormalities. ECG patterns were detected using CER-S software.

Recordings
Resting-state EEG (20 min) was recorded in a shielded booth using 34 passive Ag/AgCl electrodes (31 scalp), following the 10-20 system with impedance below 10 kΩ. Eye movements and ECG were simultaneously captured. Signals were amplified, referenced to common average, sampled at 1000 Hz, and low-pass filtered at 280 Hz.

ECG Processing
ECGs were processed with CER-S and custom scripts. Detected PACs/PVCs and sinus beats were visually inspected. Participants were excluded if P-waves were unclear, arrhythmia was present, or artefacts interfered with rhythm identification. For PVCs, the highest deflection was taken as the R-peak. R-peak timepoints were imported as events into EEGLAB/FieldTrip. Only isolated PCs and sinus beats without EEG artefacts were analysed. Beats were labelled as PC, PC-1 to PC-3 (preceding), PC+1 to PC+2 (following), and N (sinus beat) for the combined PAC and PVC groups. For analyses looking into the PAC and PVC groups individually, the labels were adapted accordingly, e.g., PVC-1. After quality control, 1443 PAC and 1622 PVC beats were included, resulting in an average of 28.86±16.64 PCs per person for PAC and 31.19±15.03 for PVC.

EEG Processing
EEG preprocessing used custom scripts and EEGLAB/FieldTrip. Data were bandpass filtered (0.5–20 Hz) and downsampled to 500 Hz. ECG events were marked in both EEG and ECG traces. Independent component analysis (extended Infomax) was applied to epochs (−200 to 200 ms around R-peaks) to remove artefacts (ocular, muscle, heartbeat). Components with >0.8 correlation to ECG were flagged and removed after manual review (mean 1.36 ± 0.86; range [0, 4]) cardiac field components per participant).

HEP Analysis
The EEG was segmented (−200 to 800 ms around R-peaks) and averaged for HEP analysis.
To alleviate the impact of the PC-1 beat on PC due to the shortened interbeat interval (IBI), the averaged N beat was subtracted from each PC-1 beat before averaging. Baseline correction used the time window of −150 to −50 ms before beat PC-3. Analyses were repeated with alternative baseline settings and on non-ICA-corrected data to test robustness.
The third beat before the PC (PC-3) was used as the reference condition. Cluster-based permutation tests (described in Statistical Analysis) confirmed no significant ECG or EEG differences between PC-3 and regular beats (N), even after matching epoch counts, while PC-2 and PC-1 differed from N (p < 0.05). Thus, PC-3 provided a suitable reference, ensuring a comparable signal-to-noise ratio and controlling for spontaneous brain activity.
No significant difference was found between PC+2 and the reference (p > 0.05), suggesting brain activity normalises two beats after the PC.

Statistical Analysis
Within-participant effects were tested via cluster-based permutation t-tests (FieldTrip), clustering adjacent samples with t-values above threshold (p<0.05, two-sided). Monte Carlo permutation (5000 iterations) addressed multiple comparisons. Significant clusters (p<0.05, ≥2 adjacent electrodes) were identified for all EEG electrodes and −200 to 800 ms window. Temporal cluster-based permutation tests were also applied to ECG and between-participant comparisons.

Source Space Analysis
Source analysis used the New York Head model and eLORETA. The lead field included 5004 dipoles; channels PO5/PO6 were excluded. Analyses tested different regularisation values and compared standard vs. sign-flip averaging. Source activity was averaged in the window of main effects. Ten bilateral ROIs relevant to HEP were examined with FDR-corrected dependent t-tests.

Results

HEP Analysis
We examined how premature contractions (PCs) influence brain activity by comparing HEPs around PCs and the subsequent beat (PC+1) to a reference (PC-3).
PC
Cluster-based permutation tests showed significant HEP reductions in PCs relative to the reference, with negative (central-occipital, p < 0.0132) and positive (central-frontal, p < 0.0002) clusters at 230–330 ms. Analyses of PVC and PAC were performed separately to identify the main contributors to this effect.
PVC
PVCs versus reference showed similar significant clusters (negative central-occipital, p < 0.0002; positive central-frontal, p < 0.0008; 220–350 ms), mirroring PC findings.
Given the variable morphology of PVCs and their potential to introduce artefacts in EEG, analyses were repeated on participant subsets defined by T-wave polarity. Both subgroups confirmed significant differences (p < 0.05), supporting the robustness of the findings.
PAC
No significant effect was found for PACs alone (p > 0.05), indicating that PVCs primarily drove the observed results. Therefore, subsequent analyses focused on the PVC condition.
PC+1
PC+1 showed significantly different HEPs compared with the reference (negative central-frontal, positive central-occipital clusters, 130–200 ms, p < 0.002), with earlier and larger HEPs.
PVC+1
PVC+1 versus reference showed significant clusters from 140–210 ms (both p < 0.0002), matching the PC+1 pattern.
PAC+1
PAC+1 versus reference also showed significant negative (central-occipital, p < 0.0002) and positive (central-frontal, p < 0.008) clusters (120–180 ms).
Combining PAC+1 and PVC+1
Because PVC+1 and PAC+1 showed similar effects, a direct comparison revealed no significant differences (p > 0.05). Thus, further analyses were conducted on the PC+1 condition to improve statistical power.
Multiverse Analysis
Multiverse analyses using various preprocessing choices showed consistent significant differences for PVC, PC+1, PAC+1, and PVC+1 versus the reference, demonstrating robust findings.
Source Space Analysis

Source reconstruction was applied to the averaged HEP (PVC, PC+1 vs. PC-3) within the time windows exhibiting the most pronounced differences. 

PVC
PVC versus reference showed a main HEP difference in left insula (pFDR < 0.0406), with effects in postcentral gyri, frontal orbital gyri, and right ACC in some pipelines.
PC+1
PC+1 versus PC-3 showed significant HEP changes in the left frontal orbital cortex and bilateral ACC (pFDR < 0.035), with additional effects in the postcentral gyri, the right frontal orbital cortex, and the insula in some pipelines.
Comparing the time course of PC+1 sources to PC-3 revealed that the ACC was more active in PC+1 across most pipelines (30/42; pFDR < 0.05), indicating a distinct neural source configuration for the response at PC+1.


ECG Analysis
Further, ECGs from PVC and PC+1 were examined to determine whether cardiac field artefacts could explain HEP differences.PVC
For PVC vs. PVC-3, significant ECG differences appeared before the R-peak (−120 to −100 ms, p < 0.0144), around the T-wave (90 to 10 ms, p < 0.0096), and after (410 to 560 ms, p < 0.0012).
PC+1
ECG in PC+1 vs. PC-3 differed from −200 to 600 ms (p < 0.023). Notably, the T-wave in PC+1 was delayed and smaller, whereas the HEP peak appeared earlier and larger, suggesting that ECG differences alone do not explain the HEP findings.

Conclusion

Key FindingsTwo key changes in neural signatures were found during premature contractions compared to the normal sinus rhythm. Heartbeat-evoked activity was reduced during premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), especially in the left insula. Evoked potentials were earlier and stronger during the beat following both premature ventricular and atrial contractions, mainly in the orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate cortices (OFC, ACC).
Diminished neural activity during PVCsThe insular cortex processes internal sensations, including the sensation of the heartbeat. Baroreceptors detect changes in blood pressure and signal the brain, but this reflex is weakened during PVCs due to reduced stroke volume and lower blood pressure. This disruption in afferent neural signals from the baroreceptors likely explains the decreased insular activity. PACs are less disruptive, so neural responses may remain mostly intact, consistent with our findings.
Early and increased evoked response during the beat following the PCAfter a premature contraction, the following heartbeat elicits an earlier and stronger neural response, especially in the OFC and ACC. While postextrasystolic potentiation (increased blood pressure, stroke volume, and contractility) may partly explain this, our source analysis suggests that additional mechanisms are at play. The ACC was almost exclusively active in this early window after premature contractions, a pattern not seen during sinus rhythm, indicating regular activity amplification does not fully explain these changes.

An additional neural mechanism may be involved, possibly explained by the predictive brain framework. The brain anticipates the regular heartbeat pattern, so disruptions due to premature contractions create prediction errors that require updating internal models.3 This mismatch may explain the stronger sensation of the next heartbeat and the involvement of regions (OFC, ACC) known for processing interoceptive prediction errors. Prior studies on cross-modal predictive processing support this, and our work extends these concepts to interoceptive cardiac stimuli, showing mismatch-like responses to purely interoceptive cardiac signals for the first time.5
Details of prediction errors in heartbeat sequences remain unresolved. It is unclear if neural changes relate more to the compensatory pause or the heightened response of the next beat. However, the lack of strong neural responses during premature contraction and ongoing sympathetic activation suggests the brain anticipates the next heartbeat. These prediction errors may alert individuals to internal threats, possibly explaining the anxiety often experienced with premature contractions.
Methodological ConsiderationsWe addressed methodological challenges in HEP analysis using a multiverse pipeline. To control cardiac artefacts, each premature and subsequent heartbeat was compared with reference beats from the same participant. Remaining artefacts were reduced using ICA, and analyses were stratified by ECG polarity. Although our study averaged fewer epochs than typical HEP studies, significant differences were still detected, underscoring the strength of our results. Additional ECG channels could further improve artefact control in future studies.
SummaryOur findings support computational models of heartbeat regulation, highlighting the insula, OFC, and ACC in the processing and prediction of cardiac signals. Transient disruptions in heartbeat produce substantial neural effects, underscoring the need for further research.
