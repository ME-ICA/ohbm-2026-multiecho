Introduction

Developmental Language Disorder (DLD; formerly specific language impairment), is a neurodevelopmental disorder with deficits in expressive and/or receptive language skills not attributable to other causes like hearing loss, intellectual disability, neurological damage, or a lack of early exposure to language. This heritable disorder affects ~7.6% of the population (2 children per classroom), but is virtually unknown. Children with DLD grow up to be adults with DLD who lack the necessary support to navigate tasks and challenges that heavily rely on language. In fact, research has shown that there are high rates of adults with DLD among incarcerated individuals, potentially due in part to the strong language skills needed to navigate the criminal justice system. This highlights how the lack of fundamental knowledge about DLD significantly affects those living with the disorder. An area of research that is particularly lacking is in the neural substrates underlying the disorder 1.

There are a handful of studies that characterize white matter in children with DLD (reviewed here1). As might be predicted, altered microstructure (fractional anisotropy; FA) has been found in left hemisphere language-related tracts and their right hemisphere homologues, including the arcuate fasciculus (AF), inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), and uncinate fasciculus (UF). Language tracts typically show reduced FA, but occasionally a right hemisphere homologue shows increased FA2. One study found reduced FA outside of the traditional language tracts in the genu of the corpus callosum3.

Here we extend the study of white matter in DLD to adulthood. Focusing on adults provides a controlled and stable context for studying the language system because of the relatively fixed nature of language abilities and, importantly, myelination in young adults, thereby informing both developmental models and clinical approaches to language disorders.

Methods

Participants included 43 adults, 20 of whom met criteria for DLD and 23 of whom were determined to have typical language. The cohort with typical language (TL) ranged from 18-32 years of age (M=21; SD=3.21), and the DLD cohort ranged from 18-38 years of age (M=23.1; SD=4.95). The TL group had 19 women and 4 men; the DLD group had 15 women, 4 men, and 1 genderqueer person. Participants reported having acquired English before age two, no history of neurological impairments (e.g., concussion or brain injury), no other reported neurodevelopmental disorders (e.g., ASD, ADHD, etc.) or genetic disorders, normal hearing, and no contraindications to MRI. Measures of nonverbal IQ were collected with matrix reasoning and block design from the WASI-II. Language status was determined with the Fidler protocol adjusted for online evaluation; participants were asked to spell unusual words (e.g., accommodations, peculiar) and to follow complex auditory instructions (e.g., “click the square above the yellow circle and then the green circle”). Performance was entered into a weighted equation to determine typical language development (<0) or DLD (>0).

MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma with a 64-channel head and neck coil at the University of Washington. Diffusion-weighted data were acquired with the HCP-Lifespan (development) acquisition protocol: data were acquired using a two-shell protocol with diffusion weightings centered at b=1500 and b=3000 s/mm². The acquisition included 47 diffusion directions at b≈1500 s/mm² and 46 diffusion directions at b≈3000 s/mm², along with 7 non-diffusion-weighted volumes, collected with anterior–posterior phase encoding. Additional non-diffusion-weighted images were acquired with reversed posterior–anterior phase encoding and used for susceptibility-induced distortion correction.

All processing took place in FSL. Susceptibility-induced distortions were corrected using topup, based on non-diffusion-weighted images acquired with opposing anterior–posterior and posterior–anterior phase-encoding directions. Topup was run using the default FSL (b02b0.cnf) configuration, and the corrected b=0 images were averaged to generate a high signal-to-noise reference image. 

Eddy current-induced distortions and subject motion were corrected using eddy, incorporating the topup field estimates. Eddy was run with outlier replacement enabled and with slice-to-volume motion correction using a six-parameter model. Gradient directions were rotated accordingly, and the corrected diffusion-weighted images, b-values, and b-vectors were propagated to downstream analyses. Diffusion tensor models were fit using only diffusion-weighted volumes from the b≈1500 s/mm² shell together with non-diffusion-weighted images. Tensor fitting was performed using dtifit, yielding voxelwise maps of fractional anisotropy (FA).

Voxelwise statistical analysis of white matter FA was performed using Tract-Based Spatial Statistics (TBSS). For each participant, FA maps derived from the b≈1500 s/mm² shell were used. FA images were pre-processed using tbss_1_preproc, followed by nonlinear registration of individual FA images to standard space using tbss_2_reg with the FMRIB58_FA template as the registration target. The registered FA images were merged and used to compute a mean FA image and mean FA skeleton using tbss_3_postreg, with the skeleton derived from the study cohort. The mean FA skeleton was thresholded at FA ≥ 0.2. Individual FA data were projected onto the skeleton using tbss_4_prestats. Group differences in skeletonized FA were assessed using permutation-based nonparametric testing as implemented in FSL randomise. Statistical inference was performed with 5000 permutations using threshold-free cluster enhancement with the --T2 flag as recommended for voxelwise inference on TBSS outputs.

Results

Visual inspection of the automated eddy quality control showed no group differences in motion. There were no group differences in whole-brain FA (t = 0.037, df = 39, p-value = 0.97). 

TBSS showed widespread differences in FA in white matter, all in the direction of FA in DLD lower than TL (see Figure 1). The most obvious differences are in commissural fibers, including the corpus callosum (CC), CC projecting to forceps minor (anterior), and CC projecting to forceps major (posterior). There are differences in association fibers often related to language processing in the left hemisphere, including the left superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), left inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF) or possibly uncinate fasciculus (UF), and left frontal aslant tract (FAT). There appear to be bilateral differences in the inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF). Finally, there are group differences in bilateral projection fibers, including the internal capsule and corona radiata. 

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that adults with DLD have widespread reductions in white-matter FA relative to their peers, despite no group differences in whole-brain FA. This extends prior work on white matter organization in DLD by demonstrating that microstructural differences persist into adulthood, even when language skills are sufficient for complex tasks – for example, all but one participant in this study attended college. Behavioral assays similarly indicated that this population can complete even complex language tasks, although often taking more time and (according to some self-report) effort.

Consistent with findings in children, group differences were observed in left hemisphere association pathways commonly implicated in language processing, including the SLF, IFOF, and FAT. These tracts support phonological processing, semantic integration, and speech initiation, and their continued alteration in adulthood suggests that atypical development of language-relevant pathways does not normalize at the neural level over time. In addition, bilateral differences in the ILF point to broader involvement of ventral visual-temporal pathways, which may relate to difficulties in lexical access or multimodal language processing in DLD.

The most prominent effects were observed in commissural fibers that are not specific to language, particularly the corpus callosum and its projections to forceps minor and forceps major. Alterations in these pathways suggest disrupted interhemispheric communication. Group differences were also evident in bilateral projection fibers, including the internal capsule and corona radiata, indicating that differences in white matter extend beyond cortico-cortical association tracts to pathways linking cortex with subcortical and brainstem structures. Together, these findings support a distributed systems account of DLD in which language difficulties arise from altered coordination across large-scale neural networks.

Alterations in corpus callosum microstructure have been implicated not only in DLD but also in developmental dyslexia4, pointing toward a potential transdiagnostic role for interhemispheric connectivity in language and reading disorders -- or possibly indicating that our measure of DLD in adulthood is not selective to DLD only, but included many participants with concomitant DLD and dyslexia. Although participants did not appear to have frank reading impairment, additional behavioral assays would be needed to distinguish between these interpretations. Regardless, these results indicate that interhemispheric connectivity is important for complex functions including oral language production and reading. These results are also in accordance with some accounts of DLD that indicate that complex non-linguistic tasks are impaired, suggesting a domain-general difficulty (such as processing speed) that manifests largely in language due to its complexity.

By focusing on adults, the present work provides a stable snapshot of white matter organization after the major developmental changes in myelination have largely plateaued before declining in middle and late adulthood.6 This adult phenotype offers an important complement to developmental studies and suggests that neural differences associated with DLD are enduring rather than transient “delays”. Our recent work using fMRI in this same cohort suggests that adults with DLD recruit domain-general mechanisms even to accomplish simple listening tasks; when combined with this study, that indicates that behavioral improvements in language skills are the result of compensatory efforts, rather than simply delayed maturation (an enduring theoretical account of DLD). Results have implications for theoretical models of atypical language development as well as for clinical approaches, highlighting the need for continued support for individuals with DLD beyond childhood.
