Introduction

Epilepsy affects approximately 70 million people worldwide with one-third of patients experiencing drug-resistant seizures that cannot be controlled by medication.1 For these patients, resection surgery targeting the seizure onset zone (SOZ) is the most effective treatment. However, surgical success rates remain below 50%, as accurate SOZ localization remains a significant clinical challenge.2 Current SOZ localization relies on neuroimaging, video EEG, and functional assessments. Ictal SPECT is frequently used in seizure monitoring units and provides information about seizure-related perfusion changes but is limited by high cost, logistical complexity, and poor spatial resolution, underscoring the need for more accessible and accurate biomarkers.

Both animal models and human studies have demonstrated that seizures induce blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption.3 Recent MRI studies have demonstrated peri-ictal BBB dysfunction in epilepsy patients, though temporal ambiguity regarding ictal versus postictal changes and delayed imaging timing limit clinical utility.4 CT Perfusion (CTP) can quantify BBB permeability through the Permeability-Surface Area (PS) product with improved spatial resolution in comparison to SPECT. CTP offers other advantages including widespread availability, rapid scanning within 60 minutes post-seizure, and whole-brain coverage. CTP has also been used previously to demonstrate focal postictal blood flow changes using subtraction maps. 

This pilot study investigates whether acute postictal BBB permeability changes can be quantified using CTP as a potential SOZ biomarker and provides quantitative analysis of postictal blood flow changes previously observed qualitatively.5 To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to quantify BBB permeability dynamics in the immediate postictal period in humans using CTP. We hypothesize that in focal seizures, postictal BBB permeability will be elevated within the SOZ compared to interictal baseline.

Methods

A quantitative retrospective analysis was performed on five adults (N=5; 3 female, 2 male) with drug-resistant focal epilepsy admitted to the Foothills Medical Centre seizure-monitoring unit. Participants underwent a dual-scan whole-brain CT Perfusion (CTP) protocol: a postictal scan acquired within 60 minutes following a video-EEG confirmed seizure and a baseline interictal scan acquired after a video-EEG verified seizure-free period (≥24 hours). The CTP protocol employed a second-pass technique, which enabled measurement of contrast agent extravasation and quantification of blood-brain barrier permeability.

Whole-brain CTP images were acquired using a GE Healthcare Revolution CT scanner with 5mm slice thickness (2x2mm in-plane resolution) and reconstructed at 1.25mm thickness. The scanning protocol consisted of 45mL iodinated contrast agent (Omnipaque, GE Healthcare) power-injected at 6mL/s followed by a 40mL saline chase. Scanning began 5 seconds after contrast injection and continued every 2.8 seconds for 75 seconds. CTP data were analyzed using an in-house perfusion analysis program developed by Dr. Ting-Yim Lee at the University of Western Ontario. Quantitative perfusion parameters were calculated using model-dependent deconvolution based on the Johnson-Wilson-Lee kinetic model with non-negative least squares optimization to constrain physiological parameters. This approach enables simultaneous quantification of cerebral blood flow (CBF; mL/100g/min), cerebral blood volume (CBV; mL/100g), and the Permeability-Surface Area product (PS; mL/100g/min), which quantifies contrast extravasation across the blood-brain barrier.

Regions of interest (ROI) targeting the seizure onset zone were defined by an epileptologist (PF) based on multimodal clinical data from the specific seizure event captured for CTP scanning. Available localizing data included video-EEG, ictal SPECT, PET, and clinical semiology, though not all modalities were available for every patient. Seizure onset zones were distributed across temporal and fronto-temporal regions (4 temporal, 1 fronto-temporal), with 3 unilateral left, 1 unilateral right, and 2 bilateral foci. Voxel-wise masks were generated for the ROI, non-ROI ipsilateral hemisphere, and contralateral regions. Identical ROIs were applied to both baseline and postictal scans to extract quantitative perfusion values. Regional statistics were calculated as voxel-weighted aggregates. Postictal changes were quantified by comparing baseline and postictal values within each region for all three perfusion parameters. To account for inter-subject physiological variability and isolate focal changes from global effects, Z-scores were calculated by normalizing ROI values against whole-slice distributions at each time point.

Results

Analysis of ROI perfusion parameters revealed consistent postictal changes in blood-brain barrier permeability despite concurrent blood flow alterations (Figure 1). All five patients demonstrated decreased cerebral blood flow within the ROI postictally (mean ROI CBF: 33.34±5.37 mL/100g/min baseline vs. 27.35±4.39 mL/100g/min postictal), with individual decreases ranging from -0.74 to -13.82 mL/100g/min. Blood volume changes were variable and modest across subjects (mean ROI CBV: 2.65±0.35 mL/100g baseline vs. 2.47±0.69 mL/100g postictal).

In contrast, blood-brain barrier permeability increased postictally in four of five patients (mean ROI PS: 0.35±0.13 mL/100g/min baseline vs. 0.46±0.14 mL/100g/min postictal), with individual increases ranging from +0.03 to +0.37 mL/100g/min. Median PS values increased in three patients while remaining stable or decreasing in two patients (median ROI PS: 0.38±0.08 mL/100g/min baseline vs. 0.36±0.09 mL/100g/min postictal).

Z-score normalization revealed heterogeneous spatial patterns. Z-scored PS analysis showed consistent increases across all patients (Z-score mean PS: -0.18±0.42 SD baseline vs +0.15±0.30 SD postictal), with individual increases ranging from +0.02 to +1.22 standard deviations. Two patients (CTP017, CTP032) demonstrated focal PS increases within the ROI relative to whole-brain distributions, with shifts of +0.16 and +0.89 standard deviations, respectively, while others showed concurrent whole-brain changes. Z-scored CBF analysis showed variable patterns, with some patients exhibiting ROI-specific decreases and others showing generalized reductions with mean Z-scored CBF decreased for four of the five patients (Z-score mean CBF: -0.17±0.07 SD baseline vs -0.23±0.06 SD postictal), with individual shifts ranging from -0.22 to +0.01 standard deviations. These patterns demonstrate concurrent but independent changes in perfusion and permeability parameters within the seizure onset zone.

Conclusion

This pilot study reveals a dissociation between blood-brain barrier permeability and cerebral blood flow in the acute postictal period. Four of five patients demonstrated increased BBB permeability despite universal decreases in blood flow. This dissociation suggests that postictal BBB breakdown represents a distinct pathophysiological mechanism independent of hemodynamic changes, potentially driven by seizure-induced inflammatory cascades or direct neuronal-vascular signaling rather than flow-mediated vascular stress.3

The heterogeneous responses across individual perfusion parameters (CBF, CBV, PS) indicate that multiparametric analysis may provide superior diagnostic accuracy compared to single-parameter approaches. Previous work from our laboratory generated qualitative subtraction maps from blood flow changes alone. The current quantitative multiparametric approach may better capture complex neurovascular changes following seizures and improve seizure onset zone localization.
