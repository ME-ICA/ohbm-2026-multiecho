Introduction

Brain activity, measured with fMRI, is shaped by constraints operating across structural and biological layers of information. While structural connectomes derived from diffusion MRI are generally used to approximate the spatial organisation of functional fluctuations, recent studies suggest that a much broader set of morphological, molecular, genetic, and cellular cortical gradients influences functional dynamics.1–3 To date, no study has systematically compared how these cortical features constrain functional dynamics nor the extent to which ageing affects these constraints.

In this study, we apply a multiscale eigendecomposition framework  on white-matter structural connectivity (SC), cortical similarity networks, neurotransmitter receptor density, gene coexpression, and cell-type density to determine how each axis contributes to the reconstruction of resting-state BOLD activity. We also quantify the reconstruction capacity of each modality and assess how these constraints change with ageing in older adults. This framework provides a data-driven approach to determining which underlying layers most strongly determine functional activity in older adults.

Methods

Participants

We used data from the MYPAD-PNHS4 with concurrent functional MRI and amyloid PET available. Global cortical amyloid burden was assessed using the Centiloid scale. For the present analyses, we included 480 cognitively unimpaired individuals (Aβ- (centiloid < 24.1), clinical dementia rating (CDR) 0).

fMRI Preprocessing

Rs-fMRI images were pre-processed using fMRIPrep v23.0.1, and the “simple+gsr” denoising strategy was applied as recommended by Wang et al. 2024.5 The brain was parcellated into 100 parcels using the Schaefer atlas. For each parcel, mean BOLD time series (functional signals) were extracted, normalised using standard scaling, and concatenated across time.

Multimodal Brain Network Data

We generated group-level connectomes for five complementary modalities, each mapped to the Schaefer-100 atlas for cross-modal comparison:

  • Structural Connectivity

A normative diffusion MRI connectome from the HCP summarising group-average white-matter fibre strength.

  • Cortical Similarity

Averaged Morphometric INverse Divergence (MIND)–derived cortical similarity network from cognitively unimpaired AMYPAD participants, capturing inter-regional similarities in cortical morphology.

  • Receptor Density Similarity

A neurotransmitter receptor-based connectome from normative PET receptor neuromaps, using pairwise correlations between regional receptor profiles.

  • Gene Expression Similarity

AHBA gene-expression profiles processed with abagen, yielding a gene expression similarity matrix from regional expression correlations.

  • Cell-Type Density Similarity

Cytoarchitectural similarity networks generated from BigBrain cell-type density estimates, capturing cortical microstructural correspondence across regions.

Graph Eigendecomposition and Eigenmode Extraction

For each modality-specific connectome, we constructed the corresponding graph Laplacian to characterise its intrinsic properties. The Laplacian provides a multiscale harmonic basis whose eigenmodes describe increasingly fine-grained patterns of spatial variation across the cortex. Low-frequency eigenmodes capture broad, spatially smooth gradients of network organisation, whereas higher-frequency modes represent more localised, rapidly varying patterns. We performed a graph Fourier transform to obtain the full ordered spectrum of eigenmodes; the first five (ψ₁–ψ₅), representing the smoothest large-scale modes (Figure 1A).

Functional Signal Reconstruction

Functional signals were reconstructed by expressing parcel-wise BOLD time series as weighted combinations of graph Laplacian eigenmodes. For each modality, regional fMRI time series were projected onto their full eigenbasis, and reconstructions were generated using progressively larger subsets of modes (k = 1…N). At each step, we computed the reconstructed functional connectivity (FC) matrix from the mode-limited time series and quantified correspondence with the empirical FC using Pearson correlation of vectorised upper-triangular elements. This resulted in a reconstruction accuracy curve for every subject and modality, summarising how well low- to high-frequency graph modes explained observed functional coupling patterns (Figure 1B). 

Reconstruction efficiency was quantified using three metrics derived from each subject’s normalised reconstruction curve: Area Under the Curve (AUC), indexing overall reconstruction capacity; Half-Reconstruction Point (k₅₀), the number of modes needed to reach 50% reconstruction (lower = faster capture); and Early Reconstruction Slope (Slope₁₀), the mean increase in reconstruction across the first 10 modes, reflecting early steepness and large-scale alignment. 

Assessing constraint changes with age

We used linear models to investigate the effects of age on AUC, k₅₀, and Slope₁₀ per modality. All p-values were Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) corrected across modalities and metrics. Associations were considered significant at pFDR < 0.05.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

A total of 480 cognitively unimpaired older adults were included (mean age: 64.68 ± 7.22 years; 59% female; mean years of education: 14.86 ± 4.17; mean MMSE: 29.17 ± 0.99).

Distinct Eigenmodes Across Modalities

The five modalities produced spatially distinct low-frequency eigenmodes, reflecting unique large-scale gradients embedded in each biological axis (Figure 1A). SC eigenmodes followed well-established hierarchical axes of white-matter organisation. The lowest modes captured bilateral symmetry (ψ₂) and a broad anterior–posterior gradient (ψ₃) that separated unimodal sensorimotor regions from transmodal default-mode territories. ψ₄–ψ₅ captured a lateral–medial organisation. Cortical similarity eigenmodes exhibited smooth anterior–posterior and dorsal–ventral gradients reflecting covariation in cortical morphology, with cingulate and parietal regions emerging as hotspots (ψ₅). Receptor-density similarity eigenmodes showed coherent anterior–posterior and lateral–medial gradients (ψ₂–ψ₅). Gene-expression similarity eigenmodes displayed weaker large-scale gradients but suggested broad anterior–posterior and network-specific transcriptional organisation. Cell-type density similarity modes showed minimal spatial differentiation, even in low-frequency components.

Divergent Functional Reconstruction Profiles Across Modalities

These modality-specific differences in eigenmode structure yielded distinct reconstruction profiles (Figure 1B). Cortical similarity (0.75 ± 0.04), receptor density similarity (0.74 ± 0.04), and SC (0.74 ± 0.04) achieved the highest mean AUC values across subjects, whereas gene expression similarity (0.62 ± 0.03) and cell-type density similarity (0.50 ± 0.02) had lower reconstruction accuracies. (Figure 2A). The k₅₀ metric further reflected these differences. Receptor density similarity (11.15 ± 4.83) and SC (12.67 ± 5.79) required the fewest eigenmodes to explain 50% of the functional signal, followed by cortical similarity (17.98 ± 5.04) and gene expression (22.97 ± 6.67), while cell-type density similarity required approximately half of all available modes (50.13 ± 3.68) (Figure 2A). Early-mode gain (Slope₁₀) was steepest for receptor density similarity (0.053 ± 0.008) and SC (0.050 ± 0.008), moderate for cortical similarity (0.039 ± 0.008) and gene expression similarity (0.037 ± 0.007), and minimal for cell-type density similarity (0.010 ± 0.003) (Figure 2A).

Differential Associations Between Modalities and Ageing in Older Adults

The age of the individuals was associated with the individual reconstruction efficiency (Figure 2B). Older adults had worse reconstruction scores, as indicated by their AUCs (cortical similarity: β = -0.0012, t-statistic = -4.67, pFDR < 0.001; receptor-density similarity eigenmodes: β = -0.0012, t-statistic = -4.57, pFDR < 0.001; SC: β = -0.0006, t-statistic = -2.47, pFDR = 0.0175; gene expression similarity: β = -0.0005, t-statistic = -2.48, pFDR = 0.0175). They also required more modes, i.e., more complex spatial patterns of regional receptor profiles, to achieve reconstruction accuracy comparable to younger individuals for receptor density similarity  (k₅₀: β = 0.1424, t-statistic = 4.76, pFDR < 0.001). Similarly, older adults had lower early-mode contributions for receptor density similarity (Slope₁₀: β = -0.0002, t-statistic = -4.28, pFDR < 0.001).

Conclusion

This study applied a unified multiscale network framework to examine how structural, morphological, molecular, and genetic architecture determine functional brain activity in older adults. By comparing patterns of spatial variation across the cortex derived from five distinct group-level connectomes, we showed that each underlying layer encodes unique large-scale spatial gradients that differentially determine the organisation of resting-state BOLD signals. Receptor density similarity, cortical similarity, and SC exhibited the strongest and most coherent low-frequency gradients, reflecting neuromodulatory receptor distributions, covarying cortical morphology, and hierarchical white-matter structure. These axes emerged as the dominant macroscale scaffold for functional dynamics, with cortical similarity achieving the highest reconstruction accuracy, followed closely by SC and receptor density similarity. The population-average receptor density similarity provided a strong basis for reconstructing individual functional organisation, highlighting that shared molecular architecture alone carries substantial predictive value for large-scale cortical dynamics.

Ageing was associated with reductions in functional reconstruction efficiency, reflected by lower AUC, higher k₅₀, and attenuated Slope₁₀, indicating weaker alignment between functional dynamics and the spatial constraints imposed by underlying cortical architecture. The strongest age-related effects were observed for receptor density similarity and cortical similarity networks, suggesting that ageing disproportionately impacts large-scale molecular and morphometric gradients in addition to classical white-matter organisation. The observed modality-specific ageing signatures further suggest that functional dynamics in older adults become less governed by low-frequency spatially smooth gradients and increasingly shaped by more higher-frequency, localised patterns of organisation. This large-scale effect may reflect distinct sensitivities to age-related alterations in cortical microstructure, receptor distributions, or gene expression processes that reduce the efficiency with which the brain’s intrinsic architecture constrains functional fluctuations. 

Overall, our findings demonstrate that receptor, morphological, and structural axes are the principal determinants of functional signal organisation in healthy older adults and that ageing selectively disrupts these large-scale gradients. This study provides the first comprehensive comparison of multiscale cortical constraints on functional dynamics and offers a powerful framework for identifying the biological pathways through which ageing and potentially neurodegeneration alter brain organisation.
