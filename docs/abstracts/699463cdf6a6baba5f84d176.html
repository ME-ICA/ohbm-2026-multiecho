Introduction

Neural activity unfolds as complex spatiotemporal patterns that are typically captured as neuronal avalanches – threshold-defined events that segment continuous activity into discrete propagation episodes.1 While avalanche research has traditionally focused on scale-free statistics and criticality,1,2 we exploit avalanches to retrieve recurring spatiotemporal propagation patterns from EEG data. Rather than applying global statistics to entire datasets, we identify trackable patterns that emerge reliably across time and individuals. This exploratory approach asks whether meaningful, recurring patterns can be observed in neural recordings.

We apply this framework to resting-state EEG from a Psiconnect study comparing placebo with psilocybin in the same patients across different resting state tasks.3 Psychedelic states are commonly characterized by increased entropy in EEG recordings,4 often interpreted as reflecting more complex brain dynamics. However, without identifying what patterns are actually present, we cannot distinguish between meaningful neural reorganization and simply noisier data. By directly observing and tracking spatiotemporal patterns in psilocybin EEG, we can characterize how recurring structures behave under altered consciousness.

This work offers three contributions: First, we describe a methodological pipeline for identifying recurring spatiotemporal patterns in neural data. Second, we demonstrate how individual avalanches cluster into a repertoire of propagation modes, suggesting some spontaneous brain dynamics may be more stereotyped than their scale-free statistics indicate. Third, by tracking rapid repetitions of the same pattern back-fitted to the original data, we show that psilocybin stabilizes polarity during burst sequences (patterns repeat with consistent positive or negative deflections), while control sessions exhibit high polarity switching (patterns alternate between positive and negative as if the activity rotates in phase space).

Methods

Dataset

Data comprised EEG recordings from 62 participants (PsiConnect study3) divided into mindfulness-trained (n=32, 8-week MBCT program) and control (n=30) groups. Each participant underwent baseline and acute psilocybin (19mg – regardless participant’s weight) sessions, completing four tasks: video viewing, eyes-closed rest, meditation, and music listening. EEG was recorded at 500 Hz using 64-channel BrainAmp system, preprocessed using standard pipeline (re-referencing, 1-40 Hz bandpass, ASR artifact correction, ICA decomposition), and downsampled to 250 Hz. Final dataset: 429 recordings representing approximately 35 hours.

Avalanche Detection

Signals were z-score normalized per electrode. Time points were marked active when at least one electrode exceeded ±3 z-scored standard deviations. Avalanches were defined as continuous supra-threshold activity lasting ≥40 ms. Spatial filtering retained avalanches where at least one active electrode had ≥5 of 8 nearest neighbors also active (required for both polarities). Avalanche amplitudes were binarized: positive values to +1, negative to -1, zeros to 0. The whole process can be visualized in Figure 1A.

Spatiotemporal Distance and Clustering

For each avalanche pair, we computed spatial distance at each timepoint using unbalanced optimal transport with polarity constraints (positive activations matched only to positive, negative only to negative) via Hungarian algorithm on electrode positions. Temporal alignment used subsequence Dynamic Time Warping (subDTW) allowing shorter avalanches to align to subsequences of longer ones. We applied hierarchical clustering (average linkage, distance threshold=3.5) retaining clusters with ≥30 members. How we apply subDTW on the optimal transport matrix is visualized in Figure 1B.

Pattern Validation

For each cluster, we identified the medoid (avalanche with minimum summed distance to all members) and constructed consensus patterns by warping all members to the medoid timeline. We validated patterns by back-fitting to continuous recordings using spatiotemporal correlation: at each timepoint, we computed Pearson correlation between spatially-smoothed patterns. High-correlation activations (|ρ| > 0.825) were identified via sliding window analysis. Burst detection only analyzed those activities where at least 2 clusters were identified, immediately after each other.

Results

Recurring Spatiotemporal Patterns

From all detected avalanches, we identified 12 distinct recurring patterns using hierarchical clustering. Each cluster exhibited a unique spatiotemporal pattern with characteristic propagation from specific cortical regions. Cluster sizes ranged from 30-100 members, distributed across 17-37 participants per cluster. Patterns appeared across all experimental conditions (control: 48-73% per cluster; psilocybin: 27-52%; all tasks represented), demonstrating universality across brain states. The final propagations that were fitted back can be downloaded as a video in the following link: https://github.com/filipnovicky/universal_avalanches

Psilocybin Stabilizes Polarity Patterns

Backfitting identified 985 bursts (rapid repetitions of same pattern) across all 12 clusters. All bursts consisted of 2-5 activations (mean=2.15). Critically, psilocybin dramatically altered burst polarity structure. During control sessions, bursts were evenly split: 274 (53.9%) stable (consistent polarity across activations: [+,+,+] or [-,-,-]) versus 234 (46.1%) switching (alternating polarities: [+,-,+]). Under psilocybin, this shifted to 423 (88.7%) stable versus 54 (11.3%) switching. Altogether, there is a 34.8 % increase in stability under psychedelics. This effect was robust across tasks: meditation showed increase from 52.8% to 89.7% stable; music from 71.4% to 89.2%; resting state from 21.2% to 85.4% (most dramatic shift). Specifically, clusters 5 and 6 were most represented as stable clusters. Cluster 5 was found 214 times (to have at least 2 consequential burst activities), and cluster 6 was found 373 times, out of which 350 were stable (94%). These clusters are visualized in Figure 2A and 2B. These clusters were mostly found in the psilocybin group, specifically 68% (395 out of 584). Contrary, Clusters 10 and 11 (Figure 2C and 2D, respectively) were almost only alternating (i.e., switching correlation polarities). Out of 233 (133 for cluster 10 and 100 for cluster 11), only 2 were stable, and that was in cluster 10. Only 41 out of 233 (18%) identified bursts came from psilocybin group. In addition, alternating clusters 10 and 11 are particularly interesting, as i) they are mirroring each other; and 2) as they are almost found to alternate from positive to negative correlations, this results in a rotating wave. Contrary, clusters 5 and 6 result in a stable rostral-caudal activity.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that neural avalanches in human EEG cluster into a finite repertoire of recurring spatiotemporal propagation modes that can be reliably tracked across individuals and conditions. These patterns are – at least some – not statistical artifacts but genuine neural structures validated through direct observation in continuous recordings. Psilocybin administration fundamentally altered the temporal dynamics of these patterns, selecting those activities that increase stable clusters, while inhibiting the ones that alter quickly. This methodology has been developed in order to find recurring spatiotemporal patterns, where the goal of clustering is not to show that each cluster that is found is meaningful, but to see whether we can detect at least some neural propagation activities from the data. What justifies our finding might be the symmetry of cluster 10 and 11, and the fact that these rotates, and that they show distinct properties under psilocybin. The methodology finds its biggest weakness in i) arbitrary choice of the temporal length of the clusters; and ii) backfitting it back to the data using correlation. The former misses more genuine spatiotemporal properties of EEG activity; and the latter under-represents already clustered activities that could be found in the data.
