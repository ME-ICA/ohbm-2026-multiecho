Introduction

Isolated word meanings are inherently uncertain, but this uncertainty reduces when they are anchored by context in sentences. We propose meaning uncertainty is optimized through grammar in a way that generalizes cross-linguistically, is reflected in brain, and is selectively disrupted in disorders. Using naturalistic narratives from 20 languages, we first quantified uncertainty reduction as the proportional decrease from non-contextual surprisal (estimated from lexical frequencies) to contextual surprisal (from grammar-sensitive large language models). These uncertainty measures were then related to brain activity in two fMRI datasets, one during spontaneous recall of TV episodes and another during naturalistic listening to one recall recording. Finally, we collected a number of datasets containing spontaneous speech samples from different clinical populations. We tested the hypothesis whether mental disorders affecting language function manifest with an attenuation of the uncertainty reduction effect, as modulated by the degree to which language function is affected. 

Methods

We first asked whether contextual information systematically reduces lexical uncertainty across languages. We analyzed naturalistic narrative texts from the StoryDB dataset, which collected a large number of naturalistic narratives from Wikipedia across multiple languages in 14 language families. Sentences were tokenized into individual word tokens. For each token, we computed a non-contextual surprisal value based on its relative lexical frequency using the wordfreq package implemented in Python, and a contextual surprisal value derived from corresponding monolingual grammar-sensitive BERT models. Subtoken-level surprisal from the model were aligned to word tokens and summed, and both non-contextual and contextual surprisal were averaged first within sentences and then across each discourse. To assess the contribution of grammar to this effect, we also exploited a word-order manipulation: For each sentence, we compared surprisal in its original form with surprisal when the word order was reversed, which disrupts grammatical structure while preserving the multiset of words. We next asked to what extent variability in uncertainty reduction can be explained by the two systems involved in grammar: formal syntax and lexical semantics. Three metrics of syntactic complexity were chosen: B2 balance index, subtree-size entropy, and hierarchical dependency distance.

To relate uncertainty reduction to the brain, we compared word-by-word predictors of meaning uncertainty reduction in spontaneous speech using two fMRI datasets, one during spontaneous speech production and another during naturalistic narrative comprehension. The first dataset recruited 16 participants to recall the events of the Sherlock episodes, immediately after they watched them, in an fMRI scanner. The second dataset recruited 18 subjects listening to the recording of the recall by one of the participants from the first dataset. The fMRI images were preprocessed by fMRIprep followed by regression of nuisance signals. Cortical BOLD time series were then extracted using the Schaefer-400 parcellation, and subcortical time series using the Melbourne S1 atlas. Linguistic measures were aligned with the BOLD signals using a canonical hemodynamic response function and standardised within participants. For each parcel and each dataset, we  fitted separate linear mixed-effects models with parcel-wise BOLD signal as the dependent measure, each uncertainty metric as a fixed effect, word-level syllable count as a covariate to control for basic articulatory differences, and random intercepts for participant. 

Finally, we asked whether uncertainty reduction computations are selectively disrupted in clinical populations with clinically significant impairments in language function. In total, we analyzed spontaneous speech from nine clinical cohorts, comparing the clinical groups to healthy controls and correlating the meaning uncertainty to clinical and neuropsychological metrics. The disorders were: post-stroke aphasia, right hemisphere damage (RHD), primary progressive aphasia (PPA), Alzheimer's disease (pAD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD).

Results

A) Across all 20 languages selected, contextual surprisal was consistently lower than frequency-based surprisal. Despite substantial diversity in linguistic typology, there was a robust pattern: uncertainty reduces when predictions are conditioned on grammatical context rather than lexical frequencies alone. Shifting from lexical-conceptual to context-sensitive probabilities captures a broadly universal way in which languages constrain meaning uncertainty. As expected, reversing word order considerably increased the contextual surprisal, consistent with a loss of grammatical predictability. Moreover, the normalized uncertainty reduction, defined as the proportional differences from frequency-based to contextual surprisal, was positively and strongly correlated with the normalized uncertainty reduction from reversed to original form of sentences; meta-analytic Spearman’s ρ=0.963, 95% CI: 0.929 to 0.950). Sentences for which grammar-sensitive models benefited most from context in the original order, were also those that incurred the largest surprisal cost when word order was reversed.

In all 20 languages, uncertainty reduction positively correlated with syntactic complexity indices: the B2 index (meta-analytic Spearman’s ρ=0.213, 95% CI: 0.176 to 0.251), and subtree-size entropy (meta-analytic Spearman’s ρ=0.264, 95% CI: 0.219 to 0.310). Sentences whose dependency trees exhibited more graded and hierarchically diverse branching, achieved greater compression of lexical–conceptual uncertainty. These correlations extended further to dependency optimality (meta-analytic Spearman’s ρ=0.231, 95% CI: 0.188 to 0.275), and mean hierarchical distance (meta-analytic Spearman’s ρ=0.229, 95% CI: 0.184 to 0.274). Greater uncertainty compression was observed in sentences which maintain sufficient hierarchical depth while efficiently packing dependencies in their linear sequences. 

B) (See results figure.) During comprehension, all three uncertainty measures showed reliable but topographically distinct associations with the BOLD signals. Surprisal from both frequency and BERT was negatively related to brain activations, while their differences were positively related. All three measures were related to a wide range of default mode regions (e.g. posterior temporal–parietal regions, posterior cingulate cortex, and parahippocampus gyrus), the superior and inferior frontal control regions, and in the subcortex to the caudate, putamen, and nucleus accumbens. Effects of frequency-based surprisal extended to the early sensory cortices in visual and somatomotor networks and the anterior temporal pole, which functions as a lexical-semantic hub, showing reduced activation when upcoming input was lexically less predictable at the perceptual and conceptual level. Contextual surprisal from BERT and the normalized surprisal differences, showed more restricted relations to cortical activations, and had extended effects to subcortical regions such as basal ganglia (pallidum and putamen), and thalamus. 

During speech production (recall), there were overall less regions related to the surprisal metrics. As before, surprisal from both frequency and BERT was negatively related to brain activations, while their differences were positively related. Frequency-based surprisal was additionally negatively related to BOLD in bilateral precentral and postcentral regions and medial frontal areas, with additional effects extending to the precuneus but no significant effect seen in subcortical regions. Contextual surprisal from BERT and the normalized surprisal differences, additionally engaged the thalamus and caudate. 

C) Findings from 9 different pathologies consolidated the hypothesis of decreased uncertainty reduction in disorders with prominent language and higher-order cognitive impairment, and an attenuation of this effect pattern when language is relatively spared, as in right-hemisphere lesions, ADHD, schizoaffective disorder, and major depression. 

Conclusion

in this study, we quantified linguistic uncertainty as we move from lexical-conceptual meanings in isolation, to referential meanings anchored in context, contrasting frequency-based estimates with LLM-derived surprisal to show the grip of grammar in meaning uncertainty compression. We showed that both uncertainty and its reduction have identifiable neural signatures during naturalistic comprehension and speech production, and whether these computations are selectively disrupted in clinical populations. By linking linguistic, neural, and clinical levels of description, we argue that the new foundational concept of uncertainty optimization via grammar points to a core organizing principle of human language, and a mechanistic target in brain diseases.
