Introduction

The emergence of depressive symptoms presents a critical opportunity for early intervention1. A robust and precise neurobiological characterization of these early symptoms could therefore serve as a critical biomarker to inform targeted intervention. Structural brain alterations, particularly alterations in cortical thickness, are widely implicated in a range of psychiatric disorders2 and therefore represent a promising neuroimaging modality for such neurobiological markers. The present work aims to investigate the neural signatures of depressive symptoms in cortical thickness and whether these associations change over time. To comprehensively analyze the high-dimensional neuroimaging data and assess subtle brain-behavior relationships, we employed machine learning. Specifically, we utilized a brain-based predictive modeling approach using cortical thickness data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study from multiple timepoints to establish the neurobiological basis of depressive symptoms.

Methods

We included 8943 participants at baseline (4276 females), 6305 at 2-year follow-up (2913 females), 4222 at 4-year follow-up (1957 females), and 3147 at 6-year follow-up (1485 females) with complete and quality-controlled neuroimaging, demographic and depressive disorders data, excluding siblings. Cortical thickness data was based on the Destrieux Atlas. For each participant at each time point, we summed up all depressive symptoms (past & present) from the self-reported KSADS Depressive Disorders questionnaire. We ran descriptive statistical analyses to assess the number of reported depressive symptoms at each timepoint. We then trained brain-based predictive models using linear ridge regression algorithms and k-fold cross validation to quantify the relationship between cortical thickness and the symptoms. Finally, we evaluated the brain regions the depressive symptoms mapped onto. To this end, we transformed feature weights obtained from the predictive models using the Haufe transformation.

Results

Youth reported a mean symptom count of 2.06 (SD = 3.11) at baseline, 1.14 (SD = 3.17) at 2-year follow-up, 2.62 (SD = 4.42) at 4-year follow-up, and 3.08 (SD = 4.85) symptoms at 6-year follow-up. While our brain-based predictive modeling analyses did not show a significant association between cortical thickness and depressive symptoms at baseline (r = 0.021, p = 0.194), cortical thickness significantly predicted the number of depressive symptoms at 2-year follow-up (r = 0.118, p < 0.001), at 4-year follow-up (r = 0.134, p < 0.001), and 6-year follow-up (r = 0.090, p = 0.015). At the 2-year follow-up, we observed mostly negative associations, with the strongest negative associations located bilaterally in the occipital pole, calcarine sulcus, cuneus, lingual gyrus and middle occipital sulcus. Conversely, there were fewer positive associations with their greatest magnitude bilaterally in the transverse temporal sulcus, short insular gyrus as well as the left temporal pole and right insula (Figure 1A). At the 4-year follow-up, the prediction of depressive symptoms based on cortical thickness also demonstrated primarily negative associations. Regions exhibiting  the strongest negative associations were the right superior segment of the circular sulcus of the insula, right superior transverse collateral sulcus, right posterior-ventral part of the cingulate gyrus, left marginal branch of the cingulate gyrus. Fewer positive associations were detected, with the highest magnitudes located bilaterally in the transverse temporal sulcus, left anterior traverse temporal gyrus and right pericallosal sulcus (Figure 1B). For the 6-year follow-up, associations remained predominantly negative, with strongest negative associations in the right interior and superior occipital gyrus, left medial orbital sulcus and middle temporal gyrus, and left posterior-ventral part of the cingulate gyrus. Positive associations were relatively sparse, with strongest ones located in the left sulcus intermedius primar, right orbital part of the inferior frontal gyrus, and right anterior transverse temporal gyrus (Figure 1C). Associations were relatively similar at the 2-year and 4-year follow-up (r = 0.574, p < 0.001), 2-year and 6-year follow-up (r = 0.570, p < 0.001), and 4-year and 6-year follow-up (r = 0.645, p < 0.001).

Conclusion

Our analyses demonstrate that cortical thickness significantly predicts the number of self-reported depressive symptoms in youth across multiple timepoints. All significant predictions were characterized by predominantly negative associations, indicating that a higher number of depressive symptoms is associated with a lower cortical thickness. While the associations were consistent across all three timepoints, their overall magnitude was higher at the 2-year follow-up. This elevated effect could indicate either a stronger underlying association of cortical thickness and depressive symptoms or might be due to variations in statistical power related to sample size for the subsequent timepoints. Our findings align with previous studies demonstrating a negative association between cortical thickness and depressive symptoms in older adults3 and associations between accelerated cortical thinning and emerging depression in adolescents4. Our machine-learning approach leveraged data from a large-scale longitudinal dataset offering a promising approach to extend the work based on smaller samples to predict the onset of depression5 and an early identification of depression.
