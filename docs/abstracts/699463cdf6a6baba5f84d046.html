Introduction

Chronic pain is a leading cause of disability worldwide, yet its management largely remains limited by a "one-size-fits-all" approach1. Because pain is a heterogeneous disorder, arising from varying disruptions in ascending somatosensory processing and descending cognitive control, patients often cycle through multiple ineffective treatments in a trial-and-error fashion before finding relief. To overcome this inefficient approach, objective biomarkers are needed to stratify patients to the intervention that targets their specific pain-profile. The brain’s "rich club," a dense network of highly connected hubs governing global integration, offers a potential window into these system states. In this study, we leveraged the brain's rich club network to explore the mechanisms of three distinct interventions: Duloxetine, a centrally acting serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) thought to enhance descending pain modulation2; Acupressure, a traditional therapy involving targeted somatosensory stimulation3; and Physical Therapy (PT), a movement-based intervention focused on correcting peripheral sensorimotor dysfunction and promoting physical activity4. We tested whether the baseline influence of specific rich-club nodes predicts response to these mechanistically distinct treatments.  

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were analyzed from adults with chronic musculoskeletal low back pain enrolled in a multi-site randomized clinical trial (BACPAC) and randomized to one of three 12-week interventions: duloxetine (n=22), acupressure (n=23), or physical therapy (n=19). Resting-state BOLD fMRI was acquired at the University of Michigan on two different 3T GE scanners and was preprocessed with fMRIPrep. The first 10 volumes were removed to mitigate non–steady-state effects, and images were spatially smoothed in AFNI (6 mm FWHM). Head motion was quantified using framewise displacement (FD), and participants were excluded if >30% of volumes exceeded FD >0.5 mm. 

All network analyses used baseline (pre-treatment) scans. Clinical response was defined as post–pre change in PROMIS Pain Interference. Whole-brain nodes were defined using the Brainnetome Atlas. ROI–ROI functional connectivity (FC) matrices were computed in CONN and Fisher-z transformed. To derive a stable rich-club subnetwork, nodes were ranked by degree strength (sum of weighted connections), and rich-club nodes were defined as the top-ranked set at two density thresholds (5% and 10%). Overlapping nodes across thresholds were retained, yielding a 17-node rich-club network. Hub influence within this subnetwork was quantified using eigenvector centrality (EC) computed on the 17-node FC matrix. 

In SPSS, ANCOVA interaction models tested whether baseline EC predicted clinical improvement (reduced pain interference) and whether EC–outcome slopes differed by treatment, adjusting for the scanner. Age and sex did not differ across treatment groups. An omnibus 3-group model was followed by pairwise treatment comparisons to localize treatment-specific EC–outcome relationships. Significance was set at p<0.05 (two-tailed). 

Results

A stable 17-node rich-club network was identified across both density thresholds (5% and 10%), comprising bilateral medial superior frontal gyrus (SFG), right operculum, bilateral primary motor cortex, bilateral superior temporal gyrus, bilateral inferior parietal lobule, right primary somatosensory cortex, right anterior insula, bilateral anterior/middle insula, right anterior cingulate cortex, bilateral midcingulate cortex, and intraparietal sulcus. All regions have largely been identified in other studies to be involved in pain processing.

Baseline rich-club hub influence showed treatment-specific profiles for reduced PROMIS Pain Interference. In the 3-group ANCOVA (Duloxetine vs. Acupressure vs. PT), treatment × baseline EC interactions showed trend-level effects for SFG (F = 2.3, p = 0.10) and right anterior/middle insula (F = 2.1, p = 0.13). Post hoc pairwise ANCOVAs localized both effects to the duloxetine–acupressure contrast. For SFG, higher baseline EC predicted greater improvement with duloxetine (r = −0.24) but the association was reversed for acupressure (r = 0.34; interaction F = 5.0, p = 0.03). Conversely, higher baseline right anterior/middle insula EC predicted greater improvement with acupressure (r = 0.40) while duloxetine showed the opposite association (r = −0.34; interaction F = 4.5, p = 0.04). Physical therapy showed no association between change in pain interference and baseline EC for SFG (r = 0.02) or right anterior/middle insula (r = 0.19).

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that baseline rich-club hub organization relates to treatment response in chronic low back pain in a treatment-specific manner. The 17-node rich-club spans regions commonly implicated in pain-related control and integration (medial prefrontal, cingulo-insular, and sensorimotor hubs), consistent with the idea that baseline network organization may capture differences in treatment receptivity.

Specifically, duloxetine response was preferentially linked to executive hub influence with higher baseline SFG EC predicted reduced pain interference and showed the opposite association relative to acupressure. Duloxetine is a centrally acting SNRI whose analgesic effects are thought to involve enhanced serotonergic/noradrenergic signaling and engagement of descending inhibitory pain pathways. In parallel, medial/dorsal prefrontal regions including SFG are implicated in cognitive control5 and have been linked to top-down modulation of pain and placebo-related analgesia, making it plausible that greater baseline SFG hub influence marks a network configuration more receptive to centrally mediated treatment effects.

In contrast, acupressure response was preferentially linked to right anterior/middle insula hub influence, such that higher baseline insula EC predicted reduced pain interference and showed the opposite association in the duloxetine group. Prior fMRI work in acupressure reports treatment-related modulation of insula-centered connectivity in relation to pain outcomes. Together, these results suggest that stronger baseline influence of insula within the rich-club network may reflect a system organization in which acupressure more effectively engages salience/interoceptive and somatosensory  integration processes that translate to reduced pain interference. Physical therapy showed no detectable association between baseline EC and change in pain interference, suggesting that its clinical benefit may depend less on baseline rich-club hub influence and more on factors not captured by this subnetwork.

Overall, these findings support a network-based precision approach in which baseline rich-club EC helps indicate which patients are most likely to improve with different treatments, motivating rich-club EC as a candidate biomarker to help guide treatment selection in chronic pain.
