Introduction

Researchers have explored factors underlying age-related cognitive decline among healthy older adults, including age-related white matter (WM) changes and excessive brain iron accumulation. WM degradation is characterized by volume loss, age-related white matter abnormalities (WMA), and myelin pathology. Primarily located in the WM, myelin is essential for neural transmission, and demyelination can impair neural communication and lead to cognitive impairment. Age-related myelin pathology may arise from the aging of oligodendrocyte precursor cells that support myelin repair, as well as from the senescence of other glial cells and the immune system. 

Excessive iron can cause DNA damage and lipid peroxidation via reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to neuronal death, and is also associated with the overexpression of amyloid precursor protein (APP), involved in the formation of Aβ and p-tau, two key molecules of AD pathology. Iron is the largest source of paramagnetism in the brain, and myelin is the largest source of diamagnetism within WM. 

To identify susceptibility biomarkers of cognitive decline, we employed MRI, which allows semi-quantitative non-invasive assessment of these substances. Among several MRI techniques, quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) specializes in depicting and quantifying magnetic susceptibility sources.^1 However, standard QSM cannot distinguish between paramagnetic and diamagnetic substances, which motivated the development of χ-separation technology. ^2

Using QSM and χ-separation, we reconstructed high-resolution paramagnetic and diamagnetic maps at 7T. We calculated the correlation between cognitive scores and all three MRI quantitative mappings. Finally, we investigated the relationship between diamagnetic mappings and WMAs, which, at least in part, reflect demyelination.

Methods

We studied 62 healthy aged participants (35 males) with a mean age of 73.8 ± 9.7 years who had not been diagnosed with any neurological disorders. All procedures were approved by the ethics committee of Kyoto University Graduate School and Faculty of Medicine (No. R-3074).

The participants underwent two cognitive tests: the MoCA-J and the WMS-R. Skewness was used to assess the distribution of each subtest score. When the absolute skewness exceeded 1, we treated the score as biased and excluded it from the analysis. Additionally, the MoCA-J Abstraction's score can be only 0, 1, or 2, which limits the ability to yield sufficient variance. Therefore, we chose the WMS-R-LM I & II, VR I & II, and the MoCA-J sum score, DR, and language subscores as cognitive indicators. 

Figure (A) summarizes our analysis pipeline. In brief, QSM is a post-processing technique to estimate the local field, which is the convolution of the volume susceptibility distribution with the dipole kernel, induced by biomaterials along the main magnetic field. We used a QSMnet+-based high-resolution reconstruction pipeline in the χ-separation toolbox.^(2, 3) The representative images generated are listed in the methods figure (B).

We used a voxel-wise multiple linear regression model to correlate quantitative mappings with cognitive scores, controlling for age, sex, and educational year. The statistical threshold was set at p < .001, followed by a cluster-level threshold at a family-wise error (FWE)-corrected p < .05. For the nomenclature of the results, we used the AAL3 atlas for the cerebral cortex and subcortical nuclei and the JHU atlas for WM tracts. To validate the voxel-wise analysis, we calculated the mean quantitative mapping values using atlas-defined volumes of interest (VOIs) for gray matter or white matter tracts. Then, we performed a linear regression analysis with a p-value threshold of < .05.

Results

We examined the correlation between the MoCA-J-DR and the WMS-R-LM II (narrative recall) or the VR II (visuospatial memory). Correlation analyses revealed moderate correlations (Pearson's r values of 0.611 for VR and 0.339 for LM, both with p < .05; Results Figure (A)). 

For the voxel-based analysis results, the MoCA-J sum score demonstrated negative correlations with the χ-separation paramagnetic mappings in the bilateral amygdala, putamen, pallidum, and hippocampus, as well as the right fusiform gyrus, right inferior temporal gyrus, left rectus gyrus, and left medial orbital gyrus (Results Figure B-(a)). Furthermore, we found that the MoCA-J-DR score was negatively associated with paramagnetic mappings in the bilateral amygdala, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, and cingulate gyrus, as well as the left caudate and the right parahippocampal gyrus. We also discovered that WMS-R-LM-II scores were negatively correlated with paramagnetic mappings in the left caudate nucleus. 

We found that the MoCA-J-DR score exhibited positive relationships with the χ-separation diamagnetic mappings in the body and the splenium of the corpus callosum, the bilateral corticospinal tract, the forceps major, the left superior corona radiata, the right forceps minor, and the right superior longitudinal fasciculus. We also found that the WMS-R-LM II score was positively related to the diamagnetic mappings in the left corticospinal tract. For visual memory, the WMS-R-VR I score showed positive relationships with diamagnetic mappings in the body and the genu of the corpus callosum, as well as the left anterior corona radiata. Additionally, the MoCA-J Language score was positively correlated with diamagnetic mappings in the left sagittal stratum (Results Figure B-(b)). 

Our voxel-based analysis of conventional QSM confirmed negative associations between cognitive scores and QSM values in multiple brain regions. Specifically, the MoCA-J sum score exhibited negative relationships with QSM values in the bilateral amygdala, the putamen, the fusiform gyrus, the left pallidum, the right inferior temporal gyrus, the right middle temporal gyrus, the left superior temporal gyrus, and the left entorhinal area. The MoCA-J-DR score exhibited significantly negative relationships with QSM values in the bilateral pallidum, bilateral amygdala, left hippocampus, left parahippocampal gyrus, body of the corpus callosum, right cuneus, and right superior occipital gyrus. Additionally, the WMS-R-LM II scores were negatively correlated with QSM values in the body of the corpus callosum. Moreover, we identified a negative correlation between QSM values and WMS-R-VR II scores in the right superior corona radiata (Results Figure B-(c)).

To confirm the results above, we conducted an atlas-based VOI analysis of the identified regions. In the body of the corpus callosum, we found a correlation between the MoCA-J-DR score and both QSM and diamagnetic mappings, as well as between WMS-R-VR I and diamagnetic mapping, reinforcing its relevance to memory processing (Results Figure (C)).

We segmented the WMA from T1w images (Results Figure (D)). We found that the number of voxels classified as WMA increases with age (p < .001, r = 0.556). We further investigated the relationship between WMA voxels and non-WMA voxels. We found that the non-WMA voxels showed lower diamagnetic mappings values (p<.0001) and higher QSM values (p<.0001) than the WMA voxels.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we found that the paramagnetic mappings, reflecting the accumulation of paramagnetic substances, showed a negative correlation with cognitive decline in the basal ganglia, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, rectal gyrus, inferior temporal cortex, cingulate cortex, and occipital cortex. The diamagnetic mappings revealed a correlation between cognitive decline and WM tracts, especially the corpus callosum. Moreover, we linked the altered diamagnetic signals with age-related WMAs. 

We demonstrated that the accumulation of paramagnetic substances, most likely iron, in the GM leads to cognitive decline in the aged population.^4 Our findings suggest that plaque or non-plaque iron accumulation, presumably involving iron-laden microglia or neurons in the cortical/subcortical GM regions, correlates with cognition in older individuals. 

Demyelination, the loss of myelin, not just slows signal conduction but also leads to the loss of coherence across volleys of neural transmission.^5 We shed new light on the significance of myelin in the body and the splenium of the corpus callosum in maintaining cognitive functions, particularly memory. The forceps major and minor, as the major tracts within the human brain, play essential roles in cognitive functions. Demyelination of the corticospinal tracts is reported in AD. 

Our WMA segmentation results demonstrate the effectiveness of diamagnetic mappings in representing myelin distribution in the human brain in vivo. Our diamagnetic mappings demonstrated that the level of myelination is indeed reduced in voxels with WMAs compared to those without in the WMA.  

In short, we have demonstrated that χ-separation analysis of 0.7-mm isotropic MRIs acquired at 7T enables the simultaneous characterization of diamagnetic and paramagnetic components. This methodology has a clear advantage over conventional QSM analysis, as it visualizes both diamagnetic (e.g., myelin) and paramagnetic sources (e.g., iron) separately. In our aged participants, cognitive scores were correlated with paramagnetic values in multiple cortical and subcortical GMs and diamagnetic values in specific WM fiber tracts. Moreover, we have found evidence that WMAs mediate the correlation between cognitive scores and diamagnetic values in specific WM tracts.
