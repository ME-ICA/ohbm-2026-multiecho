Introduction

Studies of human decision making have emerged from two dominant traditions. Learning theorists typically investigate choices in which options are evaluated based on experience, whereas behavioural economists and financial decision theorists focus on choices in which key decision variables, such as reward magnitude and probability, are explicitly described1. Growing behavioural evidence suggests that valuation based on these two-information experienced versus described on partially separable computational mechanisms. However, it remains poorly understood whether these mechanisms are supported by shared or distinct neural substrates, as the two modalities have rarely been investigated jointly within the same experimental framework. This question is particularly important given that real-world decisions often involve comparing alternatives that differ in their informational modality. Moreover, the influence of information modality on neural representations of core utility components, such as reward magnitude and probability, remains unclear. To date, few studies have systematically examined how experience- and description-based information shape decision-making within the same individuals, or how these processes are reflected in neural activity2. To address this gap, we designed a risky decision-making task that orthogonally manipulated whether reward probabilities and magnitudes were explicitly described or learned from feedback. Using computational modelling and neuroimaging, we compared the neural correlates of decision variables derived from experience- and description-based information during both the valuation and outcome stages of the decision process.

Methods

Thirty healthy volunteers (mean age = 24.5 ± 3.4 years; 14 males; 28 right-handed), with no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, participated in the experiment. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and gave informed consent prior to participation. The study was approved by the relevant ethics committee. In addition to a base payment, participants received a performance-dependent bonus ranging from 0€ to 16€, which served as an incentive to maintain motivation throughout the study. A full debriefing was conducted after the experiment.

Participants were tasked with performing a restless two-armed bandit task, for which they were instructed to maximise the number of points earned, which were subsequently converted into monetary payoffs. In each trial, participants were required to select one of the two coloured shapes that were presented to the left and right of the fixation point on the screen by pressing one of two buttons with their left or right index finger. They then observed the outcome of their choice (Fig. 1B). Each shape was associated with a specific magnitude of reward (in points) and probability (in percentage) of reward. These were either explicitly displayed to the participant or had to be learned by trial and error (Fig. 1A). The magnitude and probability information associated with each of the options was linked to the two shapes and displayed at the top and bottom of each shape.  The positioning of the shapes on the screen was counterbalanced across trials.

Decision variables (reward magnitude and probability) were manipulated such that each could be either explicitly presented or inferred from experience. This resulted in four distinct decision contexts: explicit magnitude – explicit probability (MP), explicit magnitude and implicit probability (Mp), implicit magnitude and explicit probability (mP), and implicit magnitude and implicit probability (mp). Each decision context was associated with a unique pair of coloured shapes. Trials from the four contexts were presented sequentially within each block, and the order of the contexts was counterbalanced across blocks. Participants completed four short blocks of the task (96 trials per block; 24 trials per decision context), with each block comprising four different pairs of coloured shapes.

Reward magnitude (from 1 to 99 points) and probabilities (from 1% to 99%) associated with each of the shapes drifted independently across trials (Fig. 1C). On each trial t, the magnitude mt was sampled from a beta distribution with mean determined by the previous trial mt-1:

α = 1 + mt-1 exp(µ), β = 1 + (1 - mt-1 )exp(t),

and then rescaled to the 0–100 range. Similarly, the probability pt was sampled from a separate beta distribution with:

α = 1 + pt-1exp(t), β = 1 + (1 - pt-1 )exp(t)

rescaled between 0–1 range. In both cases the volatility parameter t controlling the random walk, was fixed at 3.0. 

The chosen gamble was then realized and its outcome was shown to the participant with a jittered delay (1000-3000ms, mean 1500ms, Fig. 1B).  At the end of each block, participants were shown the total reward they had won, as well as a bonus of 1€ if their performance exceeded 800 points.

MRI data were acquired on a Siemens Prisma 3T scanner with a 64-channel head coil (Institut du Cerveau et de la Moelle Epinière, Paris, France). High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were obtained using a 3D MPRAGE sequence (1 mm³ voxel), and T2*-weighted multiband EPI (mb-EPI) data were acquired with a multiband factor of 3, GRAPPA acceleration of 2, 54 slices, 2.5 mm isotropic voxels, TR = 1.1 s, and TE = 25 ms.

Results

Participants’ accuracy, defined as the proportion of trials in which the option with the highest expected value was chosen, was significantly influenced by decision context (Fig. 1D). Accuracy was highest in the fully explicit context (MP: 87 ± 0.9%), and lower–but similar–across the two mixed contexts (Mp: 74 ± 1.1%; mP: 74 ± 1.3%; t(29) = 0.02, p = .981). To investigate drivers of above-chance performance, we ran trial-by-trial logistic regressions at the participant level, predicting correct choices (1 = correct, 0 = incorrect) from magnitude, probability, and previous outcome. Group-level analyses confirmed that both magnitude and probability significantly predicted correct choices even when not explicitly displayed (Mp: t(29) = 5.35, p < .001; mP: t(29) = 3.61, p = .001; mp: t(29) = 3.64, p = .001 for probability; mp: t(29) = 2.31, p = .03 for magnitude; Fig. 1E).

Based on the model-free analysis of participants’ accuracy, we developed a reinforcement learning model to explain trial-by-trial choices. This model combined reward magnitude and probability into an expected utility for each option, which was then used to generate stochastic choices. The model further assumed that participants updated the values of implicit (experience-based) decision variables according to the Rescorla–Wagner learning rule3. To assess its explanatory power, this “hybrid” model was compared with two alternative reduced models: one that assumed no learning from experience (purely descriptive model) and one that ignored descriptive information (purely experience-based model) (Methods). Bayesian model selection indicated that the hybrid model, which integrated both experiential and descriptive information, provided the best account of participants’ choices (Pexc > .99). We next evaluated the generative performance of the models by simulating choices using the fitted parameters and comparing predicted accuracy to observed accuracy in each task condition4. The hybrid model most closely reproduced participants’ accuracy across conditions. Notably, it was the only model capable of capturing the trial-by-trial effects observed in regression analyses, highlighting the necessity of incorporating both explicit and learned reward information.

To identify neural correlates of valuation under different decision contexts, we analysed BOLD fMRI data recorded during task performance. Differences in behavioural accuracy suggested possible differences in the neural networks engaged across contexts. We first conducted a one-way ANOVA on activation maps at stimulus presentation, which revealed a network associated with the main effect of decision context, including bilateral intraparietal sulcus (IPS, BA40) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC, BA44; Fig. 2A). Similar activations were observed at the outcome stage, largely overlapping with the prefronto-parietal network (Fig. 2A). Based on the intersection of these activation maps, we defined ROIs and performed two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs on BOLD estimates, with probability and magnitude as within-subject factors (each with two levels: explicit vs. implicit. Both IPS and dlPFC showed modulation by decision variables and their modality. While the parietal network exhibited consistent effects of information modality across both variables, dlPFC activations varied depending on the valuation stage, suggesting a stage-dependent role in integrating decision variables. Expected values correlated positively with activations in vmPFC (BA10) and dmPFC (BA8, 24, 32) and negatively with bilateral anterior insula at the time of choice (FWE p < .05; Fig. 2B). Bilateral ventral striatum activity scaled positively with the magnitude of obtained rewards (FWE p < .05; Fig. 2B).

Conclusion

In summary, our results demonstrate that human decision-making engages distinct yet interacting neural and computational mechanisms depending on whether reward information is explicitly described or learned from experience. Behaviourally, participants were more accurate when both magnitude and probability were explicitly presented, but both dimensions influenced choices even when learned implicitly. A hybrid reinforcement learning model that integrated both experiential and descriptive information best accounted for trial-by-trial choices, capturing above-chance performance and regression effects across contexts. At the neural level, valuation involved a prefronto-parietal network, with IPS consistently sensitive to the modality of information and dlPFC showing stage-dependent effects. Expected values were represented in vmPFC and dmPFC, while reward magnitude engaged the ventral striatum, and anterior insula tracked negative correlations. Together, these findings indicate that human valuation flexibly integrates information from multiple sources, relying on overlapping but distinct neural substrates to guide choices across different informational contexts.
