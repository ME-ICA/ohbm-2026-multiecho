Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is increasingly recognized as a risk factor for neurodegenerative disorders1, yet the mechanisms by which TBI initiates or accelerates these processes remain poorly understood. For a substantial proportion of individuals who develop post-traumatic neurodegeneration (PTND), cognitive decline is accompanied by behavioral dysregulation, depression, and in the most severe cases, suicidality. Emerging evidence suggests that these symptoms arise from abnormal large-scale brain network dynamics, particularly involving the salience network (SN) and limbic network (LN)2,3, systems central to behavioral regulation, affective control, and motivated action. Understanding how TBI alters SN and LN connectivity is therefore critical for advancing personalized prevention and developing disease-modifying therapeutic strategies for PTND. Although in vivo MRI has substantially improved our ability to map TBI-related pathology and detect early neurobehavioral decline, the limited spatial and diffusion resolution of clinical data hampers our ability to precisely define the pathways of network degeneration and ultimately slows therapeutic progress. Additionally, the lack of correlative neuropathological validation further limits the interpretability of in vivo network measures. Here, we leverage high-resolution whole-brain ex vivo diffusion MRI (dMRI) to characterize the connectivity fingerprints of chronic TBI within the SN and LN. Ex vivo dMRI provides a critical “methodological bridge” to link network-level mesoscale imaging signatures with gold-standard microscale observations made with histopathology, enabling a more precise understanding of TBI-related microstructural and connectomic injury.

Methods

Data: As part of the ongoing ENRICH Brain Health study (LEveraging Nationwide Research Infrastructure to EnriCH Brain Health after TBI), we analyzed 12 brains donated for research from subjects with a history of TBI at least one year prior to death (8 males, mean ± SD age 49.2 ± 9.6 years). Four control brain specimens were donated from donors with no history of neurological or psychiatric illness (2 males, mean ± SD age 52.4 ± 16.5 years).  Written informed consent for brain donation was obtained postmortem from family surrogate decision-makers, in accordance with a research protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board at Mass General Brigham (Protocol #2010P000243) and the Uniform Anatomical Gift Act (University of Washington). All specimens were fixed in 10% formaldehyde for at least one month prior to imaging.

Data Acquisition: At the time of scanning, specimens were transferred into sealed plastic bags filled with liquid Fomblin and imaged in a 3T Siemens Trio scanner at 750 μm isotropic spatial resolution using a product 32-channel head coil and a 3D diffusion-weighted steady state free precession (DW-SSFP) sequence. We collected 90 DW volumes and 12 b=0 volumes with the following acquisition parameters: TE=30.21 ms; TR=25.12 ms; effective b-value = 3,373 s/mm2; FOV=203x216.

Data Processing: Figure 1 provides an overview of the processing steps. Data were denoised using MP-PCA in MRtrix3 and corrected for eddy current-induced distortions in FSL. Diffusion orientations were obtained by fitting the ball-and-stick model to DW volumes, and b=0 images were processed in FreeSurfer to obtain automated parcellations of the cortex and subcortical structures through the contrast-agnostic segmentation tool SAMSEG, combined with the anatomical designations of the NextBrain atlas. For the SN we extracted the following regions: insula, rostral and caudal anterior cingulate cortex, accumbens area, and the medial and lateral orbitofrontal cortex. For the LN we extracted the following regions: orbitofrontal cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus. We investigated the structural connectivity within and between these networks using both quantitative and qualitative analyses. For quantitative analyses, we used a seed-based approach in probtrackx (default parameters: 5000 seeds per voxel, curvature threshold: 0.2, step length: 0.5 mm) to derive structural connectivity matrices between each pair of regions within these networks for both TBI cases and controls. For qualitative analyses, we generated deterministic tractograms between select pairs of regions in MRtrix using tckgen (default parameters: 2000 seeds per voxel, iFOD2 algorithm, angle threshold: 45°) on the peaks of the fiber orientations distributions (FODs) from the constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) model fit. 

Results

Probabilistic tractography applied to all pairs of the LN and SN regions of interest revealed patterns of disconnection, which we quantify as a decrease in connectivity probability (CP)4 (Figure 2A). When comparing group averages, sharp decreases in CP were found to be clustered within pairs of subcortical regions (hippocampus, amygdala and accumbens). The rostral anterior cingulate cortex displayed the largest average decrease in CP with all other LN/SN regions (CP decrease of 0.96), followed by the nucleus accumbens (CP decrease of 0.76). On an individual level, we observed relative heterogeneity in overall CP values within the chronic TBI group, with some specimens displaying more marked subcortical and cortical decreases in CP as compared to other TBI specimens or a representative control brain. We then performed deterministic tractography mapping of hippocampal and amygdala connections with the rostral anterior cingulate cortex - regions that showed large reductions in CP values in the control/chronic TBI brain pair. Both tract reconstructions displayed significant decreases in reconstructed tract volume and overall streamline counts compared to the reconstruction of the same tracts in the control specimens (Figure 2B).

Conclusion

These preliminary data show different degrees of disrupted structural connectivity within the SN and LN in brains from individuals with chronic TBI, suggesting variable network-level alterations. Such abnormalities are consistent with heterogeneous patterns of network disruption observed in neurodegenerative disorders5 and support a network-based neuropathological framework for chronic TBI. Future steps of this study will involve examining neuropathological data for these specimens and premortem behavioral data to elucidate the mechanisms underlying network disruption and their relationship to clinical outcomes. Importantly, as neuromodulation therapies hold promise for restoring network function, precise identification of disrupted neural networks may enable targeted interventions to promote recovery in individuals with chronic TBI.
