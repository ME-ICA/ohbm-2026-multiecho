Introduction

Anomaly detection in MRI is crucial for identifying rare or abnormal tissue patterns (e.g. tumors or lesions), providing information that supports diagnostic evaluation. Deep-learning approaches are commonly used for automated MRI anomaly detection. Current methods typically focus on lesions, treating healthy tissue as an undifferentiated background class. We hypothesize that the inclusion of healthy tissues will improve detection performance and enhance lesion segmentation and localization by providing additional anatomical constraints.

A synthetic-training framework was used because of its unique contrast-agnostic properties¹ and ability to rely on small datasets². Only few studies explored synthetic training to segment both lesions and healthy tissue³ ⁴ ⁵ However, these were based on specific pathologies and relied on the use of real lesion masks. We instead propose an approach that is able to detect and segment any lesion type, by relying entirely on synthetic lesion modeling.

Methods

Data from six subjects with high-quality, densely annotated head-tissue labels were used, in which 3 types of white-matter (WM) anomalies were introduced. CSF-hole anomalies were simulated by locally deforming white and gray matter to simulate abnormal geometries. MS-like lesions were obtained by modeling 45-120 ellipsoidal shapes with predefined radius
ranges randomly placed within the WM. Tumor-like lesions were simulated with up to three components: a core ellipsoid (80–960 mm diameter), a necrotic region obtained by intersecting the core with thresholded 3D Perlin noise, and an edema region as an expanded region surrounding the core. Random-contrast generation was modified to ensure that lesion intensity means differed by 0.2 from WM. Standard TorchIO augmentations (affine transformations, elastic deformations, bias field, noise) were applied, generating 6000 synthetic contrast samples (1/3 for each type). A 3D Residual Encoder (XL) was trained using the nnU-Net framework.

Results

The model was evaluated on three private datasets with manual lesion masks: a glioma dataset (N=45, T1/T2), a lymphoma dataset (N=116, T1-weighted), and an MS dataset (N=91, T1/FLAIR). We compare our method, SIAM, with LST-AI on the MS dataset and with SynthSegWMH [3] on all datasets, although SynthSegWMH³ was designed for MS lesions and does not extend to tumor-like lesions. We observed performance comparable to LST-AI, which was trained on real data using both T1 and FLAIR (Fig. 1). The lower Dice on T1-gd was expected, since the reference masks were FLAIR-based and many lesions showed limited visibility on T1-gd.

Conclusion

SIAM achieved performance comparable to competitive models with a data-efficient synthetic-training strategy. The simulation of WM lesions, combined with controlled brain distortion of synthetic shapes, avoided dependence on manually segmented datasets, which are usually small and do not cover the full range of spatial locations. This approach shifts the challenge of collecting large annotated datasets to the generation of synthetic lesions with enough variability in shape, size, and spatial distribution to reflect real pathologies. The results presented here show that this fully synthetic approach successfully establishes a strong foundation. Future work will extend this approach to stroke-like regions, which have a larger spatial extent.
