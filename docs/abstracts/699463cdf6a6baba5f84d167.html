Introduction

1. Introduction

Cognitive reserve (CR) refers to the brain’s capacity to adapt and maintain cognitive functioning despite aging, injury, or neuropathological burden.^1 It represents a form of neural and cognitive flexibility that buffers against decline and contributes to individual differences in resilience.^2 While previous studies have linked CR to both functional and structural brain markers, evidence remains inconsistent regarding its neural substrates, particularly in midlife women, where hormonal transitions may substantially influence brain morphology. Moreover, genetic risk factors such as the apolipoprotein E ε4 (APOE ε4) allele may further interact with CR-related mechanisms.

This study aimed to comprehensively characterize the structural neural correlates of cognitive reserve using five complementary morphometric measures - i.e., grey matter volume (GMV), cortical thickness, sulcal depth, gyrification, and fractal dimension - while testing how these associations are modulated by menopausal status and APOE ε4 genotype. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation integrating voxel-based and surface-based morphometry to examine how hormonal and genetic factors jointly shape the neuroanatomical signatures of cognitive reserve in healthy women.

Methods

2. Methods 

2.1 Participants

Participants were recruited through university-wide internal advertisements at the University of Belgrade, Serbia. All volunteers completed a structured clinical and cognitive screening protocol to ensure that only healthy individuals were included. Exclusion criteria comprised history of stroke, dementia, major psychiatric illness, traumatic brain injury with loss of consciousness, or use of psychoactive medication. After screening and data quality control in total 113 female participants (30~65 years old) were included in the study.

All participants completed an extensive multimodal assessment battery, including detailed neuropsychological testing across multiple cognitive domains, as well as demographic and genetic factors. Specifically, cognitive reserve was assessed using the Cognitive Reserve Index questionnaire (CRIq).

2.2. Neuroimaging preprocessing

All DICOM T1-weighted imaging data was converted to 3D-NIFTI volumes using dcm2nixx tool (https://github.com/neurolabusc/dcm2niix). SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/), the CAT12 toolbox (https://neuro-jena.github.io/cat/),  and MATLAB v2024b (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, Massachusetts, USA) were used for structural image preprocessing and analyses.

Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) preprocessing steps included bias field correction, tissue segmentation into gray matter, whiter matter, and cerebrospinal fluid, and spatial normalization to MNI space using DARTEL template, with anisotropic voxel resolution of 1.5mm. The resulting modulated GM maps were subsequently smoothed using an 8 mm full-width at half maximum (FWHM) isotropic Gaussian Kernel.

Apart from VBM, four neural features of surface-based morphometry (SBM) were used to investigate neural correlates of cognitive reserve, including cortical thickness (the distance between wither matter surface and pial surface), sulcal depth (the distance between central surface and hemispheric hull), fractal dimension (representing cortical complexity calculated by the ratio between central surface areas modeled with different resolutions), and gyrification (calculated as the absolute mean curvature of the cortical surface).^3 The preprocessing steps of SBM comprise a series of methods like surface creation, surface registration, spatial smoothing. 

2.3. Statistical analysis

Prior to investigating cognitive reserve effects, we first examined how structural brain measures varied with age in our sample. Specifically, we conducted separate multiple regression models to assess the association between age and each neuroimaging maker: grey matter, cortical thickness, sulcal depth, fractal dimension, and gyrification. This step served to validate the expected patterns of age-related brain changes and establish a baseline for interpreting cognitive reserve effects in subsequent analyses.

We used multiple linear regression models in SPM12, adding age and scanner type as covariates (TIV was additionally included for GMV data), APOE 4 status, menopausal status, and their interaction with cognitive reserve (e.g., CRI*menopause status, CRI*APOE 4 status) were included after centering continuous variables (i.e., CRI total scores and subscale scores) to examine moderation effects.

Results were considered significant only when p<.05 FWE-correct at cluster level with cluster size >150 vertices of SBM analyses and > 50 voxels of VBM analyses.

Results

3. Results 

As expected, significant age-related reductions were observed in gray matter volume and cortical thickness, consistent with established patterns of age-associated cortical atrophy.^4 These effects were widespread and bilaterally distributed. No significant age-related changes were detected for gyrification, fractal dimension, or sulcal depth.

Regarding the neural correlates of cognitive reserve, across the five morphometric indicators examined (GMV, cortical thickness, gyrification, fractal dimension, and sulcal depth), significant effects were observed only for sulcal depth.

A main effect of menopausal status was identified in the left precuneus, where sulcal depth remained relatively stable across age in premenopausal women but showed a pronounced age-related decline in postmenopausal women. No significant associations were found between precuneus sulcal depth and either age at menopause or duration since menopause, suggesting that the observed pattern reflects a categorical difference associated with menopausal status.

In addition, a significant negative association between cognitive reserve and sulcal depth, as well as a significant cognitive reserve × menopausal status interaction, was observed in the bilateral orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). Stratified analyses indicated that the negative association was present only in premenopausal women, with higher cognitive reserve corresponding to shallower OFC sulci. In contrast, postmenopausal women showed a flat or slightly positive association. Post-hoc analyses confirmed that the interaction was driven by the premenopausal group. Importantly, cognitive reserve levels did not differ significantly between pre- and postmenopausal women after adjusting for age.

Conclusion

4. Conclusions

The finding that the main effect of menopausal status emerged exclusively in sulcal depth suggests that this metric may represent a uniquely sensitive structural marker of age and hormone related variation in midlife women. In particular, the effect observed in the left precuneus, where postmenopausal women exhibited a steeper age-related decline in sulcal depth compared with premenopausal women, indicates a structural vulnerability associated with the menopausal transition. The absence of associations with either age at menopause or duration since menopause further supports the interpretation that this reflects a categorical hormonal shift rather than a gradual or time dependent influence. The lack of corresponding effects in gray matter volume, cortical thickness, gyrification, or fractal dimension highlights the specificity of sulcal depth as a structural feature that is responsive to these biological factors.

The identification of sulcal depth in the orbitofrontal cortex as a correlate of cognitive reserve, together with its modulation by menopausal status, provides additional insight into how lifelong enrichment interacts with hormonal transitions to shape cortical morphology. The orbitofrontal cortex, a region involved in decision making and cognitive affective integration, is known to be sensitive to both aging and estrogen related neurobiological changes. The negative association between cognitive reserve and orbitofrontal sulcal depth observed in premenopausal women suggests that higher reserve may support preservation of local cortical geometry, potentially reflecting more efficient neural organization or reduced susceptibility to age related morphological changes. In contrast, the absence of this association in postmenopausal women indicates a shift in how cognitive reserve is expressed structurally following hormonal decline. This pattern is consistent with theoretical accounts proposing that cognitive reserve relies on preservation-based mechanisms earlier in life and increasingly on compensatory mechanisms as biological vulnerability increases.

Importantly, cognitive reserve levels did not differ between premenopausal and postmenopausal women, which indicates that menopause does not diminish reserve itself. Instead, it appears to alter the neuroanatomical pathways through which reserve supports cognitive resilience. Taken together, these findings highlight sulcal depth as a hormone sensitive marker of age related and reserve related cortical adaptation and emphasize the importance of considering hormonal status when examining the neural mechanisms that support cognitive resilience in women.
