Introduction

Navigating familiar environments from novel viewpoints requires transforming stable allocentric maps (world- or environment-centered) into transient egocentric motor plans (self-centered). While theoretical models identify the retrosplenial cortex (RSC) as a hub for this transformation [1], it remains unclear how the broader navigation network is dynamically involved. Here, we tested the hypothesis that the parahippocampal area (PHA), RSC, parietal cortex, and hippocampus (HPC) mediate the transformation during the transition from route planning to execution.

Methods

We analyzed 3T fMRI data from 38 healthy participants (24.3±3.3 years) performing a virtual navigation task. After encoding a “three-intersection” route (Fig. 1A), participants were placed at three possible locations within the route: beginning or end (egocentric conditions), or one intermediate intersection (allocentric condition). Here they entered an imagination phase (8s) where they planned their future navigation, then a retrieval phase followed (actual navigation). Using GLM, we contrasted allocentric vs. egocentric conditions during the imagination phase to map allocentric network topography. Then, we used searchlight-based representational similarity analysis (RSA) to test three neural representational hypotheses during actual navigation: Allo-to-Egocentric transformation, egocentric planning, and distance from goal. Finally, to track Allo-to-Egocentric transformation dynamics, we segmented the imagination phase into four 2-second bins plus a 2-second decision bin. We trained classifiers using multi-voxel pattern analysis (MVPA) to test pattern stability in PHA, RSC, superior parietal cortex (SPC), and HPC.

Results

Behaviorally, allocentric transformations imposed a cognitive cost, with significantly longer reaction times and lower accuracy compared to egocentric controls. The GLM analysis showed that during imagination, allocentric transformations recruited a distributed network including the ventral stream (PHA, lingual cortex) and dorsal stream (intraparietal sulcus), distinct from the sensorimotor network (precentral gyrus, dlPFC) engaged by egocentric planning (Fig. 1B-C). During active navigation, the Allo-to-Egocentric transformation RSA model best explained the representational pattern in the bilateral PHA, RSC, and SPC (p<0.0001, FWE corrected). In contrast, the egocentric model was restricted to the left HPC, and the goal-distance model showed minimal representational fit. MVPA-based cross-time decoding revealed a chronometric hierarchy: while time-bin classification accuracy in PHA, RSC, and SPC evolved continuously from imagination to navigation, the hippocampus maintained a highly stable code throughout the period.

Conclusion

Our results show that static environmental maps are actively transformed by distinct cortical areas to support flexible behavior. These findings indicate that translating allocentric knowledge into egocentric action is not a momentary switch, but a continuous, dynamic process distributed across a flexible parieto-temporal network.
