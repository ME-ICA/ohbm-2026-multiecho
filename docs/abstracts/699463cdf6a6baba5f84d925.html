Introduction

Traditionally known for its role in motor learning and coordination, the cerebellum also supports other complex behaviors such as language, cognition, and emotion regulation.1 These critical behaviors begin to emerge throughout infancy, suggesting that the first year of life may be a consequential period during which the cerebellum shapes early learning and behavior across diverse behavioral domains manner. The cerebellum also shows consistent alterations in Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), including in its structure, function, and cellular composition2, indicating that early individual differences in cerebellar development may contribute to the emergence of ASD symptoms as well as key behavioral milestones. However, despite the cerebellum’s clear role in behavioral development and domain-general learning, studies linking infant cerebellar function to later behavioral or psychiatric outcomes are scant. Even rarer are neuroimaging-genetics studies examining how genetic variation shapes the infant brain. To our knowledge, no prior work has applied neuroimaging-genetics approaches to investigate how genetic variation may shape cerebellar function in infancy. In the present study, using MRI, genetic, and behavioral outcome data from the Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP)3, we tested how biological sex, genetic liability for ASD, and their interactions may influence the functional configuration of the neonatal cerebellum using seed-based resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC).

Methods

Our final sample (N = 198; 105 Males, 93 Females) was composed of term-born infants (37-44 weeks gestation) with high-quality functional MRI (fMRI) and genetic data obtained from the NIMH National Data Archives (NDA). Genetic data were collected through saliva samples and genotyped using the Illumina Infinium Omni5-4 array v1.2 to identify over 4 million single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). After SNP data were downloaded from the NDA, missing variants were imputed using the TOPMed Imputation Server. K-nearest neighbors clustering was then applied, using the 1000 Genomes Project as reference, to assign genetic ancestry and to calculate quantitative principal components. SNPs were filtered for minor allele frequency <0.01, missingness per individual >0.01, missingness per marker >0.01, and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p < 10-6) using PLINK v2.0. We then used PRS-CS in combination with summary statistics from the leading genome-wide association study of ASD4 in 18,381 cases and 27,969 controls to calculate polygenic scores for ASD (ASD PGS), which provides a quantitative measure of each individual’s cumulative genetic liability for ASD based on common genetic variants. Functional MRIs underwent the dHCP’s minimal preprocessing3 before undergoing additional processing for functional connectivity analyses. These included spatial smoothing (3mm), bandpass filtering, ICA-AROMA and the regression of white matter, CSF, and global signal. The cerebellum was partitioned into a SensoriMotor seed (Lobules I-IV, V, VIIIa/b) and a SocialCognitive seed (Crus I/II), informed by prior studies of cerebellar function and topology5. Time series were extracted from each seed (left and right hemispheres separately) and correlated with that of every other voxel in the cortex, excluding the cerebellum and brainstem, at the single-subject level. At the group level, connectivity patterns were modeled in FMRIB’s Software Library (FSL) as a function of biological sex, ASD PGS, and their interaction. Sex comparisons controlled for the effects of gestational age at birth and postnatal age at the time of MRI scan, while the ASD PGS regression additionally controlled for the first five principal components of genetic ancestry. Voxelwise rs-FC results were cluster-corrected for multiple comparisons (P < 0.05) and plotted at multiple thresholds (Z = 1.7, 2.3, 2.7, 3.1) for visualization, but only clusters that met a significance threshold of Z > 2.3 were considered significant. Connectivity strength for significant clusters was extracted and correlated with key outcome measures assessed in early toddlerhood (18 months), including the Quantitative Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (Q-CHAT), the cognitive, motor, and language subscales of the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition (BSID-III), and the Child Behavioral Checklist (CBCL). Behavioral regressions controlled for the effects of sex, gestational age at birth, postnatal age at scan, and postnatal age at the time of behavioral assessment.

Results

Sex Differences. Across all four ROIs tested, we found widespread sex differences in cerebello-cerebral connectivity patterns. For the SensoriMotor seeds, males had greater connectivity with somatosensory and supplementary motor cortices, as well as with bilateral frontal cortex, whereas females had greater connectivity with superior temporal, primary visual, and dorsolateral prefrontal cortices. For the bilateral SocialCognitive seeds, males had greater connectivity with the cingulate, posterior occipital, and superior frontal cortices, while females had stronger connectivity with superior temporal, medial occipital, sensorimotor, auditory, and subcortical areas.

ASD PGS predicts alterations in cerebellar functional connectivity (Figure 1). Males and females did not significantly differ in their ASD PGS scores (β = 0.084, p = 0.45). Infants with higher ASD PGS showed stronger connectivity between the left sensorimotor seed and a cluster localized to the left temporal white matter (Figure 1A; Z > 2.3). Greater connectivity between these two regions predicted higher expressive language scores (β = 5.34, p = 0.026) and greater attention problems (β = 13.03, p=0.04) at 18-month outcome (Figure 1E), in boys only. Elevated ASD PGS also predicted stronger connectivity between the right SensoriMotor seed and right parietal cortex (Figure 1B; Z > 2.3), as well as weaker connectivity with bilateral posterior superior and middle temporal gyri (Z > 2.7). In males only, connectivity strength between the right SensoriMotor seed and this temporal cluster predicted attention problems on the CBCL at 18 months (Figure 1E; β = -14.28, p = 0.029). 

Higher ASD PGS was also associated with hyperconnectivity between the right SocialCognitive seed and a cluster localized to the posterior cingulate and parietal cortices (Figure 1D; Z > 2.7). In females only, stronger connectivity between the right SocialCognitive seed and this cluster significantly predicted lower emotional reactivity scores as indexed by  the CBCL at outcome (Figure 1E; β = -8.60, p = 0.030), but this pattern was not detected in males.

Several ASD PGS-connectivity relationships are sex-specific (Figure 2). No significant sex-by-ASD PGS interactions survived thresholding for the left cerebellum seeds (Figures 1F and 1H), but we observed a significant interaction for the right SensoriMotor seed, such that ASD PGS was associated with stronger connectivity between the right SensoriMotor seed and precuneus in males (Figure 1G; Z > 2.3), but not in females. Stronger connectivity between these regions in boys was also predictive of higher expressive language scores at outcome (Figure 1J; β = 3.48, p = 0.019). Finally, for the right SocialCognitive seed, elevated ASD PGS was associated with weaker connectivity with the right sensorimotor cortex in males. However, in females, elevated ASD PGS was associated with stronger connectivity between the right SocialCognitive seed and the left sensorimotor cortex (Figure 1I; Z > 2.3) – a connectivity pattern that predicted lower emotional reactivity at outcome across both sexes (β = -5.88, p = 0.023), but especially in females (Figure 1J; β =-10.75, p = 0.005).

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine how common genetic variants associated with ASD influence functional connectivity of the infant cerebellum. We report widespread sex differences in neonatal cerebellar functional connectivity, including some patterns that may be cerebellum-general, and others that may be regionally specific. Across both sexes, elevated polygenic scores for ASD were related to altered cerebellar connectivity, including atypical temporal connectivity for the SensoriMotor cerebellum, and hyperconnectivity between the right SocialCognitive cerebellum and the posterior cingulate. Interestingly, behavioral associations were largely sex-specific, suggesting potential underlying compensatory mechanisms that may depend on biological sex. ASD PGS was also associated with several sex-specific alterations in cerebellar connectivity that also predicted later behavior, including individual differences in expressive language for males, and emotional reactivity for females, at 18-month outcome.

In sum, we identify multiple robust alterations in functional connectivity of the cerebellum that are associated with elevated genetic predisposition for ASD, with sex-specific relationships that may be consequential for later behavioral development. Remarkably, we show that ASD PGS already exerts effects on the cerebellum at birth, and that these early-life alterations may have cascading impacts on later behavioral milestones and clinical symptom profiles. This work highlights the critical utility of infant imaging-genetics for understanding the early biological origins of ASD, critical periods for intervention, and strategies that may improve long-term outcomes.
