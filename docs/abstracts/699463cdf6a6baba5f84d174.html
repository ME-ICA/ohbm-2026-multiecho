Introduction

Human brain function relies on dynamic inter-areal synchronization of neuronal oscillations.1 This synchronization can be investigated through the undirected statistical interactions between neurophysiological signals (i.e. functional connectivity) or the directed influence of one neural system over another (i.e. effective connectivity).2 Connectivity can be assessed using non-interventional resting state data or with an interventional approach via stimulations. While interventional approaches provide insights into directed information flow, the majority of network neuroscience research focuses on non-interventional data. The association between these approaches remains elusive.

Intracranial electroencephalography (EEG) provides a rare opportunity to study the human brain connectivity in vivo with exceptional temporal and spatial resolution. Intracranial EEG with stereotactic depth electrodes (SEEG) is used in the presurgical evaluation of drug-resistant epilepsy and typically includes spontaneous activity recorded at resting state and stimulation-elicited cortico-cortical evoked potentials (CCEPs).3 CCEPs provide a direct measure for the causal interactions in the brain and thus describe effective connectivity during stimulations.4

Understanding the relationship between interventional and non-interventional approaches is essential for determining the inter-modality correspondence in connectivity studies. Clinically, it may provide insights in defining biomarkers for neurological pathologies, such as epilepsy. Since connectivity can be assumed to be constrained by neural structures, it is plausible to also assume that the interventional and non-interventional methods should result in similar patterns.5 However, a comprehensive evaluation of correlation measures is lacking. In the present study, we investigated the association of interventional connectivity via CCEPs and non-interventional connectivity via orthogonalized envelope correlation in spontaneous SEEG.

Methods

The study included 65 consecutive patients with drug-resistant epilepsy that underwent a multiday, presurgical intracranial evaluation (SEEG including CCEPs) in Helsinki University Hospital in 2013-2023 and fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: 1) age > 16 years, 2) at least 20 bipolar channels stimulated with at least 15 consecutive biphasic pulses with 1000 µs pulse duration, 1 Hz pulse frequency, and 3 or 4 mA pulse intensity to investigate the CCEPs. SEEG (white matter common reference, 512/2048 Hz sampling frequency) was recorded with Cardinal Healthcare/Natus NicOne C64 OR/SSU (N = 20) or Micromed SD LTM 64 EXPRESS/SD LTM 64 PLUS systems (N = 45). The study was approved by The Helsinki University Hospital Institutional Research Review Board, with a waiver of consent because of the study’s retrospective and observational nature.

From the stimulation recordings, we extracted all segments with 1 Hz stimulation at 3 or 4 mA. Additionally, an ~7.3 min artefact-free segment of wake data during the 1 hour preceding the first stimulations was selected from the spontaneous SEEG (artefacts identified visually). The stimulation data were bandpass filtered to the frequency range of interest, 0.1-100 Hz, and notch filtered at 50 Hz to remove line noise. Bipolar referencing was used to reduce volume conduction, and to match the bipolar stimulations. For each stimulated channel, the first 15 trials were averaged in all recording channels separately. Next, the ratio between the root mean square (RMS) of the evoked response (10 to 500 ms with stimulation at 0 ms) and the RMS of the baseline (-200 to -10 ms) was calculated for each recorded channel and each stimulation. These ratios resulted in a directed connectivity matrix (referred to as the stim matrix) that described the effective connectivity between the stimulated channels and the recording channels.

The spontaneous data were down-sampled to 512 Hz,  filtered to the canonical frequency bands (delta: 2–4 Hz; theta: 5–7 Hz; alpha: 8–12 Hz; beta: 15–29 Hz; low-gamma: 30–59 Hz; and high-gamma: 60–90 Hz), notch filtered at 50 Hz (only the low-gamma band), and re-referenced to a bipolar montage. Functional connectivity was calculated between all channel pairs via orthogonalized envelope correlation in non-overlapping 4-second epochs. Epoch connectivities were averaged together resulting in a non-directed connectivity matrix (referred to as the spont matrix) describing the patient’s functional connectivity before the stimulations.

Only stimulated channels were selected for the stim and spont matrix comparison, and connections that shared an electrode contact were discarded. The stim matrix was made symmetric by taking the direction that had a higher value. Next, the upper triangles of stim and spont matrices were compared using Spearman correlation. The statistical significance of the result was assessed by permutation tests. Specifically, the spont matrix rows and columns were permutated, and the correlation was calculated with the stim matrix. An empirical null distribution was generated from 1000 iterations and compared to the original correlation values. The resulting p values were corrected for multiple comparisons within each patient with the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Figure 1 visualizes the analysis pipeline.

Results

We found significant correlation (alpha = 0.05) between stim and spont connectivity matrices in most patients (N = 49/65) with variation across frequency bands. The median correlation was highest in the delta band (rho = 0.15), and the lowest in the theta band (rho = 0.10). The number of patients with significant correlations was highest in the theta band (N = 39/65), and the lowest in the beta band (N = 14/65). The correlation coefficients were relatively low (median rho = 0.10-0.15). However, there was prominent individual variability in the correlation coefficients, with specific patients showing correlation coefficients up to rho = 0.33. Conclusion

To better understand the inter-modality correlation of different connectivity approaches, we compared interventional and non-interventional connectivity across different frequency bands in 65 patients with drug-resistant epilepsy using cortico-cortical evoked potentials (CCEPs) and orthogonalized envelope correlation as the connectivity measures. We found that most patients showed significant correlation between the connectivity approaches. However, the Spearman correlation coefficients between the stim and spont connectivity matrices were moderate. Taken together, the results hint at a shared network between spontaneous and stimulation-induced networks even with large variability in electrode positions, individual etiology, duration of illness, medication, and age.  

Limitations of the present study include the limited number of channels that are placed in patient-specific locations for clinical reasons in intracranial EEG. Thus, intracranial EEG cannot be as spatially spread as non-invasive methods. The data are heterogeneous across patients which may impact the correlations between the connectivity measures. In addition, the generalization of findings to a healthy population is not straightforward. However, epilepsy patients are typically heterogeneous in disease onset and etiology, which alleviates concerns of findings related to a certain pathology. Furthermore, ethical reasons allow recording of intracranial EEG only in clinical populations. The limitations can be somewhat alleviated by choosing appropriate analysis methods and by only considering channels in non-epileptogenic areas.

The present results show the feasibility of comparing interventional and non-interventional connectivity approaches and open exciting new windows for further insight into their interrelations. To get a more comprehensive view of brain networks and connectivity across modalities in the challenging epilepsy population, our future research will focus on examining different metrics of connectivity, limiting the analysis to channels in non-epileptogenic areas and including subnetwork analysis and graph measures.
