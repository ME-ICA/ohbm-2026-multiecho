Introduction

Effective reinforcement learning requires balancing exploration of uncertain options with exploitation of known outcomes. In real-world contexts, the same action may yield rewards in some situations and punishments in others, yet how these learning processes influence each other remains unclear. To address this issue, we designed a probabilistic instrumental learning task in which a single option was associated with rewarding and punishing outcomes in separate contexts. We investigated the underlying neurobiological mechanisms of the interaction between reward learning and punishment learning through functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), computational modeling, and pharmacological manipulation across two experiments (N = 102).

Methods

In an fMRI experiment (N = 30), healthy participants performed an instrumental learning task with repeated binary choices. Options were associated with reward-only contexts, punishment-only contexts, or mixed contexts (i.e., the same option could yield both reward and punishment), allowing us to test whether reward and punishment learning influence each other. In a separate behavioral experiment (N = 72), we used a double-blind, randomized, between-subject pharmacological design to examine whether dopaminergic signaling modulates this interaction, administering amisulpride (a dopamine D2/3 receptor antagonist) versus placebo. In both experiments, behavior was analyzed using computational modeling based on the Rescorla–Wagner learning rule and a softmax choice rule (and variants) to estimate learning and decision parameters capturing the interaction between reward learning and punishment learning.

Results

Results showed that when both reward and punishment learning were concurrently engaged, the performance of reward learning was selectively impaired, while punishment learning remained intact. Computational modeling revealed that this impairment was predicted by increased exploration linked to prior punishment associations in reward-learning choices, accompanied by enhanced blood oxygen level-dependent responses in prefrontal regions related to exploration behavior and a correlation between the extent of behavioral impairment in reward learning and increased prefrontal activities. Furthermore, we found amisulpride eliminated both the impairment in reward learning and the accompanying increase in exploration. 

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that reward learning can be disrupted by punishment-driven exploration in a dopamine-dependent manner, offering a mechanistic account of how the brain resolves competing value signals and insights into dopamine-related learning disturbances in neuropsychiatric conditions.
