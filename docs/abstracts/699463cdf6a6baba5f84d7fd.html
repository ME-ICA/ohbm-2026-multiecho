Introduction

Everyday social interactions frequently involve ambiguous emotional signals that require accurate interpretation. In several neuropsychiatric disorders, this process is impaired, leading to systematic deviations in emotion perception and evaluation. However, the neural mechanisms underlying the processing of ambiguous emotions—both in neurotypical individuals and in patient populations—remain incompletely understood.

To investigate these mechanisms, experimental paradigms commonly employ facial expressions morphed to varying degrees between different emotions, requiring participants to judge which emotion is more prominently perceived during fMRI acquisition. To date, most fMRI studies of emotional ambiguity have been limited to morphs between only two emotions, typically combining a positive and a negative expression, or have relied on alternative, non-facial methods to induce ambiguity.

In the present study, we developed a novel paradigm (EmoDet) that systematically morphs positive and negative facial emotions across multiple graded levels of ambiguity. Thirty-one participants were examined, and both behavioral and fMRI data were analyzed to characterize neural responses to increasing and decreasing emotional ambiguity. Increasing ambiguity was associated with activation of dorsal prefrontal cortex regions, whereas decreasing ambiguity engaged ventral prefrontal areas, largely replicating previous findings and supporting theories of functional specialization within the prefrontal cortex. Additionally, increased ambiguity elicited activation in the insula and inferior frontal gyrus, consistent with prior work.

The flexible design of the EmoDet paradigm enables future investigations to address more fine-grained questions, such as emotion-specific morph effects and differential neural responses across distinct levels of ambiguity.

Methods

Thirty-four participants were recruited; data from three were excluded due to left-handedness, excessive head motion, or self-reported concentration difficulties. The final sample comprised 31 right-handed participants (17 women; mean age = 24.4 years, SD = 2.8). Handedness was assessed using the short version of the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (cut-off > +30).

Participants completed a battery of questionnaires assessing emotion-related traits, including the Empathy Quotient (EQ), a short version of the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ), the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ), the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20), and the Persönlichkeitsstil- und Störungsinventar (PSSI). A post-experimental questionnaire assessed subjective task experience.

The EmoDet (Emotion Detection) paradigm consisted of 480 facial stimuli depicting four basic emotions (happy, angry, fearful, sad) across varying levels of emotional ambiguity. Stimuli were generated from eight identities (four female) taken from the Radboud Faces Database. Using JavaPsychoMorph, six emotion continua were created (angry–sad, angry–happy, fearful–happy, sad–fearful, sad–happy, angry–fearful) with nine non-linearly spaced morph levels (0–100%). Images were visually optimized and standardized in size, and low-level properties (luminance, contrast) were controlled using the SHINE toolbox.

Stimuli were presented in an event-related design across six blocks (one per morph continuum), each containing 80 trials. Block and stimulus order were randomized. Faces were shown for 1 s, followed by a jittered interstimulus interval (2.6–3.4 s). Participants performed a two-alternative forced-choice task indicating the predominant emotion. The paradigm lasted approximately 35 minutes.

MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens TIM Trio scanner using a T2*-weighted EPI sequence (TR = 1296 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel size = 3 mm³, 38 slices, multiband factor 2). A high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical image was also collected (1 mm³). Preprocessing was performed in SPM12 and included slice-time correction, realignment, coregistration, segmentation, normalization to MNI space (2 mm³), and spatial smoothing (6 mm FWHM).

Behavioral data were modeled using logistic psychometric functions, yielding parameters for curve steepness (α) and decision threshold (xhalf). Group differences were assessed using one-way ANOVAs. fMRI data were analyzed using a GLM approach. Five regressors representing ambiguity levels were modeled at the first level, with motion parameters as nuisance regressors. Group-level analyses used a within-subject ANOVA. Contrasts assessed overall face processing, increasing ambiguity, and decreasing ambiguity. Brain–behavior relationships were examined using second-level multiple regression analyses relating αto ambiguity-related brain activation.

Results

The results are structured into four complementary sections addressing behavioral performance, general face-related neural activity, ambiguity-related brain responses, and brain–behavior relationships.

Behavioral analyses revealed sigmoidal psychometric functions for all emotions, confirming systematic emotion categorization across morph continua. The slope of the curves (α), reflecting discrimination sensitivity, and the decision threshold (xhalf), indicating the proportion of emotional information required for recognition, differed significantly across emotions. Happy expressions showed the steepest slopes, suggesting superior discrimination accuracy, likely due to their positive valence. In contrast, happy and angry expressions exhibited the highest xhalf values, indicating that a larger proportion of emotional content was required for reliable identification. These results demonstrate emotion-specific differences in both sensitivity and decisional bias during emotion perception.

Across all experimental conditions, face perception elicited widespread neural activation. Strong responses were observed in occipital visual regions, medial prefrontal areas, and, most prominently, in the right fusiform face area (FFA), consistent with its established role in face processing. This activation pattern was independent of the displayed emotion and ambiguity level, reflecting a core face-processing network.

Ambiguity-related analyses revealed a clear functional dissociation within prefrontal cortex, in line with a priori hypotheses. Increasing ambiguity predominantly recruited dorsal prefrontal regions, including the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, bilateral insula, supplementary motor area (SMA), and inferior frontal gyrus (IFG). These regions are commonly associated with cognitive control, conflict monitoring, and decision uncertainty. In contrast, decreasing ambiguity was associated with ventral prefrontal engagement, including the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and ventral anterior cingulate cortex, as well as the left superior frontal gyrus. Additionally, decreasing ambiguity extended activation toward occipito-parietal regions, and at a more liberal statistical threshold, also engaged the amygdala and hippocampus, suggesting increased involvement of affective valuation and memory-related processes as perceptual certainty increased.

Finally, brain–behavior correlations linked neural activation to individual differences in emotion perception. Steeper psychometric slopes (higher α), indicating better emotion discrimination, were positively correlated with activity in the IFG and SMA during increasing ambiguity, highlighting the role of frontal control mechanisms in resolving perceptual uncertainty. During decreasing ambiguity, positive correlations were observed in the precuneus and middle cingulate cortex, as well as in the left temporoparietal junction, regions implicated in integrative processing and decision confidence. Together, these findings demonstrate how distinct dorsal and ventral neural systems support emotion perception as a function of ambiguity and behavioral performance.

Conclusion

Behavioral results were consistent with previous emotion perception studies1. Psychometric curves exhibited the expected sigmoidal shape, confirming systematic emotion categorization across morph continua. Differences in curve steepness (α), reflecting discrimination sensitivity, were observed across emotions. Steeper curves indicate more accurate emotion recognition, and comparative analyses showed that happy expressions were discriminated most reliably. This effect is likely related to happy being the only positive emotion among the stimuli, whereas the remaining emotions were negative, replicating well-established findings of emotion-specific differences in recognition performance.

The fMRI results demonstrated a clear dissociation between neural responses to increasing and decreasing ambiguity. Increasing ambiguity was associated with significant activation in frontal and salience-related regions, including the supplementary motor area (SMA), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), and bilateral insula (FWE-corrected, p < 0.05). Decreasing ambiguity, in contrast, engaged a distinct network comprising the temporoparietal junction (TPJ), middle cingulate cortex (MCC), precuneus, left superior frontal gyrus, and ventromedial prefrontal cortex/ventral anterior cingulate cortex (vmPFC/vACC) (FWE-corrected, p < 0.05). These findings confirm the hypothesis that ambiguity primarily modulates frontal brain regions and support a dorsal–ventral functional organization of the prefrontal cortex.

The dorsal activation pattern observed during increasing ambiguity is consistent with prior reports implicating dmPFC, IFG, and insula in emotional uncertainty2,3. Although SMA activation has not always been explicitly described, overlapping coordinates and variable anatomical definitions suggest that SMA, pre-SMA, dACC, and dmPFC likely form a closely related evaluative network. Functionally, these dorsal regions are associated with emotional evaluation, cognitive control, and uncertainty processing.

Conversely, ventral prefrontal engagement during decreasing ambiguity aligns with models assigning regulatory and confidence-related functions to the vmPFC and vACC. Reduced ambiguity likely lowers evaluative demands and enhances conflict regulation. Additional activation in TPJ, MCC, and precuneus may reflect integrative or task-specific processes related to certainty and decision formation.

Finally, exploratory brain–behavior correlations revealed that steeper psychometric slopes were associated with increased activation in the IFG and SMA during increasing ambiguity, supporting the role of dorsal frontal regions in accurate emotion discrimination. No corresponding correlations were observed in ventral prefrontal regions during decreasing ambiguity; instead, associations emerged in TPJ, MCC, and precuneus. These findings warrant further investigation to clarify the underlying network mechanisms, potentially using approaches such as dynamic causal modeling.
