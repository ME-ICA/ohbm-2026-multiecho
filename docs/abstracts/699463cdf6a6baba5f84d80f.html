Introduction

Cognitive impairment (CI) affects up to 70% of people with MS (PwMS) and substantially impacts daily functioning and quality of life. CI in MS is highly heterogeneous, with diverse profiles of impairment involving different cognitive functions. However, current clinical practice often reduces cognition to a binary impaired/preserved classification or relies only on the most frequently impaired domain (i.e., processing speed). This approach oversimplifies the complexity of CI and risks misclassifying patients, particularly those with non-speed-related impairment. To address this limitation, recent studies1 have proposed cognitive phenotypes (CogPs) that group PwMS by similar profiles using theory-driven or data-driven clustering approaches. Although these works highlight the potential of CogPs for capturing CI heterogeneity, most were limited by cross-sectional designs, small single-country cohorts, and clustering methods that do not model temporal progression. More advanced machine-learning (ML) approaches (e.g., the Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) algorithm) can model phenotypic and temporal heterogeneity simultaneously, identifying cognitive subtypes and stages participants are at. Despite this potential, it remains unclear how SuStaIn-derived CogPs relate to those obtained with conventional clustering or theory-driven approaches.

Determining whether these methods capture complementary aspects of CI heterogeneity is essential for clinical translation: it would reveal whether they converge on common underlying cognitive subtypes or highlight distinct, yet clinically relevant, patterns of CI, with direct implications for risk stratification, follow-up strategies, and treatment planning in PwMS. 

Using a large, international multicentre MS cohort, we aimed to (1) identify CogPs using SuStaIn, LPA, K-means, and two theory-driven methods, and (2) compare the concordance of SuStaIn subtypes and stages with CogPs from these alternative approaches.

Methods

We analyzed cognitive and clinical data from 3,156 PwMS from 17 centers (Europe, US, Canada) including PwMS and clinically isolated syndrome (CIS). Cognitive performance was assessed using either BRB-N or MACFIMS batteries. All tests were mapped to their corresponding primary domains, harmonized using country-specific norms, and missing data were imputed using predictive mean matching. 

  • Identifying cognitive phenotypes 

To identify SuStaIn-derived CogPs and stages, we split the cohort into a training (N=2018) and an external dataset (N=1128) using simulated annealing to optimize between-cohort comparability while keeping centers intact. We then used four different conceptual approaches:

  1. SuStaIn was trained and cross-validated with ten-fold stratified cross-validation on the training cohort. We used cross-validation information criterion (CVIC) and log-likelihood to determine the optimal number of subtypes. We then applied the trained SuStaIn model to an external dataset to obtain subtype and stage membership. 

  1. LPA models the multivariate distribution of cognitive scores as a mixture of Gaussian components, estimating latent classes that differ in their mean cognitive profiles and, optionally, in their variances. Individuals are assigned to classes probabilistically. We fit LPA to Z-scored cognitive measures using tidyLPA, testing models with diagonal covariance and either equal variances across classes (Model 1) or class-specific variances (Model 2), evaluating 1 to 6 classes. Optimal class number was chosen using a multi-criteria framework: lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), entropy (≥0.70), parsimony, and avoidance of unstable micro-classes (<4% of the sample), balancing statistical fit with clinical interpretability.

  1. K-means clustering groups participants into K clusters by minimizing within-cluster variance and maximizing between-cluster separation. We applied K-means to Z-scored cognitive data using 200 random starts and up to 1000 iterations. We determined the optimal number K of clusters using a combination of: (1) total within-cluster sum of squares (elbow criterion), (2) mean silhouette coefficient, and (3) non-parametric bootstrap stability. For the latter, 50 bootstrap samples (80% subsampling) were used to compute the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) between iterations, quantifying stability of cluster assignments. We also assessed minimum and maximum cluster size to avoid unstable micro-clusters.

  1. Following previous work on theory-driven CogP classification, we applied two impairment thresholds (Z below -1.5 SD and -2 SD) across cognitive domains. Participants were classified into four mutually exclusive groups: (0) CP, (1) single-domain impairment, (2) bi-domain impairment, or (3) multi-domain impairment (≥3 impaired domains). 

  • Cross-method concordance

To quantify agreement between methods, we computed the ARI and Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) for each pairwise comparison for both SuStaIn subtypes and stages. Alluvial plots were used to visualize transitions of participants across different CogPs identified by different methods and to assess whether participants grouped together under one framework tended to cluster similarly under others.

Results

- SuStaIn-derived cognitive phenotypes and progression patterns 

Ten-fold cross-validation identified three SuStaIn-derived CogPs with distinct temporal trajectories (Figure 1): (1) verbal memory–led (VM, 47%), showing early verbal learning/recall impairment followed by slowing in visual processing speed and visuospatial memory, (2) verbal fluency–visual processing speed–led (VPS, 42%), characterized by early verbal fluency deficits and early-to-intermediate visual processing speed abnormalities progressing to broader memory involvement; (3) executive function–processing speed–led (ExPS, 11%), marked by early auditory processing speed and executive dysfunction, with memory impairment emerging only at later stages.

- Latent profile analysis (LPA)

LPA Model 2 with class-specific variances and four classes showed the best BIC, entropy balance and avoidance of micro-classes. It identified the following four CoPs: (1) cognitively preserved (CP, 25%), (2) mild processing speed and visuospatial memory impairment (50%), (3) mild multidomain impairment (8%), and (4) severe multidomain impairment (17%).

- K-means clustering 

The optimal number of clusters using the K-means clustering approach was four, consisting of: CP (22%), mild processing speed and visuospatial memory (34%), mild multidomain (26%), and severe multidomain (18%).

- Concordance across ML and theory-driven methods

The concordance between SuStaIn-derived CogPs and other cognitive classification methods was negligeable (ARI<0.06) (Figure 2). Overall, this seemed to be driven by a classification into severities of overall impairment versus classification into different domain-based subgroups. A moderate accordance was found between LPA and K-means models (ARI=0.36), and between LPA and theory-driven approaches (1.5 SD and 2SD) (ARI=0.18 and ARI=0.21, respectively) and between K-means clustering and theory-driven methods (ARI=0.31 and ARI=0.33, respectively). For SuStaIn, most people from the VM group were grouped as CP or mildly impaired by the other clustering approaches, while those from the ExPS group were more likely to be included in the severe multidomain groups (Figure 2). 

Compared to SuStaIn-derived subtypes, the accordance was higher for SuStaIn-derived stages with the other clustering techniques (LPA: ARI=0.20, K-means: ARI=0.35, theory-driven with 1.5 SD: ARI= 0.22, theory-driven with 2SD: ARI=0.27). 

Most people classified as CP by the LPA and the theory-driven (1.5SD and 2SD) methods fell within SuStaIn-derived stages 0 and 1, corresponding to the earliest disease stages. Individuals in the mild groups were mainly distributed across intermediate stages (3 to 9), whereas those in the severe multidomain group were predominantly assigned later stages (10 to 19).

Conclusion

This large, international study shows that different methodological frameworks capture distinct and only partially overlapping CogPs and hence different aspects of cognitive heterogeneity in MS. While LPA, K-means, and theory-driven classifications consistently identified groups reflecting severity gradients of impairment, SuStaIn uncovered three qualitatively distinct cognitive subtypes characterised by different patterns and sequences of domain involvement that were not detectable using conventional clustering approaches.

Despite this difference in subtype definition, SuStaIn-derived stages showed meaningful alignment with the severity profiles identified by the other methods, indicating that SuStaIn offers an additional dimension of phenotyping that is orthogonal to impairment level. Rather than providing a temporal interpretation, these findings indicate that SuStaIn provides complementary and novel clinically meaningful information, complementing existing frameworks by capturing qualitative heterogeneity in how cognitive domains are affected, enriching the representation of phenotypic architecture beyond the severity alone. 

Overall, our results highlight that cognitive impairment in MS does not cluster uniquely by severity or by domain, but rather that a multimodal approach is needed to reflect the full multi-dimensional heterogeneity. 

Further work integrating cognitive, neuroimaging, and biological markers may help determine how these complementary phenotyping approaches can be combined to optimize clinical prediction and treatment planning.
