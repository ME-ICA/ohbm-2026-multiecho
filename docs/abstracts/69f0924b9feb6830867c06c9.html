Introduction

Despite occupying only 10% of the brain’s volume, the cerebellum houses over 50% of its neurons, many of which are integral to higher-order cognitive processes. The posterior part of the cerebellum is involved in the fine tuning of social behavior, which is different in autism. Consequently, the cerebellum has emerged as a key region in the physiology of autism and may represent a potential therapeutic target for non-invasive brain stimulation1. However, understanding its structural and functional organization in autism remains challenging2. While our recent work revealed no significant structural difference in the cerebellum3 of participants with autism, the literature on cerebellar functional connectivity in autism remains inconsistent and at times even contradictory. These discrepancies likely stem from limited sample sizes and methodological heterogeneity. Here, we performed a comprehensive methodological evaluation in multiple large samples to reconcile these conflicting findings on cerebellar functional connectivity in autism, with the ultimate goal to guide future targeted interventions4. We systematically assessed the impact of region-of-interest definitions, diagnostic specificity, symptom associations, and longitudinal stability. Furthermore, we examined the generalization of cerebellar functional connectivity alterations across multiple experimental conditions (resting-state, movie-fMRI) and leveraged functional connectivity gradients5 to map the underlying cerebellar topography in autism. Finally, we utilized movie-fMRI to investigate dynamic cerebellar functional connectivity during the processing of ecological social stimuli, moving beyond static measures towards more naturalistic behavior. 

Methods

We analyzed fMRI data from neurotypical and participants with autism across three cohorts: European Autism Interventions (EU-AIMS) longitudinal cohort; INFOR Autism; Healthy Brain Network, HBN), as well as individuals from a pediatric transdiagnostic cohort (HBN), from a schizophrenia cohort (Human Connectome Project Early Psychosis, HCP-EP), and from a bipolar disorder cohort (BP-OLF), totaling 1,384 participants (Figure 1A). Standard fMRI preprocessing was performed with fMRIprep2 and with consensus preprocessing for EU-AIMS data, including combination of three echos, primary head motion correction, spatial smoothing with full-width half-maximum (FWHM) kernel of 6 mm, secondary head motion correction with ICA-AROMA and removal of signal from white matter and CSF. 

To evaluate robustness cerebellar-cortical seed-based connectivity, we defined cerebellar regions-of-interest (ROIs) using three complementary functional parcellations: a data-driven Independent Component Analysis (ICA), the Buckner atlas defining 7 resting-state networks in the cerebellum, and the Nettekoven atlas characterizing hierarchical probabilistic atlas of functional cerebellar organization. Cortical ROIs were defined using the Schaefer-400 atlas. Cerebellar-cortical functional connectivity was quantified as the Pearson’s correlation between the time series of each cerebellar and cortical ROI.

For each cerebellar ROI, comparisons between neurotypical participants and participants with autism were performed with a linear model including age, sex, diagnosis, mean framewise displacement and site of inclusion as covariates. Results were corrected with a False Discovery Rate correction (Figure 1B). 

We quantified the results similarity across datasets and conditions (resting state, movie data) as the correlation between t-stat maps for the effect of diagnosis. To map the underlying cerebellar functional organization in autism, we computed connectivity gradients of the cortico-cerebellar connectivity5. Finally, we studied group differences in dynamic cerebellar-to-cortical connectivity during the movie “The Present” (available in the HBN cohort) using a sliding window approach (Figure 1B).

Results

We identified increased resting-state functional connectivity between cognitive cerebellar regions and widespread cortical areas, notably the superior temporal region, a key hub for Theory of Mind and previously associated with autism (Figure 2A). This pattern was robustly replicated across two independent datasets (INFOR, HBN), generalized to a naturalistic movie watching paradigm, and remained stable longitudinally (Figure 2B). Crucially, these alterations were specific to autism and were not observed in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder cohorts (Figure 2B). Clinically, increased cerebello-cortical connectivity correlated with autism symptom severity (Social Responsiveness-Scale - SRS v2) within the autism group (Figure 2C) but was unrelated to social traits (SRS v2) in a large transdiagnostic cohort of patients without autism (Figure 2B). Mechanistically, the principal gradient derived from cortico-cerebellar connectivity to the cognitive cerebellar ROI (Buckner Network 7) revealed a compressed cortical hierarchy in autism, indicating that atypical cerebellar-cortical coupling drives excessive functional integration (Figure 2D). Finally, dynamic functional connectivity analysis of movie watching fMRI data demonstrated that cerebellar-cortical intersubject agreement in autism differed significantly from neurotypical participants, specifically during scenes requiring social mentalizing (Figure 2E).

Conclusion

We delineate a robust signature of increased functional connectivity between cognitive cerebellar regions and widespread areas of the cortex, notably the superior temporal sulcus - a critical hub for social cognition. This profile was specific to autism, longitudinally stable, and reproducible across two independent cohorts and diverse fMRI paradigms (resting-state and naturalistic movie watching). Mechanistically, connectivity gradient analysis indicates that this cerebellar-cortical hyper-connectivity drives a compression of the cortical hierarchy, leading to excessive functional integration in participants with autism. Finally, dynamic functional connectivity analysis captured the neural correlates of these differences during ongoing behavior, revealing disrupted cerebellar-cortical synchronization during social mentalizing in an ecological setting. Taken together, our findings reconcile previous discrepancies by establishing cerebellar-driven cortical compression as a robust, specific, and ecologically relevant mechanism underlying social differences in autism, providing a validated foundation for future cerebellar-targeted neuromodulation strategies.
