Introduction

Sensory categorization involves mapping incoming sensory information into discrete categories and requires a range of neural resources to process sensory stimuli, extract key features, decide on a response, and incorporate feedback to fine-tune representation and future categorization. Dorsal striatum critically contributes to learning new categories thanks to its many-to-one projections from distributed cortical networks that subserve motor planning, feedback processing, and decision-making, along with recent recognition of dorsal striatum’s role in task-relevant sensory processing.^1

Though clearly demonstrated in non-human animal models, the possibility of anatomically distinct subdivisions within human striatum and its relevance for auditory-related behaviors has not yet been specified. Addressing this gap requires high-resolution focus on human striatum to understand the anatomical structural connectivity between striatal subdivisions and auditory cortical regions. In the current study, we address this gap by analyzing structural connectivity from 7T diffusion-weighted MRI through the Human Connectome Project. 

Further, there is need for more precise functional specificity of striatal subdivisions as it relates to human auditory learning behavior. Though dorsal striatal responses in auditory category learning tasks are robust, prior work has focused on broad descriptions of the involvement, rather than focused investigations of functionally relevant subdivisions within striatum.^2 This is important because these auditory category learning tasks involve a diverse set of behaviors including processing of the auditory stimulus information and processing corrective feedback information over the course of learning. 

In the current study, we address this gap by investigating the entire time course of a non-native speech category learning task during 7T fMRI, honing in on responses involved in distinct components of the learning task. 

Methods

To identify structural connections supporting auditory learning and categorization, we conducted probabilistic tractography using publicly available 7T diffusion-weighted MRI data from 62 participants in the Human Connectome Project.^3 Data were acquired at 1.05-mm isotropic resolution using a two-shell diffusion scheme (b = 1000/2000 s/mm²). Using MRtrix3, we performed multi-shell, multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution and generated 100 million whole-brain probabilistic streamlines per participant in native space. Streamlines were weighted using SIFT2 to obtain biologically plausible connectivity estimates. Unthresholded connectivity matrices were computed using predefined cortical and striatal regions of interest (ROIs), with streamline counts normalized by striatal ROI volume.

Functional MRI participants were twelve native English speakers (18–30 years; 9 female) with no prior tonal language experience. All procedures were approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board. Auditory stimuli consisted of 16 monosyllabic /di/ tokens spoken by four native Mandarin talkers (2 male, 2 female) in each of the four Mandarin lexical tones (T1–T4), normalized for duration (300 ms) and intensity.

Participants performed a tone categorization task during 7T fMRI scanning. On each trial, participants categorized a single stimulus into one of four tone categories via button press and received visual feedback (“correct” or “wrong”) after a brief jittered delay. Each trial lasted 6 s, corresponding to two TRs. Each run contained three stimulus blocks, and participants completed six runs (288 total trials).

MRI data were acquired on a 7T Siemens scanner using high-resolution MP2RAGE anatomical imaging and sparse-sampling T2*-weighted BOLD fMRI. Data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep 22.1.1, including motion correction, distortion correction, co-registration, and normalization to MNI space. No spatial smoothing was applied.

Striatal ROIs were defined using the Tian subcortical atlas, including anterior and posterior caudate and putamen. Cortical ROIs were selected from superior temporal and inferior frontal cortex based on prior auditory category-learning studies.^1,2

Univariate fMRI analyses were conducted using GLMs implemented in Nilearn. Two contrasts were modeled: sound presentation (stimulus vs. baseline) and feedback (“correct” vs. “wrong”). First-level GLMs were estimated for each run. Adjacent runs were combined into three learning stages (early, middle, final) to improve reliability while preserving learning-related temporal structure. Mean beta estimates were extracted for each ROI. Motion parameters and framewise displacement were included as nuisance regressors.

For representational similarity analysis (RSA), event-specific responses to each of the 16 stimuli were estimated using a least-squares separate (LSS) approach. RSA compared neural dissimilarity matrices from striatal ROIs with categorical models of tone category (task-relevant) and talker identity (task-irrelevant), using Pearson correlation. Statistical inference was performed using ANOVA and paired-samples t-tests with FDR correction.

Results

We mapped structural connectivity between auditory-related cortex and dorsal striatum to identify pathways supporting human auditory category learning. Although auditory–striatal connectivity has been demonstrated in non-human primates,^4 and non-auditory corticostriatal connectivity has been studied in humans,^5 human auditory–striatal structural connectivity remains poorly characterized. Using 7T diffusion-weighted MRI from the Human Connectome Project, we performed probabilistic tractography between auditory cortical ROIs and dorsal striatal subdivisions.

Whole-brain tractography was computed for each participant and reduced to cortical–striatal connections of interest, yielding individual connectivity matrices that were averaged across participants. An across-subjects ANOVA revealed a significant three-way interaction between striatal subdivision (rostral vs. caudal), cortical ROI, and hemisphere. Across all cortical regions, connectivity with auditory cortex was stronger for putamen than caudate. Within both structures, caudal (posterior) subdivisions exhibited significantly stronger connectivity with auditory cortex than rostral (anterior) subdivisions, an effect that was consistent across auditory cortical regions.

In a separate cohort, participants with no prior tonal language experience learned to categorize Mandarin lexical tones during 7T fMRI scanning. Behavioral accuracy improved robustly across learning stages. Response and stimulus confusion matrices revealed increasing categorical consistency across learning, with well-defined category structure emerging by the middle learning stage.

Using univariate fMRI analyses, we examined auditory processing-related responses (sound > baseline) in dorsal striatum and cortex. All striatal ROIs showed significantly greater responses to sound than baseline. A repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a region-by-hemisphere interaction, with stronger sound responses in left compared to right anterior and posterior putamen, and a main effect of region, with stronger responses in anterior than posterior putamen. No effects of learning stage were observed. In cortex, all regions except pars triangularis exhibited significant sound responses, with left-lateralized effects in planum temporale and pars triangularis.

We next assessed representational content using representational similarity analysis (RSA). Neural dissimilarity matrices were more strongly correlated with the task-relevant Tone model than the task-irrelevant Talker model in both striatum and cortex. Model difference scores (Tone – Talker) revealed no significant effects across striatal regions. In cortex, there was a significant effect of region, with posterior superior temporal gyrus showing stronger category representations than primary auditory cortex and planum temporale. Collapsing across learning stage and hemisphere, all cortical regions showed significant category representations, whereas striatal effects did not survive correction.

Finally, we examined feedback-related processing by contrasting “correct” versus “wrong” feedback. In dorsal striatum, anterior putamen showed significant feedback-related responses. Feedback responses were stronger during early learning compared to final learning stages, with region-specific effects in caudate subdivisions. In cortex, feedback-related contrasts showed a main effect of learning stage but no region exhibited significant feedback responses after correction.

Together, these results demonstrate strong caudal auditory–striatal structural connectivity, robust behavioral learning within a single session, widespread auditory processing responses across cortex and striatum, cortical—but not striatal—representations of task-relevant categories, and learning-stage-dependent feedback responses localized primarily to rostral striatum.

Conclusion

Dorsal striatum plays a critical role in sensory learning and decision-making. Animal work converges on the existence of an “auditory striatum” in the posterior dorsal striatum that receives convergent input from auditory cortex and auditory thalamus, but functional specialization within human dorsal striatum has not been directly tested. In this study, we combined high-resolution 7T structural and functional MRI to characterize auditory corticostriatal connectivity and functional organization during auditory category learning in humans.

Using diffusion-weighted MRI from the Human Connectome Project, we identified putative white matter pathways linking auditory cortex to caudal subdivisions of dorsal striatum. Structural connectivity was stronger between superior temporal cortex and putamen than caudate and was consistently stronger for caudal than rostral subdivisions. This rostrocaudal organization parallels findings from tract-tracing studies in rodents and non-human primates and supports the existence of an auditory corticostriatal pathway in humans that could support auditory learning and categorization.

Using 7T fMRI during an auditory category learning task, we dissociated dorsal striatal contributions to auditory processing and feedback processing. Univariate sound responses were observed across most dorsal striatal regions, whereas feedback-related responses were strongest in anterior striatum. Anterior putamen showed stable feedback responses across learning, while anterior caudate exhibited strong feedback responses early in learning that declined significantly by the final learning stage. These findings suggest functional differentiation within dorsal striatum, with rostral regions preferentially supporting feedback-related processes during learning.

We further examined stimulus representations using representational similarity analysis. Neural responses in both cortex and striatum were more strongly related to task-relevant Tone category structure than to task-irrelevant Talker identity. In cortex, category representations were strongest in posterior superior temporal gyrus, consistent with prior work showing the emergence of categorical representations in non-primary auditory cortex during learning. Although striatal representations were weaker and less regionally differentiated, they nonetheless reflected task-relevant category structure.

Together, these results provide converging structural and functional evidence for rostrocaudal specialization within human dorsal striatum. Structurally, caudal striatum shows stronger connectivity with auditory cortex; functionally, rostral striatum is preferentially engaged in feedback processing during learning, while caudal regions are positioned to support sensorimotor integration. This organizational pattern aligns with contemporary models of striatal function across species and supports subdivision-specific contributions of caudate and putamen to human auditory category learning and decision-making.
