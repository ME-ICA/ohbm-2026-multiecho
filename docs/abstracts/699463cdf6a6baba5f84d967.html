Introduction

When viewing a crowd of faces, the brain does not analyze each individual item separately but rapidly extracts summary statistics such as the average gender and the variability in emotional expressions. Early fMRI studies identified regions in the ventral visual pathway (e.g. the parahippocampal cortex)  as a potential locus for encoding ensemble statistics ^1, while later work demonstrated that average orientation information can be reconstructed in early visual areas, with additional frontoparietal engagement when attention is focused on ensemble information.^2 However, it remains unclear whether ensemble perception relies on a single, feature-independent mechanism or distinct neural systems for different visual features and summary descriptors, such as the mean and the variability of a visual ensemble.

Methods

We conducted an fMRI study (N=24) using carefully controlled stimuli that independently varied ensemble predominant class and variance across three levels of visual complexity: low-level orientation (gratings), mid-level shape (spiky-stubby objects), and high-level animacy (living vs. non-living objects). Participants viewed ensembles of twelve objects arranged in a grid (Figure 1A) varying in both the predominant class (i.e. [Class A] 9:3 & 7:5 vs [Class B]  7:5 & 3:9) and the abstract ratio (variance) (i.e. [Class A] 9:3 & 3:9 vs [Class B]  7:5 & 5:7). There were 216 trials per ensemble type across 2 different scanning sessions of 1h. Processing included brain extraction using FSL’s BET, removal of motion artifacts with AROMA, spatial smoothing with a 3 mm Gaussian kernel, high-pass filtering (60 s cutoff), co-registration across runs, z-scoring, and linear detrending. First, a searchlight decoding analysis with a 6 mm sphere was used to identify cortical regions representing ensemble the predominant class and variance (i.e. abstract ratio)

Since the searchlight results show no evidence of variance decoding, we were inspired by recent research suggesting that brain activity follows a manifold-based geometric structure, and accordingly we further assessed whether both ensemble predominant class and variance could be isolated in the ventral visual pathway by using different manifold learning methods. Manifold learning aims to reveal low-dimensional structures embedded in high-dimensional neural data, providing insight into how neural information is organized.^3

Twelve manifold learning algorithms both linear and nonlinear, including deep learning–based are evaluated to analyze the underlying neural mechanisms, and their performances are compared to determine which manifold learning approach best captures the neural structure underlying ensemble perception. Analyses were performed in a ROI mask of the ventral visual pathway, including occipital areas in lingual and pericalcarine cortex, lateral occipital areas, the fusiform and inferior temporal cortex.

The nonlinear methods consisted of CLIP, supervised and unsupervised CEBRA(20), T-PHATE(50), PHATE(50), TRACE(200), LLE(50), and UMAP(50), while the linear baselines included LDA(1), NCA(50), and PCA(0.85) (the numbers in parentheses indicate the embedding dimension.) Among the nonlinear methods, CLIP, CEBRA, and TRACE rely on deep-learning architectures. Both CLIP and TRACE were slightly modified for the present study.^4^5 All embeddings produced by methods other than CLIP and trace-based approaches were subsequently classified using a support vector machine (SVM).

In the modified CLIP model, two additional layers are introduced to map fMRI features into the CLIP visual embedding space. The fMRI input is first transformed into a lower-dimensional latent representation via a linear layer followed by a nonlinear activation. This representation is then projected to match the dimensionality of the CLIP visual projection matrix, aligning fMRI-derived embeddings with the CLIP vision encoder. A classifier operating in the CLIP embedding space—comprising a normalization layer and a final linear unit—outputs a single logit for binary classification.

TRACE is similarly adapted. While the original TRACE architecture optimizes a bottleneck classifier loss and a reconstruction loss, the present implementation employs a three-component objective: (i) a bottleneck-predominant classifier loss, (ii) an abstract-ratio classifier loss (both optimized using binary cross-entropy), and (iii) a reconstruction loss computed using mean squared error.

Results

First, searchlight decoding results in Figure 1B showed that the predominant class could be decoded in the different areas of the visual cortex for the different ensemble types, but no significant clusters were found in the ventral visual pathway for ensemble variance. We then used different manifold learning methods to probe further the representation of both ensemble variance (i.e. ratio) in the ventral visual pathway ROI, as well as assessing whether isolating the neural representation of the ensemble predominant class could be enhanced by different manifold techniques.

As shown in Figure 2, when performing classification across the three distinct tasks in this dataset, classical non-linear manifold learning methods—such as LLE, UMAP, PHATE, and T-PHATE—proved insufficient for data separation. These methods hovered near chance-level performance and lagged behind linear methods; in particular, PCA and NCA showed promising results. In contrast, Deep Learning-based approaches like CLIP and TRACE achieved high accuracy rates by effectively resolving complex underlying relationships within the data. When comparing linear and deep learning methods, TRACE and TRACE (soft label) performed slightly better than the linear methods.

Finally, we assessed whether the representation of variance (abstract ratio) generalized across the different ensemble stimuli. We systematically held out one stimulus type as the test set while training the model on the remaining two stimulus types. This procedure was repeated for each stimulus category (shape, orientation, and animacy), allowing us to evaluate whether learned representations generalized across different stimulus dimensions, with training performed using a three-loss TRACE–based objective. When shape stimuli were held out for testing and the model was trained on orientation and animacy trials, mean decoding accuracy reached 58.2%. Holding out orientation stimuli for testing yielded comparable performance, with mean accuracies of 58.8%. Similarly, when animacy stimuli were used as the test set and the model was trained on shape and orientation trials, decoding accuracy reached 57.5% for abstract ratio classification.

Conclusion

This study examined whether ensemble predominant class and ensemble variance rely on shared or distinct neural representations across multiple levels of visual complexity. Using fMRI decoding combined with a systematic comparison of manifold learning approaches, we show that ensemble predominant class and variance differ markedly in how they are represented in the visual system.

Searchlight decoding revealed robust representations of ensemble predominant class across visual cortical regions for low-, mid-, and high-level stimuli, consistent with previous evidence that summary statistics can be extracted from visual cortex. In contrast, ensemble variance (abstract ratio) could not be decoded from visual regions using standard searchlight analyses, suggesting that variance is not encoded in a spatially localized or linearly separable format within the ventral visual pathway.

To address this limitation, we applied a range of linear, nonlinear, and deep learning–based manifold learning methods. Nonlinear techniques (e.g., LLE, UMAP, PHATE) performed near chance. In contrast, deep learning–based models, particularly TRACE and the modified CLIP models achieved reliable decoding performance, and tended to outperform baseline linear models such as PCA or LDA when addressing the representation of ensemble variance. These results indicate that ensemble variance is encoded in a distributed and possibly nonlinear representational space that may benefit from supervised representational learning. Ongoing work is improving the modeling to better characterize the geometry of these representations and to determine which architectural components are critical for capturing abstract variance information.

Critically, variance representations generalized across stimulus domains. Models trained on two stimulus types successfully decoded abstract ratio in a held-out category, demonstrating feature-invariant representations of ensemble variance. This cross-domain generalization supports the notion that ensemble variance is represented at an abstract level, independent of specific visual features such as orientation, shape, or animacy.

Together, our findings suggest that a single, feature-independent ensemble mechanism is unlikely to fully account for how summary statistics are computed in the human brain. The representation of ensemble predominant class appears to rely on more localized and readily decodable visual representations, whereas the representation of ensemble variance in the ventral visual pathway is more distributed, and encoded in more abstract format - likely becoming more accessible through advanced decoding and representation-learning approaches. More broadly, this work highlights the importance of moving beyond conventional fMRI decoding when probing high-level summary statistics and demonstrates the potential of manifold learning techniques to reveal latent neural structure underlying ensemble perception.
