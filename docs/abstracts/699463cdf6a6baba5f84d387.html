Introduction

Mental time travel, the ability to re-experience past events and imagine future scenarios, is often assumed to depend on visual imagery and scene construction. Blindness provides a critical test of this assumption. If visual experience were indispensable, individuals blind from birth should show profound deficits in episodic simulation. Yet behavioral evidence suggests otherwise, though neural mechanisms remain unclear. We investigated whether mental time travel persists without vision, and how its scaffolding and neural architecture adapt in congenital and late blindness compared to sighted individuals.

Methods

We studied 81 participants: 22 congenitally blind (CB), 22 late-blind (LB), and 37 sighted controls (CTL). Structured interviews assessed autobiographical memory, scene construction, and episodic future thinking. Language analyses quantified perceptual versus conceptual detail density and lexical diversity. Neuroimaging included task-based fMRI, resting-state connectivity, and diffusion tensor imaging. Tasks used auditory cues to elicit autobiographical and imagined events. Group comparisons were adjusted for age; multimodal data were integrated using principal component analysis.

Results

Mental time travel remained phenomenologically intact across groups, but its scaffolding diverged. Sighted participants relied on perceptual detail and visual imagery, whereas CB participants emphasized conceptual elements such as thoughts and emotions. LB showed intermediate profiles. Lexical diversity was highest in CTL, intermediate in LB, and lowest in CB, indicating reliance on stereotypical templates in blindness. Neuroimaging revealed a common episodic network across groups, including hippocampus, PCC, and vmPFC. Group-specific adaptations emerged: CTL showed stronger parahippocampal place area (PPA) activation, while CB recruited occipital cortex via cross-modal plasticity. Brain–behavior correlations demonstrated a double dissociation: PPA activity tracked perceptual detail, occipital activation tracked conceptual detail. Connectivity analyses showed increased coupling between PPA and occipital cortex in CB, alongside reduced occipital white matter integrity. Principal component analysis identified a perceptual–conceptual axis explaining 30% of variance, with CTL > LB > CB.

Conclusion

Our findings challenge the assumption that visual imagery is essential for episodic simulation. Mental time travel persists in blindness, but its scaffolding shifts from perceptual to conceptual strategies, supported by occipital reorganization and strengthened network coupling. These results highlight the brain’s remarkable plasticity and demonstrate that coherence of event construction, rather than sensory fidelity, is the cornerstone of episodic memory and imagination.
