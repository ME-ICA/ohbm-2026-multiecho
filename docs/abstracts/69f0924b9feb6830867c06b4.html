Introduction

The complexity of playing a musical instrument motivates researchers to use musicians as a model for studying multimodal human brain plasticity. A recent meta-analysis1 identified more than 80 MRI studies comparing musicians and non-musicians. Yet despite this extensive literature, music-based neuroplasticity studies tend to treat “musicians” as a homogeneous group or focus on a single instrument. Because a flute player requires distinct motor, somatosensory, and auditory skills compared to those of a drummer, we believe that the diversity of musical instruments is likely to shape brain structure and function in instrument-specific ways. However, these differences remain understudied, largely due to the lack of datasets that enable direct between-instrument comparisons.

Naturalistic imaging uses continuous, real-life–like stimuli rather than simplified, constrained laboratory tasks, making it particularly suitable for studying complex and dynamic experiences. Because music cannot be reduced to isolated components (notes, rhythms, timbre) without altering its essential structure, this approach offers a more ecologically valid way to examine music perception and its neural representation2. Despite their growing popularity, naturalistic fMRI studies lack standardization in stimulus design and selection, a limitation that is particularly evident in music-listening paradigms.

To address these gaps, we created a set of auditory stimuli specifically tailored for naturalistic fMRI music-listening paradigms. The stimuli span multiple genres and instruments while remaining continuous, ecologically valid, and engaging. Using these stimuli, we demonstrate that individuals’ musical backgrounds systematically modulate their neural responses during naturalistic music perception.

Methods

Participants

Fifty-eight participants (52 musicians, 6 non-musicians) underwent MRI scanning, including anatomical, diffusion, and functional MRI (naturalistic auditory fMRI, and resting-state fMRI). The musicians (mean age: 31.3 years, SD: 11) had been playing their primary instrument for at least seven years (mean: 19.8 years, SD: 9.1), and most began at an early age (mean starting age: 11.6 years, SD: 6.5). The non-musicians had never learned or practiced a musical instrument. All participants reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders.

Stimuli

Participants listened to a 17.5-minute naturalistic musical piece titled Brain Movements, composed specifically for fMRI research to provide a continuous, ecologically valid listening experience. The piece consists of two separate parts (1st movement, 2nd movement) that dynamically transition across genres and instrument combinations to evoke a wide range of musical and emotional responses.

The musical pieces were composed by the distinguished Israeli composer Adi Rennert, performed by 24 professional musicians, and recorded in a state-of-the-art studio. It includes 18 musical instruments spanning six instrumental families: woodwinds, brass, guitars, strings, keyboards, and drums. Each instrument or instrumental group receives dedicated moments of prominence, including solo passages for all instruments except bass guitar and drum-set (whose timbrally related instruments, the contrabass and auxiliary percussion, were given solo parts instead).

The first movement (8:08 min) includes sequential segments of approximately 40 seconds in a classical-jazz hybrid style, 2 minutes of classical music, 3 minutes of rock, 30 seconds of classical, 1 minute of jazz-rock fusion, and a concluding minute of classical-jazz. The second movement (9:37 min) comprises 1.5 minutes of classical music, 2 minutes of jazz-rock, 2 minutes of classical, and a final 4 minutes of jazz. Overall, the stimulus includes approximately 3 min of rock, 6 min classical, 4 min jazz, and 4.5 min mixed/transitional material.

The stimulus was rated on average at 5.5 (SD = 1.15) on a 1-7 liking scale by the musicians’ group who had heard it once prior to scanning, indicating generally positive engagement. The two-part structure and stylistic diversity of Brain Movements enable investigation of brain responses to complex, naturalistic, and stylistically evolving musical experiences.

 

fMRI acquisition

Functional images were acquired on a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanner using multiband EPI (TR = 750 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 52°, multiband factor = 8). Seventy-two interleaved axial slices were collected (2 mm isotropic; FoV = 204 mm; matrix = 102×102; phase-partial Fourier = 7/8; bandwidth = 2334 Hz/Px) with fat suppression. The two movements were acquired as separate runs, containing 667 volumes (1st movement) and 786 volumes (2nd movement), corresponding to 8:08 and 9:37 minutes.


 Dynamic ISC

The fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIprep and parcellated with the 4S456Parcels atlas from the PennLINC AtlasPack3, which integrates the 400-region Schaefer cortical parcellation4 with subcortical, thalamic, hippocampal, amygdala, and cerebellar parcels. 

Dynamic intersubject correlation (ISC) was computed using the phase-randomization method described in the naturalistic-data notebook5. A sliding-window approach was used to match the temporal structure of the music (window size = 28 TRs, 21 s ≈ 8 musical bars, a “phase”; step size = 7 TRs, ~5 s ≈ 2 bars).

The dynamic ISC scores were for all musicians combined (N=52) and separately for three musician subgroups based on primary instrument group (Guitars, Wind instruments, Piano; N = 6 each), as well as for the non-musician group (N = 6).

Results

Inter-subject correlation (ISC) scores reflect the degree of synchronized brain activity within a group exposed to the same stimuli. Using dynamic ISC with sliding time windows allows us to identify which segments of the relatively long, detailed naturalistic stimuli Brain Movements drive synchronization and where this occurs in the brain.

Dynamic ISC revealed early high scores in the first two windows, an effect seen in all dynamic ISC analyses, across all groups and protocols, including resting-state fMRI. As this early effect is not stimulus-related, it will not be considered further

When considering all the musicians (N=52) as one group, dynamic ISC revealed that there are windows in which the ISC scores are relatively high across most regions (vertical lines in the heatmap,  Figure 1), specifically starting at TRs 35–42, 77–84, and 623–630 (Windows 5, 11, 90). Highest correlation within these windows primarily includes areas related to auditory cortices (LH-A1: r = 0.37; RH-A1: r = 0.46), and in somatomotor regions (Window 5, LH/RH-SPL: r = 0.54 /0.50, p = 0.0002), reflecting shared processing of salient musical features. Those ROIs showed consistently elevated ISC across most windows (horizontal lines in Figure 1), indicating relatively synchronized auditory processing during the stimulus.

Examining musicians' sub-groups, grouped by primary instrument, revealed instrument-specific effects, as shown in Figure 2. Peak synchronization for wind instrument players (W) occurred in window 71, with prominent ROIs including LH/RH VisCent (r = 0.7/0.76, p = 0.0002/0.0004); similarly, guitarists (G) peaked in window 17, with LH/RH VisPeri_ExStrSup (r = 0.86/0.79, p = 0.0002). Both those groups’ peak-synchronous windows reflect visual imagery of musical features despite the absence of visual input. Pianists (P) peaked in window 5, with strong LH-M1 upper limb activity (r = 0.75, p = 0.0004), consistent with instrument-specific motor simulation. This window includes a piano solo, which likely engages motor simulation related to piano performance. Non-musicians (NM) exhibited peak synchronization in window 5, with elevated ISC in LH-SPL (r = 0.84, p = 0.0002), suggesting sensorimotor engagement similar to all musicians (Figure 1). 

Analysis of dominant instruments during these peak windows revealed that the peak for pianists (P) and non-musicians (NM) aligned with a piano solo, the peak for wind players (W) aligned with a “conversation” of solos between saxophone and trumpet, and the peak for guitarists (G) aligned with an oboe solo, which was unrelated to the group’s primary instrument. Although pianists, all musicians, and non-musicians share a peak in the same time window, the ROIs involved differed: the LH primary motor area of the upper limb for pianists during the piano solo, bilateral auditory-motor regions in all musicians, and only left SPL for NM. This pattern suggests that musical background (here, primary instrument) shapes synchronization in each subgroup, particularly during instrument-specific salient events in the stimulus.

Conclusion

Our study introduces a new, uniquely designed naturalistic musical stimulus that can serve both neuro-musical and naturalistic imaging research. These fields are rapidly growing but still lack standardized, ecologically valid auditory materials. By offering a continuous, genre-spanning, instrument-rich composition tailored for fMRI, we provide a tool that can support future systematic investigations of music perception under real-world-like conditions.

Using this stimulus, we show that musical background systematically modulates neural synchronization during music listening, revealing both shared and instrument-specific patterns across groups. When all musicians were considered together, dynamic inter-subject correlation (ISC) showed peaks in auditory and somatomotor cortices, reflecting synchronized processing of salient musical features such as rhythm, melody, and timbre. These regions maintained relatively elevated ISC throughout the piece, suggesting that musicians engage in stable, coordinated auditory processing even during long, complex, and stylistically shifting musical passages.

Subgroup analyses based on primary instrument revealed distinct patterns of synchronization. Pianists showed ISC peaks in left primary motor cortex during a piano solo, consistent with motor simulation of performance. Wind players and guitarists exhibited peak ISC in visual cortices, suggesting recruitment of visual imagery or visually grounded representations of musical gestures despite the absence of visual input. Non-musicians showed peak ISC predominantly in left superior parietal regions, indicating more limited sensorimotor engagement with salient musical events. Notably, although several groups shared the same temporal ISC peaks, the spatial distribution (across ROIs) of these peaks differed, underscoring how training and expertise shape the topography of synchronized neural responses.

These findings highlight that the category “musicians” is far too broad for understanding neural variability during music perception. Instead, instrument-specific experience (and likely additional aspects of training such as primary genre, accumulated practice hours, and years of learning) meaningfully influence how the brain tracks and interprets complex musical structure. By expanding our dataset to include larger groups of both musicians and non-musicians, we aim to test how these broader musical-background variables contribute to individual differences.

Together, these results underscore the value of naturalistic, ecologically valid fMRI paradigms for probing complex cognitive and sensorimotor processes. They also open the door for future work examining cross-instrument differences, longitudinal training effects, and how instrument-specific neural systems integrate with broader auditory, motor, and multimodal networks.
