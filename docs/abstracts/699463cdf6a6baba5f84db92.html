Introduction

Advances in non-invasive neuroimaging, particularly functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), have expanded the use of computational connectomics to study excitation–inhibition (E–I) balance in the human brain. Excitatory neurons promote postsynaptic firing, whereas inhibitory neurons suppress neural activity, and their dynamic equilibrium—the E–I ratio—is disrupted in many neurological and psychiatric disorders.1

Several approaches have been developed to estimate the E–I ratio. Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (MRS) quantifies the metabolite concentrations of excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters, such as glutamate and GABA, within a single voxel, providing a biochemical proxy of E–I balance. However, MRS is limited by low spatial resolution, restricted brain coverage, and high cost. Electrophysiological recordings offer the gold standard for directly measuring E–I balance but are invasive and largely restricted to animal studies. To address these limitations, we have developed a fMRI-based computational framework termed Large-scale nEural Model Inversion (LEMI) to estimate regional E-I balance at the whole-brain scale.2 Nevertheless, this model relies on linear dynamics with restricted parameter ranges and fixed decay rate and E-to-I coupling, resulting in oversimplified representations of brain dynamics. Importantly, this model has not been validated with empirical data.

In this study, we extend the LEMI model into a new nonlinear LEMI (nLEMI) framework by incorporating nonlinear activation function, relaxing parameter constraints, and estimating decay rate and E-to-I coupling to more accurately model large-scale nonlinear brain dynamics. In addition to validation using ground-truth simulations, we further evaluate the model against MRS data to assess whether the inferred regional E–I ratios are associated with the empirical glutamate-to-GABA ratios. Our work represents an important step towards non-invasive brain-wide inference of E-I balance.

Methods

The overview of the nLEMI framework with associated optimization algorithm is shown in Figure 1. The neural activity (x) is generated by a neural mass network model consisting of multiple brain regions. Each network node consists of an excitatory (E) and an inhibitory (I) neural population with recurrent self-connections (WEE, WII). Within each node, the excitatory neural population excites the inhibitory neural population (WEI) while receiving feedback inhibition from the latter (WIE).  Across nodes, excitatory neural populations are coupled via inter-node effective connectivity (EC) (WRR). In the model, we set WII to zero as the I-I inhibition is much weaker compared to the I-E inhibition.

To infer E-I balance, empirical blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals are first deconvolved to obtain neural measurement y(t), which is modeled as a linear mapping of the neural activity with Gaussian noise (y(t) = Hx(t)+v(t)).2 The deconvolved signal y(t) is then used as input to a backpropagation Kalman filter,3 which estimates the latent neural state x̂(t) based on the neural mass model. The estimated state x̂(t) is mapped to the predicted neural measurement ŷ(t) via the observation matrix H. Model connectivity W and decay α are optimized using gradient descent algorithm by minimizing the loss function J, which quantifies the discrepancy between predicted and empirical neural measurement. Compared to the original LEMI model,2 we imposed a sigmoid activation function on neural activity, incorporated backpropagation via extended Kalman filter, relaxed the constraint on parameters, and estimated both the decay rate and E-to-I coupling, greatly enhancing model identifiability and generalizability.

For ground-truth validation, we generated 10 synthetic subjects with 80 brain regions by randomly sampling model parameters from uniform distributions: WRR ~ U(-1, 1), WEE ~ U(1, 3), WEI ~ U(1, 3), WIE ~ U(-4, -2), and α ~ U(0.3, 0.6). Ground-truth neural activity and measurement are generated using the neural mass model and the measurement model with Gaussian white noise, respectively. We simulated 2,000 seconds of neural activity with a repetition time (TR) of 2 seconds. The neural measurement is then convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function to generate synthetic BOLD time series.4 The nLEMI model runs for 3,000 iterations to ensure convergence, and at each iteration, 20 time points are randomly selected 5 times for Kalman filtering.

For MRS validation, we used the Aging Adult Brain Connectome (AABC) Release 1 dataset from the Human Connectome Project (HCP).5 This multi-site dataset includes preprocessed resting-state fMRI data from 1,248 participants. Among these participants, 138 individuals (75 females, 63 males; mean age = 66 years) underwent 7T MRS acquisition in the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC). For subjects who received MRS, BOLD time series were extracted from the preprocessed fMRI data using the Desikan–Killiany atlas with 84 regions of interest (ROIs). The fMRI data have a TR of 0.8 s, and each BOLD time series contains 1,912 time points. The nLEMI model was applied to BOLD signals to estimate the connectivity matrix W and decay rate α. For MRS validation, we defined both an intra-regional E-I ratio (EI-Intra) and an overall E-I ratio (EI-ALL). The intra-regional E-I ratio was computed as the ratio of local excitation (sum of WEE and WEI) to local inhibition (WIE), for the left and right PCC. The overall E–I ratio was calculated as the ratio of total excitation (sum of local excitation and all incoming positive EC) to total inhibition (absolute sum of local inhibition and all incoming negative EC), for left and right PCC. From the MRS data, the empirical E-I ratio was defined as the ratio of glutamate to GABA concentration and was computed for each subject. Finally, we assessed the relationship between the MRS-derived E-I ratios and the nLEMI-derived E-I ratios by calculating the subject-wise correlation.

Results

The results of ground-truth validation for one representative synthetic subject are shown in Figure 2A-F. The estimated neural state and measurement closely match the ground-truth neural states and deconvolved BOLD signals, respectively (Fig. 2A). Moreover, the estimated parameters show good agreement with the ground-truth values (correlation r > 0.59), with particularly strong correlations for WRR (Fig. 2B), WEE (Fig. 2D), and the decay parameter α (Fig. 2F) (all r > 0.75). In contrast, the correlations for WIE (Fig. 2C) and WEI (Fig. 2E) are lower, likely because excitatory populations contribute most strongly to the simulated BOLD signal, while the activity of inhibitory populations is only partially observable. Figure 2G and H shows the results of MRS validation. Both the intra-regional E-I ratios and the overall E-I ratios are significantly correlated with the empirical glutamate/GABA ratios (EI-Intra ratio: r = 0.283, p = 0.00087; EI-All ratio: r = 0.240, p = 0.00508), indicating that nLEMI can recover parameters related to the underlying excitatory and inhibitory physiology. The running time of the ground-truth validation and the MRS validation is 20 minutes and 24 minutes for each subject, respectively. Overall, the ground-truth and MRS validation results demonstrate that nLEMI can reliably and efficiently recover model parameters and yield physiologically meaningful E-I estimates when applied to empirical fMRI data.

Conclusion

We extend the LEMI model into the nLEMI framework by incorporating nonlinear activation functions and relaxing parameter constraints, enabling the model to more closely approximate real brain dynamics. In addition to validation using ground-truth simulations, we validate the model using MRS data to demonstrate its biological applicability to empirical human fMRI data. These results suggest that nLEMI has strong potential to accurately estimate whole-brain E–I balance based on fMRI data to support early detection of neurological or psychiatric disorders as well as the evaluation of treatment effects. Nevertheless, there remains substantial room for further improvement. Future extensions of the nLEMI model could incorporate anatomical or distance-based masks for long-range connections and include validation against electrophysiological recordings from animal models to further establish biological plausibility.
