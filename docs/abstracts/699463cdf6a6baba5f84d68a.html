Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) affects more than 300 million people worldwide, and roughly one third of cases are difficult to treat, as evidenced by the 20–30% of patients who do not respond to multiple therapies.1 Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) applied to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) is an established intervention, but remission rates vary widely, in part due to heterogeneity in target selection.2 Neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that stimulation sites in the dlPFC most anticorrelated with the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) yield superior antidepressant outcomes,3 yet a quantitative framework for target optimization is lacking. Despite advancements in neuroimaging-guided rTMS, variability in patient response highlights the necessity for mechanistic frameworks that can integrate structural and functional connectivity to inform individualized treatment planning. Network control theory (NCT) offers a formal, mathematically grounded approach to characterize the influence of regional stimulation on distributed brain dynamics and provides metrics such as average and modal controllability to quantify the capacity of brain regions to steer network states.4 We investigated which brain regions structurally and functionally control the sgACC and are controlled by the dlPFC. We hypothesized that NCT-derived controllability metrics would (i) replicate known dlPFC–sgACC anticorrelation targets, that is, the dlFPC is the best controller of the sgACC and (ii) differentiate MDD patients from healthy controls.

Methods

We analysed sturctural and functional imaging data from 972 participants (age: M = 37, sex: 65% female), comprising 362 healthy controls and 610 patients with the following diagnoses (326 MDD, 126 Anxiety disorder with comorbid MDD, 73 Bipolar Disorder, 68 Schizophrenia, 11 Anxiety Disorders) from the MACS (Marburg-Münster Affective Disorders Cohort Study).5 Diagnoses were given based on Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID). Data were acquired at two sites using Siemens 3T MRIs with aligned sequence parameters. T1-weighted images were segmented by applying the Brainnetome atlas using FreeSurfer. Diffusion-weighted images (DWI) were co-registered, distortion corrected with reverse phase encoding and corrected for eddy currents using FSL. Structural connectivity was reconstructed using constrained spherical deconvolution combined with diffusion tensor imaging using the CATO toolbox, yielding weighted adjacency matrices (210x210x972) based on number of streamline and fractional anisotropy. Resting-state functional images were co-registered, motion corrected global mean corrected, bandpass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz) and motion scrubbing (max FD=0.25, max DVARS=1.5) were applied. Functional connectivity was estimated using Pearson correlations, yielding weighted adjacency matrices (210x210x972). Brain dynamics were modeled as linear time-invariant systems (x(k+1)=A∗x(k)+B∗u(k)), where A is the structural or functional connectivity matrix and B defines input at stimulation sites (dlPFC). Controllability Gramian matrices were computed to answer how control input at a given node (dlPFC) propagates through the structural and functional networks and how much influence it has on each region, with specific interest in the sgACC (Figure 1). We also computed how much influence the sgACC receives from all other regions of the networks. As both regions of interest are differently located and defined in the literature, we tested two candidate nodes for each by using the node described as such in the Brainnatome Atlas and the node that covers the commonly reported MNI coordinates for the regions of interest.

Results

Results indicate that while the dlPFC node exerts control over the sgACC in both the structural and functional networks, it has more control over other brain regions. In the structural network, the dlPFC controls prefrontal regions and tempo-parietal regions the most, including the medial superior prefrontal cortex, the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex and the supramarginal and angular gyrus. In the functional network, the dlPFC controls insular, cingulate, inferior frontal regions and the right dlPFC the most (Figure 2). These regions are largely part of the salience network in the Yeo-7 atlas. The sgACC receives control from different regions of the brain. In both the structural and the functional network, the sgACC receives the most control input from neighboring regions, including the left and right medial and lateral orbitofrontal cortex as well as the rostral ventral anterior cingulate cortex. The controllability estimates for both exerted by the dlPFC and received by the sgACC based on the structural network correlate significantly with the estimates based on the functional network (r = 0.29, p < .001). In the functional network, more controllability is exhibited in general. Regions that are controlled by the dlPFC or that the sgACC receives control from in the structural network are also present in the functional network (Figure 2). Sex, age, their interaction and scanner site did not significantly influence any of the controllability estimates. When comparing controllability estimates between MDD patients and healthy controls, difference show that control exerted by the dlPFC in the functional network differs significantly in 19 regions, with maximum effect sizes reaching d = 0.25 in the right ventral superior temporal gyrus, left and right precuneus, however none survive FDR-correction. In the structural network, control exerted by the dlPFC differs in 40 regions between MDD patients and healthy controls, with maximum effect sizes reaching d = 0.3 and 3 regions surviving FDR-correction (right dorsal and superior superior frontal gyrus and left lateral angular gyrus).

Conclusion

NCT-derived controllability measures support existing anticorrelation-based TMS targets and are largely similar for MDD patients from healthy individuals. Controllability estimates on structural and functional brain networks showcase overlapping but also diverging results. In the functional network there is more control exerted and received than in the structural network. This can be explained by the fact that functional connectivity matrices are full whereas structural connectivity matrices are sparse and thus strucutral networks will show less controllability than functional networks. General patterns emerging are that the dlPFC does exert control over the sgACC, however more so over other regions and that the sgACC does receive control from the dlPFC, but more so from other regions as well. The findings support the integration of NCT as a quantitative framework to investigate and optimize individualized TMS target selection in MDD. Future work will validate these findings in patients undergoing rTMS. NCT may inform precision medicine approaches in neurostimulation beyond depression.
