Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent psychiatric disorder characterized by persistent depressed mood, anhedonia, and impaired daily functioning and quality of life 1. Cognitive dysfunction is a core feature of MDD and is closely linked to psychosocial impairment across the lifespan 2,3. Deficits in working memory, executive function, and processing speed have been associated with abnormal intrinsic brain activity and functional connectivity (FC), suggesting that cognitive symptoms in first-episode, treatment-naive MDD may reflect underlying brain alterations. 

Structural MRI studies report widespread abnormalities in MDD, particularly in cortical thickness (CTh), which reflects microstructural properties such as neuronal and glial density, dendritic architecture, and intracortical myelination 4. However, findings are heterogeneous, potentially due to differences in sample characteristics and antidepressant exposure. Structural abnormalities may be accompanied by FC disruptions, yet few studies have examined FC changes anchored to regions with abnormal CTh. Evidence implicates dysfunction of glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), which may impair synaptic plasticity and weaken top-down signaling to limbic regions 5, highlighting the need to assess effective connectivity (EC). Granger causality analysis (GCA) enables estimation of directional influences from resting-state fMRI, but the relationship between EC alterations and cognitive impairment remains unclear. 

In addition, MDD-related imaging abnormalities are modulated by neurotransmitter systems, and spatial associations with receptor/transporter distributions may inform molecular mechanisms. 

Therefore, we applied a cross-sectional multimodal MRI framework in first-episode, treatment-naive MDD to characterize CTh abnormalities and associated FC/EC alterations, and to relate these neural changes to cognitive performance and neurotransmitter profiles.

Methods

A total of 152 first-episode, treatment-naive patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) and 168 healthy controls (HCs) were enrolled at West China Hospital, Sichuan University. MDD was diagnosed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Axis I Disorders.

Clinical symptoms were assessed using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) and the 14-item Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA-14). Cognitive function was evaluated with standardized neuropsychological tests, including Digit Span Forward (attention), Digit Span Backward (working memory), and Trail Making Test A and B (processing speed and executive function).

MRI data were acquired on a 3.0T Siemens Trio scanner with an 8-channel head coil. T1-weighted structural images were collected using a 3D MPRAGE sequence. Resting-state fMRI data were obtained using a GRE-EPI sequence. During scanning, participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed, remain awake, and minimize head motion.

CTh was estimated using FreeSurfer (version 7.4.0), including motion correction, skull striping, intensity normalization, tissue segmentation, topology correction, and surface reconstruction. Individual cortical surfaces were registered to fsaverage5 (20,484 vertices), and thickness was parcellated into 68 regions (34 per hemisphere) using the Desikan–Killiany atlas. Intracranial volume was extracted for covariate control. Resting-state preprocessing followed a surface-based pipeline: removal of the first four volumes, slice-timing correction, motion correction with quality control, linear detrending, nuisance regression (motion parameters, white matter and CSF signals), band-pass filtering (0.01–0.10 Hz), spatial smoothing (6-mm FWHM), registration/resampling to fsaverage5, and atlas-based parcellation.

Regions showing significant between-group CTh differences were defined as ROIs for connectivity analyses. FC was calculated as Pearson correlations between each ROI and other cortical regions, followed by Fisher r-to-z transformation. EC was estimated using residual-based GCA, quantifying bidirectional directional influences between ROI time series and other regions; standardized residual F statistics (F′) were used for group inference.

Neurotransmitter receptor/transporter density maps were obtained from the Neuromaps toolbox, registered to fsaverage5. Maps included serotonin receptors (5HT1a, 5HT1b, 5HT2a, and 5HT6), dopamine receptors (D1, D2), GABA receptor (GABAa), acetylcholine receptor (a4b2), cannabinoid receptor (CB1), Mu-opioid receptor (MOR), glutamate receptor (mGluR5), histamine receptor (H3), acetylcholine receptor (M1), dopamine transporter (DAT), norepinephrine transporter (NET), acetylcholine transporter (VAChT), serotonin transporter (5-HTT), and synapse marker (SV2A). Spatial correlations between CTh alteration patterns and neurotransmitter maps were assessed.

Statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS 27.0. Group differences in CTh, FC, and EC were tested using general linear models controlling for age, sex, and total intracranial volume (TIV) (and mean framewise displacement for connectivity), with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Associations between altered imaging metrics and clinical/cognitive measures were examined using partial correlations with false discovery rate correction. Spatial neurotransmitter-thickness correlations were evaluated using Spearman correlation with 10,000-permutation testing to account for spatial autocorrelation.

Results

No significant differences were found in age, sex, or TIV between patients with MDD and HCs. Compared with HCs, MDD patients exhibited poorer overall cognitive abilities, particularly significant deficits in attention, processing speed, and executive function.

MDD patients exhibited significant cortical thinning in nine regions that primarily involved the prefrontal cortex, parietal cortex, and default mode network (DMN) hubs. These regions included the left medial orbitofrontal cortex, left pars opercularis, left rostral middle frontal gyrus, left superior frontal gyrus, left posterior cingulate cortex, left superior parietal cortex, right pars triangularis, right supramarginal gyrus, and right angular gyrus.

Functional analyses based on the structurally affected regions revealed FC and EC abnormalities within prefrontal circuitry and to other regions. MDD patients exhibited decreased functional connectivity between left superior frontal gyrus and bilateral medial orbitofrontal cortices, reduced directional influence from the left to the right medial orbitofrontal cortex, and enhanced effective connectivity from the left medial orbitofrontal cortex to the left insula.

Within the MDD group, altered EC was meaningfully related to cognitive variation. Stronger EC from the left medial orbitofrontal cortex to the right medial orbitofrontal cortex and to the left insula was associated with better cognitive performance, particularly in domains of attention, working memory, and executive control.

Finally, the spatial distribution of CTh alteration was negatively correlated with maps of serotonin receptor and the norepinephrine transporter.

Conclusion

This study found significant cortical thinning in first-episode, treatment-naïve major depressive disorder (MDD), mainly involving the prefrontal cortex and key regions of the frontoparietal network and DMN. Focusing on structurally affected regions, we further observed reduced FC and EC within the prefrontal cortex, together with increased EC from the left medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC) to the left insula. Importantly, altered EC in MDD showed positive associations with cognitive performance in attention, working memory, and executive function, suggesting coexisting deficits and compensatory processes.

The mOFC and lateral frontal gyri support high-order functions including emotion regulation, attentional control, and working memory. Consistent with prior finding, prefrontal cortical thinning was evident in MDD. Using the left superior frontal gyrus as a seed, FC with the bilateral mOFC was reduced. GCA further demonstrated decreased EC from the left mOFC to the right mOFC, and stronger connectivity was associated with better Digit Span Backward performance, indicating that reduced directional integration within the prefrontal cortex is linked to working-memory impairment.

Beyond the prefrontal cortex, cortical thinning extended to the left posterior cingulate cortex and parietal regions within the default mode and frontoparietal networks. Default mode network disruption is closely related to rumination and sustained negative effects in MDD, and our first-episode sample showed thinning in key default mode nodes despite inconsistencies in previous reports. The frontoparietal network is crucial for cognitive control and executive function, and structural compromise across these systems may reflect an imbalance between internally oriented processing and top-down control.

We also found increased EC from the left mOFC to the left insula. Prior work has reported reduced EC from the insula to the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex in MDD, and meta-analytic evidence indicates increased intrinsic activity and perfusion in the left insula. The insula acts as an integrative hub coordinating sensory, affective, and cognitive processes, and its abnormalities are implicated in emotion dysregulation and cognitive deficits. As a salience network core node that supports switching between the DMN and the central executive network, the insula helps coordinate attention, working memory, and executive control during cognitive demand. Therefore, strengthened prefrontal–insula EC may represent compensation, supported by its positive correlations with better cognitive performance within the patient group.

At the molecular level, CTh alterations showed significant spatial correlations with neurotransmitter receptor/transporter density maps. 5-HT1B is implicated in MDD pathophysiology and may modulate dopaminergic function. 5-HT6 is relatively highly expressed in cortex and is involved in cognition and affect, with several antidepressants showing notable affinity and antagonistic activity . NET is a key monoamine transporter and an important pharmacological target. Monoaminergic modulation of synaptic efficacy within prefrontal circuits can substantially influence executive functions, including attention and working memory.

In summary, multimodal MRI evidence indicates that first-episode, treatment-naive MDD is characterized by cortical thinning in prefrontal, default mode, and frontoparietal regions, accompanied by reduced within-prefrontal functional and EC. Meanwhile, enhanced EC from the left mOFC to the left insula, positively associated with cognition, suggests a compensatory mechanism potentially mediated by salience-network dynamics. The spatial coupling between cortical thinning and monoaminergic receptor/transporter profiles further supports a neurochemical basis for affective and cognitive dysfunction, providing convergent evidence for biomarker development and mechanistic clarification in MDD.
