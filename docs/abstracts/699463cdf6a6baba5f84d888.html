Introduction

Despite the growing body of research and the inclusion of Compulsive Sexual Behavior Disorder (CSB, code 6C72) in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), published in the 11th edition in recent years, many of the neural mechanisms underlying the development and persistence of symptoms of compulsive sexual behaviors (CSB) are poorly understood. The Incentive Sensitization Theory of addiction [1,2] suggests that maladaptive attentional and motivational processes directed toward reward-associated cues, coupled with impaired extinction of these previously learned associations, may drive addictive behaviors through alterations of the brain reward system.

The aim of this study was to investigate whether individuals with CSB exhibit altered appetitive associative learning, including both appetitive conditioning and extinction of cue–reward relationships and its underlying functional connectivity alternations within the reward system. Additionally, it was examined whether these alterations are specific to erotic rewards or extend to other appetitive stimuli (e.g. monetary), whether neurofunctional differences also manifest outside of any task (at rest). To this end, men struggling with CSB healthy control subjects underwent neuroimaging examinations whilst performing behavioral tasks, allowing to probe their self-assessed, behavioral and neuronal effects of associative learning.

Methods

Sixty-six right-handed heterosexual men participated (33 CSB and 33 healthy controls). Groups were defined using validated self-report screening for problematic pornography use and compulsive sexual behaviors, with exclusions for elevated gambling, obsessive–compulsive, or anxiety/depression symptoms and for MRI contraindications. During 3T fMRI, participants completed a 14-min resting-state, two 10-min conditioning runs followed by two 10-min extinction runs. Each trial presented a neutral shape cue predicting erotic reward, monetary reward, or a scrambled-image control, followed by a button press and outcome presentation. Conditioning used adaptive timing to maintain ~70% reinforcement; extinction withheld rewards. Cue-related valence and arousal were rated using the 7-point Self-Assessment Manikin before conditioning, after conditioning, and after extinction. Behavioral effects were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVAs on CS+–CS− difference scores. The fMRI data was analyzed with ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity analysis during task and rest (gPPI and Pearson-correlation, accordingly), using CONN toolbox. The 6 bi-lateral ROI were selected as key brain reward system regions implicated in associative learning and previously identified as altered in its functioning in CSB - ventral striatum, amygdala, anterior and posterior orbitofrontal cortices, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and ventromedial prefrontal cortex.

Results

Subjective ratings (CS+ minus CS−) showed successful acquisition in both groups but stronger and more persistent cue preference in CSB. For valence, ANOVA revealed effects of Group (F(1,58)=10.25, ηg²=.047, p=.002), Time (F(1.69,97.76)=24.64, ηg²=.199, p<.001), and Group×Time (F=4.63, ηg²=.045, p=.017), with no Condition Type effects (p=.333). CSB rated CS+ cues as more positive than HC (t=3.20, p=.002), and the between-group difference was localized to post-conditioning (t=2.81, p=.041). Differential valence was higher post-conditioning than pre-conditioning (t=6.10, p<.001), higher post-extinction than pre-conditioning (t=2.99, p=.011), and lower post-extinction than post-conditioning (t=4.31, p<.001). Extinction check (pre vs post-ext) remained significant in CSB (t=3.81, p=.007) but not in HC (t=0.59, p>0.999). For arousal, ANOVA showed effects of Group (F(1,58)=10.78, ηg²=.043, p=.002) and Time (F(1.70,98.61)=27.25, ηg²=.204, p<.001), and a trend for Group×Condition Type (F(1,58)=3.60, p=.063). CSB reported higher differential arousal than HC (t=3.28, p=.002), especially for CS+erotic (t=3.44, p=.004; CS+cash n.s.). Differential arousal was higher post-conditioning than pre-conditioning (t=6.63, p<.001), higher post-extinction than pre-conditioning (t=3.61, p=.002), and lower post-extinction than post-conditioning (t=4.16, p=.001). Extinction check was significant in CSB (t=4.11, p=.003) but not HC (t=1.15, p=.977).

Behaviorally, RT differences (Yeo-Johnson transformed) during conditioning showed a Group effect (F(1,56)=5.02, ηg²=.037, p=.029) and Group×Time (F=6.10, ηg²=.040, p=.017): CSB was faster than HC for CS+ cues (t=2.00, p=.050), specifically in late conditioning (t=3.11, p=.009) but not early conditioning (t=0.07, p>0.999). No Group effects emerged during extinction (all p≥.291). Condition Type effects were robust in both phases (conditioning F=29.74; extinction F=24.82; both p<.001), with longer RTs for CS+erotic than CS+cash (conditioning: t=5.59, p<.001; extinction: t=4.98, p<.001).

Generalized PPI analyses identified cue-specific connectivity alterations. During conditioning, Group×Condition Type effects showed stronger ventral striatum–anterior OFC coupling for CS+erotic in CSB (F=5.44, p=.005; t=3.24, p=.002) and a larger erotic>cash connectivity bias vs HC (t=2.41, p=.019). During extinction, ventral striatum–vmPFC coupling showed Group×Condition Type (F=4.95, p=.01) and Group×Time (F=7.19, p=.002) effects, with weaker connectivity for CS+erotic in CSB vs HC (t=3.15, p=.002) and weaker early-extinction coupling in CSB (t=3.80, p<.001).

Resting-state functional connectivity within reward system ROIs did not differ between CSB and control group.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into behavioral and brain reward system functioning aspects of appetitive conditioning and extinction in CSB. Both monetary and erotic rewards were included in the experimental design to study the specificity of associative learning alterations. Obtained results show that men struggling with CSB are prone to short-term general conditioning and extinction alterations towards cues of both erotic and monetary rewards, as reflected by self-assessment and reaction times. Intriguingly, the notion of generalized aberrant associative learning, however, was not supported by task-based functional connectivity analyses, which provided an account of erotic-biased processing of appetitive cues in both conditioning and extinction. 

These findings contribute to the ongoing debate regarding the classification of CSB. While CSB shares phenomenological similarities with substance use disorders and other behavioral addictions, such as heightened cue reactivity and compulsive engagement despite adverse consequences [3,4], the neurobiological underpinnings may differ between substance use disorders and CSB. The absence of resting-state functional connectivity changes in key brain reward region suggest that CSB might involve different neural mechanisms than those seen in substance use disorders or that the neuroadaptations are more subtle and context-specific.

In conclusion, in line with Incentive Sensitization Theory, the present findings contribute to the growing evidence that CSB may share certain neurobiological characteristics with addictive disorders, particularly regarding enhanced conditioning, cue-reactivity and disrupted extinction learning.
