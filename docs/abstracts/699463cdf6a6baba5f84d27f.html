Introduction

Schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (SSD) are characterized by substantial clinical and biological heterogeneity, with symptoms spanning positive, negative, disorganized, and cognitive domains. Understanding how common genetic risk shapes this multidimensional variation remains a challenge, as most studies rely on case-control disorder-specific polygenic scores which do not fully capture the broader liabilities shared across psychiatric conditions.1

A recent GWAS study has identified two higher-order factors of transdiagnostic genetic architecture of psychopathology in adulthood.2 Specifically, factor F1 reflects liability for common mood-related disorder symptoms, whereas F2 indexes rarer and more severe forms of mental illnesses. These genetic factors offer a framework to test whether broad genomic liabilities map onto symptom presentations in SSD, as well in brain structure. 

Cortical morphometry provides a sensitive index of neurodevelopmental and illness-related processes and may reflect biological pathways through which polygenic liability manifests in the brain.3 Yet it remains unclear whether genetic load of F1 and F2 relate to cortical structure in SSD, whether these associations differ from those observed in healthy individuals, or whether genetic risk influences longitudinal trajectories of cortical change and response to antipsychotic medication.

To address these questions, we combined transdiagnostic Poligenic Risk Scores (PRSs) of psychopathology, dimensional symptom measures, and vertex-wise cortical morphometry measures from a well-characterized cohort of individuals with SSD and healthy controls. Our aim is to determine how genetic liabilities for F1 and F2 relate to symptom dimensions, and to identify cross-sectional and longitudinal neuroanatomical correlates of transdiagnostic genetic risk.

Methods

Data were obtained from 385 unrelated adults aged 15-60 years (mean age: 30+8.6 years; 40% females) participating in the longitudinal 20-year-follow up Program for the Initial Phases of Psychosis (PAFIP) in Santander, Spain. All participants completed at least one T1-weighted structural MRI scan (1.5 and 3T scanners) and provided blood samples for genome-wide genotyping. The included sample comprises 147 healthy control individuals, and 238 individuals with SSD whose initial diagnosis was reviewed after a 6-month period and updated, if necessary, at that time or during follow-ups. Of which, 47% have a diagnosis of schizophrenia, followed by 28% with a schizophreniform diagnosis. 

Genome-wide SNP data underwent standard quality control and imputation, and analyses were restricted to individuals of European ancestry. Two PRSs were derived (see Fig 1a): PRS-F1, capturing genetic liability for symptoms and disorders related to mood-disturbance (including depressive, mania, psychotic symptoms and bipolar II disorder and Major Depressive Disorder), and PRS-F2, indexing liability for severe psychotic disorder (including schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, and bipolar I disorder). All PRSs were calculated using posterior SNP weights (PRS-cs), applied to imputed genotypes via PLINK, and standardized (z-scores).

Clinical symptomatology was modeled using a five-factor structure derived from a validated framework from Legge et al.,4 which integrates items from the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS), the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS), and standardized cognitive tests. This structure captures positive symptoms, negative symptoms related to diminished expressivity, negative symptoms related to motivation and pleasure, disorganization, and cognition (Fig 1c). We tested associations between each transdiagnostic PRS and the five symptom dimensions using linear regression models adjusting for age, sex, and the first 5 ancestry principal components (PCs).

Structural scans underwent rigorous visual quality control and were processed using FreeSurfer’s longitudinal pipeline to create within-subject templates prior to map extraction (v7.4.1). Cortical reconstructions were inspected and mean euler index was used as a proxy of in-scanner motion. Baseline vertex-wise cortical volume and surface area maps were extracted in fsaverage space, and surface maps were smoothed with a 15-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel (Fig 1b).

Cross-sectional PRS associations with cortical morphometry were first examined using a general linear model framework with cluster-wise correction. We applied both exploratory and conservative vertex-wise cluster-defining thresholds  (Z>1.3, 2.3 and 3.3, respectively) . For each morphometric measure and hemisphere, cluster significance was evaluated at a cluster-wise p=0.05, correcting across hemispheres. Directionality of effects was assessed by evaluating positive and negative cluster growth. We then performed mass-univariate vertex-wise linear mixed-effects modeling incorporating all available T1 images across time for each participant using fslmer in R.5 We included random intercepts for subjects and fixed effects for age, sex, 5 ancestry PCs, intracranial volume, mean euler, scanner, and time. Within the SSD group, we tested three contrasts: the main effect of PRS, the PRS-by-time interaction, and the PRS-by-antipsychotic dose interaction based on chlorpromazine-equivalent medication exposure. For each contrast, we computed group-level F-statistics, vertex-wise p-values, and signed direction maps. Multiple comparisons were controlled using False Discovery Rate (FDR). Significant clusters were interpreted using both the magnitude of F-statistics and the directionality of the effects. The formulas used in the models are described in Fig. 1d.

Results

PRSs showed limited associations with clinical symptom dimensions. Among the five factors analyzed, only negative experiential symptoms showed a trend-level association with PRS-F2 (β=0.18,95% CI=−0.00–0.37, p=.05), explaining 2.2% of the variance. No significant associations were observed for PRS-F1, or for other symptom dimensions.

Cross-sectional vertex-wise analyses (Fig. 2a) revealed a negative association between PRS-F1 and cortical volume in the right lateral occipital cortex across the overall sample, which remained after cluster correction (only at Z>1.3). For PRS-F2, cross-sectional effects after stringent cluster-wise threshold (Z>3.3) appeared mainly as PRS-by-group interactions in the right supramarginal and postcentral regions, showing opposite patterns: individuals with SSD had lower surface area with higher PRS-F2, whereas healthy controls had higher surface area. No PRS-F2 main-effect cluster remained after correction.

Longitudinal mixed-effects analyses (Fig. 2b) showed widespread associations between polygenic liability and cortical volume. Both PRS-F1 and PRS-F2 demonstrated negative main effects across bilateral frontal, temporal, and parietal cortices, (i.e., higher polygenic burden was associated with lower overall cortical volume across time). Significant regions included middle frontal, precentral, supramarginal, posterior cingulate, and superior temporal cortices for PRS-F1, with overlapping precentral, supramarginal, insular, and orbitofrontal areas for PRS-F2. PRS-by-time interactions showed both positive and negative effects, indicating region-specific moderation of cortical volume trajectories. For PRS-F1, positive PRS-by-time effects emerged in lingual, fusiform, parahippocampal, and lateral occipital regions (i.e., higher PRS was associated with slower volume decline), whereas negative PRS-by-time effects appeared in frontal and parietal association cortices (i.e., higher PRS predicted steeper volume loss). PRS-F2 showed negative interactions in precuneus, posterior cingulate, and inferior parietal areas (steeper decline with higher PRS) and positive interactions in precentral, middle temporal, superior frontal, and medial orbitofrontal cortices (slower decline with higher PRS). In contrast, PRS-by-antipsychotic dose interactions were uniformly positive (i.e., higher PRS was associated with larger cortical volumes at higher medication exposure), suggesting that antipsychotic treatment may moderate the impact of genetic liability on cortical structure, though the mechanisms remain to be clarified. Future work will determine whether similar patterns are present for surface area.

Conclusion

In this work, we combined transdiagnostic PRSs, dimensional symptoms, and cortical morphometry measures to characterize how broad genomic liabilities relate to brain structure in SSD. F1 and F2 PRSs, indexing mood-related and severe psychotic genetic liability respectively, showed distinct neuroanatomical signatures. PRS-F2 displayed a specific positive association with negative experiential symptoms.

Cross-sectional analyses identified specific cortical correlates of both PRSs, with PRS-F1 linked to reduced lateral occipital volume and PRS-F2 showing group-dependent effects in supramarginal and postcentral regions, highlighting differential genetic expression in individuals with SDD and healthy controls. Longitudinal models revealed more widespread and robust associations: higher F1 and F2 scores were consistently related to lower cortical volume across frontal, temporal, and parietal association cortices. PRS-by-time interactions showed that genetic liability shapes cortical volume trajectories in a regionally-specific manner, while PRS-by-antipsychotic interactions positive effects indicated that medication exposure modulates the expression of genetic vulnerability.

Overall, our results show that transdiagnostic polygenic risk of psychopathology influences both negative symptom expression and cortical structure, highlighting the value of combining genetic, clinical, and neuroimaging data to understand SSD and other severe mental illnesses.
