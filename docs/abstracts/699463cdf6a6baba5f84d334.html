Introduction

Prospective time estimation, the ability to predict the duration of upcoming events, is essential for planning and goal-directed behavior. Neuroimaging and lesion studies have implicated the hippocampus and frontal–temporal cortices in temporal cognition1-5, yet the functional role of the hippocampal-cortical network in prospective time estimation remains unclear. The present study aimed to clarify the functional role of hippocampal-cortical network in prospective time estimation by combining fMRI, intracranial electrophysiology, and longitudinal behavioral assessment following neurosurgical treatment.

Methods

Participants performed a prospective time estimation task in which they observed a briefly presented visual pattern that vanished after a predetermined interval and were required to estimate the time to disappearance. Estimated duration was reported via button press, followed by feedback indicating estimation error. A passive waiting task with masked stimuli served as a control condition to dissociate active estimation from monitoring of elapsed time.

To identify brain networks engaged during prospective time estimation, we analyzed task-based fMRI data from healthy adults (n=15) performing this task. To characterize fast neural dynamics within these networks, patients (n=7) with drug-resistant epilepsy undergoing intracranial monitoring performed the task while sEEG and ECoG signals were recorded from hippocampal, temporal, and frontal regions. Spectral power from theta to high gamma bands during stimulus presentation was related to trial-by-trial estimated duration.

To assess the functional relevance of hippocampal-cortical interactions, longitudinal behavioral data were analyzed from patients who underwent multiple hippocampal transection (MHT) (n=5). These patients performed both the prospective time estimation task and a time reproduction task. In the latter, patients learned a target duration defined by the presentation of a fixation cue and subsequently reproduced the learned interval via button press.

Results

Task-based fMRI identified a distributed network engaged during prospective time estimation, including the inferior parietal cortex, posterior visual areas, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and medial prefrontal cortex. Trial-by-trial analysis further revealed positive correlations between estimated duration and BOLD activity in the hippocampus, precentral gyrus, parietal operculum, superior temporal gyrus, and medial prefrontal cortex.

Using ECoG and sEEG recordings, we examined spectral power across theta to high-gamma bands during prospective time estimation and passive waiting. While multiple frequency bands showed task-related modulation, the robust effects were observed in the gamma and high-gamma ranges. During prospective time estimation, gamma- and high-gamma-band activity in the hippocampus, temporal cortex, and frontal cortex showed significant negative correlations with trial-by-trial estimated duration. In contrast, during passive waiting, gamma- and high-gamma-band activity in the hippocampus and temporal cortex increased with waiting duration, with no significant modulation observed in frontal regions.

Longitudinal behavioral performance was examined in the MHT patients with drug-resistant epilepsy. Prospective time estimation and time reproduction tasks were performed before surgery and at 1 and 6 months postoperatively. Following surgery, patients showed altered performance in prospective time estimation, characterized by overestimation for shorter intervals (<8 s) and underestimation for longer intervals (>10 s), whereas time reproduction remained preserved across postoperative time points.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that prospective time estimation is supported by the hippocampal–cortical mechanisms. By combining fMRI, intracranial electrophysiology, and longitudinal behavioral assessment following MHT, our findings clarify the neural architecture underlying prospective time estimation and dissociate it from passive monitoring of elapsed time and time reproduction. These results provide a unified framework for understanding how the hippocampal–cortical network contribute to predictive aspects of temporal cognition and may inform clinical evaluation of temporal deficits following neurosurgical intervention.
