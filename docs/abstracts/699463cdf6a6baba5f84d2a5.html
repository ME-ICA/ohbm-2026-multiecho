Introduction

Awake functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) in mice plays a pivotal role for advancing our understanding of brain function. Imaging mice while awake, allows access the full-range of brain and behavioral states, providing insights into neural dynamics that are otherwise suppressed or altered under anesthesia1. These are the key motivational considerations alongside the greater inter-species translational relevance of imaging without anesthesia1. However, fMRI studies on awake mice are rare, due to the difficulty that acclimating animals to the scanner environment entails1. In contrast, optical imaging techniques, including wide-field calcium (WF-Ca2+) imaging, are routinely applied in awake and behaving animals2. Here, we leverage the advantages of our simultaneous multimodal imaging platform that integrates WF-Ca2+ and fMRI3, and apply it to awake mice in a longitudinal experimental framework. Mice are imaged whilst anesthetized and awake at multiple timepoints to facilitate across time and between state (awake vs. anesthetized) comparisons. The success of the acclimation procedure is assessed, data quality quantified, and differences in cortex-wide (multimodal), and whole-brain (unimodal, fMRI), connectivity associated with anesthesia status are investigated. 

Methods

Transverse sinus injections were used to deliver the GCaMP6s Ca2+ indicator in 11 pups (P0-1), Fig. 1A,B4. For immobilization and permanent optical access to the cortex, at 3 months of age (m), all mice underwent skull-thinning and head-implant attachment (Fig. 1B)5.  All animals were imaged under low-dose anesthesia (isoflurane, ~0.5%) at 4 and 6m and then imaged again either awake or under anesthesia at 9 and 12m (Fig. 1A). Animals that underwent awake imaging were trained following a two-step acclimation protocol (Fig. 1E-F). Phase One: ‘Initial Progressing Training’, for naïve mice, was 14-days and involved the incremental introduction of the multimodal imaging procedure. Four sources of stress (stressors) were added with incremental duration and intensity: animal handling, head restraint, LED light illumination (necessary for WF-Ca2+ imaging) and MRI noise (Fig. 1G). Dual imaging followed (awake_9m). Phase Two: ‘Refresher Training’, occurred before subsequent imaging sessions, was 2-days in duration and reintroduced all stressors at maximum intensity and duration. Dual imaging followed (awake_12m). Data acquisition. fMRI: 11.7T Bruker scanner, gradient-echo, echo-planar-imaging, TR=1.8s, TE=11ms, 0.31x0.31x0.31mm voxels, 25 slices, 334 volumes. Three, 10-minute, runs per session (Fig. 1E-F). Structural MRI data were also acquired for (multi-modal) image registration, as we have described previously. Preprocessing. RABIES (Rodent automated BOLD improvement of EPI sequences) software with frame-to-frame displacement scrubbing >0.075mm5. WF-Ca2+ imaging: Acquired simultaneously with fMRI, 0.25μmx0.25μm pixels, at an effective (background corrected) 10Hz. Data were preprocessed as we have described previously5. Data were spatially and temporally aligned across imaging modalities. 

Results

Overall, motion in fMRI data was comparable to previously published literature1 and indicated a reduction in motion following Phase Two: ‘Refresher Training’ (Fig. 1H), whereby fewer imaging frames were discarded relative to the first awake imaging session (mean 30/334 frames discarded in awake_12m compared to 80/334 frames discarded in awake_9m). The fMRI temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR) was lower in data acquired from awake relative to anesthetized mice, whereas the WF-Ca2+ imaging data showed the opposite pattern. This resulted in different trajectories for tSNR correlation across modalities, at different imaging sessions (Fig. 1I). High variability in data acquired from anesthetized mice, absent or low correlation at the first awake imaging timepoint, and significant positive correlation at the final awake imaging timepoint. So-called “specific connectivity”, a measure of relative functional connectivity designed to identify ‘high-quality’ data was investigated next. Functional MRI showed high “specific connectivity” in data acquired from awake and anesthetized mice (85–100% of runs). Surprisingly, WF-Ca2+ imaging showed less “specific connectivity” in data acquired from both anesthetized (67 and 73%) and awake (33 and 58%) mice. When cortex-wide connectivity strength was compared between awake and anesthetized imaging session, fMRI showed the expected enhancement of within-network specificity and reduced global synchrony. On the other hand, WF-Ca2+ imaging showed locally enriched but more heterogeneous patterns. Average connectivity within and across hemispheres, within a priori functional networks, demonstrated some of these observed inter-modal differences (Fig. 2A-B). Finally, inter-modal cross correlation of connectivity, within and between a priori networks and hemispheres, showed a robust pattern across awake and anesthetized mice (Fig. 2C-D). At all imaging timepoints, higher inter-modal agreement was uncovered within hemisphere, and networks, whereas low or absent cross-modal correlations were uncovered across hemispheres (Fig. 2B). 

Conclusion

Biphasic acclimation suppresses subject motion and produces high-quality data. “Specific connectivity” may need to be revisited as a means to gauge data quality given the heterogeneous measures of inter-hemispheric connectivity obtained with WF-Ca2+ imaging alongside the fMRI results. This may reflect underlying differences between structural and functional connectivity. Established as well as new patterns in connectivity associated with wakefulness are uncovered – especially in the WF-Ca2+ imaging data where these analyses are uncommon. These, alongside the differences in WF-Ca2+ and fMRI measures of connectivity, warrant further investigations using data driven approaches (e.g., functional parcellations) and dynamic analyses (e.g., quasi-periodic patterns or co-activation patterns). This work introduces longitudinal, simultaneous WF-Ca2+ and fMRI in awake mice and sets the stage for future studies where a wider spectrum of behaviors and brain states can be examined in healthy animals and models of disease.
