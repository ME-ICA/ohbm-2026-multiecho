Introduction

The human connectome models the brain’s complex functional and structural relationships [1]. Structural connectomics relies on tractography to estimate white matter pathways, typically derived from diffusion MRI (dMRI). These relationships are multi-scale and regionally distinct, yet a whole-brain and anatomically reliable model is essential for surgical planning and therapeutic targeting. Increasing image resolution helps resolve intricate fibre architectures for clinical targets [2], but emerging techniques generate increasingly large and complex datasets, creating major challenges for storage, computation, and analysis. 

Tractography produces widely used macroscopic models, but the limited resolution of dMRI on which it is often based- typically exceeding 500 µm voxels - struggles to resolve complex local fibre architecture below its resolution limit. These structures can be imaged with microscopy techniques, which are destructive and have a limited field of view. As a result, connectome models remain siloed by anatomical scale or imaging modality. Graph networks provide a flexible framework for interpreting tractograms and capturing hierarchical organisation, but are typically limited to tract endpoints and lack integration with underlying white matter anatomy. 

Hierarchical Phase-Contrast Tomography (HiP-CT) [3] enables multi-scale imaging of intact human brains at 20, 10, and 0.85 um isotropic voxel sizes over progressively smaller fields of view, generating terabyte-scale datasets accessed through chunked data formats. Fibre orientation can be estimated using structure tensor analysis, enabling deterministic tractography in small rodent samples [4,5], but dense whole-brain human tractography rapidly becomes prohibitive. 

Here, we present a multilayer model of spatially distributed graph networks as a scalable alternative to tractography for terabyte-scale ex vivo brain imaging, enabling efficient multi-scale connectivity analysis with reduced computational cost.

Methods

The increasing size of imaging datasets poses fundamental challenges to streamline-based tractography, which struggles with rapidly growing computational complexity and redundancy. We address this by reframing tractography as a problem of distributed spatial graph networks. Local spatial graphs are constructed within many chunked volumes and hierarchically stitched together into a sparse representation of the whole brain connectome. This platform allows for selection of streamline batches, prior to reconstructing the full path data across chunks. Because local graphs can be constructed independently and at different resolutions, this approach enables multiple connectome representations to be stitched together, providing greater resolution around clinical targets. This framework, termed Brain Reconstruction Integrating Distributed Graph Estimates (BRIDGE), provides a modular graph-based approach for connectomics analysis of massive hierarchical datasets (Fig.1). 

To extract connectomic data from large synchrotron image datasets, firstly image volumes were converted to a chunked NIfTI-Zarr format, defining local graph boundaries and registering all data to a shared coordinate system. Within each chunk, oriented nodes were sampled from white matter using adaptive image-intensity Z-score thresholds. Local fibre orientation was estimated using structure tensor analysis via eigendecomposition of linear image intensity gradients, where the third eigenvector defines the predominant fibre axis. 

Local graphs were constructed from overlapping chunk-wise node sets to ensure continuity across boundaries. An initial dense graph connected nodes within a maximum spatial radius and was filtered by alignment to node orientations. From this filtered graph, three-node path segments were generated for each valid edge combination through a node and constrained by maximum bending angle and minimum radius of curvature. Each segment was abstracted as a node in a derived graph, enabling GPU-accelerated search in which all graph paths correspond to valid spatial trajectories. Unique bidirectional paths were indexed and assigned endpoints and path-averaged image and geometric properties. A sparse local representation retained only endpoints, path IDs, and a central spatial reference, analogous to an adjacency matrix. Local graphs were constructed independently per chunk, enabling distributed processing. 

Sparse local graphs were hierarchically linked through overlapping regions to form a global network. Groups of eight graphs (2x2x2) were merged to form new layers of sparse graphs covering greater areas of the brain, paths are linked, and path attributes are summed. This binning continues until all the whole brain is represented by a single sparse graph, describing all paths and their endpoints. This produced a multi-layer hierarchy encoding connectivity across increasing spatial scales without explicitly storing dense streamline representations, enabling filtering of global paths while delaying their full reconstruction. 

Selective analysis was performed on the sparse global network prior to streamline reconstruction. Paths were filtered and classified using anatomical labels and path-level metrics. Full streamlines were reconstructed on demand by resolving sparse paths into chains of local segments and stitching them in real space. Outputs were batched and spatially cropped to regions of interest, with optional extraction of corresponding image subvolumes and local graphs for contextual analysis and visualisation. For multi-scale integration, networks derived from registered higher-resolution subvolumes were nested into whole-brain graphs by linking aligned endpoints across scales.  

All methods were implemented in BRIDGE packages for Python and Julia, supporting GPU-accelerated graph construction, distributed processing, and hierarchical connectomic analysis at terabyte scale.

Results

To demonstrate that graph-based representations can recover high-resolution connectomes, we applied the BRIDGE pipeline to diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and multi-scale synchrotron datasets from a single 63-year-old control donor. Diffusion MRI remains the gold standard for estimating whole-brain white-matter orientations. An ex vivo DTI dataset acquired at 630 µm isotropic resolution provided a reference orientation field and probabilistic tractogram for comparison. Nodes were densely sampled within voxels exceeding a generalized fractional anisotropy threshold and assigned orientations by interpolating local maxima of diffusion orientation distribution functions. Within spatial chunks, local graph networks were constructed using search constraints similar to streamline propagation. These local paths were hierarchically stitched into a sparse graph-based reconstruction of the hemisphere-scale tractogram. BRIDGE recovered a full-hemisphere network with an associated dense tractogram, yet the underlying hierarchical graph representation was substantially more compact (9.6GB vs 22GB). 

Secondly, we applied BRIDGE to paired Hierarchical Phase-Contrast Tomography (HiP-CT) of the same donor. Whole-hemisphere imaging at 20 µm/voxel provided a macroscopic reference for registering higher-resolution deep-brain scans at 10 µm/voxel and submicron imaging of excised tissue at 0.85 µm/voxel (Fig.2a). After preprocessing and conversion to NIfTI-Zarr, parameter sensitivity analyses were used to balance node density, connectivity, and computational performance. 

Across the hemisphere, approximately 30 million nodes were sampled (Fig.2b,c), yielding millions of dense local paths that formed the base level of the BRIDGE hierarchy (Fig.2d). Hierarchical merging produced a sparse whole-brain network encoding billions of unique paths while occupying orders of magnitude less storage than a vector field required for conventional tractography (120GB vs 12TB) (Fig.2e,f). Local graph reconstructions revealed coherent fibre trajectories through deep-brain structures, consistent with known anatomy. Increasing sampling density in regional volumes yielded denser mesoscale reconstructions, capturing finer fibre architecture while remaining linked to the hemisphere-scale network. To integrate multi-scale datasets, higher-resolution regional graphs were nested directly into lower-resolution networks by linking aligned endpoints. Applying this approach to submicron imaging of the substantia nigra yielded paths tracing bundles of individual axons with morphology consistent with underlying HiP-CT images.  

Finally, we applied BRIDGE to non-human primate and mouse HiP-CT datasets. In macaque, graph-based reconstruction captured mesoscale subcortical connectivity consistent with known anatomy. In mouse, BRIDGE scaled to multi-terabyte volumes, enabling whole-brain graph-based reconstruction of dense axonal architecture beyond the field of view of prior synchrotron studies. Together, these results demonstrate that BRIDGE provides a scalable, modality-agnostic framework for graph-based connectomics across species and spatial scales.

Conclusion

BRIDGE demonstrates a distributed and hierarchical approach to graph-based connectomics applied to diffusion tensor imaging and terabyte-scale synchrotron brain imaging across species. These results show the feasibility and advantages of constructing connectomes directly from high-resolution image volumes, enabling mesoscale models that capture white matter organisation beyond the scale limits of conventional tractography. Such mesoscale connectomes are particularly relevant for clinical targeting, where complex subcortical connectivity remains incompletely characterised, despite evidence that increased local resolution can improve therapeutic outcomes. 

Structure tensor–based orientation fields impose limitations, particularly in regions of complex fibre architecture where a single predominant orientation may not capture crossing or merging populations at lower resolutions. However, these effects are mitigated at higher resolutions, where individual fibre populations fall within a single kernel. Multi-scale HiP-CT acquisition therefore provides a practical route to resolving such ambiguities while maintaining global context. 

Forming connectomes from terabyte-scale volumes is computationally demanding due to the sheer number of visible paths. Graph-based representations substantially reduce redundancy and enable sparse processing, yielding orders-of-magnitude reductions in storage and computation. Nevertheless, careful parameter selection and targeted reconstruction remain essential, even with access to large-scale computing resources. 

Conventional graph-based connectomes collapse white matter structure into coarse edge statistics an classify anatomical regions as single nodes, limiting analysis of deep brain organisation. By decoupling nodes from anatomical segmentation, BRIDGE offers a new mesoscale for graph-based connectomics in which white matter organisation is modelled directly. More broadly, this framework provides a foundation for extending connectomic analysis to dynamic and functional graph models. As additional HiP-CT datasets become available through the Human Organ Atlas, BRIDGE offers a scalable path toward cohort-level mesoscale connectomics and improved characterisation of clinically relevant brain networks.
