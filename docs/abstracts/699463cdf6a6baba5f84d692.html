Introduction

Understanding the complex relationship between the nervous system and brain tumors has become crucial in neuro-oncology and the developing field of cancer neuroscience. One key issue is that tumors infiltrate healthy neural tissue, damaging and compromising connections with both nearby and distant brain areas, including those on the contralateral hemisphere. These alterations induce functional reorganization of brain networks both before and after surgery. Preoperatively, this reorganization is driven by tumor expansion and influenced by tumor histology. Postoperatively, it reflects the effects of surgical resection, therapies, and potential tumor recurrence. In this respect non-invasive brain stimulation techniques can offer useful insights into how brain tumors affect cortico-subcortical networks, with implications for surgical planning and prognostic assessment.  Moreover, the integration of transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) with electroencephalography (EEG) can offer a direct, non-invasive method for examining brain excitability and connectivity patterns. Using this approach, signal propagation can be potentially traced from the stimulation site through interconnected regions in patients with brain tumors, thereby revealing causal relationships between cortical areas. By following the spatio-temporal dynamics of effective brain connectivity, we can highlight how brain regions interact and adapt in response to tumor expansion or surgical intervention.   The present study applies this methodology to patients with brain tumors targeting the visual system, and provides proof of concept for its broader applications to lesions affecting other functional networks. Specifically, we employed TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs) to track tumor infiltration and plastic reorganization across the preoperative and postoperative phases, aiming to identify potential local and network signatures of these processes.

Methods

Three patients with tumors located in the right occipital cortex (one high-grade glioma, HGG; one low-grade glioma, LGG; and one metastasis) took part in the present study. A group of 19 healthy volunteers (mean age, 27 years; SD, 9.58; 17 females; partly from Bonfanti et al. 20241) served as the control group. 

All patients underwent the same experimental procedure. Firstly, an MRI scan was performed. Anatomical images were acquired with a 3 Tesla magnet (GE Signa Architect, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) with 32-channel head RF receive coils.  A whole-brain high-resolution T1-weighted image with FSPGR 3D sequence and a diffusion-weighted (DWI) image with EPI 2D sequence were acquired. Subsequently, participants took part in two assessment sessions: one a few days before surgical intervention (preoperative session) and another approximately three months following tumor resection (follow-up session) (Figure 1). Each session consisted in a TMS-EEG procedure. Single-pulse TMS was applied using a 70 mm figure-of-eight coil connected to a Magstim Rapid2 device. Eighty single-pulses were delivered to both right (perilesional) and left (contralateral) occipital regions at an intensity set to 110% of the resting Motor Threshold (rMT), defined as the minimum stimulation intensity that elicited motor evoked potentials (MEPs) of at least 50μV in at least five out of ten consecutive trials. Stimulation targets corresponded to the O1 and O1 electrode positions from the 10-20 International EEG system. The order of the stimulation sites was counterbalanced across participants. Neuronavigation with the Softaxic software, combined with individual structural MRI scans, was used to monitor coil position, maintaining accuracy within 2 mm and confirming the stimulation target. To mask the TMS click, participants wore disposable earplugs.

We performed diffusion MRI tractography analysis and visualization using 3D Slicer, courtesy of the SlicerDMRI project. After the identification of the whole-brain tractography, we performed anatomical tract identification using the white matter analysis package and the ORG tractography atlas.

Global Mean Field Power was computed for both perilesional (right occipital) and contralesional stimulation (left occipital), as a measure of global cortical reactivity induced by TMS perturbation, as well as the cortico-cortical connectivity degree between the targeted area and distant brain regions. Similarly, the maximum global spatiotemporal dynamics of TEPs were estimated by quantifying the temporal complexity of the principal components of the EEG response to TMS (PCIst2), for both stimulation sites. 

We compared GMFP and PCIst values obtained for each patient with the 99% confidence interval (CI) computed in the healthy control group, perturbing the same areas, separately for perilesional (right occipital) and contralesional (left occipital) stimulation. The overlap between each patient’s result and the 99% CI of the healthy population was used as a criterion to determine whether the patient’s value deviated from the normative range.

Results

Stimulation of peri- and contralesional cortical sites in brain tumor patients produced sequences of polarity deflections, indicating altered but preserved reactivity to TMS perturbation. After perilesional stimulation, the early component reflecting local target reactivity and cortico-cortical connectivity showed enhanced activation in HGG but reduced activation in LGG, compared to the healthy population. Later components, reflecting signal propagation, revealed enhanced global cortical activity in all patients, exceeding the normal 99% CI (Figure 2. B. & C red lines). Spatio-temporal complexity induced by perilesional perturbation was reduced in all patients (Figure 2. F). Contralesional stimulation mimicked perilesional results, with reduced early local response in LGG and enhanced later activation in LGG and metastasis, but not in HGG. Left occipital stimulation in HGG revealed reduced early local reactivity (Figure 2. B & C blue lines). Spatio-temporal complexity following contralesional perturbation was also reduced in all patients before surgery (Figure 2. F). At three-month follow-up after surgery, perilesional perturbation showed reduced hyperactivation in early TEPs in HGG and attenuation of later components in both HGG and LGG. In contrast, metastasis patients maintained systemic hyperactivation, with further increases in later components (Figure 2. D. & E red lines). Postoperative spatio-temporal complexity increased in LGG and metastasis, reaching values comparable to controls, but remained impaired in HGG (Figure 2. F). After contralesioanl stimulation, LGG showed partial recovery in the first component and reduced hyperactivation in later, though not reaching healthy values (Figure 2. D. & E blue lines). Metastasis continued to exhibit hyperactivation, while HGG showed no significant changes. Recovery of spatio-temporal complexity was observed in LGG and metastasis, but not in HGG (Figure 2. F).

Conclusion

This proof-of-concept study shows the potential of TMS-EEG to investigate neural network changes induced by focal brain tumors involving the visual cortices across the clinical evolution3. Through pre- and postoperative TEPs analysis, we identified an electrophysiological signature characterized by hyperactivation coupled with a marked reduction in spatiotemporal complexity. As hypothesized, these alterations extended beyond the lesional and perilesional areas to more distant regions, consistent with diaschisis phenomena. This pathological signature seemed to progressively normalize during the postoperative recovery. 

Hyperactivation and reduced complexity may constitute hallmarks of focal brain lesions. Indeed, both ischemic strokes and brain tumors disrupt cortical balance within affected tissue. Previous studies on acute stroke have reported sleep-like slow waves in the perilesional cortex, characterized bydecreased local perturbational complexity (PCIst), along with amplified responses to stimulation4,5. These findings provide a reference framework for our tumor data. The hyperactivation observed across all cases (high-grade glioma, low-grade glioma, metastasis) resembles post-ischemic hyperexcitability patterns. At the same time, concurrent PCIst reduction indicates loss of the richly interactive neural dynamics characterizing healthy cortical tissue.

Following surgical resection, evolution of the hyperactive profile varied among patients, possibly reflecting histological differences, infiltration grade, and tumor progression. In the low-grade glioma patient, hyperactivation decreased, accompanied by PCIst values approaching those of healthy controls, suggesting functional reorganization through the recruitment of alternative cortico-cortical pathways and network reintegration. Instead, the high-grade glioma demonstrated no recovery in either activation or complexity measures, while the metastasis exhibited only partial recovery. These divergent outcomes likely reflect key factors related to the nature of the tumor. Specifically, low-grade gliomas are known to exhibit slower growth, which facilitates compensatory reorganization.

Although preliminary, given the very limited sample size, these findings suggest that brain tumors manifest a characteristic network signature. TMS-EEG may,thus, represent a valuable tool for characterizing local and systemic-level neurophysiological alterations induced by brain tumors and for monitoring their postoperative evolution.
