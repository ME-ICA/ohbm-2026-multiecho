Introduction

A dynamic balance between glutamatergic excitation and GABAergic inhibition is a necessity for the brain to function well. Disruptions in this balance contribute to the symptomatology of various neuropsychiatric conditions.1 Non-invasive methods that quantify excitation-inhibition dynamics may aid biomarker development and individualized treatment strategies.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) are widely used to probe cortical neurochemistry and physiology. MRS provides in vivo estimates of neurometabolites, whereas TMS engages glutamatergic and GABAergic circuits through electromagnetic pulses. Yet, despite conceptual links, attempts to establish consistent associations between MRS-derived neurochemistry and TMS-derived physiology have largely been unsuccessful.2 One reason for these null findings may be the reliance on peripheral electromyographic (EMG) readouts to quantify TMS effects, as EMG measures not only reflect cortical dynamics, but also downstream spinal processes.

Here, we integrated TMS with electroencephalography (TMS-EEG) to quantify direct cortical responses through TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs), and to test their relationship with MRS-derived GABA+ and Glx concentrations. Compared to TMS-EMG, TMS-EEG also offers the advantage of studying cortical regions outside of the sensorimotor cortex (SM1). We measured neurochemistry at rest (resting-state MRS) and during performance of a bimanual motor task (task-related MRS) in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and SM1. Single-pulse TMS was applied to index general GABA/Glx dynamics, and a paired-pulse long-interval cortical inhibition (LICI) paradigm was used for quantifying GABAB specific inhibition. We hypothesized that neurochemical-electrophysiological relationships depend on brain state, region, and TEP component.

Methods

Twenty-five healthy younger adults were initially recruited to participate (12 females; mean age = 22 years; range = 18-31). The study consisted of four experimental sessions embedded within a larger six-session protocol (for an overview, see Figure 1).

In session 1, participants were screened for MRI and TMS contraindications, neurological and psychiatric history, handedness, and cognitive functioning.

In session 2, an anatomical T1-weighted scan was acquired and later uploaded into the TMS neuronavigation software for individualized targeting. Participants also completed an N-back task during functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), to functionally localize the left DLPFC and later create a personalized mask during the TMS-EEG and MRS sessions.

In session 3, single- and paired-pulse TMS-EEG data were recorded over left SM1 and DLPFC. Single pulses were delivered at a suprathreshold intensity yielding ~1 mV motor-evoked potentials over SM1. Paired-pulse trials followed a LICI protocol (interstimulus interval: 100 ms) to probe GABAB-mediated inhibition. Per region, canonical TEP components were quantified at the local sensor level (based on topographical maps) and at the global field level.

In session 4, MRS data were recorded over the left DLPFC and SM1 at rest, and during a bimanual motor task, the bimanual tracking task.3 Analyses focused on GABA+ and Glx concentrations, as well as their ratio.

To examine links between neurochemistry and electrophysiology, we fit four separate linear mixed-effects models for each TEP component and brain region. Each model contained only one neurochemical predictor, representing a specific MRS-derived measure: resting-state GABA+ and Glx, functional GABA+ and Glx, resting-state GABA+/Glx ratio, or functional GABA+/Glx ratio. Pulse type (single vs paired) and its interaction with the neurochemical predictor were included as fixed effects, and participant was included as a random intercept. Following backward model building, models were compared using AIC, BIC, and R² values to determine which neurochemical profile(s) best explained each TEP component.

Results

Figure 2 summarizes MRS concentrations, single- and paired-pulse TEP waveforms, and significant neurochemical-electrophysiological relationships in SM1 (left panels) and DLPFC (right panels). The bottom panels indicate which TEP components showed significant associations with local neurochemistry at the sensor level (solid lines) or only at the global field level (dashed lines). EEG sensor C3 (SM1) is highlighted in dark red; sensors F3 and Cz (DLPFC) are highlighted in light and dark blue, respectively.

For SM1, sensor-level analyses showed that early- to mid-latency TEP components were consistently best predicted by task-related GABA+ levels. Specifically, P30 (p = 0.011, F = 8.00), N45 (p = 0.006, F = 9.66), and P60 (p = 0.001, F = 14.66) amplitudes increased with higher task-related GABA+. No pulse type effects were observed for these components. For N100, no sensor-level effects were observed. However, global field analyses revealed significant effects of task-related GABA+ (p = 0.009) and pulse type (p < 0.001). Overall, SM1 results indicate that early-to-mid latency TEPs best reflect the local inhibitory tone during a region-relevant brain state, whereas N100 appears less region-specific and may reflect broader network-level activity.

For DLPFC, no sensor-level associations were found for N50 or P65. At the global field level, resting-state GABA+ predicted N50 amplitudes, whereas P65 showed an interaction between resting-state Glx and pulse type. Given the absence of sensor-level effects, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. In contrast, paired-pulse stimulation at the sensor-level produced significantly smaller N100 amplitudes compared to single-pulse stimulation (p < 0.001, F = 20.632), mirroring the global-field SM1 results for N100. In addition, a significant interaction between resting-state GABA+/Glx ratio and pulse type (p = 0.008, F = 9.78) indicated that a higher amount of GABA+ relative to Glx was associated with larger absolute N100 amplitudes in the single-pulse condition, but smaller absolute amplitudes in the paired-pulse condition, potentially due to a stronger inhibitory effect of the first TMS pulse.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates state- and region-dependent links between cortical electrophysiology and local neurochemistry by integrating single- and paired-pulse TMS-EEG with resting-state and task-related MRS in SM1 and DLPFC. 

In SM1, early to mid-latency TEP components were reliably predicted by task-related GABA+ levels, indicating a strong coupling between the local inhibitory tone and cortical responsiveness. In contrast, DLPFC showed only global field–level associations of early-to-mid latency TEP components with resting-state metabolites. The paired-pulse, LICI, paradigm almost exclusively modulated the late N100 component, implying that single-pulse TEPs already capture a substantial variation in regional inhibitory-excitatory dynamics. 

Together, these findings provide the most convincing evidence to date for associations between MRS-derived neurochemistry (assessed over minutes) and TMS-EEG excitability markers (assessed over milliseconds). In doing so, we establish several TEP components as accessible non-invasive markers to index the dynamic inhibitory-excitatory balance in the human cortex.
