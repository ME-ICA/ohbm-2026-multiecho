Introduction

Women, relative to men, have double the lifetime risk of developing AD and experience faster cognitive decline. They also exhibit different patterns of neurodegeneration, which indicates that women – and men – may benefit from stratified methods of AD diagnosis and treatment. However, sex-specific differences in AD-related brain function remain poorly understood and understudied – especially in animal research where males are still grossly overrepresented. 

Multimodal imaging techniques for studying brain function in animal models includes our groups unique implementation of simultaneous wide-field fluorescent calcium (WF-Ca2+) and BOLD-fMRI (blood-oxygen level dependent functional magnetic resonance imaging). This technology combines a more direct measure of neuronal activity (WF-Ca2+ imaging) alongside the clinically accessible, but cell-type agnostic, BOLD contrast; thus, providing two complementary measures of brain function from the mouse cortex. 

Recently, we reported age-, AD-, and treatment-related changes in whole-brain BOLD-fMRI measures of functional connectivity within a mixed-sex sample. Building on this study, in the present work we use the same animal model, and investigate sex-, age-, modality-, and AD-related differences in simultaneous WF-Ca2+ and BOLD-fMRI data during the prodromal disease stage. Our preliminary results establish a framework for studying sex-specific changes in AD-related brain network dysfunction and for developing sex-stratified methods of diagnosis and treatment.

Methods

Longitudinal simultaneous WF-Ca2+ and fMRI data from N=38 mice were included. The multimodal data are acquired using a custom-built in-house system, imaging protocol, and data processing pipeline we have described previously1,2. All data were acquired from lightly anesthetized mice (0.75% isoflurane). Data from three multimodal imaging sessions – acquired at 4, 6, and 9 months-of-age (m) – were included (Figure 1). These timepoints correspond to young-mid adulthood in humans (~20-40 years). Mice were either wild-type (WT) or a double-knock-in (DKI) mouse model for Alzheimer’s disease (AD): AppNL-G-F/hMapt (amyloid-precursor protein/human microtubule-associated protein tau) 3,4. 

To enable pan-neuronal WF-Ca2+ imaging, mice underwent transverse sinus injections of an AAV9 (Adeno-associated virus serotype 9) at birth 5 and a cranial implant surgery at 3m for permanent optical access to the cortex and head immobilization1. WF-Ca2+ and fMRI data were registered to an in-house out-of-sample brain template that has been registered to the Allen Atlas reference space CCfv3 (common co-ordinate framework version 3) using open-source tools that were developed for this purpose in-house within BioImage Suite2. Cortical fMRI data were masked to match the 2D WF-Ca2+ imaging field-of-view (FOV). Data are spatially averaged within Allen Atlas derived regions of interest (ROIs) that appear in both imaging modalities (N=46 bilaterally symmetric ROIs) and used to compute cortical connectomes as we have described previously. An analysis of the whole-brain fMRI data (N=166 ROIs) was also conducted.

To identify differences in connectivity across time (age), between biological sexes, and across groups (WT or DKI/AD) data were examined using linear mixed effect (LME) modelling. For fMRI data, results were computed with and without global signal regression (GSR) applied (Figure 2).   

Results

Outputs from the LME model [Edges strength ~ 1+ Age + Female or Male + (1|animal), MATLAB, lme] were computed using data from WT or DKI (AD) mice using simultaneously acquired WF-Ca2+ or BOLD-fMRI data (Figure 2). For BOLD-fMRI, results were computed using data with and without GSR applied. Further, outputs were computed using data from early timepoints (4 and 6m) as well as all timepoints (4, 6, and 9m). This was based on our prior work 5 where age- and AD-related changes in BOLD-fMRI connectivity were explored at these as well as later timepoints (12, 20, and 22m). 

Clear and widespread differences in cortical connectivity were observed in WT mice when the 9m imaging timepoint was included in the model and when the cortical BOLD-fMRI data were considered (Figure 2. C. & D., right). This effect persisted with and without the application of GSR. Further, the implicated brain networks were in-line with our previous work in older animals5. Curiously, these age-related effects were not present in the DKI (AD) group (with or without GSR applied), nor were they observed in the simultaneously acquired WF-Ca2+ imaging data (Figure 2. A.). 

On the other hand, differences between biological sexes (female and male) were observed in the DKI (AD) group using both WF-Ca2+ and BOLD-fMRI data – but only when GSR was not applied (Figure 2. B.). Notably, these differences were not observed within the WT group (in either modality). Intriguingly, these differences were present when only the early imaging timepoints (at 4 and 6m) were included in the model (Figure 2. A. & B., left). Relative to the aforementioned widespread age-related differences in cortical connectivity, these biological sex-related differences were concentrated within the default mode (DMN) and motor (MO) networks as well as in connections between these two canonical functional networks. Finally, good agreement between imaging modalities was also noted in terms of which edges were implicated. 

Our parallel analyses of the whole-brain BOLD-fMRI data (extending beyond the 2D WF-Ca2+ imaging FOV) uncovered similar findings which extend to subcortical regions (which are not visible with WF-Ca2+ imaging) (data not shown). 

Conclusion

These data suggest that biological sex-specific differences in AD-related connectivity emerge earlier in females relative to males and that data preprocessing methods (e.g., the application of GSR) may have a very significant impact on some sex-specific observations. Altogether, our findings support further investigation of the neurovascular underpinnings of AD pathology using multimodal methods and a sex-stratified approach.
