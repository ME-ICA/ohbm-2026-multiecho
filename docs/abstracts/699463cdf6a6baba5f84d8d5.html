Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) targeting the subcallosal cingulate cortex (SCC) produces rapid antidepressant effects in treatment-resistant depression (TRD)1-3, yet the underlying neurochemical mechanisms remain poorly understood. While dopamine (DA) and serotonin (5-HT) are implicated in depression pathophysiology and are targets of pharmacological interventions, their rapid dynamics during DBS have never been directly measured in living humans. The SCC is uniquely positioned as a neuromodulatory hub, with direct projections to both the ventral tegmental area and dorsal raphe nuclei, enabling it to influence both dopaminergic and serotonergic transmission. Traditional neuroimaging methods lack the temporal and spatial resolution to capture subsecond neurotransmitter dynamics critical for understanding mood regulation and motivated behavior. Here, we leveraged machine learning-enhanced fast-scan cyclic voltammetry during intraoperative SCC DBS surgery to measure DA and 5-HT transients in real-time while TRD patients performed decision-making tasks. We hypothesized that acute neurochemical responses to subtherapeutic stimulation would predict subsequent behavioral improvements and clinical outcomes. This study represents the first direct measurement of monoamine dynamics in human SCC and tests whether these signals serve as mechanistic biomarkers for DBS efficacy and depression recovery.

Methods

We recorded DA and 5-HT dynamics in 10 patients with TRD (mean age 44.0 years, 5 female) undergoing bilateral SCC DBS electrode implantation. During surgery, carbon fiber microelectrodes inserted in the right hemisphere measured subsecond neurotransmitter fluctuations using fast-scan cyclic voltammetry. Recordings were obtained during two behavioral tasks: (1) a social decision-making task involving economic exchanges with another person, and (2) a reward learning task with probabilistic feedback. Tasks were completed twice, once before and once after subclinical stimulation (130 Hz, 2 mA, 90 μs pulse width) was administered in the left hemisphere for 2 minutes. Voltammetric data were processed using established pipelines which leverage machine learning to identify DA and 5-HT levels4,5. Estimated neurotransmitter levels were defined as the sum of samples within a 500 ms window following outcome reveal in the reward learning task and offer presentation in the social exchange task. We quantified neurotransmitter changes as the difference between pre-stimulation versus post-stimulation periods for each task. Behavioral measures included choices, response times, and subjective mood ratings collected during task and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS-17) scores assessed at baseline, one week, and 1, 3, and 6 months post-operatively. Primary outcomes were: (1) acute changes in DA and 5-HT levels following stimulation, (2) correlations between acute neurochemical changes and longitudinal behavioral improvements, and (3) prediction of changes in clinical severity at 6-month follow-up. Statistical analyses included linear mixed-effects models to assess task-specific neurotransmitter responses, correlation analyses between neurochemical and behavioral changes, and linear regression to predict symptom change.

Results

Acute DBS significantly increased both DA and 5-HT levels in SCC following stimulation (DA: p < 0.05; 5-HT: p < 0.05). Critically, these neurotransmitter changes showed task-specific patterns: DA responses were more pronounced during the reward learning task (p < 0.001), while 5-HT showed greater modulation during the social exchange task (p < 0.001), suggesting distinct computational roles for each monoamine. Acute DA increases during social exchanges correlated with improved mood ratings at 1-month (r = 0.79, p = 0.007) and 3-month (r = 0.77, p = 0.009) follow-ups. Similarly, acute 5-HT enhancement during reward learning predicted faster reaction times longitudinally (Month 3: r = -0.88, p = 0.007; Month 6: r = -0.76, p = 0.037), consistent with serotonin's role in action vigor. Most importantly, when examining clinical symptom changes after 6 months of chronic DBS treatment, we found that combined DA and 5-HT responses during the social exchange task jointly predicted lower depression severity (b = -0.14, t = -4.86, p = 0.003). In other words, patients who experienced the greatest reduction of symptom severity also showed the greatest increases in both DA and 5-HT. After six months of chronic DBS treatment, participants exhibited an average 60.9% reduction in HDRS symptom severity scores (min = 23.4%, max = 90.7%).

Conclusion

This study provides the first direct evidence that acute DA and 5-HT dynamics in human SCC predict long-term clinical outcomes following DBS for TRD. The task-specific patterns of neurotransmitter release suggest that DA and 5-HT play distinct yet complementary roles in mood regulation and motivated behavior. The finding that both monoamines jointly predict remission challenges traditional depression theories that emphasize serotonergic mechanisms alone and highlights the importance of dopaminergic function in recovery. These results have several important implications. First, they demonstrate that subsecond neurochemical measurements obtained during a brief surgical window can forecast treatment response over months, potentially enabling early identification of patients most likely to benefit from DBS. Second, our findings indicate that successful DBS treatment may require modulation of multiple neurotransmitter systems, suggesting that combination approaches targeting both DA and 5-HT pathways could enhance therapeutic efficacy. The mechanistic insights from this work could inform the development of personalized DBS protocols, where stimulation parameters are optimized based on individual neurochemical profiles. Furthermore, these neurochemical biomarkers may guide patient selection and help stratify patients for different treatment approaches. Future studies should examine how pharmacological interventions interact with DBS-induced neurotransmitter changes could optimize combination therapies. Our approach also demonstrates the feasibility and safety of combining advanced neuroscience techniques with clinical neurosurgery to identify potential mechanisms of psychiatric interventions in humans.
