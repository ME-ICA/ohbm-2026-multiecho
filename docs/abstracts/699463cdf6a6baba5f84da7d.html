Introduction

The aging process entails a series of multidimensional changes, ranging from the molecular to neuroanatomical to the behavioral aspects of a person’s life. When it comes to brain anatomy, there is an overall loss in gray and white matter tissues, and an increase in white matter hyperintensities (WMH), reflective of vascular and neurodegenerative processes, among other changes. Different studies have examined the heterogeneity of healthy aging processes through identifying aging subtypes and their associated patterns, beyond the classification of healthy versus pathological aging^1,2. Furthermore, different algorithms have been developed to study heterogeneity within disease processes. Among those, the Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn)^3 has been frequently used in recent years. 

SuStaIn is an unsupervised machine learning approach used to uncover distinct disease phenotypes with different temporal progression patterns from cross-sectional data. It relies on event-based modeling and clustering to identify both the most likely sequence in which features become abnormal (i.e. stages) as well as subtypes of feature abnormality patterns. SuStaIn has been applied to different diseases, and is particularly suited for subtyping of neurodegeneration, as its assumption of monotonic change in biomarkers is reasonable for atrophy measures in these disorders^3. Prior studies have examined the transferability of SuStaIn models either between Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) cohorts or between AD and at risk populations, reporting that subtypes can be uncovered in different populations and at different disease phases^4.

In this work, we sought to apply the SuStaIn algorithm to a general aging population, and evaluate its reliability to identify aging subtypes based on neuroimaging features across different sample sizes, as well as evaluate its test-retest reliability using longitudinal data.

Methods

Participants were included from the UK Biobank (UKBB) dataset. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were preprocessed using the PELICAN^5 pipeline, from which we obtained voxelwise voxel based morphometry (VBM) values, as well as tissue segmentations that allowed us to quantify ventricular volume and WMH burden. We defined 11 features which included four lobar values of WMH burden, ventricular volume, and 6 gray matter features namely frontal regions, occipital regions, parietal regions, a parahippocampal feature comprising the parahippocampal, fusiform, and entorhinal gyrus, and the hippocampus, temporal regions (excluding the parahippocampal, fusiform, and entorhinal gyrus), and a subcortical feature (excluding the hippocampus), for which we obtained a mean VBM value.

We examined six different SuStaIn models (Figure 1), all trained using piecewise linear z-score and 10-fold cross-validation schemes. Models 1-4 were trained using data from the subset of UKBB participants with only one imaging visit available. Following quality control, this subset included a total of 25,721 participants, referred to hereafter as the 25K sample. Based on this sample, we created a subsample of 10,615 (referred to as 10K sample) participants, and from the 10K sample, a final subsample with 2,615  participants (referred to as the 2K sample) was generated, to evaluate concordance in subtype progression and assignments across models trained using different sample sizes. Furthermore, we trained comparable models on baseline and follow-up visits of another subset of 3,315 participants who had two imaging visits available (~2 years between acquisitions), to assess test-retest reliability. Age and total intracranial volume (TIV) were regressed out for all features, and the residuals were transformed to z-scores relative to a control group. To examine the impact of regressing out the effect of age and TIV on the derived subtypes, a sixth model was further trained without regressing out these covariates, using the 10K sample. In all cases, the z-scores for gray matter features were adjusted (i.e. multiplied by -1) such that increases correspond to increasing atrophy for consistent directionality across features. To define a control group, we selected participants who a) did not present any “mental and behavioral disorders” (group F of the ICD-10), “nervous system disorders” (group G of the ICD-10), or health conditions that are known risk factors for dementias, such as diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemias, and b) had an ε3ε3 APOE status. Finally, we included a small set of participants identified as an enrichment disease group (615 for 25K and 10K samples, and 52 for the longitudinal sample) who had a diagnosis of epilepsy, stroke, AD, another unspecified dementia, or Parkinson’s disease. 

We then compared the identified subtypes and their progression patterns across the six models. For the samples with only cross-sectional data (25K, 10K, and 2K), we also explored the participants’ subtype and stage assignment consistency. For the longitudinal sample, we evaluated subtype and stage consistency between the two sessions for each participant. We performed chi-squared tests per model to evaluate differences in subtype assignment per sex, and paired t-tests to assess changes in the probabilities of subtype assignments in different sample sizes. Finally, we assessed the prevalence of diseases known to be risk factors for dementia, namely cardiovascular disease, dyslipidemia, or hypertension, across the different subtypes.

Results

For all scenarios, based on the Cross Validation Information Criteria (CVIC), the test-set log-likelihood across folds, and the positional variance diagrams obtained from SuStaIn, we derived two subtypes. Across all models, one subtype was consistently characterized by an early increase in WMH burden (referred to as WMH predominant), while the other subtype presented earlier gray matter atrophy (referred to as GM atrophy predominant), generally starting with the parahippocampal and frontal features. Regardless of the sample size, at least 40% of the sample, due to their low score across features, was not assigned to any of the subtypes (referred to as “subtype 0”); this proportion went from 41.1% in the smallest sample to 46.9% in the largest, and 46.8% when age and TIV were not regressed out. In all analyses where we regressed out age and TIV, there was a greater proportion of females assigned to the WMH predominant subtype (2K sample: X2(2, N = 2,615) = 15.78, p < 0.01; Lng. ses 1 sample: X2(2, N = 3,315) = 26.27, p < 0.01; Lng. ses 2 sample: X2(2, N = 3,315) = 21.60, p < 0.01; 10K sample: X2(2, N = 10,615) = 33.76, p < 0.01; 25K sample: X2(2, N = 25,721) = 112.25, p < 0.01; all the tests were statistically significant after Bonferroni correction). When we did not regress out age and TIV, there was a greater proportion of females assigned to Subtype 0, and more males assigned to the GM predominant subtype ( X2(2, N = 10,615) = 410.09, p < 0.01). Evaluating the presence of known risk factors for dementia across subtypes, we found higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease, dyslipidemia, or hypertension in the WMH predominant group across all models. Furthermore, all models assigned over 80% of the participants to the first 10 stages. In the models where age and TIV were regressed out, there was a small correlation between stage and age, once the participants assigned to stage 0 were removed (2K sample: r = 0.15; Lng. ses 1: r = 0.13; Lng. ses 2: r = 0.15; 10K sample: r = 0.10; 25K sample: r = 0.10), suggesting that stage assignment was not dependent on age. As expected, this correlation was higher (r = 0.39) for model 6, where we did not regress out age and TIV.

When comparing subtype assignments between the 2K, 10K, and 25K samples, 93.3% of the participants in the 2K sample were assigned to the same subtype by all three models; and this proportion increased to 96.8% for the 10K sample participants’ assignment by models 1 and 2. Additionally, the probabilities of subtype assignment did not differ significantly between sample sizes when we compared the assignments of models 1 and 2 (t(2,614) = 0.51, p= 0.6), however, they were statistically different when we compared models 1 and 3 (t(2,614) = 3.82, p < 0.01), as well as when we compared the assignments for the 10K sample between models 2 and 3 (t(10,614) = 14.14, p < 0,01). Including the participants assigned to stage 0 (i.e. Subtype 0), 69.2% of the participants in the 2K sample were assigned to the same stage across the three analyses, and this proportion grew to 85.1% when we evaluated participants in the 10K sample across two analyses. Correlations between the stages assigned by different models was similarly high (r>0.97). Furthermore, 70.3% of the participants in the 10K sample were assigned to the same subtype between the models that did and did not regress out age. 

In the test-retest analysis, 86.3% of the participants in the longitudinal sample were assigned to the same subtype in both sessions, with 25.3% assigned to the WMH predominant subtype in both cases and 20.3% to the GM predominant, and only 4.3% of participants shifted from the WMH predominant subtype to the GM predominant or viceversa, with the rest moving between those and Subtype 0. The correlation between stage assignments between the 2 sessions was r=0.91, and r=0.89 once we removed the participants that were assigned to stage 0 (i.e. Subtype 0) in either of the analyses.

Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated the reliability of the SuStaIn algorithm in identifying aging subtypes in samples comprised mainly of healthy adults. Leveraging the UKBB neuroimaging sample, we applied the SuStaIn algorithm to gray matter, WMH, and ventricular neuroimaging features to different subsamples to assess reliability in subtype identification and assignment. We found consistency in subtypes and stages across all models. Finally, it is worth noting that the subtypes we identified, namely a WMH predominant subtype, characterized by an early increase in WMH, and a GM predominant subtype, with earlier gray matter atrophy, are consistent with the results described by Skampardoni et.al. (2024)^1 in a different aging cohort (the Imaging-Based Coordinate System for Aging and Neurodegenerative Diseases international consortium) and using a different subtyping approach (clustering), where they described two subtypes of accelerated aging, one characterized as vascular aging, with a high and fast increase in WMH, and another atrophy-predominant aging.
