Introduction

Exercise therapy effectively reduces depressive and anxiety symptoms, yet the neural mechanisms underlying these therapeutic effects remain poorly understood. Traditional neuroimaging approaches assume static brain connectivity, potentially missing dynamic reorganization processes central to treatment response. We investigated whether and how running therapy reorganizes dynamic brain functional connectivity in affective disorders using co-activation patterns (CAPs) analysis—a data-driven method that identifies recurrent whole-brain network states characterized by spatial patterns of regional co-activation/co-deactivation and temporal metrics quantifying occurrence frequency. We hypothesized that: (1) patients with affective disorders would exhibit altered CAP temporal dynamics compared to healthy controls at baseline, (2) running therapy would induce specific reorganization of brain network states accompanied by clinical improvement, and (3) the magnitude of brain network reorganization would correlate with symptom improvement. This study provides the first examination of exercise-induced changes in dynamic brain networks and their coupling to clinical outcomes in affective disorders, addressing a critical gap in understanding neurobiological mechanisms of non-pharmacological interventions for psychiatric conditions. By characterizing transient brain states rather than averaged connectivity, this approach may reveal treatment-sensitive neural markers not captured by conventional static connectivity methods .

Methods

At baseline, resting-state fMRI was collected from 116 participants: 66 healthy controls (36 male, 30 female) and 50 patients with affective disorders (28 male, 22 female) diagnosed with major depressive disorder, social phobia, generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, or agoraphobia according to DSM-IV criteria verified by the Composite International Diagnostic Interview. Patients then received 16 weeks of treatment in the MOTAR trial using a partially randomized patient preference design: running therapy (N=35; at least two supervised 45-minute outdoor sessions weekly at progressive intensity 50-85% heart rate reserve) or SSRI antidepressant treatment (N=15; escitalopram 10-20mg/day or sertraline 50-200mg/day). Post-intervention MRI data were collected from 39 participants (26 running therapy, 9 SSRI). Clinical symptoms were assessed using the Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (IDS-30 items), Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI-21 items), and Fear Questionnaire. Dynamic functional connectivity CAPs analysis^1 identified recurring whole-brain co-activation states by applying k-means clustering (k=2-17) to concatenated fMRI frames from all participants. The optimal clustering solution (k=5) was determined through multi-criteria validation: clustering stability (consensus index across 100 repetitions), intercluster consistency (normalized mutual information), and biological plausibility (Dice overlap with Harvard-Oxford atlases). Spatial CAP maps depicted which brain regions co-activate together; temporal metrics quantified occurrence frequency (percentage of fMRI frames assigned to each CAP). Robustness was verified across three independent clustering scenarios: all participants, healthy controls only, and patients only (Dice overlap >94%, temporal correlation r>0.9). Statistical analyses included: (1) baseline case-control comparisons using independent t-tests and ANCOVA controlling for age and sex, (2) treatment effects examined via linear mixed models with random subject intercepts, comparing within-group changes (running therapy: T0→T1) and treatment-specific trajectories (VISIT×TREATMENT interactions for running vs. SSRI groups), with False Discovery Rate correction (q<0.05) applied separately to brain (5 CAPs) and clinical (9 measures) outcomes, (3) brain-symptom associations assessed using Spearman correlations at baseline and post-treatment among treatment-responsive variables, (4) correlation stability tested using Steiger's Z-test and permutation testing (5,000 iterations) to determine whether brain-symptom relationships reorganized following treatment, and (5) change-change correlations examining whether magnitude of CAP reorganization (ΔT1-T0) predicted symptom improvement (ΔT1-T0).

Results

CAPs analysis identified five distinct brain states: AT-SMCAP (attention-sensorimotor: 73% salience/sensorimotor regions), DMCAP (default mode: 69% default mode network), VS-SCCAP (visual-somatomotor-subcortical network), DM-FPCAP (default mode-frontoparietal: 70% convergence), and FPCAP (frontoparietal: 73% control-attention). At baseline, patients showed decreased VS-SCCAP occurrence rates compared to controls (F=5.4, P=0.006, η²=0.04, β=-0.43, d=0.40, 95% CI[0.03,0.76]), the only finding surviving covariate adjustment and with confidence intervals excluding zero. Running therapy significantly reorganized three brain systems with large effect sizes: AT-SMCAP decreased (β=-0.92, P=0.006, d=-0.89, 95% CI[-1.34,-0.44], ICC=0.51, PFDR=0.006), DMCAP decreased (β=-0.88, P=0.006, d=-0.92, 95% CI[-1.38,-0.46], ICC=0.43, PFDR=0.006), and VS-SCCAP increased (β=0.87, P=0.006, d=0.89, 95% CI[0.45,1.34], ICC=0.43, PFDR=0.006). These reorganizations accompanied significant improvements in depressive symptoms (IDS-total: β=-1.23, P<0.001, d=-1.15, 95% CI[-1.70,-0.61], ICC=0.76, PFDR<0.001; IDS-severity: β=-0.98, P=0.004, d=-1.02, ICC=0.70, PFDR=0.006) and anxiety symptoms (BAI: β=-0.98, P=0.008, d=-0.94, 95% CI[-1.39,-0.48], ICC=0.78, PFDR=0.008). Nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirmed all findings. Treatment-specific trajectory analyses revealed significant VISIT×TREATMENT interactions for DMCAP (F=8.18, P=0.01, η²=0.13) and VS-SCCAP (F=8.10, P=0.01, η²=0.13). Post-hoc comparisons showed running therapy produced large changes (DMCAP: d=0.88, p<0.001; VS-SCCAP: d=-0.85, p<0.001) while SSRI showed no significant within-group changes (all p>0.18, |d|<0.61). Between-group trajectory differences were large (DMCAP: d=-1.47, P=0.01; VS-SCCAP: d=1.47, P=0.01). Inter-CAP correlations showed strong baseline relationships (AT-SMCAP×DMCAP: RHO=0.68, PFDR<0.001; both negatively correlated with VS-SCCAP: RHO=-0.63 and -0.56, PFDR≤0.001) that persisted post-treatment with attenuated magnitudes (RHO=0.61, -0.49, -0.39). CAP-symptom correlations were weak at baseline (all |RHO|<0.17, PFDR>0.05) and post-treatment (all |RHO|<0.43, PFDR>0.05). Critically, DMCAP-symptom coupling changed significantly between timepoints (DMCAP×IDS: ΔRHO=-0.48, Z=-2.00, P=0.05; DMCAP×BAI: ΔRHO=-0.48, Z=-2.05, P=0.04), confirmed by permutation testing (PPERM=0.06-0.08). Reductions in DMCAP occurrence rates showed marginal correlations with symptom improvement (ΔDMCAP×ΔIDS: RHO=-0.29, P=0.17; ΔDMCAP×ΔBAI: RHO=-0.31, P=0.12).

Conclusion

This study provides the first evidence that running therapy reorganizes dynamic brain networks with coupling to clinical symptom improvement in affective disorders. Our three hypotheses were supported:

(1) Baseline brain network alterations: Patients with affective disorders exhibited significantly reduced occurrence rates of the visual-somatomotor-subcortical network (VS-SCCAP) compared to healthy controls, demonstrating transient disruptions in specific brain systems rather than constant, widespread functional alterations.

(2) Treatment-induced brain network reorganization: Running therapy selectively reorganized three brain systems—AT-SMCAP, DMCAP, and VS-SCCAP—accompanied by significant reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms. Importantly, treatment-specific trajectory analyses showed that changes in DMCAP and VS-SCCAP were unique to running therapy and not observed in SSRI-treated patients, despite similar clinical outcomes in the larger trial, highlighting distinct neurobiological pathways that differentiate exercise from pharmacotherapy. 

(3) Brain-symptom coupling and treatment mechanisms: DMCAP-symptom correlations changed significantly between baseline and post-treatment (ΔRHO=-0.48, Z≈-2.0, P<0.05), indicating treatment-induced reorganization of brain-behavior relationships. Moreover, individuals showing greater DMCAP reductions demonstrated larger symptom improvements (ΔDMCAP×ΔIDS: RHO=-0.29; ΔDMCAP×ΔBAI: RHO=-0.31), positioning default mode temporal dynamics as a potential biomarker for therapeutic response. In contrast, AT-SMCAP and VS-SCCAP showed significant reorganization without symptom coupling, suggesting these networks may mediate exercise's broader cognitive and somatic benefits not captured by standard affective symptom questionnaires.

Together, these findings establish that exercise-induced therapeutic effects operate through systematic reconfiguration of transient brain state engagement patterns, demonstrating the utility of dynamic connectivity approaches for characterizing treatment-specific neural mechanisms in psychiatric disorders that traditional static connectivity methods fail to capture^2.
