Introduction

Anosognosia is a common clinical feature in neurodegenerative conditions, defined as a reduced awareness of one’s cognitive, emotional, or behavioral deficits. Its assessment is challenging when relying only on questionnaires, as some studies suggest the presence of implicit forms of knowledge operating at a preconscious level, which may influence behavior even in the absence of explicit awareness[1]. This implicit component can be investigated through the Emotional Stroop paradigm, a color-naming task that measures interference from emotionally salient words. In this task, participants name the ink color of visually-presented neutral, negative, and disease-related words. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a typical finding is that responses are slower for disease-related words, indicating explicit or implicit awareness[2]. However, behavioral data often show substantial inter-individual variability, reducing the sensitivity of traditional statistical methods. To address this issue, the present study introduces a mechanistic framework based on Active Inference to generate and explain response-time patterns in the Emotional Stroop task. Here, we present predictive simulations obtained from this generative model and a parameter-recovery analysis evaluating its ability to identify the underlying parameters.

Methods

In our version of the task, neutral, negative, and disease-related words are displayed in three colors (red, green, or blue), and participants are required to press the button corresponding to the ink color. We developed a single-level Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) model grounded in Active Inference to simulate behavioral responses. Active Inference offers a unified account of perception, action, and decision-making, describing behavior as an inferential process in which actions are selected probabilistically based on prior beliefs and sensory evidence[3]. The model was implemented in MATLAB/SPM and includes four hidden-state factors: ‘word meaning’ and ‘word color’ (determining sensory outcomes), a ‘mental action’ factor representing attentional set (attend-to-color vs. attend-to-meaning), and a ‘task sequence’ factor modelling pre-stimulus, stimulus viewing, and response periods. Policies control the attentional set and are chosen to minimize expected free energy (Figure 1). Emotional salience for negative and disease-related words is encoded by two subject-specific parameters, cneg and cdis, which modulate the precision of the likelihood mapping from ‘meaning’ states to ‘perceived meaning’ outcomes. Response times were computed from the predictive entropy over response outcomes, with Gaussian noise added in log-space. This produces positively skewed log-normal RT distributions, as is commonly observed in empirical response-time data.

We tested whether model parameters could be accurately recovered from behavior. Synthetic data were generated for each combination of cneg and cdis in {–2, 0, 2}, and model inversion was performed using Variational Laplace. Because the task is relatively easy, simulated accuracy (i.e., responding correctly according to the font color) was consistently high; therefore, accuracy would have contributed little information during inversion. For this reason, parameter recovery relied exclusively on response times, with choices informing only the generative process.

Results

Simulated data showed the expected non-normal distribution and reproduced the expected condition differences (Figure 2, panel A). Specifically, a selective slowing for disease-related words compared to negative words — interpreted as a marker of implicit awareness or salience of dementia-related information — emerged when a high value for the disease-related salience parameter was combined with a low value for the negative-word salience parameter. Parameter-recovery results (Figure 2, panel B) indicate that both cneg and cdis could be reliably recovered, as original values consistently fell within the credible intervals of estimated ones, and indeed they were strongly correlated (r = 0.99 for both cneg and cdis).

Conclusion

Our generative model successfully reproduces behavioral patterns typically observed in Emotional Stroop tasks and shows good stability under model inversion. The two “implicit salience” parameters provide mechanistic indicators of altered self-referential processing and could serve as quantitative markers of selective cognitive processing (implicit or explicit). In future empirical applications, these parameters may be compared with standard anosognosia scales (e.g., Anosognosia Questionnaire-Dementia; AQ-D). Moreover, our model could be integrated into task-based fMRI studies, as Posterior Cingulate Cortex (PCC) responses to dementia-related words differ between aware and unaware AD patients[4], and resting-state PCC alterations have also been linked to anosognosia[5]. In the longer term, our work may contribute to operationalizing anosognosia as a computational phenotype, mapping implicit disease-related salience onto quantitative indices that can be related to neural and behavioral data.
