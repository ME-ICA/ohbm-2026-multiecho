Introduction

Musical semantic memory, assessed through familiarity-based recognition—the ability to identify previously encountered musical information without contextual recall—, remains relatively preserved in healthy aging.1 This process engages a predominantly left-lateralized fronto-temporal network.2 However, factors such as the nature of the stimulus or the recency of learning may modulate underlying neural mechanisms, yet remain understudied, particularly for musical stimuli. Music strongly elicits familiarity responses and may rely on partially distinct neural pathways compared with verbal or visual material, such as paintings, which are less emotionally and autobiographically charged.

Clarifying whether recently learned musical information engages the same neural architecture as long-established musical knowledge is especially relevant given the surprising preservation of musical memory and learning capacities reported in Alzheimer's disease.1

Therefore, this fMRI study investigates whether recency influences neural recruitment during passive listening and familiarity-based memory processing in healthy older adults. We predict that recently acquired musical material will rely primarily on medial temporal structures, particularly the hippocampal complex. In contrast, remotely acquired music is expected to engage a broader cortical network supporting musical semantic memory, including prefrontal regions — a distinction reflecting the known corticalization of consolidated memories. We further predict distinct activation patterns for unfamiliar versus familiar (recent and remote) music, with familiar pieces recruiting regions associated with musical semantic memory and, by extension, musical familiarity.

Methods

Twenty healthy older adults (mean age = 77.10 years, SD = 4.90; 16 women, 4 men) with no history of cognitive impairment, neurological disorders, or contraindications to MRI participated in this study. This work is part of the INCAS project, which investigates the neural mechanisms of new musical and pictorial learning in healthy aging and in severe stages of Alzheimer’s disease. The study design and methodology are therefore adapted to be suitable for the Alzheimer’s population. Global cognitive functioning was confirmed using a standardized neuropsychological battery. Visual and musical perception assessment and a standardized music culture questionnaire ensured normal vision and hearing and controlled for musical expertise. 

During an initial familiarization phase (maximum four sessions of ~30 minutes), participants engaged in incidental learning of eight previously unknown French songs and eight unknown paintings. Familiarity was assessed with a 6-point Likert scale combining verbal and behavioral responses. Learning continued until at least 75% of the stimuli reached a minimum familiarity score of 4/6, a threshold validated in aging and cognitively impaired populations as reflecting familiarity-based recognition rather than uncertainty or episodic recall. Within 1–2 days, participants underwent structural and functional MRI. During scanning, they performed a passive listening and viewing task including: (1) sixteen recently learned items, (2) sixteen remotely known items (popular songs from their youth and famous paintings), and (3) sixteen completely unknown items. Stimuli were presented for 10–20 seconds each, in randomized order, with 2-second inter-stimulus intervals. Following scanning, familiarity was reassessed using the same Likert scale, constrained to responses 1–4, to specifically capture familiarity-based recognition. 

Functional images were acquired on a 3T scanner using a whole-brain BOLD-sensitive 2D T2*-weighted EPI sequence (voxel size 2.8 × 2.8 × 2.8 mm³, TE = 30 ms, TR = 2.382 s, 42 slices, 182 volumes), along with a high-resolution T1-weighted structural scan. Data preprocessing was carried out using fMRIPrep (v25.0.0). The workflow included anatomical brain extraction and tissue segmentation, spatial normalization to MNI space, motion correction, and alignment of functional data to anatomical reference images. Noise reduction was performed using anatomical and temporal CompCor regressors. Preprocessed images were then spatially smoothed. Statistical analyses were carried out in « Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 » (Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, London, UK) running under MATLAB R2024a. 

For each participant, a first-level general linear model was specified, with familiarity conditions modeled as regressors of interest. Musical excerpts were modeled as block events and convolved with SPM’s canonical hemodynamic response function. Motion parameters derived from realignment were included as regressors of no interest to account for movement-related artifacts. Individual contrast maps were generated for each participant to compare activation patterns between conditions, including: (1) recently learned > unfamiliar songs, (2) remotely learned > unfamiliar songs, and (3) remotely learned > recently learned songs, as well as the corresponding inverse contrasts. Second-level whole-brain random-effects analyses were conducted using one-sample t-tests for each contrast to assess neural responses associated with recent, remote, and unfamiliar songs. Statistical maps were thresholded at p< .001 (uncorrected, voxel level) with a minimum cluster extent of k = 9 voxels, and clusters surviving FWE correction at p< .05 were considered significant.

Results

On average, participants required 2.5 sessions to reach the familiarity criterion (4/6 for at least 75% of stimuli). Behavioral results showed a significant effect of item familiarity condition on musical recognition scores (χ²(2) = 36.95, p< .001, W = 0.92). Recognition accuracy was significantly higher for both recently learned and remotely known songs compared with unfamiliar items (z = 3.92, p< .001). No significant difference emerged between recently learned and remotely known songs (z = 0.40, n.s.). 

At the neural level, both recently and remotely learned music elicited stronger activation than unfamiliar items in a predominantly left-lateralized fronto-temporo-parietal network. Within this network, recently learned songs, compared with unfamiliar items, produced greater responses in bilateral angular gyrus and basal ganglia, as well as in left superior and middle temporal gyri and anterior hippocampus extending into the amygdala, and in right inferior and middle frontal regions. Remotely learned songs, relative to unfamiliar items, engaged a broader left-lateralized cortical network including superior and medial frontal gyri, precuneus/posterior cingulate, thalamus, angular and supramarginal gyri, orbitofrontal cortex, inferior frontal regions, and anterior hippocampus with adjacent inferior and middle temporal cortex, together with right basal ganglia. (see Figure 1A)

Direct contrasts between recently and remotely learned music further revealed a clear recency-dependent dissociation: recent learning was associated with greater activation in left superior and middle temporal cortices, precuneus/superior parietal lobule, and angular/inferior parietal regions, whereas remote learning preferentially recruited bilateral superior and medial frontal gyri extending into the anterior cingulate. No brain regions showed stronger activation for unfamiliar songs than for either recently or remotely learned items. (see Figure 1B)

Future analyses will extend these findings by incorporating the visual artistic items (paintings), primarily as a control, presented during the learning phase. This will allow us to investigate whether recency-dependent neural patterns observed for musical memory also apply to visual memory and to examine potential cross-modal interactions between music and paintings. These secondary analyses could clarify whether the mechanisms of memory consolidation and familiarity processing are domain-general or domain-specific, and provide insights for multimodal cognitive enrichment and rehabilitation strategies.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight a neural dissociation in musical familiarity processing depending on the recency of learning. Both recently and remotely learned music activated a predominantly left-lateralized fronto-temporo-parietal network typically associated with musical semantic memory and familiarity-based processing.2 However, recently acquired musical memories preferentially engaged distributed temporo-parietal and medial temporal regions within this network, whereas remotely learned music seemed to rely more strongly on frontal and prefrontal cortical regions associated with the musical and autobiographical semantic memory network.3,4 Behaviorally, recognition performance was equivalent across conditions, confirming that the observed differences primarily reflect neurofunctional rather than behavioral effects. This graded shift in neural engagement with memory age aligns with multiple trace theory, which posits that older memories remain connected to hippocampal structures but increasingly depend on medial prefrontal regions over time.5 Importantly, our results provide novel evidence for a temporally graded neural distinction in musical memory within the same individuals, extending prior research that mostly contrasted familiar versus unfamiliar music without manipulating recency.

Together, these findings refine our understanding of musical memory organization in healthy aging and may contribute to explaining the remarkable resilience of musical learning and retention observed in Alzheimer’s disease. They also open new perspectives for comparing the neural substrates of music and visual artistic memory. Such investigations could clarify whether similar principles of recency and consolidation apply across artistic domains, with potential implications for multimodal cognitive enrichment and rehabilitation strategies.
