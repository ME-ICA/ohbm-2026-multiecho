Introduction

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) and social anxiety disorder (SAD) are often comorbid, decreasing treatment success rates. One reason may be that distinct mechanism may underly AUD severity with and without SAD. Reduced inhibitory control is widely studied in AUD and is reflected in prolonged stop-signal reaction times and reduced activation of the ventral attention network (VAN), but findings are inconsistent. Potential sources of this inconsistency are insufficient control for the degree of substance use1 or disregard of comorbidity. It is unknown whether inhibitory control deficits also relate to AUD severity in comorbid AUD and SAD (COM), or whether SAD-specific factors explain AUD severity in COM. Hyperactive self-focused attention has been described in SAD and is related to lower deactivation of the default mode network (DMN). During inhibitory control, DMN activation is typically suppressed.2 Alterations in DMN network activation during inhibitory control might reflect an alternative mechanism contributing to AUD severity in COM.  

We investigated whether inhibitory control differed between individuals with AUD, SAD, COM, and healthy controls (HC). Compared to HC, we expected reduced activation in the VAN for the AUD and COM groups, and altered activation in the DMN for the COM and SAD groups. Using dimensional analyses, we tested whether alterations in VAN and DMN activation during inhibition were related to AUD severity without and with controlling for the degree of alcohol use in the whole sample and within the AUD and COM groups (https://osf.io/e5ktf). 

Methods

Participants (N=175; M= 26.0 years, SD=5.9; 87 female) were recruited in four groups that were characterized by either an AUD (n=43), comorbid AUD and SAD, (n=41), SAD (n=49), or no mental disorder (healthy controls, HC, n=42). AUD and SAD severity did not differ between COM and AUD, and COM and SAD group, respectively. For a visual Stop Signal Task (SST) during MRI, we modeled trial type as a categorical factor and motion parameters as nuisance regressors. Inhibition-related activity was assessed by the contrast successful stop > successful go trials. F-tests for the main effect of group were followed by post-hoc one-sided t-contrasts testing our directional hypotheses regarding group differences (VAN activation: AUD < HC; COM < HC, SSRT: AUD > HC, COM > HC, and DMN deactivation: SAD < HC; COM < HC). 

To assess dimensional associations with AUD severity across the whole sample, we regressed SSRT and inhibition-related activation on AUD severity. Separate models were computed without and with controlling for the degree of alcohol use, and with group as a between-subject factor. In case of significant effects, mean activation in the significant clusters was extracted to examine whether the association with AUD severity was mediated by drinking motives, and whether effects in the COM group were independent of SAD severity. We restricted our search volume to two regions of interest (ROIs), the VAN, and the DMN core (anteromedial and left angular gyrus), derived from the brain network parcellation of the Multiresolution Intrinsic Segmentation Template (MIST) – 12.3 Network specific hypotheses were tested using small-volume correction (SVC) with voxelwise family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < .05. 

Results

Network activation during inhibitory control and SSRT did not differ between groups (all p > .05). In the whole sample inhibition-related activation in the VAN was not related to AUD severity, but to reduced activation in the right inferior frontal gyrus (peak t = 3.32 pFWE = .04) when controlling for the degree of alcohol use, replicating Hildebrandt et al. (2023). SSRT was unrelated to AUD severity (p > .05). Within groups, we observed a negative association between neural activation in the VAN (peak in the pre-supplementary motor area (preSMA): 15, 8, 65; t = 4.43, pFWE = .026) with AUD severity only within the AUD group both, with and without controlling for the degree of alcohol use. In contrast, in the COM group, AUD severity was negatively related to inhibition-related activation in the DMN (peak in the left superior frontal gyrus (SFG): –15, 50, 41; t = 4.04, pFWE = .047). Exploratory analyses revealed that the association between AUD severity and left SFG activation was partially mediated by coping motives in the COM group (bootstrapped indirect effect: -.18, 95% CI [-.42, -.03]) but not in the AUD group. Although in COM left SFG activation was positively associated with social anxiety severity (β =.31, p=.027), the association with AUD severity remained significant when including social anxiety severity in the model (p<.001).

Conclusion

This study showed that distinct underlying neural mechanisms are relevant in AUD with and without comorbid SAD. While in the AUD group, symptom severity was associated with reduced inhibitory-related activity in the VAN, in the COM group, AUD symptom severity was associated with reduced inhibition-related activation in the DMN. This indicates distinct mechanisms contributing to AUD severity with and without comorbidity. We replicate our earlier finding showing that reduced inhibition related activation in the VAN is specific to substance-related problems. The DMN effect in the COM group could indicate higher go-related DMN activation pointing to greater engagement with self-referential processes. Coping related drinking motives mediating this association further pointing to different mechanisms underlying the comorbidity of AUD and SAD. Taken together, these findings provide first evidence for multiple distinct pathways to AUD depending on degree of use and comorbidity.
