Introduction

Disorders of consciousness (DoC) are states following insult to the brain where patients display no signs of conscious awareness for protracted periods. In some cases, patients may regain consciousness but in many the loss of consciousness is chronic. That some patients can recover suggests that a latent capacity for consciousness remains in their brain.1 What substrates may correspond with such a capacity remains unclear, however. DoC are known to correspond with alterations in glucose metabolism within brain tissue, with the degree of change related to patient prognosis.2 Although informative, glucose metabolism alone provides only a partial window into brain metabolism. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) can allow the measurement of additional neurochemicals, including some neurotransmitters. To further specify what metabolic changes occur in DoC, we therefore combined MRS with 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose PET (FDG-PET). With these measures we aimed to identify cellular metabolites that are altered in non-conscious states and those that are retained at normal levels. We further sought to identify what metabolites may be associated with recovery of consciousness over time.

Methods

Fifteen patients with DoC (six female; mean age = 48.3 years) and fourteen healthy controls (nine female; mean age = 32.5 years) underwent MRI and PET scanning. Patient consciousness was assessed with the coma recovery scale (CRS-R). The MRI session included a T1-weighted anatomical scan and a MEGA-PRESS MRS scan from the posterior-medial cortex (PMC). The FDG-PET scan (mean FDG dose = 11.2 ± 1.4 mCi) was conducted while the participants were at rest with their eyes closed. The Osprey toolbox3 was used to process the MRS data. This was used to estimate concentrations of GABA+macromolecules (GABA+), glutamate+glutamine (Glx), choline, creatine, and N-Acetylaspartic acid (tNAA) relative to tissue water content. The ratio of Glx to GABA+ was taken as an indicator of local excitation/inhibition balance (EIB). Reconstructed FDG images were converted to a standardised metabolic index by referencing within-brain values to non-brain head tissue.2 MRS metabolite estimates were compared between patients and controls through linear regression. Comparisons included age, frequency drift, MRS voxel grey matter and CSF proportions, and spectral fit quality metrics as covariates. Associations between MRS metabolites and glucose metabolism within the MRS voxel were tested in the same manner. Finally, MRS metabolites were correlated with the change in CRS-R scores between the time of scanning and a follow-up assessment conducted around four months later (mean time to follow-up = 127 days, range = 68 - 195). Statistical tests were FDR corrected for multiple comparisons. Example spectra and FDG-PET images are shown in Figures 1A&B.

Results

In line with prior work, PMC glucose metabolism was reduced in DoC patients (Figure 1B). Reductions in Glx, creatine, and tNAA were also observed (Figure 2A). The change in these metabolites was correlated with local glucose metabolism (Figure 2B). No metabolites were correlated with CRS-R scores at the time of scanning. The change in CRS-R between the time of scanning and follow-up was positively correlated with choline concentrations (r = 0.84, 95%CI = 0.44 - 0.96, q = 0.015). 

Conclusion

These findings further specify the metabolic changes that can occur in DoC. Glutamate and glutamine have roles in both cellular energy production and, in the case of glutamate, as a neurotransmitter. With GABA not being found to be altered, it is likely that the change in Glx seen in DoC is more connected with its metabolic role. Similarly, both tCr and tNAA are connected with energy synthesis within neurons and astrocytes. Interestingly, although no measured metabolites were correlated with the level at consciousness at the time of scanning, tCho was associated with an improvement in consciousness at follow-up. This may be connected to its role in cell membrane synthesis, suggesting that it may be a potential marker of an ability to repair damaged brain tissue. Of course, it must be noted that these findings are based upon a small sample and may only point towards potential future directions in larger samples and with more focused methods for particular metabolites.
