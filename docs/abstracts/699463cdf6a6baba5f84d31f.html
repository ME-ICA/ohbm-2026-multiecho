Introduction

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) relies on T2*-weighted contrast generated by blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) mechanisms1, which has become one of the key methods in neuroscience2. Despite the widespread application of fMRI, traditional Cartesian sampling-based imaging methods still have significant limitations: primarily limited temporal resolution and susceptibility to motion and magnetic field inhomogeneity artifacts. To address these limitations, various non-uniform sampling methods have been developed. Among them, radial k-space sampling has received considerable attention. However, while radial MRI acquisition improves motion robustness to artifacts3 and provides richer low-frequency information, it also introduces problems such as non-uniform sampling density, streak artifacts, and increased complexity in image reconstruction. 

This work develops a complete reconstruction framework specifically tailored for radially acquired fMRI data, aiming to improve image fidelity and achieve accurate functional localization of small primary cortical structures (size 2-4mm) such as the human olfactory bulb. This framework combines k-space phase alignment of sampling spokes, non-Cartesian resampling and non-unitary Fourier Transform (FT), aiming to apply fMRI sampling techniques with higher temporal resolution and less susceptibility to motion and magnetic field inhomogeneity artifacts to functional neural imaging. Furthermore, we investigated the impact of downsampling and interpolation on image quality to determine the acquisition-reconstruction trade-offs associated with high temporal resolution fMRI. The proposed methods collectively improve the signal-to-noise ratio, mitigate radial fringe artifacts, and support reliable activation mapping in complex, very small anatomical regions.

Methods

Data acquisition for 6 subjects was conducted on a 3T MRI system equipped with a 20-channel receiver head array (MAGNETOM Prisma Scanner. Siemens AG, Erlangen, Germany). We employed a radial gradient-echo sequence (GRE) to capture high-fidelity dynamics of functional signals in the human brain. Anatomical images (T1- and T2-weighted) were also obtained for references. The parameters of the MR sequences are given in a list (Fig.1A).

The radial acquisition sequence developed in this work utilizes a protocol in which 34 spokes are sampled in k-space, each consisting of 160 acquisition points (Fig.1 (B, C)). The gradient waveforms exhibit the typical characteristics of radial acquisition, where the Gx and Gy gradients show oscillatory patterns corresponding to successive readout directions for individual spokes. The sequence initiates with the concurrent application of a radio-frequency (RF) pulse and a Gz gradient for slice selection, followed by a refocusing gradient lobe to correct for phase dispersion before the radial readout is executed. We are currently aiming to further optimize the 34-spoke MRI imaging sequence using the “Pulseq toolbox”4. 

Olfactory stimulation was delivered using a custom-built device that alternated between 15-second rest intervals and 5-second odor presentation (Fig.1D), enabling stimulus-synchronized assessment of BOLD responses. During the rest phase, fresh air flows into the delivery tubing, whereas during the presentation (smell) phase, jasmine odorant molecules are introduced into the tubing. 

The preprocessing workflow of radial k-space (using Matlab, SPM and FSL toolboxes) incorporated: 

  • k-space echo-center phase alignment through either zero-filling or circular shifting, both intended to correct radial center displacement arising from eddy-current related phase inconsistencies (Fig.1E). 
  • Radial k-space reconstruction involved mapping the original 160×34 acquisition matrix onto a 160×160 spatial grid, accompanied by an enhanced averaging-based density compensation filter and subsequent normalization to reduce bias from center oversampling. 
  • Two distinct downsampling methods—direct reduction of radial raw data (method 1) and selective elimination of spatial grid points (method 2)—were examined to characterize their differing impacts on spectral structure (Fig.1G). 
  • In addition, 1-D spline interpolation was applied to extend individual spokes, which was then combined with 2-D interpolation schemes (Fig.1F) to systematically evaluate spoke density and spectral balance, although the latter produced minor artifacts at the boundaries . 
  • Final image reconstruction relied on an inverse fast FT with quadrant realignment, followed by multi-channel integration using the “square-root of the sum of squares” method (Fig.1H). 

These reconstruction methods were initially tested using the American College of Radiology (ACR) MRI phantom to select the optimal option.

Results

We performed anatomical MRI and radial fMRI imaging of the human brain. Regridding experiments using ACR phantom data confirmed that radial and Cartesian reconstructions converged with increasing spoke count, with low-frequency features well preserved even with 34 spokes. 

K-space phase shift correction significantly improved image contrast by restoring coherent low-frequency alignment, with minimal difference between zero-fill and circular shifting, except for negligible high-frequency variations. Downsampling showed trade-offs: spoke-based methods kept radial geometry but reduced intensity, while grid-point removal increased high-frequency noise and mixed trajectories. Moderate 1-D spline interpolation improved boundary sharpness but influenced the spectral balance at high spoke densities. In contrast, 2-D natural-neighborhood interpolation achieved consistently lower Normalized Mean-Squared Error (NMSE) and higher Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR). Multi-channel reconstruction improved signal uniformity within and between slices. The anatomical images allowed us to locate the olfactory bulb (Fig.2A) and create a mask. fMRI analysis revealed stimulus-locked activation in the olfactory bulb by highlighting the activated voxels (Fig.2B) and the peaks in the power of FT spectrum of the signal, S(t), in activated voxels 1-4 (Fig.2C) confirmed the synchronization with applied odor presentation.

Conclusion

The results confirm that radial acquisition of k-space is well suited for olfactory fMRI, where motion tolerance and temporal stability are critical. Reconstruction image quality depends strongly on central k-space alignment, density compensation, and appropriate interpolation5. While 1D spoke interpolation amplifies spectral imbalance at high densities, 2D adaptive methods preserve structural fidelity. Downsampling results highlight the importance of balancing temporal acceleration against spatial artifacts. Current limitations include modest spatial resolution and challenges in distinguishing true activation from reconstruction-induced contrast variations with standard fMRI toolboxes (SPM12 and FSL FEAT).

Finally, this work establishes a full reconstruction pipeline for radially acquired fMRI and demonstrates its feasibility for detecting olfactory bulb activation. By integrating echo phase alignment, regridding and systematic evaluation of downsampling and regridding strategies, the framework provides a foundation for future high-precision, motion and magnetic field susceptibility-robust fMRI applications.
