Introduction

Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) noninvasively modulates the interoceptive brain network through vagal afferent pathways and has been shown to enhance several aspects of cognitive function. Although fMRI-based prediction of taVNS treatment response has been demonstrated in clinical populations such as depression1 and insomnia2, it remains unclear whether brain reactivity during taVNS can explain individual differences in its cognitive benefits in healthy adults.

To address this gap, we examined fMRI responses evoked by taVNS stimulation during a learning task. Importantly, brain activity was analyzed based solely on stimulation periods, independent of cue type, to capture neural reactivity specifically associated with taVNS. We further applied machine learning methods to identify brain regions whose stimulation-evoked activation predicts individual differences in taVNS-related learning enhancement.

Methods

Forty-seven healthy adults completed a pain-prediction task during 3T fMRI at Hiroshima University. The task followed a Pavlovian conditioning design with high-, low-, and no-risk visual cues (Fig. 1). The visual cue (defined as time 0) remained on screen throughout each trial. TaVNS (25 Hz, 500 µs pulse width) was delivered from –3 to 6 s relative to cue onset at the left cymba conchae (active) or earlobe (sham), while pain stimulation occurred only after a jittered delay following taVNS offset. In every trial, participants reported their expected pain intensity based on the cue, enabling behavioral quantification of learning accuracy. Because taVNS began prior to cue onset and the hemodynamic response exhibits an approximate 3-s delay, taVNS-evoked neural activity was captured by extracting BOLD signals from 0–9 s after cue onset, corresponding to the expected hemodynamic response to the full stimulation period. Participants completed two active and two sham runs in randomized order.

Brain parcellation was performed using the Human Brainnetome Atlas (246 ROIs) and the SUIT cerebellar atlas (28 ROIs). Two types of features were derived from stimulation-evoked BOLD responses. Time-domain features were computed for 90 regions implicated in interoception3 and pain processing using the 22 characteristics from the catch22 toolbox together with the mean and standard deviation of each regional time course. In parallel, functional connectivity (FC) features were obtained by computing pairwise correlations between all 274 regions, yielding a whole-brain FC profile for each participant.

Behaviorally, participants were classified as taVNSresponders or nonresponders according to the difference in prediction accuracy between active and sham stimulation (∆Accact-sham). A univariate feature-selection procedure and a support vector machine (SVM) classifier were applied separately to the time-domain and FC feature sets, with hyperparameters optimized via grid search and five-fold cross-validation. Features that consistently emerged in at least three of the five folds were designated as stable predictors and interpreted as putative neural biomarkers, which were subsequently visualized to illustrate differences between responders and nonresponders.

Results

Twenty-nine participants (62%) were classified as responders. The SVM classifier trained on temporal features achieved the best performance when eight features were selected through univariate feature selection (F1 = 0.73; sensitivity = 0.75; specificity = 0.64). In contrast, the classifier trained on eight FC-derived features showed markedly lower performance (F1 = 0.56; sensitivity = 0.54; specificity = 0.42), indicating that taVNS-evoked temporal dynamics were more informative for predicting individual responsiveness.

Stable temporal features consistently selected across cross-validation included mean BOLD responses in cerebellar regions (right Crus I, Crus II, VIIb, VIIIa, and V), the left dysgranular insula (INS_L_6_4), and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (OrG_R_6_4). Group comparisons revealed significantly lower mean BOLD signals in responders across all stable regions (two-sample t tests, FDR-corrected p < 0.05; Fig. 2A), with effect sizes ranging from d = −0.55 (right V) to d = −0.87 (right Crus I), reflecting medium-to-large differences.

Across participants, mean BOLD responses in the left dysgranular insula (INS_L_6_4) and right cerebellar VIIIashowed significant negative correlations with the behavioral improvement index ∆Accact-sham (both r = −0.39, p= 0.025; Fig. 2B). Time-course analysis further showed that both regions exhibited similar taVNS-evoked signal reductions in responders and nonresponders, with responders demonstrating consistently lower activity throughout the stimulation period (Fig. 2C).

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that taVNS-evoked fMRI responses, particularly within cerebellar and insular regions, predict individual differences in taVNS-induced learning enhancement. Together with our previous findings showing structural and structural–covariance differences between responders and nonresponders in prefrontal, insular, and cerebellar regions4, these results converge on a set of neuroanatomical and functional substrates integrating interoception and cognition. These substrates have been implicated in learning and decision-making and may underlie variability in neuromodulation efficacy. Although taVNS has demonstrated therapeutic benefits across interoception-related conditions—including depression1, insomnia2, and migraine5—approximately 25–33% of patients show limited or no clinical improvement, underscoring the need for reliable predictors of responsiveness. Our findings suggest that both structural integrity and stimulation-evoked functional reactivity within these networks may serve as predictive biomarkers to guide personalized taVNS interventions.
