Introduction

Opioid Use Disorder (OUD) represents one of the most devastating public health crises in modern history, claiming over 79,000 American lives annually and costing an estimated $1.5 trillion annually to the U.S. economy.1 OUD is characterized by persistent, overwhelming cravings that drive a relapsing-remitting cycle of compulsive drug-seeking behavior, making long-term recovery extraordinarily difficult. Current FDA-approved pharmacotherapies, while life-saving for many, are fundamentally limited: medications such as methadone and buprenorphine do not eliminate cravings but rather substitute one opioid dependency for another, with relapse rates reaching 74%.2

Ibogaine, an indole alkaloid derived from the African shrub Tabernanthe iboga, has emerged as a promising yet scientifically understudied candidate for OUD treatment. Anecdotal reports and preliminary clinical observations document its remarkable ability to reduce or eliminate opioid withdrawal symptoms and cravings after a single treatment session, with therapeutic effects persisting for months.3 Unlike conventional maintenance therapies requiring daily dosing, ibogaine appears to induce a rapid neuroplastic reset of addiction circuitry. To date though, there have been no human functional magnetic resonance imaging studies of ibogaine in individuals with OUD, leaving its mechanisms of action largely unknown.

The pathophysiology of OUD involves profound dysregulation of brain reward and motivation circuits, including the nucleus accumbens, basal ganglia, and anterior insula. Chronic opioid exposure produces aberrant synaptic plasticity and hyperactivity in these regions, with glutamatergic signaling playing a critical role. This study represents the first multimodal neuroimaging investigation to characterize ibogaine's acute and sustained effects on brain function, chemistry, and electrical activity in individuals with OUD, providing essential mechanistic insights needed to guide future therapeutic development.

Methods

This longitudinal, observational study examines adults with DSM-5 diagnosed OUD who independently elected to undergo ibogaine treatment at Ambio Life Sciences in Mexico. Our target enrollment (N=20) includes both male and female participants aged 21-65 years capable of providing informed consent. Exclusion criteria include contraindications to MRI, current psychotic disorder, and unstable cardiovascular conditions. The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the University of California, Irvine and is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (ID# NCT07226570). 

Prior to baseline assessment, all participants undergo a standardized opioid transition protocol at Ambio Life Sciences. Participants are transitioned from their opioid of addiction to a standardized dose of morphine under medical supervision. This transition is completed before the baseline visit to ensure participant safety, as ibogaine significantly lowers the seizure threshold and can produce toxic interactions when combined with certain opioids, particularly long-acting or synthetic formulations. The morphine stabilization protocol also reduces variability in baseline opioid exposure across participants, allowing for more controlled assessment of ibogaine's effects. Participants remain on the standardized morphine regimen through Visit 1 and discontinue opioid use as part of the ibogaine treatment protocol.

Participants complete study visits at the research facility at UC Irvine at three primary timepoints: Visit 1 baseline (pre-treatment), Visit 2 acute post-ibogaine treatment (1-3 days after treatment), and Visit 3 sustained follow-up (1-month post-treatment). Additional clinical follow-ups occur at 3- and 6-months post-treatment to assess long-term outcomes before completing participation (Figure 1).

Neuroimaging data acquisition is performed on a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma MRI scanner at the UCI Facility for Imaging and Brain Research using a 32-channel head coil. Structural imaging consists of high-resolution T1-weighted images acquired using a 3D MPRAGE sequence with 1.0mm isotropic voxels. Task-based functional MRI employs a multiband gradient-echo EPI sequence during a block-design opioid cue-reactivity task that contrasts BOLD activation to opioid-related versus neutral images.4 Participants view drug-related and neutral images while brain activation patterns are assessed, with a focus on reward-related regions including the nucleus accumbens, basal ganglia, anterior insula, and ventral striatum. Resting-state fMRI (results not reported here) is acquired using identical parameters during a 10-minute eyes-open scan to assess intrinsic functional connectivity within reward networks. Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (results not reported here) uses a single-voxel PRESS sequence with voxels placed in the right nucleus accumbens and right anterior insula to quantify glutamate plus glutamine concentrations relative to creatine.

We use a 64-channel BrainVision EEG system to record 5 minutes of eyes-open and eyes-closed resting-state data at each visit. EEG data undergoes preprocessing in EEGLAB to remove artifacts, followed by Fast Fourier Transform to calculate power spectral density.

Clinical outcomes are assessed using validated psychometric instruments. The Drug Taking Confidence Questionnaire measures perceived ability to resist drug use in high-risk situations, with higher scores indicating greater resilience against relapse. The Opioid Craving Visual Analog Scale captures momentary craving intensity at the time of assessment (0=no cravings and 100=worst cravings imaginable). The Subjective Opioid Withdrawal Scale quantifies withdrawal symptom severity. MATLAB Conn Toolbox was used for preprocessing and statistical modeling. Task-fMRI data underwent general linear modeling to identify regions showing significant changes in activation to opioid cues across timepoints.

Results

To date, we have successfully recruited and scanned three individuals with OUD at both baseline and acute post-ibogaine treatment timepoints. One-month follow-up scans for two participants were completed in November 2025. An unanticipated fMRI scanner malfunction unrelated to our study occurred on the day of post-treatment imaging for two participants, limiting immediate post-ibogaine fMRI acquisition to one individual at the acute timepoint. Two participants, though, successfully completed their Visit 3 1-month post-treatment scan. All Visit 1 and Visit 2 post-treatment EEG data were successfully obtained for all three participants with one missing Visit 3.

Following ibogaine treatment, participants demonstrated substantial improvements across multiple psychometric measures (Figure 2a). On the Subjective Opioid Withdrawal Scale (SOWS), all three participants showed marked reductions in withdrawal symptoms, with scores declining from a baseline range of 20-32 at Visit 1 to near-zero levels by Visit 3. Opioid Craving Visual Analog Scale (OC_VAS) scores showed a dramatic and uniform response across all participants. Starting from nearly maximal baseline values (80-90) at Visit 1, all three participants experienced near-complete elimination of craving by Visit 2 and remaining at these minimal levels through Visit 3. On the Drug Taking Confidence Questionnaire (DTCQ), which measures perceived ability to resist drug use across high-risk situations, all participants showed substantial increases at V2, with the average trend continuing to V3. The Spiritual Transcendence Scale (STS) showed more variable patterns. Participant 1 demonstrated modest increases from baseline (155) to Visit 3 (210), Participant 2 showed no change over time, while Participant 3 showed a much greater change, starting lower than the other two (80) and reaching 175 by Visit 3. 

Neuroimaging results from the participant with complete fMRI data revealed reduced BOLD activation evoked by opioid images in reward-related brain regions following ibogaine treatment (Figure 2b). During the opioid cue-reactivity task at V2, we observed decreased activity in the ventral striatum when viewing opioid-related images compared to baseline; these effects persisted at the 1-month follow-up, V3.

EEG analyses showed that Participant 1 (Figure 2c, red) demonstrated a sharp decline in theta power from Visit 1 to Visit 2, showing the most dramatic reduction among all participants. In the alpha band, Participant 1 exhibited a substantial decrease in power from Visit 1 to Visit 2. No data were available for Participant 1 at Visit 3. Participant 2 (blue) showed consistently elevated theta power across all three visits compared to the other participants, with gradual decreases from Visit 1 through Visit 3, but maintained the highest theta power throughout the study period. In the alpha band, Participant 2 began with the highest baseline power at Visit 1, showed a moderate reduction at Visit 2 consistent with acute psychedelic effects, then demonstrated a rebound at Visit 3 with alpha power partially recovering toward baseline levels, suggesting neural recovery. Participant 3 (green) started with relatively low theta power at Visit 1, showed a modest decrease at Visit 2, and exhibited a substantial decline by Visit 3. In the alpha band, Participant 3 began at moderate levels at Visit 1, showed slightly reduced power at Visit 2, and continued to decline markedly at Visit 3. Unlike Participant 2, Participant 3 showed no rebound in alpha power, instead demonstrating continued suppression through the final assessment.

Overall, the average theta and alpha power (black dashed line) showed progressive decreases across visits. The consistent Visit 2 reduction in alpha power across all participants may reflect acute alterations in thalamocortical dynamics and cortical excitability.5

Conclusion

This study represents the first multimodal neuroimaging investigation of ibogaine treatment in individuals with opioid use disorder, providing critical initial evidence for its neurobiological mechanisms of action. Our preliminary findings demonstrate that ibogaine produces profound and rapid reductions in opioid craving alongside measurable changes in brain reward circuitry function. The near-complete elimination of cravings and more than doubling of confidence to resist drug use following a single ibogaine treatment session supports anecdotal reports of its remarkable therapeutic potential. Importantly, these dramatic clinical improvements were accompanied by reduced neural activation in the ventral striatum during exposure to opioid cues, suggesting a normalization of the hyperactive reward circuits that perpetuate addictive behavior.

The consistency of decreased alpha power across all participants provides additional evidence of ibogaine's neurophysiological effects and aligns with known signatures of psychedelic states. These converging lines of evidence from clinical, functional, and electrophysiological data suggest that ibogaine may induce a rapid neuroplastic reset of addiction circuitry, fundamentally distinguishing it from conventional maintenance therapies that merely substitute one opioid dependency for another.

Despite the promising nature of these initial results, several important limitations must be acknowledged. Our current sample size remains small with only three participants completing baseline and acute assessments, and technical difficulties limited fMRI acquisition. The observational design without a control group prevents definitive causal attribution, and the lack of long-term neuroimaging follow-up at 3 and 6 months limits our ability to correlate sustained clinical improvements with persistent neural changes. Additionally, individual variability in treatment response and the heterogeneity inherent in OUD populations necessitate larger sample sizes to establish generalizable biomarkers.

The critical knowledge gap regarding ibogaine's mechanisms has been the primary barrier preventing its integration into mainstream medicine and FDA approval pathways. By establishing objective brain-based biomarkers of therapeutic response, this research provides essential foundational data to guide future drug development. These neural signatures could serve as target engagement markers for pharmaceutical companies developing safer ibogaine analogues, reveal novel therapeutic targets for addiction treatment, and provide policymakers with evidence needed for informed regulatory decisions.

Continued recruitment toward our target enrollment of 20 participants will enable more robust statistical analyses and examination of individual differences in treatment response. The integration of multiple neuroimaging modalities positions this study to create a comprehensive neurobiological blueprint of ibogaine's therapeutic action, transforming anecdotal promise into rigorous scientific evidence and offering new hope for millions struggling with the devastating cycle of opioid addiction.
