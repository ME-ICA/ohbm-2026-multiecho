Introduction

Foundation models for human fMRI promise shared latent spaces that generalize across tasks, participants, and datasets. Yet it remains unclear whether these representations truly capture perceptual states in a way that transfers across changing task demands, even with or without explicit report. Most current evaluations focus on coarse distinctions (e.g., task vs rest, visual category) rather than the internal structure of subjective experience.

In this project we probe these promises using a zero-transfer logic: train decoders on one regime, then test on a different regime without any task-specific finetuning of the foundation model. In a binocular rivalry paradigm, we use a contrastively pretrained 4D Swin Transformer (SwiFT) to extract fMRI embeddings from non-rivalrous replay blocks and ask whether classifiers trained only on replay can decode three-way rivalry percepts (Face/House/Mixed) when sensory input is held constant. This replay→rivalry setting tests whether the latent space tracks endogenous alternations in percept under identical stimulation.

Then we extend the same framework to color qualia and to report vs no-report. Using an open fMRI dataset in which participants judge the similarity between sequentially presented colors from a well characterized hue space, we train decoders on trials with explicit similarity ratings (Report) and evaluate zero-transfer generalization to trials without color similarity evaluation (No_Report), where only stimulus-defined similarity is available. SwiFT is kept frozen; only lightweight classifiers are learned on top of its embeddings. By comparing SwiFT-based decoders to conventional baselines and quantifying group-level and single-subject performance, we ask whether foundation model representations encode a geometry of color similarity that transfers from report to no-report, complementing our replay→rivalry benchmark and jointly testing percept sensitive, generalizable fMRI foundation models.

Methods

We used our zero-transfer framework across two paradigms, binocular rivalry and color similarity, to test whether embeddings from a contrastively pretrained 4D Swin Transformer (SwiFT) support percept decoding without task-specific finetuning; SwiFT was kept frozen and only support vector classifiers were trained.

In the binocular rivalry experiment, we acquired fMRI data where participants viewed dichoptic Face/House stimuli in alternating rivalry and replay blocks across four runs (330 volumes per run, TR = 1.75 s, 2.5 mm isotropic voxels). Replay blocks presented non-rivalrous monocular sequences scheduled to match each participant’s rivalry dynamics; rivalry blocks presented constant stimulation while participants reported their dominant percept (Face / House / Mixed). Functional data were preprocessed with FSL and normalized to MNI space. For each TR we constructed a 20-TR window (10-TR stride), resampled/cropped to 96×96×96, z-scored per voxel, and passed the 4D block into SwiFT, yielding a 46,080-dimensional embedding per window. Replay labels followed the scheduled monocular sequence; rivalry labels were derived from button presses, temporally smoothed and HRF-aligned, and constrained by a minimum dwell time. Zero-transfer decoding trained a 3-way linear SVM on replay embeddings and evaluated on rivalry embeddings.

In the color similarity fMRI dataset from Hirao et al.2 35 healthy adults (4 runs, TR = 1 s, 3.2mm isotropic voxels, 862 volumes per run) viewed sequential colors drawn from nine hues (HSV angles 0–320° in 40° steps). A cue indicated whether an explicit similarity rating was required (Report) or withheld (No_Report). Each trial comprised a stimulus ("Stim") followed by an evaluation period ("Eval"), during which participants rated similarity on a −4…+4 scale (Report) or omitted evaluation of the pair (No_Report). Using the provided preprocessed data, we resampled each run to 2 mm isotropic MNI space (96×96×96) and z-scored voxelwise. Eval onsets were shifted by 3 s, and for every shifted Eval time we extracted a 20-TR window centered on that time and encoded it into a 46,080-dimensional embedding.

For Report trials, score_eval was mapped to four ordinal classes: very dissimilar (score < −2), dissimilar (−2 ≤ score < 0), similar (0 < score ≤ 2), and very similar (score > 2), and also collapsed to binary similar vs dissimilar. For No_Report trials, we computed objective similarity from the hues of the current and previous stimuli using circular distance on the HSV hue circle (normalized to [0, 1]) and discretized into the same four-level and binary schemes, providing pseudo-labels for zero-transfer evaluation. Analogous to replay→rivalry, we trained on Report embeddings and tested on No_Report embeddings.

As a conventional baseline in both paradigms, we constructed matched 20-TR windows and summarized the BOLD signal using the Schaefer-10003 parcellation in MNI space. For each window we averaged the z-scored signal within each parcel at each TR, yielding a 1000×20 parcel-by-time matrix per trial, vectorized into a fixed-length feature vector for decoding.

All classifiers were SVMs with class-balanced weights (scikit-learn). For rivalry, we trained 3-way SVMs on replay and evaluated on rivalry, summarizing performance with accuracy and macro-F1 and comparing to label-shuffled permutation nulls. For color, we trained 2-class and 4-class SVMs on Report trials and evaluated on No_Report trials using accuracy, balanced accuracy, and macro-F1. SwiFT was implemented in PyTorch/MONAI using a contrastive checkpoint pretrained on Human Connectome Project resting-state data with an InfoNCE objective.

Results

In the binocular rivalry dataset, we trained linear SVMs on SwiFT embeddings from non-rivalrous replay blocks and evaluated them on rivalrous blocks with identical visual input. At the group level, replay-trained SwiFT decoders achieved clear above-chance three-way Face/House/Mixed classification (accuracy = 0.54; chance = 0.33), whereas the Schaefer-1000 parcel baseline reached 0.39. At the subject level, SwiFT again outperformed Schaefer: mean accuracy across participants was 0.44 (SD = 0.11) for SwiFT versus 0.37 (SD = 0.03) for Schaefer. Label-shuffled permutation nulls remained at chance (not shown), and misclassifications were concentrated around perceptual transitions, with the Mixed class under-represented, consistent with the ambiguity of brief or intermediate rivalry states. Together, these results indicate that the SwiFT latent space tracks endogenous fluctuations in rivalry percept beyond what is captured by a standard parcellation-based representation.

We then applied the same zero-transfer logic to color similarity judgments. Here, classifiers were trained only on Report trials, using behavioral ratings to label SwiFT embeddings as similar vs dissimilar (and, in a finer analysis, into four ordered similarity levels), and evaluated on No_Report trials where labels were derived solely from hue geometry. For the binary similar–dissimilar task, group-level accuracy on No_Report trials was slightly above chance for SwiFT (0.50 vs 0.50 chance) and comparable for Schaefer (0.50). At the subject level, SwiFT showed a small but consistent advantage: mean accuracy was 0.52 (SD = 0.03) versus 0.50 (SD = 0.04) for Schaefer. Most participants exhibited at least a modest positive transfer from Report to No_Report when using SwiFT embeddings, suggesting that the foundation model preserves some information about subjective similarity even when overt reports are withheld.

We also asked whether SwiFT supports four-way decoding of graded similarity (very dissimilar, dissimilar, similar, very similar). In this harder setting, performance remained close to chance for both representations. At the group level, accuracy on No_Report trials was 0.26 for SwiFT and 0.27 for Schaefer (chance = 0.25). Subject-level mean accuracies were 0.28 (SD = 0.03) for SwiFT and 0.27 (SD = 0.02) for Schaefer. For SwiFT, the most reliable decoding was observed for the extreme categories, with intermediate similarity levels more frequently confused, indicating that the model more readily supports a coarse partition of similarity space than fine-grained ordinal readout.

Across both paradigms, SwiFT-based decoders outperformed Schaefer-1000 parcel features, but the magnitude of this advantage differed by task. In binocular rivalry, SwiFT yielded clearly higher accuracies than Schaefer at both group and subject level, whereas in the color similarity dataset its advantage was smaller and most evident for binary similar–dissimilar decoding, with both methods performing close to chance in the four-way setting.

Conclusion

We stress-tested a fMRI foundation model along two percept-relevant axes: (i) tracking endogenous percepts under fixed input (binocular rivalry) and (ii) generalizing across the presence vs absence of explicit report (color similarity). Using a frozen SwiFT encoder and a shared zero-transfer protocol, we find converging—though clearly graded—evidence that its latent space carries percept-sensitive structure.

In binocular rivalry, replay-trained decoders generalized robustly to rivalrous blocks, with substantial above-chance three-way decoding of Face/House/Mixed and a clear advantage over Schaefer-1000 parcel features at both subject and group level. Because the visual input is controlled and rivalry labels never enter training, this supports the view that SwiFT embeddings covary with perceptual dominance rather than trivial confounds such as motor output or block timing. The concentration of errors around transitions and in the Mixed class further points to genuine perceptual ambiguity rather than model artifacts.

In the color similarity dataset, the same zero-transfer logic yielded a more modest picture. Report-trained classifiers generalized to No_Report trials for a binary similar–dissimilar distinction, with a small but consistent subject-level advantage of SwiFT over Schaefer, yet only minimal separation at the group level and performance near chance for four-way similarity decoding. Several factors likely contribute. First, the rating distribution was skewed toward extreme values, which inflates apparent performance for coarse splits but makes intermediate categories hard to learn. Second, No_Report labels were derived from hue distance in HSV space, which is at best an approximation to each participant’s subjective similarity structure. Noise and systematic mismatch in these pseudo-labels will selectively depress zero-transfer performance, especially when pooling across subjects with idiosyncratic color spaces, potentially explaining why subject-level decoding looks cleaner than the group aggregate.

Taken together, the replay→rivalry and report→no-report results sharpen what current fMRI foundation models deliver. On the positive side, a single contrastively pretrained transformer, without any task-specific finetuning supports zero-transfer percept decoding across very different domains and distribution shifts, in line with the promise of general purpose neural representations. On the cautionary side, the strength of the percept signal depends strongly on the paradigm, data and label quality, and granularity of the readout: coarse states under well-defined labels (Face vs House) are captured far more robustly than fine grained similarity judgments under noisy, geometry-based pseudo-labels.

More broadly, our findings argue that coarse supervised benchmarks are not sufficient to evaluate percept sensitivity in fMRI foundation models. Zero-transfer tests across regimes; replay→rivalry, report→no-report, and ultimately across tasks, datasets, and labeling schemes offer a stricter probe of whether these models encode perceptual state and its geometry. The two benchmarks introduced here suggest that current contrastively trained transformers are already somewhat percept-sensitive, but still leave substantial headroom for models that better capture the structure of human qualia and can tolerate imperfect or implicit labels.
