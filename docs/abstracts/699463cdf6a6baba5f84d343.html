Introduction

Decoding the contents of human thought from brain activity remains a major challenge in neuroscience. Most decoding work has emphasized externally driven perception (e.g., visual stimuli). Yet waking cognition is often shaped by spontaneous, internally generated states such as autobiographical memory, rumination, and distraction. functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) provides a noninvasive window into blood-oxygen-level–dependent (BOLD) dynamics, but existing approaches often depend on subject-specific models and highly controlled tasks. Whether internally generated mental states can be decoded across diverse subjects using standard fMRI data remains unclear.1

We propose a cross-brain decoding framework grounded in predictive coding theory, which views the brain as a generative system that reduces prediction error between internal expectations and sensory input.2 Motivated by sequence prediction, we hypothesize that modeling fMRI temporal dynamics as a predictive process can improve decoding of spontaneous mental states. To test this, we developed BrainStateNet, a multi-task architecture for fMRI time-series prediction and augmented with spatial attention to capture inter-regional interactions. BrainStateNet is trained to jointly predict future fMRI signals and classify ongoing mental states, with observed signals as priors, predicted signals as expectations, and classification outputs as state inferences.

BrainStateNet is pre-trained on CoRR resting-state fMRI and fine-tuned on the Rumination_Beijing dataset,3 where each subject transitions among rest, memory, rumination, and distraction. We evaluate generalization on the independent Rumination_Suzhou dataset4 acquired at a different site. We analyze attention maps and gradient-based measures in Rumination_Suzhou and in the DIRECT phase II multi-site resting-state dataset5 spanning neurotypical controls and major psychiatric diagnoses to relate decoded states and diagnostic differences to network dynamics.

Methods

We developed and evaluated BrainStateNet in a pretraining–fine-tuning–transfer setting using four fMRI datasets. CoRR resting-state fMRI was used for pretraining to learn generic BOLD dynamics. The model was then fine-tuned on the Rumination_Beijing dataset, where each participant transitioned among four internally generated states: rest, autobiographical memory (“memory”), rumination, and distraction. Cross-site generalization and mechanistic analyses were performed on the independent Rumination_Suzhou dataset, which was acquired on a different scanner in another city and cohort. For large-scale clinical transfer, we further applied the same framework to DIRECT phase II multi-site resting-state fMRI spanning neurotypical controls and three psychiatric diagnostic groups: major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (BD), and schizophrenia (SCZ).

We formulated decoding as a joint sequence modeling problem. fMRI time series were segmented using a sliding window of 64 time points as input. For time-series forecasting, the model predicted the next time point and predictive performance was quantified as the correlation between predicted and observed signals. As an initial model-selection step, we benchmarked representative sequential backbones: recurrent neural network (RNN), long short-term memory (LSTM), gated recurrent unit (GRU), state space model (S5), and generative pre-trained transformer (GPT), and Informer by fine-tuning them on Rumination_Beijing for next-step BOLD prediction using 10-fold cross-validation with five repeated runs. Based on this benchmarking, we adopted Informer as the backbone for BrainStateNet. BrainStateNet augments the long-range forecasting backbone with an encoder that integrates temporal attention and a spatial attention mechanism to capture inter-regional interactions, and it is trained with a multi-task objective that jointly optimizes (i) next-step fMRI prediction and (ii) four-way mental-state classification; these objectives are combined in a joint loss.

Decoding performance was evaluated at both the segment level and the subject level. Segment-level predictions were produced for each sliding-window segment. Subject-level labels were obtained by aggregating segment predictions via ensemble voting. To assess robustness to site shift, models trained on Rumination_Beijing were directly applied to Rumination_Suzhou without subject-specific retraining; we additionally used a model ensemble (50 trained models) for cross-site evaluation and downstream analyses.

To interpret what the model learned about mental-state organization, we extracted spatial attention maps in Rumination_Suzhou and quantified state-dependent network reconfiguration. For each pair of mental states, we performed connection-wise t-tests and computed Cohen’s d for each connection, applying Bonferroni correction across 102,831 connections (p < 0.05/102,831) and retaining effects with d > 0.5. Regions of interest (ROIs) were grouped into eight large-scale networks: visual network (VN), somatomotor network (SMN), dorsal attention network (DAN), ventral attention network (VAN), limbic network (LN), frontoparietal network (FPN), default mode network (DMN), and subcortical nuclei, and we summarized within- and between-network patterns by counting significant positive and negative connections. In parallel, we computed vanilla gradient maps under three training objectives (prediction-only, classification-only, and joint) in both Rumination_Suzhou and DIRECT to probe objective-specific regional contributions. ROI-wise comparisons used pairwise t-tests with false discovery rate (FDR) correction (q = 0.05), and we summarized the top 20 regions showing the largest statistical differences for mental-state and diagnostic contrasts.

Results

Benchmarking sequential backbones for long-range BOLD forecasting revealed marked differences in predictive performance. Informer achieved the highest agreement between predicted and observed fMRI signals (example trace R = 0.9946), outperforming S5 (R = 0.9140), RNN (R = 0.8838), GRU (R = 0.8705), LSTM (R = 0.8415), and GPT (R = 0.7522). Building on Informer as the backbone, BrainStateNet, which integrates spatial attention with temporal attention and a multi-task objective, further improved the joint modeling of fMRI prediction and mental-state decoding. Relative to the model without spatial attention, BrainStateNet showed higher segment-level classification accuracy (0.784 ± 0.242 vs. 0.751 ± 0.275; p < 0.001) and stronger prediction correlations (0.604 ± 0.050 vs. 0.477 ± 0.060; p < 0.001). When performance was summarized by a normalized score that jointly captures prediction and classification metrics, BrainStateNet ranked highest among all evaluated architectures on the Rumination_Beijing dataset.

Cross-site generalization was assessed by training on Rumination_Beijing and directly testing on the independent Rumination_Suzhou dataset collected on a different scanner in another city, without subject-specific retraining. Under this challenging setting, BrainStateNet maintained robust decoding performance, achieving up to 83.72% subject-level classification accuracy (chance = 25%), indicating that the predictive multi-task framework learns representations that transfer across cohorts and acquisition conditions. Model comparison on Rumination_Suzhou confirmed that BrainStateNet remained the top-performing method under the same normalized scoring scheme, supporting the stability of its cross-site advantage.

Spatial attention analyses in Rumination_Suzhou revealed distinct patterns of network reconfiguration across mental states. Pairwise contrasts among mental states produced characteristic distributions of significant positive and negative connections across the eight canonical networks. Rest was associated with stronger segregation of default-mode connections and relatively fewer cross-network positive couplings. In contrast, distraction showed broad integration between default-mode and frontoparietal systems and increased coupling with attention networks, consistent with externally oriented cognitive engagement. Memory and rumination exhibited intermediate patterns, with enhanced default-mode and limbic connectivity relative to rest, highlighting their reliance on internally focused, self-referential processes.

Gradient-based analyses provided complementary evidence on how different cortical systems support predictive modeling and state decoding. Gradients derived from the prediction objective were largest in primary and unimodal cortices, including visual and sensorimotor regions, indicating that accurate time-series forecasting relies heavily on structured fluctuations in early sensory and motor systems. In contrast, gradients from the classification objective concentrated in higher-order association and self-referential regions, such as posterior cingulate cortex, medial prefrontal cortex, and lateral temporal areas, which are known to support autobiographical memory, rumination, and internal mentation. Under the joint objective, gradient maps combined these patterns, showing distributed contributions from both sensory and higher-order networks. Applied to the DIRECT phase II multi-site dataset, gradient magnitude and distribution differed systematically across neurotypical controls, MDD, BD, and SCZ, with affective disorders showing increased weighting in default-mode and memory-related regions and schizophrenia showing altered engagement of attention and frontoparietal systems.

Conclusion

BrainStateNet shows that internally generated mental states—rest, memory, rumination, and distraction—can be decoded from conventional fMRI using a predictive-coding–inspired framework that couples long-range sequence modeling with spatial attention and multi-task learning. By benchmarking multiple sequential architectures and selecting Informer as the backbone, the model achieves high-fidelity BOLD forecasting and leverages predictive dynamics to support accurate mental-state classification. When trained on the Rumination_Beijing dataset and applied directly to the independent Rumination_Suzhou dataset collected on a different scanner in another city, BrainStateNet maintains strong segment- and subject-level performance, demonstrating robust cross-site generalization without subject-specific adaptation. This indicates that predictive modeling can capture stable temporal and spatial regularities shared across cohorts and acquisition environments.

Spatial-attention analyses reveal reproducible network-level signatures of spontaneous mental states. Transitions from rest to memory and rumination are characterized by increased engagement of default-mode and limbic systems, whereas distraction involves widespread integration across attention and frontoparietal networks. These patterns are reflected in both the polarity and distribution of connections, with rest showing greater segregation and distraction showing broader cross-network coupling. The consistency of these effects across pairwise contrasts highlights that spontaneous states recruit distinct large-scale network configurations even in the absence of external stimuli.

Gradient-based analyses provide complementary evidence on how different cortical systems contribute to predictive modeling and classification. Gradients from the prediction objective emphasize primary sensory, sensorimotor, and lower-level unimodal cortices, reflecting the model’s reliance on temporally structured fluctuations for forecasting. In contrast, classification gradients concentrate in higher-order association regions, including posterior cingulate cortex, medial prefrontal cortex, and lateral temporal areas—regions known to support autobiographical memory, self-referential processing, and internally directed thought. Joint-task gradients combine these signatures, yielding distributed maps that integrate both temporal-forecasting and cognitive-state components. Across both Rumination_Suzhou and DIRECT datasets, gradient magnitude and distribution differ systematically between mental states and diagnostic groups, suggesting that the model captures stable representational axes tied to spontaneous cognition and clinical variability.

Applied to large-scale resting-state data in DIRECT phase II, BrainStateNet identifies diagnosis-related differences consistent with known alterations in spontaneous cognition. MDD and BD show increased weighting in regions associated with memory and internally focused thought, while SCZ displays altered engagement of attention and frontoparietal systems. These findings demonstrate that predictive modeling not only enables decoding of cognitive states but also highlights neural dimensions that differentiate psychiatric conditions at scale.

Together, this work establishes a generalizable and interpretable framework for modeling spontaneous thought with fMRI. By aligning predictive-coding principles with modern sequence modeling, BrainStateNet provides both accurate decoding and mechanistic insights, linking internal mental states and diagnostic differences to reproducible spatial-attention patterns and gradient-derived cortical contributions. This approach offers a foundation for large-scale state-based phenotyping and for advancing our understanding of how spontaneous cognition is instantiated in distributed brain dynamics.
