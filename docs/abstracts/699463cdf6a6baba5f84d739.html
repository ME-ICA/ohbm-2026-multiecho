Introduction

Thalamic atrophy is a prominent feature of multiple sclerosis (MS) and is strongly associated with both physical disability and cognitive impairment.1 Consequently, thalamic volume has emerged as a promising imaging biomarker of neurodegeneration and disease progression. However, interpretation at the individual patient level remains challenging, as thalamic volume loss reflects both normal aging and MS-specific pathological processes.2 Currently, age-related normative reference data tailored to the MS population are lacking, limiting the clinical utility of thalamic volumetry.

The development of MS-specific normative atrophy charts could enable age-adjusted interpretation of thalamic volume and support personalized disease monitoring. A major obstacle to this approach is that accurate thalamic segmentation typically relies on high-resolution 3D T1-weighted MRI, which is not routinely acquired in clinical MS imaging. In contrast, FLAIR imaging is widely available but poses technical challenges for reliable deep grey matter segmentation.3

This study therefore addresses two key questions: (1) how can the thalamus be segmented most accurately on FLAIR images in people with MS, and (2) can reliable FLAIR-based thalamic segmentation be leveraged to generate age-related normative atrophy charts for thalamic volume in MS? Answering these questions is an essential step toward translating thalamic volumetry into clinically applicable tools.

Methods

To address the first research question, how to optimally segment the thalamus on FLAIR MRI in people with multiple sclerosis (pwMS), a subset of 100 patients was selected from a research cohort with both 3D T1-weighted and FLAIR scans acquired in the same session. High-quality 3D T1-weighted images were used to establish ground truth thalamic volumes, as thalamic boundaries are clearly delineated on T1 contrast but are poorly visible on FLAIR images.

Ground truth segmentations were generated using automated thalamic segmentation with FSL-FIRST, followed by manual correction. These reference volumes were compared with automated FLAIR-based thalamic segmentations obtained using SynthSeg and DeepGRAI. Agreement between FLAIR-based segmentations and T1-derived ground truth was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC3), with 10.000 bootstrap iterations. The method demonstrating the highest agreement was selected for subsequent analyses.

To address the second research question, whether FLAIR-based thalamic segmentation can be leveraged to create age-related normative atrophy charts, this method was applied to a large clinical MS cohort from Amsterdam UMC, comprising all pwMS who provided consent for research use of their medical data. Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years and availability of at least one brain MRI scan. From nearly 40.000 available clinical MRI scans obtained from approximately 7500 pwMS, rigorous quality control procedures were applied, resulting in a final dataset of approximately 8000 scans from 2400 individuals with valid thalamic segmentations.

Thalamic volumes were extracted from FLAIR images and adjusted for intracranial volume. To explore age-related patterns in thalamic volume, descriptive visualization was performed using locally estimated smoothing (LOESS), stratified by sex and MS subtype. These exploratory analyses informed the planned development of age-related normative thalamic atrophy charts using Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS), which allow flexible modeling of non-linear volume trajectories across adulthood.4 Sex, MS subtype, and disease duration will be included as covariates. Model performance will be evaluated using an 80/20 train–test split, with prediction accuracy assessed through residual analyses and mean percentage error.

Results

For the first research question, automated thalamic segmentation performance on FLAIR MRI differed substantially between the evaluated methods. When compared with manually corrected ground truth volumes derived from 3D T1-weighted scans, SynthSeg demonstrated high agreement, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC3) of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.86-0.90). In contrast, DeepGRAI showed lower agreement with ground truth (ICC3 = 0.75; 95% CI: 0.69-0.79). SynthSeg was robust across scans of varying quality and required no additional preprocessing, supporting its suitability for large-scale application in heterogeneous clinical data. Based on these results, SynthSeg was selected for further analyses.

To explore the second research question, SynthSeg was applied to a large clinical MS cohort from Amsterdam UMC. From nearly 40.000 available FLAIR scans obtained from approximately 7500 people with MS, quality control procedures resulted in a final dataset of 8756 scans from 2628 individuals with valid thalamic segmentations. The cohort had a mean age of 42.3 ± 12.6 years and was predominantly female (68.7%). Relapsing–remitting MS was the most frequent subtype (71.4%), with secondary progressive (12.9%) and primary progressive MS (10.1%) also represented. Longitudinal data were available for 1577 individuals, with a mean follow-up duration of 3.6 ± 4.4 years.

Descriptive visualization of baseline thalamic volume across age revealed a clear age-related decline in normalized thalamic volume. Locally estimated smoothing (LOESS) curves stratified by MS subtype and sex demonstrated differences in both absolute thalamic volume and apparent age-related trajectories. Progressive MS subtypes showed lower thalamic volumes across the age range compared with relapsing-remitting MS, while males consistently exhibited lower thalamic volumes than females across subtypes. Although substantial overlap between groups was observed, marked inter-individual variability was present at all ages.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that accurate thalamic segmentation on routinely acquired FLAIR MRI is feasible in people with multiple sclerosis and can be scaled to large, real-world clinical datasets. Among the evaluated methods, SynthSeg showed high agreement with T1-derived ground truth, supporting its suitability for clinical application where high-resolution T1-weighted imaging is not consistently available.

Descriptive analyses of thalamic volume across a large and heterogeneous MS cohort revealed a clear age-related decline, with substantial variability across individuals, MS subtypes, and sex. These findings underscore that thalamic atrophy in MS cannot be adequately interpreted using a single reference curve and highlight the need for age- and disease-specific contextualization.

Together, these results provide a strong empirical foundation for the development of MS-specific, age-related normative thalamic atrophy charts. Such tools have the potential to facilitate individualized interpretation of thalamic volume, supporting personalized disease monitoring and, ultimately, more informed clinical decision-making. Future work will focus on formal statistical modeling of normative trajectories and validation of these reference charts in independent MS cohorts.
