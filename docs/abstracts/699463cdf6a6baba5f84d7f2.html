Introduction

Effectively regulating attention in the presence of emotional distractors is critical for adaptive behavior and is often impaired in internalizing disorders. Previous studies suggest that threat-related distractors recruit anterior cingulate (ACC) and limbic systems involved in salience detection and emotional processing. However, in many tasks emotional distractors carry task-relevant or facilitating information (e.g., congruent Emotional Stroop trials), leaving open whether threat-related effects reflect emotional salience per se or merely informational relevance. Using a simplified version of the Emotion Interference Paradigm (EFIT) (1) with fully irrelevant emotional distractors, the present study examines load-dependent limbic processing and behavioral, neural, and trial-by-trial carry-over effects of prior emotional distractors.

Methods

Forty-eight healthy adults (mean age = 26.84 ± 7.69 years; 31 female) performed an adapted EFIT during fMRI. Emotional faces (neutral, anger, fear) were overlaid with letter arrays, and participants indicated whether a predefined target letter appeared (low load: identical letters; high load: unique letters). We assessed Load × Emotion Interaction effects on behavioral (reaction time, error rates) and neural markers at whole-brain level and in predefined regions of interest (amygdala and ACC). Threat vs. neutral contrasts in both load conditions were calculated for the current trial and carry-over effects examining how the emotional valence of the previous trial influenced reaction times and neural responses on the next trial, were assessed. 

Results

Robust load × emotion interactions were observed on both behavioral and neural levels. Under low load, task-irrelevant threat stimuli increased neural responses in limbic structures (amygdala) and salience network regions (insula, dorsal ACC/MCC), as well as in visual and somato-motor areas (all pFWE < .05). In contrast, under high load, these effects reversed: threat stimuli led to decreased activation in the salience network (dorsal ACC/MCC), visual processing areas, putamen (pFWE < .05) but not in the amygdala. Behaviorally, longer reaction times were observed for threatening compared to neutral distractors in the high load condition (p = .037, η² = .13), whereas no significant behavioral effect of emotional valence was observed in low load trials (p = .796, η² = .01). Carry-over effects were only observed in the low load condition: Trials following threat stimuli were associated with faster reaction times (p < .001, η² = .30) and reduced activation in the amygdala and thalamus (pFWE < .05) compared to trials following neutral stimuli.

Conclusion

The adapted EFIT reproduces key load-dependent emotional interference effects at both behavioral and neural levels. Under low cognitive load, threat-related distractors engage limbic and salience network regions and exert carry-over effects on subsequent trials, highlighting deep processing of emotional information when resources are available. In line with previous findings, high cognitive load was associated with slower reaction times for threat compared to neutral trials. In contrast to studies employing task-relevant emotional distractors (e.g., congruent vs. incongruent trials), we observed reduced rather than increased activation in task-related networks. By rendering emotional stimuli irrelevant and isolating emotional valence without informational overlap, these findings suggest that under high load emotional distractors interfere with the efficient recruitment of task-related processing rather than eliciting enhanced emotional responses or their suppression. Together, these findings indicate that emotional interference effects are shaped by task relevance and cognitive load, and that adapted EFIT designs can help disentangle these mechanisms in both healthy and clinical populations.
