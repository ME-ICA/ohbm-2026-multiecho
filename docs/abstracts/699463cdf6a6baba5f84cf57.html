Introduction

Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) is an innovative MR technique that is used to non-invasively characterize the mechanical properties of tissue by combining a specialized sequence along with low-frequency vibrations to the head resulting in the generation of elastograms; maps of tissue mechanical properties. The relation of shear stiffness, µ, to specific microscale elements is highly complex, however, is proposed to reflect aspects of tissue composition. 

 Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) – a more ubiquitously studied brain MR technique – can similarly be used to study microstructure. While higher fractional anisotropy (FA) is often interpreted as a simple marker of white matter “integrity” or health, and in gray matter is more equivocal, research shows that the interpretation of this metric may be more nuanced, and rather, influenced by the complex interplay between different microstructural factors. It is therefore challenging to disentangle the individual contributions of various factors – including fiber diameter, fiber density, membrane permeability, directional coherence within fibers, water and glial cell content in the extracellular environment, and myelination - to FA values.  All of these microscale elements are known to restrict diffusion1 making the measure non-specific and challenging to interpret in isolation. 

Together these modalities may provide complementary information and insights that are not evident using either technique in isolation. The aims of this project were to incorporate MRE and DTI measures within both global white and gray matter, the full corpus callosum (CC), cortical gray matter (CGM), and subcortical gray matter (SGM) structures in the brains of healthy young athletes, and to study the association between metrics derived from these modalities. 

Methods

90 athletes (n = 47 male) aged 20-25 (mean ± SD: 23.04 ± 1.69) underwent MRI acquisition (T1w, T2w, MRE, dMRI) and a semi-structured interview assessing demographic and sports-history, including possible head impact exposure in contact-sport athletes. 

Structural images were processed using the FreeSurfer (FS) software (7.10.0) to perform automated anatomical segmentations, excluding skull stripping. Segmentation and parcellation were based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas using the T2w image to improve pial surfaces. All segmentations were visually quality control (QC) rated.

          NIfTI diffusion images were processed using an adapted version of the Psychiatry Neuroimaging Laboratory NiPype; a diffusion-weighted imaging processing pipeline (https://github.com/pnlbwh/pnlNipype). The images were corrected for the influence of motion, EPI distortions, and Eddy current distortions and underwent visual and automated QC using FSL QUAD and SQUAD. Tractography analysis based on an Unscented Kalman Filter was used to recursively construct the white matter fibers in the brain using a two-tensor model and correcting for free water. Finally, employing the whitematteranalysis package and 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2), white matter fibers were parcellated into anatomical white matter tracts based on the ORG-800FC-100HCP atlas. To extract FA values from the CGM, SGM, and cerebral white matter (CWM), DIPY’s FreeWaterTensorModel was used to fit the diffusion tensor and generate free water corrected scalar maps. The b0 image and FA maps were registered to FS space and the function mri_binarize created a mask based on the FS segmentation regions of interest (ROIs), fslmaths applied the mask to the FA maps, and mri_segstats extracted mean FA values for the mask ROIs. 

          Using MATLAB (R2023a) to process the MRE data, the magnitude and phase images were merged to create a complex image averaged across all directions, polarities, and phase offsets. Images were skull stripped, phase unwrapped, and a Fast-Fourier Transform was applied to determine the complex displacement field at each voxel. Scans with an octahedral shear strain signal-to-noise ratio below 3 were excluded. 

To process the CC tract data, the MRE magnitude images were moved to diffusion space. Binary masks of the CC, based on tractography output files, were created using the 3D Slicer cli-modules modeltolabelmap and FiberBundleToLabelMap. For the CGM, SGM, and CWM analysis, a mask based on the T1w and T2w FS output was created, capturing these regions. The mask was registered to the MRE magnitude image. 

          A nonlinear inversion algorithm converted wave displacement fields into mechanical property maps of viscoelastic shear stiffness. A necessary additional step during inversion for the CGM, SGM, and CWM ROIs employed soft prior regularization (SPR) to incorporate anatomical information for smaller brain areas, thus reducing the variability that can arise from contamination by neighboring tissue and CSF. All property maps were visually inspected. 

MRE post-processing was performed in MATLAB. To quantify the average shear stiffness in each CC tract the ROI masks were multiplied with the global shear stiffness map, filtering out any non-CC areas. For all other ROIs the region-specific stiffness map from the SPR inversion was used. The resulting maps were visually inspected (Fig. 1).

All data was analyzed using RStudio (2025.05.1). One-way analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) using type III sum of squares quantified differences between global white and gray matter in stiffness and FA with Tukey’s HSD post hoc pairwise comparisons. All ANCOVAs included age, sex, BMI, years of contact-sport play, and motion between executive frames (extracted from SQUAD and used for DTI data only) as covariates. The false discovery rate was used to correct for multiple comparisons. Pearson’s correlation coefficient quantified the association between mean stiffness and mean FA across segments of the CC, SGM structures, and CGM. 

Results

Global white matter (mean ± SD µ: 3.15 ± 0.15 kPa, FA: 0.51 ± 0.017) was both stiffer (F(1, 174) = 300.73, p < .001) and had a higher FA (F(1, 173) = 26,277.90, p < .001) than global gray matter (mean ± SD µ: 2.78 ± 0.13 kPa, FA: 0.18 ± 0.0076). Across the 7 segments of the CC, there was a strong negative correlation between mean stiffness and mean FA, r(5) = -0.88, t(5) = -4.12, p = 0.009, 95% CI [-0.98, -0.37] and across 28 bilateral regions of CGM there was a moderate positive correlation between mean stiffness and mean FA, r(52) = 0.37, p = 0.006, 95% CI [0.12, 0.58]. There was no correlation between mean stiffness and mean FA across 7 bilateral SGM structures, r(10) = 0.43, t(10) = 1.51, p = 0.16, 95% CI [-0.19, 0.81] (Fig. 2). 

Conclusion

           Global white matter was both stiffer and had a higher FA than global gray matter. This likely reflects the difference in composition between the two tissue types and aligns closely with the literature. Where white matter is composed primarily of highly organized myelinated axons that create a dense and rigid interconnected network, gray matter is more disorganized and composed of cell bodies, dendrites, and unmyelinated fibers2. The highly ordered and densely packed nature of white matter axons both restricts water diffusion making it more anisotropic and, simultaneously, increases isotropic tissue stiffness when compared to gray matter. 

To our knowledge, there is only one other study directly comparing FA and stiffness in white matter, and none in gray matter. Johnson et al. (2013) reported correlations between various MRE-derived isotropic mechanical properties and DTI-derived properties across the CC, suggesting that these modalities reflect differences in axonal microstructure, and emphasizing the need for further studies characterizing these microstructural differences3. In our data there was a negative correlation between stiffness and FA across the white matter of the CC, an association which was flipped in the CGM. This difference in directionality of the correlations may suggest that these measures reflect distinct aspects of tissue microstructure in different tissue types. There is significant microstructural heterogeneity across segments of the CC and regions with higher FA are thought to have more tightly packed and coherently oriented axons, however, this may not necessarily correlate to higher observed isotropic stiffness. For example, axons, but also their myelin sheaths as well as glial cells such as astrocytes and oligodendrocytes, altogether form a mechanically complex unit that influences the way that shear waves propagate through white matter, possibly all contributing to stiffness in our data4. Stiffness and FA may thus provide insights on different aspects of white matter microstructure in this population. In CGM on the other hand, there is much less directional coherence, hence lower FA values when compared to white matter. In CGM, FA reflects the density of both unmyelinated axons and apical dendrites5 which may be also captured by stiffness values. We therefore propose that these modalities reflect complementary, yet unique, information in various tissue types and are sensitive methods in detecting fine microstructural differences within brain tissue. 

There was no correlation between stiffness and FA across the following bilateral SGM structures: the thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, amygdala, and nucleus accumbens; all structures with significant microstructural heterogeneity, including various neuronal types and densities, connectivity, degrees of myelination, and more, which is fundamental to their diverse function. We suggest that MRE and DTI may provide insights into various aspects of microstructure, and despite being sensitive to microstructural heterogeneity, may not be specific, explaining the lack of an association between heterogenous SGM structures. Indeed, the CC and CGM may exhibit more continuous gradients of microstructural variation, and less defined boundaries between different substructures, explaining the observed correlations. More work is needed to understand these associations. 

In conclusion, MRE and DTI in combination are sensitive in the detection of fine microstructural differences not only between different brain tissue types, but also different subcomponents across the same anatomical structures. More research in diverse populations is needed to elucidate this relationship and to investigate its utility in investigating pathologic changes in the brain. Further work should integrate multimodal approaches, using different MR techniques to assist in the interpretation of these metrics.
