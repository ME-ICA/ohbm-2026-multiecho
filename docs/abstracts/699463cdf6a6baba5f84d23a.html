Introduction

Inter-individual variability limits brain activity decoding in population neuroimaging. Anatomical templates reduce morphological differences but overlook functional variability. Functional alignment addresses this by mapping voxelwise correspondences across individuals and enabling the construction of group-level functional templates. Yet these templates remain underused due to unclear guidelines and heterogeneous methods, and they are rarely evaluated beyond movie-watching paradigms. Here, we systematically compare alignment methods and template-building strategies within task decoding, using decoder accuracy as an objective benchmark. Across multiple tasks and datasets, we show that functional templates achieve varying decoding accuracy between alignment methods, and greatly differ in their ability to generalize to unseen subjects.

Methods

We benchmark functional alignment methods for improving between-subject fMRI decoding. Each subject is represented by its task-based activation maps normalized in a common anatomical space. We compare three families of pairwise alignment techniques, Procrustes, Optimal Transport (OT), and Ridge regression, as well as the Shared Response Model (SRM) [1]. To ensure computational tractability and avoid implausible long-range mappings, all methods are applied in a piecewise manner within 400 Schaefer parcels.

We evaluate both pairwise and template-based alignment. Template construction follows an alternating scheme that iteratively estimates subject-to-template mappings and updates a group-average functional template; SRM instead learns a shared low-dimensional latent space. Template and alignment procedures are implemented via our open-source package fmralign.

Experiments are conducted on three diverse datasets: five tasks from the Individual Brain Charting project [2], music-genre responses from StudyForrest [3], and image-evoked responses from CNeuroMod-THINGS [4]. All data are preprocessed with standard fMRI pipelines, single-trial or condition-level z-maps are computed, and all subjects are registered to MNI space.

Performance is assessed with task decoding using a leave-one-subject-out framework. For each alignment method, we test three strategies highlighted in [5]:

(1) In-sample template alignment, using a single population template;

(2) Pairwise alignment, aligning each subject directly to the left-out subject;

(3) Out-of-sample template alignment, building a template from all but the test subject.

Alignment and decoding data are kept disjoint via nested cross-validation, and decoding uses a linear SVM. Across experiments, we compare accuracy, quantify differences between strategies, and visualize how alignment reshapes classifier weight maps.

Results

Across seven decoding tasks, we evaluated four functional alignment methods (Optimal Transport, Procrustes, Ridge regression, and SRM) under three alignment strategies (in-sample template, pairwise, and out-of-sample template). Performance was quantified using leave-one-subject-out decoding accuracy, with statistical comparisons based on corrected paired tests to account for dependence across folds.

1. OT-based templates provide the strongest and most reliable decoding improvements.

Using the standard out-of-sample template strategy, Optimal Transport (OT) consistently outperformed anatomical alignment on every task, yielding the highest gains among template-based methods (see the attached figure). As to other methods, Procrustes and Ridge showed inconsistent behavior and sometimes degraded performance relative to the anatomical baseline. Out-of-sample SRM was at chance level.

2. OT has by far the lowest failure rate.

Failure cases, defined as subjects whose decoding accuracy dropped below anatomical alignment, were far less frequent for OT (5 out of 67 subjects) than for Procrustes (50), Ridge (65), or SRM (70). This highlights that functional alignment can be harmful if applied blindly, and that OT is the most reliable method across subjects and tasks.

3. Pairwise alignment benefits some methods but yields mixed outcomes.

When directly aligning training subjects to the test subject, Ridge benefited most, outperforming its template-based version on five tasks. Procrustes also improved, though to a lesser extent. OT showed more nuanced results: pairwise alignment was advantageous on only two tasks and produced little difference elsewhere, indicating that OT templates already capture much of the subject-specific variability that pairwise mapping is designed to reduce.

4. OT templates show the smallest empirical bias.

Comparing in-sample and out-of-sample templates revealed prominent inclusion bias for Procrustes and Ridge: decoding accuracy increased markedly when the test subject was included during template construction. OT exhibited this bias on only two tasks, and with substantially smaller effect sizes. This demonstrates that OT templates are uniquely robust to the common (but often unavoidable) practice of including all subjects in template estimation.

Conclusion

This work introduces a cross-validated framework for evaluating functional alignment in task decoding, designed to minimize variability beyond inter-subject differences and to operate directly on existing task data without requiring additional alignment scans. By testing a diverse set of tasks and methods, we provide a systematic and practically oriented assessment of alignment strategies under conditions that mirror real neuroimaging workflows.

Across all analyses, Optimal Transport emerges as the most effective and reliable method for template-based alignment. OT templates consistently improve decoding accuracy, show the smallest discrepancy with exhaustive pairwise alignment, and exhibit the lowest failure rate across subjects and tasks. Crucially, OT is also the method that minimizes the inclusion bias when the test subject contributes to template construction. This enables the use of simple, population-level in-sample templates, substantially reducing computational demands without compromising validity.

Taken together, our results motivate two practical recommendations for neuroscientists. When computational constraints or cohort size prohibit pairwise mapping, an OT-based in-sample template provides an efficient, scalable, and interpretable approach with strong decoding performance. More broadly, our findings highlight that functional alignment should be applied carefully, its benefits depend on both the method and the data available, and insufficient data may limit its utility.
