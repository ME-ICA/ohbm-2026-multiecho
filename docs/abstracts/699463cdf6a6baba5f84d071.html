Introduction

Cerebellar strokes account for only 2–3% of ischemic strokes but represent a clinically important and under-studied subgroup. Once considered primarily motor lesions, cerebellar infarcts are now recognized to produce impairments consistent with Cerebellar Cognitive Affective Syndrome (CCAS), affecting executive function, attention, language, and affect.1 Despite this recognition, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying cognitive outcomes after cerebellar stroke remain poorly understood.

Most post-stroke neuroimaging studies emphasize supratentorial lesions studying infarct location and volume, while largely overlooking cerebellar microstructure.2 However, cognitive dysfunction following cerebellar stroke may arise not only from focal infarction, but also from subtle microstructural alterations in normal-appearing cerebellar tissue that are invisible to conventional MRI. Diffusion MRI provides sensitivity structural alterations, yet cerebellar diffusion properties remain understudied relative to supratentorial white matter.

Our group has shown that diffusion-derived measures of normal-appearing tissue, using both conventional tensor metrics and non-Gaussian diffusion models, are sensitive to vascular injury and white matter microstructure. In particular, anomalous diffusion measures derived from the Mittag-Leffler3 formulation capture microstructural heterogeneity beyond Gaussian assumptions and demonstrate sensitivity to white matter organization4 and vascular risk.5

Here, we apply high-resolution, multi-shell diffusion MRI to characterize cerebellar gray and white matter microstructure at post-stroke in cerebellar versus supratentorial stroke patients. We further test whether non-Gaussian diffusion complexity is associated with global cognitive performance. This work aims to characterize structural trajectories that may provide mechanistic insight into post-stroke cognitive dysfunction that is attributable to the cerebellar white and gray matter microstructure.

Methods

Participants: Thirty adults with first-time unilateral ischemic stroke (age: 65.6 ± 12.1 years) were studied at approximately 1 month post-stroke (1M), including 6 cerebellar stroke (CS) and 24 supratentorial stroke (STS) participants. All strokes were confirmed by clinical MRI obtained during acute admission. Cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) at the time of imaging. A subset of participants (4 CS, 10 STS) also underwent follow-up imaging at approximately 6 months post-stroke (6M). All analyses are adjusted for age.

MRI Acquisition: Imaging was performed on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner using a 64-channel head coil. High-resolution T1-weighted images were acquired using an MP-RAGE sequence (TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.94 ms, TI = 900 ms, 1.0 mm isotropic voxels). Diffusion MRI was acquired using a spin-echo echo-planar imaging sequence (TR = 5 s, TE = 95 ms, FOV = 225 × 225 mm², matrix = 150 × 150, 90 slices, 1.5 mm isotropic voxels). A multi-shell diffusion protocol was used with four b-value shells (b = 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000 s/mm²), each with 60 diffusion directions, and eight b = 0 images.

Lesion Masking and Regions of Interest: Lesion masks were manually delineated on acute diffusion images and confirmed by a vascular neurologist. Lesion masks were coregistered to each subject’s T1-weighted image and to standard space for visualization and estimation of a group-level cerebellar infarct probability mask. Normal-appearing cerebellar tissue was defined as cerebellar gray and white matter outside the infarct mask. Cerebellar gray and white matter segmentations for the left and right hemispheres were obtained using FreeSurfer. Prior to statistical analysis, all diffusion maps, cerebellar segmentations, and lesion masks were normalized to ICBM 2009c Nonlinear Asymmetric standard space.

Diffusion Modeling and Metrics: Standard diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics were computed using FSL, including fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD). In addition, we derived a non-Gaussian diffusion complexity metric (C = 1 − α)5 using an anomalous diffusion model based on the Mittag-Leffler function, where α reflects the deviation from Gaussian diffusion behavior.3 Complexity indexes microstructural heterogeneity arising from factors such as cellular packing, membrane barriers, and intra-voxel structural diversity, providing sensitivity beyond conventional DTI metrics.

Statistical Analysis: Group differences (CS vs. STS) in diffusion and complexity metrics were assessed separately for left and right cerebellar gray matter and white matter using general linear models with age as a covariate. Associations between cerebellar diffusion metrics and MoCA scores were evaluated using age-adjusted partial correlations across the full cohort. For the longitudinal subset, within-subject change maps (6M − 1M) were computed and compared between groups to assess differential microstructural trajectories. Voxelwise statistical analyses were performed using FSL randomise with permutation testing and Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement for multiple-comparison correction (p < 0.05, TFCE-corrected).

Results

At 1 month post-stroke, cerebellar stroke (CS) participants showed significant alterations in cerebellar microstructure compared to supratentorial stroke (STS) participants after adjusting for age. MoCA scores did not differ between groups at this time point (two-sample t-test, p = 0.51), indicating comparable global cognitive performance despite divergent lesion locations.

Figure 1 displays axial, coronal, and sagittal cerebellar slices summarizing voxelwise statistical results. Voxels showing a significant CS > STS or CS < STS contrast are overlaid in blue–cyan, while the group-level infarct probability mask for the right cerebellar hemisphere is shown in orange–yellow. In the top row, mean diffusivity revealed widespread CS > STS effects in ipsilesional cerebellar gray matter, with significant clusters observed both within the infarct mask and extending into normal-appearing tissue. Furthermore, axial and radial diffusivity were also significantly elevated in CS. These findings indicate microstructural alterations beyond visibly injured regions. In the middle row, anomalous diffusion modeling revealed higher α values in CS, corresponding to reduced diffusion complexity within the infarct volume and extending beyond it. Notably, reduced complexity was also observed in contralesional cerebellar gray matter, revealing bilateral microstructural differences not captured by conventional diffusivity metrics. In the bottom row, voxelwise partial correlations demonstrated that diffusion complexity—but not Gaussian diffusivity—was significantly associated with MoCA, with significant clusters observed bilaterally in both cerebellar gray and white matter, indicating distributed associations between cerebellar microstructure and cognitive performance across the cohort.

Finally, in the longitudinal subset with imaging at 1 and 6 months post-stroke, voxelwise analyses of within-subject change (6M − 1M) revealed differential microstructural trajectories between groups. In ipsilesional gray matter, CS participants showed greater increases in diffusivity over time relative to STS, including axial, mean, and radial diffusivity (AD: p = 0.034, t = 2.29; MD: p = 0.029, t = 2.48; RD: p = 0.020, t = 2.61). Notably, axial diffusivity increased in CS while decreasing in STS, whereas MD and RD increased in both groups but to a greater extent in CS. In contrast, for non-Gaussian diffusion measures in contralesional white matter, STS participants exhibited a larger reduction in α over time compared to CS (p = 0.038, t = 3.29), corresponding to a greater increase in diffusion complexity in STS and a comparatively attenuated complexity change in CS.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that cerebellar stroke is associated with early and spatially extensive microstructural alterations in normal-appearing cerebellar tissue that differ systematically from those observed following supratentorial stroke. At 1 month post-stroke, elevated diffusivity within cerebellar gray matter—extending beyond the infarct—suggests microstructural injury outside the visibly damaged region.

Anomalous diffusion modeling revealed higher α and correspondingly reduced diffusion complexity in cerebellar stroke participants within the infarct and in contralesional cerebellar gray matter, indicating bilateral reductions in microstructural heterogeneity not captured by conventional diffusion metrics. Reduced complexity is consistent with tissue simplification following neural injury, potentially reflecting processes such as axonal loss, altered cellular organization, or glial responses.4 These findings support a network-level impact of cerebellar stroke, rather than a purely focal injury model.1

Longitudinal analyses further indicated divergent microstructural trajectories between groups. Cerebellar stroke participants exhibited greater increases in diffusivity over time within ipsilesional cerebellar gray matter, suggesting ongoing degeneration or remodeling. In contrast, supratentorial stroke participants showed larger increases in diffusion complexity over time in contralesional cerebellar white matter, whereas complexity change was attenuated in cerebellar stroke, consistent with blunted microstructural reorganization following cerebellar injury. These findings emphasize the importance of characterizing normal appearing tissue5 which may undergo diffuse changes following vascular injury.

Importantly, diffusion complexity was associated with MoCA across both cerebellar gray and white matter, despite comparable cognitive performance between groups at 1 month. This suggests that preserved microstructural heterogeneity may support cognitive function and that non-Gaussian diffusion metrics capture cognition-relevant tissue properties not reflected by standard diffusion measures.

Together, these findings highlight diffusion complexity as a sensitive biomarker of cerebellar microstructural integrity and recovery. By revealing injury-related reductions in complexity, differential recovery trajectories, and cognition-relevant microstructural variation, this work underscores the value of advanced diffusion modeling for understanding cerebellar contributions to post-stroke cognitive outcomes and Cerebellar Cognitive Affective Syndrome.
