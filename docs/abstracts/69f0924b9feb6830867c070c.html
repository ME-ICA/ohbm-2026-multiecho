Introduction

Deaf individuals lack access to auditory phonology and therefore rely more heavily on visual-orthographic information to develop proficient reading skills. Although a substantial literature has shown that deaf readers can achieve reading proficiency comparable to hearing peers, the mechanisms that support their reading success remain less well understood. In particular, the mechanisms underlying their orthographic coding—particularly the representation of letter position versus identity—remain unclear. It is also unknown whether enhanced visual processing in deaf readers is language-specific or extends to other symbolic systems such as digits, which share similar visual complexity but carry no linguistic meaning. Addressing these gaps is essential for understanding how reading is supported when phonological resources are limited or absent.

The present study examined two key questions. First, we investigated whether auditory deprivation leads to compensatory mechanisms that result in hyper-precise orthographic coding, enabling more sensitive discrimination of position-specific letter information. Second, we examined whether this increased sensitivity is specific to the orthographic domain or generalizes to non-linguistic symbolic processing. Based on prior behavioral work and evidence for enhanced visuospatial abilities in deaf individuals1, we hypothesized that deaf readers would exhibit superior orthographic processing, particularly for letter-position encoding, and that this advantage might extend to digit processing in ways not observed in hearing readers.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-five skilled deaf readers participated in the fMRI experiment. All participants were either congenitally deaf or acquired deafness before the age of three. They were native or highly proficient users of Spanish Sign Language (LSE) and reported little to no residual hearing. All participants were proficient readers of written Spanish. Lexical proficiency was assessed using the Lextale-Esp test2, and only individuals scoring above 80 out of 100 were included.

fMRI Experiment

Functional (TR=1s) and structural MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner equipped with a 64-channel head coil. An event-related same–different matching task3 was employed using visually presented four-character strings composed of either letters or digits. Letter strings were constructed from uppercase consonants (G, N, D, K, F, T, S, B, L), and digit strings were composed of numbers 1–9. Each trial included a reference and a target string belonging to one of three conditions: Same (identical strings; e.g., KGBT–KGBT), Transposed-character (two internal characters swapped; e.g., KGBT–KBGT), or Replaced-character (two internal characters replaced; e.g., KGBT–KPRT). For each task block, the stimulus set comprised 180 same trials, 90 transposed-character trials, and 90 replaced-character trials. Trials began with a fixation cross (300ms), followed by the reference stimulus (350ms) and the target stimulus (350ms), with inter-trial jitters ranging from 1 to 6s. Participants responded within a 1s response window, indicating whether the two strings were identical or different. Reference and target stimuli were presented slightly above and below the central fixation point, respectively, to minimize visual overlap. Trial order and jitter timing were optimized using Optseq II, and letter and digit blocks were randomized across participants.

Behavioral Analysis

Reaction times (RTs) from correct trials were analyzed, excluding values exceeding ±3 standard deviations for each participant, run, and condition. Runs with excessive omissions were excluded. Group-level outliers in accuracy and RT were identified using ±3 standard deviation criteria and manually inspected. Mixed-effects ANOVAs were conducted on error rates and RTs. Transposition costs were computed as the difference between transposed-character and replaced-character conditions, and paired-sample t-tests were used to compare transposition costs across character types.

fMRI Analysis

fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep (v25.1.1), with NORDIC denoising applied prior to reduce thermal noise. Preprocessing steps included  brain segmentation, motion correction, registration and normalization to MNI space. First-level GLMs were implemented in AFNI toolbox, modeling Same, Transposed, and Replaced target conditions and convolving them with the HRF function. Functional data were scaled to percent signal change, and the first six volumes of each run were discarded. Motion parameters, motion outliers (FD>0.5 mm), and white matter and CSF signals were included as nuisance regressors.To align with a previous study3 in hearing participants to enable inspection of group differences, the processing pipeline was replicated. Group-level analyses employed a flexible factorial model with character type (letters, digits) and condition (transposed, replaced) as fixed effects and subject as a random factor. First, the interaction effect of  task and conditions and their main effect were inspected. Then, statistical t-map contrasting transposition versus replacement across tasks were thresholded at a cluster-level p < 0.001 (cluster size > 100). To constrain group analyses in reliable transposition effects while avoiding overly conservative whole-brain correction, significant clusters were extracted and masked using the AAL atlas to define anatomical boundaries. Within this mask, interaction effects were tested to identify transposition-cost effects specific to orthographic position processing.

Results

Behavior

In the letter task, mean reaction times (RTs) differed reliably across conditions. Participants responded fastest in the Same condition (M = 654.83 ms, SE = 38.64), followed by the Replaced condition (M = 688.64 ms, SE = 41.53), and slowest in the Transposed condition (M = 744.07 ms, SE = 36.86). This pattern yielded a substantial transposition cost of 73.33 ms (SE = 24.46), reflecting increased processing demands when internal letter order was disrupted while character identity was preserved. Error rates mirrored the RT findings. Performance was highly accurate in the Same (M = 5.66%, SE = 1.06) and Replaced (M = 5.02%, SE = 1.27) conditions, whereas the Transposed condition elicited markedly higher error rates (M = 18.00%, SE = 2.61). The resulting transposition cost in accuracy was 12.97% (SE = 2.30), indicating a reliable decrease in performance specifically attributable to positional uncertainty.

A repeated-measures ANOVA on error rates revealed a significant interaction between task (letter, digit) and condition (Same, Replaced, Transposed; F(2,48) = 12.79, p < 0.001). Post hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests showed that, within each task, the Transposed condition differed significantly from both Same and Replaced conditions, whereas no reliable difference emerged between Same and Replaced (all FDR-corrected p < 0.05). Between-task comparisons further revealed significant differences for the Same condition (t = 2.37, FDR q < 0.05) and the Transposed condition (t = 3.92, FDR q < 0.05), but not for the Replaced condition, suggesting that positional manipulation selectively impacts orthographic processing. For RTs, repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant main effects of task (F(2,48) = 29.26, p < 0.001) and condition (F(2,48) = 23.60, p < 0.001), with no interaction. Post hoc analyses confirmed significant differences among all condition pairs within each task and significant task differences across all conditions (all FDR-corrected q < 0.01). Importantly, transposition costs differed significantly between letter and digit tasks for error rates (t = 3.43, p < 0.01), but not for RTs.

fMRI Results

Contrast between transposition and replacement in either letter or digit task revealed overlapping neural substrates more sensitive to character position than identity. Significant activations (cluster-level inference p < 0.001) were observed in bilateral inferior parietal gyri, bilateral inferior frontal gyrus, right medial frontal gyrus, and left middle temporal gyrus. In addition to these shared regions, the letter task uniquely recruited subcortical and cerebellar regions, including the thalamus and cerebellum, suggesting neural engagement of language-specific processing. Whole-brain voxelwise interaction analyses did not reveal suprathreshold effects at FDR-corrected q < 0.05.

Across character types, the transposition cost effect (Transposed > Replaced; FDR q < 0.05) elicited widespread activations encompassing inferior and superior parietal cortex, inferior frontal gyrus and insula, inferior temporal cortex, precentral gyrus, and right middle frontal gyrus. To further isolate regions associated with transposition cost effects while mitigating the multiple-comparison burden, anatomically constrained cluster masks were generated for bilateral angular gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus (pars opercularis, triangularis, and orbitalis), insula, superior and middle occipital cortex, superior and inferior parietal cortex, supramarginal gyrus, and inferior temporal cortex. Within these masks, direct comparisons revealed significantly greater transposition-related activation for letters than digits in the left inferior parietal cortex and right inferior frontal gyrus (pars opercularis; cluster-level p < 0.05). Notably, the effect in the left inferior parietal cortex survived FDR correction (q < 0.05).

Conclusion

Deaf readers demonstrated sensitivity to positional and identity coding like hearing readers1,3, with both transpositions and replacements cost more cognitive effort for letters than digits. These results indicate that letter recognition depends critically on precise positional encoding, and disruptions to serial order could potentially interfere with orthographic encoding, consistent with prior findings emphasizing the position in fluent word reading. 

Although positional changes affected both character types, letters showed a stronger reliance on serial-order information, whereas digits appeared to use a more flexible strategy. This dissociation supports the view that multiple position-coding mechanisms contribute to visual character processing, including a specialized system for letter strings that enables fine-grained orthographic representations. Reaction time further confirmed measurable position processing costs; however, the cost was disproportionately larger for letter. 

At the whole-brain level, transposition relative to replacement engaged a distributed fronto-parietal–occipitotemporal network for both letter and digit processing. Across character types, transposition costs recruited bilateral inferior and superior parietal cortices, inferior/middle frontal gyrus, insula, inferior temporal cortex and precentral regions. This pattern indicates the involvement of attentional control and visuospatial integration during position processing. The substantial overlap between letter and digit tasks suggests that transposition effects in deaf readers are partially supported by domain-general mechanisms for spatial order processing.

Critically, cluster model revealed letter-specific enhancement within the left inferior parietal cortex and the right inferior frontal gyrus. This pattern contrasts with previous findings in hearing reader3, which emphasized the left superior parietal cortex as a primary locus of orthographic position coding and was closely related to behavior. In the present deaf group, no significant relationships between percent signal change in clusters and behavioral performance were observed, and orthographic sensitivity was instead centered in left inferior parietal region. This region has been implicated in visuospatial integration, symbolic processing, and grapheme-level representations, suggesting that orthographic position encoding in deaf readers may rely more strongly on visualspatially mediated mechanisms.

This divergence likely reflects group-specific neurocognitive adaptations associated with deafness and sign language experience. Deaf signers are known to exhibit enhanced visuospatial attention and working memory, which may alter reading processing. Consequently, orthographic position processing in deaf readers may be supported by partially reorganized neural pathways that prioritize visuospatial computations. The weak interaction effects under conservative correction further indicates that position processing at sublexical level is subtle and spatially constrained. Additional recruitment of cerebellar and thalamic regions during letter processing may reflect increased demands on sequencing when orthographic order is disrupted.

In summary, the present findings demonstrate that skilled deaf readers show robust behavioral and neural sensitivity to orthographic position, supported by a largely overlapping but partially reorganized reading network relative to hearing readers. The prominent role of the left inferior parietal cortex underscores the flexibility of the reading system and suggests that orthographic representations can be shaped by linguistic experience independent of auditory phonology. These results provide novel evidence that sign language experience and enhanced visuospatial processing modulate sublexical language organization in the brain, offering important insights into the modality-independent and experience-dependent nature of reading networks.
