Introduction

fMRI (functional MRI) has emerged as a powerful tool for measuring brain activity, enabling scientists to explore active brain areas during various functions. However, a human can spend only limited time inside an fMRI machine, and different people’s brain functionality differs significantly. This results in fMRI datasets too small to span the huge space of brain functionality or visual stimuli.

Image-to-fMRI encoding models, which predict fMRI responses to natural images, have greatly advanced the field. However, these models are primarily subject-specific and machine-specific, requiring extensive individual data for effective training. This limits practical use and prevents leveraging cross-subject data. Previous attempts to create multi-subject encoders had limited success.

In this paper, we propose a Universal Brain-Encoder, which trains jointly on data from many different subjects, datasets, and machines (e.g., 3-Tesla, 7-Tesla). What makes this possible is our new voxel-centric architecture, which learns a unique "voxel-embedding" per brain-voxel. Our Encoder trains to predict the response of each brain-voxel on every image by directly computing the cross-attention between the brain-voxel embedding and multi-level deep image features. This allows the functional role of each brain-voxel to naturally emerge. Our approach contrasts with previous fMRI-centric methods that treat each scan as a single entity. They can thus exploit only shared information of the entire fMRI across different scans. Our approach builds on the observation that similarity between individual voxels, both within & across brains, is more likely to occur than similarity between entire fMRI scans.

We show the power of this approach to: (i) combine data from multiple subjects (a "Crowd of Brains") to improve individual encoding; (ii) enable Transfer-Learning across subjects and machines with few training examples; (iii) providing a new way to explore the brain via the learned voxel embeddings.

Methods

An overview of our Universal Encoder is provided in Fig. 1. Our voxel-centric Encoder trains to predict the fMRI response of each individual brain-voxel on any input image, by aggregating the cross-attention between the brain-voxel embedding and multi-level deep-features of the image. Each brain voxel (of every subject) has a corresponding voxel embedding. This embedding is merely a vector of size 256, which learns to capture what this brain-voxel is sensitive to in images: whether it attends to low-level image features or to high-level ones; whether it cares about the position of the feature in the image or not; etc. This voxel-embedding is initialized randomly, and is optimized end-to-end during training.

Other than the voxel-specific embedding vectors, all other network weights are shared across all brain-voxels of all subjects. The per-voxel embedding puts a focus on individual voxel characteristics, independent of subject identity or fMRI dataset. This strategy provides several unique benefits: (i) The functional role of each brain-voxel naturally emerges. (ii) Being voxel-centric, the Universal Brain-Encoder architecture is indifferent to the number of voxels per fMRI scan, which enables joint training on data from fMRI machines of different scanning resolutions (e.g., 3T, 4T, 7T). (iii) Once trained, Transfer-Learning to a new subject or a new fMRI dataset/machine requires only a few new training examples.

Given any image, our encoder extracts image features (from low-level to high-level) using our feature extraction block (DINO-v2), allowing each brain-voxel to attend to the appropriate semantic levels of features that align with its functionality. These image features, along with a specific voxel-embedding, are processed through a cross-attention block, to effectively integrate and predict the voxel's activation in response to the given image. Note that this architecture is indifferent to the number of brain-voxels in each fMRI scan, hence applies to any fMRI scan.

Our training process optimizes all 3 components simultaneously -- the voxel-specific embedding, the feature extraction block, and the cross-attention block -- with the sole goal of predicting the voxel-specific fMRI response to the input image. Each training batch contains randomly sampled voxels from multiple brains. This joint learning framework develops meaningful voxel embeddings that not only improve voxel response prediction, but also implicitly `encodes' its functional role in the brain. Our encoder and all associated weights are shared by all brain-voxels (for all subjects/datasets/machines), differing only in the per-voxel embeddings. This design ensures that each brain-voxel embedding is determined by its functional role, rather than by its physical location in the brain or the subject's identity. The shared voxel embedding space supports integration of information across different voxels (whether within a single brain or across different brains). Importantly, our approach does not require subjects to view the same images, nor to be scanned on the same machine. This allows us to integrate information from many fMRI datasets collected by different groups around the globe!

We further demonstrate the ability of our Encoder to learn the functional role of each brain-voxel, and map functionally-similar brain-voxels (within the same brain, and across different brains) to nearby voxel-embeddings. This provides a powerful tool to explore the human brain on huge amounts of data collectively acquired by many brains, thus discovering new functional brain regions.

Results

We evaluate the Universal-Encoder's ability to integrate data from multiple subjects and datasets. First, we demonstrate that jointly training on multiple subjects (exploiting the union of training sets) exceeds the performance of any individual subject. Second, we show that old fMRI datasets can be significantly improved by leveraging a new high-quality 7 Tesla dataset. Finally, we show that a pre-trained Universal-Encoder adapts to new subjects with minimal training data via Transfer-Learning.

Data & Datasets: We use 3 public fMRI datasets: (i) VIM1 ^1: ~1750 train &120 test grayscale images, 4T fMRI. (ii) fMRI-on-ImageNet ^2: 1200 train & 50 test ImageNet images, 3T fMRI (5 subjects). (iii) Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) ^3: 7T dataset with 8 subjects, each viewing ~9000 unique subject-specific images & ~1000 shared test images. This results in ~73,000 total training images from COCO.

Evaluation Metrics: (i) Pearson Correlation (per voxel): Normalized correlation between predicted and ground-truth fMRI activations. (ii) Image Retrieval (per image): We identify the correct Test-image for a fMRI scan ("Query") out of N candidates. We search for the Nearest-Neighbor of the test-fMRI among predicted fMRIs. Results are reported as Top-1 & Top-5 accuracy.

The Wisdom of a "Crowd of Brains": We demonstrate the ability to exploit data from multiple subjects without shared stimuli using the 7T NSD dataset. We trained on the union of all 8 subject-specific images and tested on the 1000 shared images. We compared 3 models: (i) Baseline single-subject encoder (^4), (ii) Universal-Encoder (single subject), and (iii) Universal-Encoder (multiple subjects). Results (Fig. 2a) show the Multi-Subject Universal-Encoder consistently outperforms subject-specific models. Retrieval results (Fig. 2b) confirm this superiority in both Top-1 & Top-5 accuracy. This demonstrates the model effectively aggregates data from multiple subjects to enhance individual performance.

The Wisdom of a "Crowd of Datasets": The Universal-Encoder can combine datasets with different resolutions and image domains. We trained the encoder jointly on the high-quality NSD dataset and lower-quality datasets (VIM1 and fMRI-on-ImageNet). Results (Fig. 2c) show that adding data from the NSD significantly enhances encoding performance on the older, lower-resolution datasets. The multi-dataset model outperformed both single-subject baselines and models trained only on their own dataset.

Transfer-Learning to New Subjects: We show that training from scratch is not needed for new subjects. We pre-trained the Universal-Encoder on 6 NSD subjects, froze the weights, and optimized only the 256-dimensional Voxel-Embeddings for new subjects. Results (Fig. 2d) show that Transfer-Learning significantly outperforms models trained from scratch. Remarkably, using as few as 100 subject-specific training examples with transfer learning yielded better results than a subject-specific model trained on its entire training set.

Exploring the Brain using Voxel-Embeddings: Our encoder maps functionally similar voxels to similar voxel-embeddings, facilitating brain exploration. Unlike standard parcellations based on anatomical alignment, our approach is functionally driven. We applied k-means clustering to voxel embeddings of NSD subjects to detect shared functional regions. We identified the functional role of voxel-clusters by examining images with the highest cluster activation. Fig. 2e shows distinct clusters for Food, Faces, Text, and Outdoor-Scenes. These shared functional regions emerged automatically across subjects without anatomical alignment. We also detected fine-grained sub-regions, such as distinct "Indoor" vs. "Outdoor" clusters within the PPA brain region (Fig. 2f). This suggests voxel-embeddings capture meaningful functional roles and offer a powerful tool for discovering shared and unique brain functionalities.

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced the first-ever Universal Image-to-fMRI Brain-Encoder, a unified framework capable of jointly training on diverse fMRI datasets acquired across different years, stimuli, scanners (e.g., 3T, 4T, 7T), and subject populations. This achievement is made possible by shifting from the traditional "fMRI-centric" paradigm, which treats scans as monolithic entities, to a novel "voxel-centric" architecture. Our approach builds on the fundamental observation that functional similarity is far more likely to occur between individual voxels (both within and across brains) than between entire fMRI scans. By assigning a uniquely learned "voxel-embedding" to every brain voxel, our model captures specific local functionalities while effectively leveraging shared patterns across a vast "crowd of brains."

We demonstrated that this universal approach significantly outperforms subject-specific models and allows high-quality 7T data to boost the performance of legacy lower-resolution datasets. Furthermore, we showed that the learned voxel-embeddings naturally organize into semantically meaningful clusters, offering a powerful new tool for exploring shared brain functionality without the constraints of anatomical alignment.

Beyond immediate performance gains, this framework opens several critical avenues for future research. First, the ability to jointly train on massive, heterogeneous datasets paves the way for the development of "Foundation Models" for the human brain, creating models that aggregate knowledge from thousands of diverse scans to learn general principles of neural processing. Second, our efficient Transfer-Learning capabilities address a major bottleneck in typical neuroscience research: the scarcity of data per subject. By adapting to new subjects with as few as 100 training examples, this approach makes high-performance encoding models accessible for studies with limited scanning time or for populations where long data-collection sessions are impossible. Finally, the shared voxel-embedding space offers a promising direction for multi-subject analysis without shared input. It allows researchers to align and compare brain activity across subjects who never viewed the same stimuli, effectively bypassing the historical need for rigid experimental controls or shared anatomical mapping.
