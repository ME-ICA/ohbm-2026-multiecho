Introduction

Diffusion imaging (dMRI) offers promising biomarkers for predicting Parkinson’s Disease (PD) progression by modeling the microstructure of the white matter tissue.^1,2 Recently, the freewater corrected diffusion tensor imaging (fwDTI) has been identified as a potentially useful biomarker of PD progression.^3 fwDTI is a modification of a DTI model (the 3x3 Diffusion Tensor Imaging) that estimates an additional “free water” component in addition to the diffusion tensor.^2 This model describes both the general orientation of axons as well as the amount of unconstrained movement within the same space. The amount of unconstrained free water is hypothesized to correspond to changes in the brain associated with neurodegeneration. However, this finding has proven challenging to reproduce.^1,2

One reason for this may be differences in preprocessing the diffusion data. Analytical flexibility (AF) is defined as the heterogeneity in results that arises when different - but similar in their goals - computational protocols are applied to analyze the same data, stemming from variations in software packages, specific algorithms, and processing parameters (e.g., denoising algorithms, motion correction strategies). Ultimately, a deeper understanding of how free water (FW) estimation varies with data and pipeline processing decisions would help advance PD imaging research based on this biomarker. In this work, we explore FW estimation in the Pardinson’s Progression Marker Initiative (PPMI) dataset using two pipelines and two fwDTI models, providing preliminary information on the impact of AF on the robustness of FW as a diagnostic and progression biomarker of PD. In particular, we estimate how the correlation of FW in PD-related brain regions varies with pipeline choice. The goal is to explore the variability of FW estimates induced by preprocessing decisions compared to other sources of variation within the data.

Methods

The Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI, N=452 baseline scans)^1 was processed through Tractoflow^3 (2.4.2) and QSIPrep^4 (0.23.0). Subjects with multi-shell data (b = 700, 1000, 2000; 64 directions per shell) were used from the PPMI sample to provide the highest quality information to the subsequent DTI and fwDTI models. The sample consists of a relatively equal male:female ratio (222:195) overall with the same average age (66.72+/- 6.92), but over twice as many men are diagnosed with PD (80:35). For clinical measures, the MOCA has an average score of 26.95 +/- 2.32 while the UPDRS-III has an average of 10.01 +/- 10.85. Only the baseline scans were used for this analysis, but the longitudinal scans are being processed as well to explore the temporal variation in future work. These pipelines perform an equivalent series of denoising operations, broadly consisting of: denoising, susceptibility field removal, motion and eddy current removal, bias field correction, and normalization. Coregistration and basic alignment of the diffusion sequence to the T1w structural image were also performed. Standard QC metrics were estimated based on the outputs of each pipeline's standard steps. Additionally, SNR was estimated from each pipeline to compare performance directly on the corrected data.

We also investigated two models for fwDTI parameterization implemented in AMICO and DIPY on each of the preprocessing pipelines.^5 A regular diffusion tensor model (DTI) was fit as well for reference. fwDTI is a bi-compartmental model designed to distinguish diffusion originating from tissue microstructure from the isotropic diffusion arising from Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF), thereby correcting standard DTI metrics for extra-cellular isotropic signal.^2 Conventional DTI metrics conflate changes in extracellular fluid with tissue damage, whereas compartmental modeling isolates the FW effect, enabling earlier detection of neurodegeneration.^3 This additional parameter has been shown to improve the correspondence of fwDTI features with changes in cognitive status and progression of degenerative conditions.

ROIs were taken from the JHU DTI atlas (Figure2) and a curated collection of additional cortical, subcortical, and brainstem regions from different templates and algorithms that have been associated with PD (PD-Atlas).^2 All labels exist in MNI space, so a combination of a linear / non-linear registration of the coregistered T1w structural image to template space was used to estimate the alignment of the cortical labels. Labels were moved from template space to subject space so average values of the DTI and fwDTI parameter maps could be extracted for analysis. Visual QC of the alignments was used to verify good correspondence of the transformed template regions to the subjects. Many of the ROIs important to PD are small and challenging to map through standard templates to individuals.

Results

The correlation of the PD-Atlas regions FA between QSIPrep and TractoFlow is high when averaging across regions (FA R=0.763; DIPY fwFA R=0.842; AMICO fwFA R=0.835). There is considerable variation in the correlation between pipelines across different regions (min R=0.007 in the Right Raphe Magnus / max R=0.913 in the Left Posterior Middle Temporal Gyrus). The JHU Atlas and the PD-Atlas regions showed a similar range of variability. FA and fwFA for each region of these two atlases are shown in Figure 2a. Other features (MD, FW, etc.) have similar relationships across all PD-Atlas regions (DIPY FW R=0.769; AMICO FW R=0.772). Importantly, the strength of the relationship between pipelines does not correspond with ROI size. The correlation between fwFA of the DIPY and AMICO models for each pipeline is higher than the corresponding relationship between preprocessing pipelines (QSIPrep R=0.957; TractoFlow R=0.965 across PD-Atlas regions). The variation between individual ROIs is lower overall (min R=0.660 in the Right Raphe Magnus / max R=0.988 in the Left MTG-Posterior) than when comparing preprocessing features. Figure 2b shows this comparison for the PD-Atlas ROIs. 

fwDTI parameters for every pipeline-model combination were correlated with the MOCA and UPDRS-III scores to see if a specific neuroimaging feature was related to standard clinical measures. Correlations were calculated for each ROI. The correlation of FW with MOCA was weak with no observation above R=|0.20|. The strongest relationship between MOCA and a pipeline x model combination was in the Right Precuneus (QSIPrep x DIPY=-0.192; QSIPrep x AMICO=-0.178; TractoFlow x DIPY=-0.179; TractoFlow x AMICO=-0.178). The correlation between FA and FW with the UPDRS-III score was similarly small (R<|0.20|), with the most prominent relationship being between FW and UPDRS-III in the Dorsal Motor Nucleus of Vagus (DMV) (QSIPrep x DIPY=-0.150; QSIPrep x AMICO=-0.109; TractoFlow x DIPY=-0.155; TractoFlow x AMICO=-0.106).

Overall, the relationships between these ROIs and the clinical measures were very weak. The 10th, 50th, and 90th quartile values for the correlation between QSIPrep DIPY fwFA and MOCA were R=[-0.058, 0.015, 0.058] and for TractoFlow DIPY fwFA and MOCA R=[-0.067, 0.024, 0.069]. The same quartile values for the correlation between QSIPrep AMICO fwFA and MOCA were R=[-0.038, 0.014, 0.086] and for TractoFlow AMICO fwFA and MOCA R=[-0.050, 0.020, 0.080]. The correlations of fwFA with MOCA found no strong relationship within the JHU DTI or PD-Atlas, with all the correlations being below R=|0.15|. 

The 10th, 50th, and 90th quartile values for the correlation between QSIPrep DIPY fwFA and UPDRS-III were R=[-0.071, 0.028, 0.099] and for TractoFlow DIPY fwFA and UPDRS-III R=[-0.053, 0.014, 0.101]. The same quartile values for the correlation between QSIPrep AMICO fwFA and UPDRS-III were R=[-0.062, -0.021, 0.075] and for TractoFlow AMICO fwFA and UPDRS-III R=[-0.078, 0.045, 0.122]. The correlation of fwFA with UPDRS-III found similarly weak relationships within the JHU DTI and PD-Atlas, with all correlations being below R=|0.20|.

The 10th, 50th, and 90th quartile values for the correlation between QSIPrep DIPY FW and MOCA were R=[-0.124, -0.056, 0.012] and for TractoFlow DIPY FW and MOCA R=[-0.132, -0.059, 0.017]. The same quartile values for the correlation between QSIPrep AMICO FW and MOCA were R=[-0.152, -0.071, 0.012] and for TractoFlow AMICO FW and MOCA R=[-0.167, -0.069, 0.037]. The same quartile values for the correlation between QSIPrep DIPY FW and UPDRS-III were R=[-0.109, -0.008, 0.084] and for TractoFlow DIPY FW and UPDRS-III R=[-0.083, -0.011, 0.035. The same quartile values for the correlation between QSIPrep AMICO FW and UPDRS-III were R=[-0.090, 0.015, 0.079] and for TractoFlow AMICO FW and UPDRS-III R=[-0.067, -0.007, 0.068]. The FW estimates showed a similar pattern of variability with fwFA, but with the sign inverted.

Conclusion

We assessed the analytic flexibility of diffusion biomarkers for Parkinson's Disease research and found that Tractoflow and QSIprep lead to different estimates of signal quality that vary by region. For example, the range in correlations between specific ROIs FW between UPDRS-III for the TractoFlow preprocessed DIPY fwDTI model is R=[-0.155 - 0.061], while the same correlation between QSIPrep preprocessed DIPY fwDTI model is R=[-0.150, 0.151]. However, these differences do not appear to make a large impact on the resulting interpretation of any clinical comparison. We show that freewater corrected diffusion tensors are more similar, despite substantial differences in how the models are estimated. While both pipelines (AMICO and DIPY) estimate an equivalent additional parameter, they do so with different assumptions. AMICO uses a fixed parameter of water transfer to estimate the radius of the freewater compartment. In contrast, DIPY requires multi-shell data to compute an estimate of this parameter based on differences in the observed shells to estimate the same freewater compartment. The correlation between models is notably higher than between preprocessing pipelines (R>0.95) and shows less variability between regions.

The correspondence between all model parameters and the observed clinical features is weak overall. The Prodromal cohort compared to the PD cohort is over-represented by nearly 2:1 Prodromal to PD. The clinical metrics, like UPDRS-III, have a strong positive skew, with many in the PD group having only mildly elevated expression. This may be due to selecting baseline observations for analysis. The strongest relationships were found in the literature: the precuneus is associated with executive functioning and is tied to the cognitive function metric, while the DMV is tied to a measure of motor function. Further analyses are planned to take into account additional demographic composition, clinical features, and more targeted ROIs. We hope to use the variability of these estimations, as well as other advanced image derived phenotypes, to improve the prediction of disease progression. This includes the substantia nigra (SN), pars compacta (SNc), and the ventral tegmental area (VTA). We are collaborating on estimating a cutting edge deep-learning segmentation of these regions on this dataset.

The pervasive nature of pipeline variability underscores that transparency and standardization are prerequisites for trustworthy neuroimaging research. Given that outcome validity is highly sensitive to methodological choices, particularly the use of different software packages, strict adherence to reproducible computational practices is necessary for reliable, generalizable research findings. In this work, we make use of the Nipoppy project to track the exact computations and their run time parameters. This exploration is necessary to clarify which analysis decisions and interpretation of findings for PD are dependent on analysis pipelines.
