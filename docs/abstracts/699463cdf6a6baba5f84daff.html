Introduction

Previous studies have shown that when listening to a story, the listeners exhibit brain activity patterns similar to those of the storyteller, and that this neural similarity is associated with better comprehension. Such storyteller–listener neural coupling is observed across broadly distributed brain regions, largely overlapping with the default mode network. The present fMRI study investigates dyadic neural coupling during a unique form of verbal communication: hypnosis. 

Methods

To evaluate the effectiveness of hypnotic instruction, participants viewed a movie with a narrative twist (The Sixth Sense). At the end of the first viewing, participants gained the insight that the main character, a psychotherapist, had been dead since the beginning of the movie, fundamentally altering the interpretation of other characters’ behaviors toward him. One week later, participants viewed the same movie again after listening to a prerecorded hypnotic instruction, including both induction and suggestions designed to suppress memory of the first viewing.

We collected fMRI data during the first movie viewing, the hypnotic induction, and the second movie viewing. In addition, fMRI data were collected from the hypnotist, a trained clinical professional, during the audio recording of the hypnotic induction. To assess the behavioral effectiveness of hypnosis, we acquired eye-tracking data during movie viewing and administered a post-scan questionnaire following the second viewing.

Results

In the first nineteen participants, we found that during the second viewing, individuals whose gaze trajectories more closely resembled those from the first viewing were more likely to report a naïve interpretation of the narrative in the post-scan questionnaire (e.g., “When Mike came to Cole’s house, what do you think Cole’s mother was talking about?”; Fig. 1). At the neural level, we observed coupling between the hypnotist and participants in regions previously implicated in storyteller–listener communication.

Conclusion

Together, these preliminary findings provide both subjective and objective evidence for memory suppression following hypnotic instruction and demonstrate hypnotist–participant neural coupling in brain regions similar to those reported in prior studies of naturalistic storytelling. Ongoing analyses will examine whether stronger hypnotist–participant neural coupling predicts greater memory suppression effects at both behavioral and neural levels.
