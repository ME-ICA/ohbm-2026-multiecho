Introduction

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor (bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor) and non-motor symptoms (neuropsychiatric disturbances, sensory abnormalities, autonomic dysfunction), reflecting widespread dopaminergic and monoaminergic alterations1. Functional connectivity (FC) analysis provides a non-invasive tool to investigate compensatory network reorganization and early functional disruptions that may precede structural degeneration2. Most FC studies have focused on cortical and striatal regions, reporting reduced basal ganglia activity, altered motor and associative connectivity, and disrupted resting-state network interactions2. However, growing evidence indicates that PD may originate in the brainstem1,3, making this region a crucial target for identifying early markers.

Despite recent efforts to assess brainstem FC4, its reliable characterization remains challenging due to the small size and depth of nuclei, complex cytoarchitecture, physiological noise, and low SNR. Moreover, most studies rely on Pearson correlation, which characterizes mediated FC but cannot isolate direct interactions from indirect ones3. This is a limitation for the study of highly interconnected neuromodulatory structures such as the brainstem4.

To close this gap, we characterized both mediated and direct brainstem FC in PD patients and healthy controls (HC) using 7T fMRI. Specifically, we exploited PCA-partial correlation to highlight direct brainstem FC. This method, recently validated on 3T fMRI data, overcomes multicollinearity issues in partial correlation (ρPC) and reduces the effect of mediator regions3. Using this approach, we identify anatomically plausible patterns of direct brainstem connectivity and reveal focal alterations in PD compared to healthy controls. We propose the use of a ρPC-based framework, alongside standard FC approaches, to investigate FC alterations in PD and improve the interpretation of functional pathways.

Methods

We used 7T rs-fMRI data from 41 PD patients (age 35-80) and 15 age-matched HC subjects (age 44-77) acquired with a GE SIGNA 7T scanner at the Imago7 Foundation, Calambrone (PI), Italy. Participants provided written informed consent prior to participation, and the study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee.
For each subject, a T1-weighted image was acquired for functional-anatomical registration, along with a short 4-volume fMRI sequence with reversed phase-encoding for distortion correction. Physiological recordings were simultaneously collected, including pulse oximetry (sampling rate, fs=10ms) and respiratory gating (fs=40ms). fMRI acquisition parameters were as follows: isotropic voxel size=1.5mm, TR=3000ms, 160 volumes, for a total scan duration of 8 minutes.  
We excluded 18 PD and 1 HC participants from further analysis due to excessive head motion, corrupted or missing physiological recordings, apnea-like respiratory patterns, or an unmatched number of fMRI volumes relative to the rest of the dataset. We then randomly selected 15 PD patients to match the numerosity of the HC group.

Preprocessing of fMRI images included despiking, distortion correction, and normalization to the MNI template using linear and non-linear warping. A GLM was used to regress out nuisance confounds (12 motion parameters, ventricular and white matter mean signals, and a 4th order polynomial model of signal drift). Physiological noise—particularly prominent in 7T acquisitions in and around the brainstem—was reduced using the RETROICOR algorithm. The data were then spatially smoothed with a 3 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel, and the residual time series were band-pass filtered (0.01-0.1Hz). All subsequent analyses were performed on the grey-matter voxels extracted from the residuals.

We performed a ROI-based connectomic analysis to characterize both mediated and direct brainstem FC. A total of 62 brainstem nuclei (24 bilateral) were selected as seeds from the “Brainstem Navigator Toolkit v0.9”, while 82 bilateral cortical and subcortical ROIs from the Freesurfer atlas were used as targets. We averaged timeseries within each ROI and performed FC analysis using both Pearson Correlation (ρP) and PCA-based Partial Correlation (PCA-ρPC)3.

The full analysis workflow is shown in fig.1. 

PCA-ρPC, previously tested on a 3T rs-fMRI dataset, combines standard ρPC with PCA-based regularization, as in PC-regression. This approach isolates direct interactions between pairs of regions by excluding the influence of other non-involved brain regions. Since brainstem nuclei exhibit high interconnectivity, complex topology, and strong neuromodulatory role, their timeseries were included as confound variables in the computation of ρPC. PCA was used to reduce overfitting to noise and collinearity by extracting the most informative components. Specifically, PCA-ρPC coefficients were estimated by (i) regressing out principal-component–derived predictors from each brainstem time series using multiple linear regression models and (ii) correlating the resulting residuals. The number of principal components was selected using a 5-fold cross-validation procedure, choosing the configuration that minimized the mean squared error between predicted and to-be-predicted (observed) time series.

After computing single-subject correlation matrices and applying a Fisher-z transformation, we performed two group-level analyses. First, we identified statistically significant correlations within the PD and HC groups using two-tailed one-sample t-test (α=0.05). For this analysis, left and right seed nuclei were averaged to assess symmetry and potential lateralization effects. Second, we tested the hypothesis that, on average, the PD group exhibits significantly different connectivity from HC group using a non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test (a=0.05), after averaging left and right correlations for both seed and target ROIs.

Results

In this section, we present results obtained with the proposed pipeline based on PCA-ρPC, designed to characterize the direct brainstem FC and its alterations in PD.

To validate our method, we assessed the physiological plausibility of the estimated brainstem connectomes by comparing the most relevant connections with anatomical and functional pathways. As examples, fig.2A shows the Dorsal Raphe (DR) connectome in a circular graph, while fig.2B displays the Superior Colliculus (SC) connectome projected onto a cortical surface for visualization purpose. Both results refer to PCA-ρPC FC. 

The DR, a raphe nucleus involved in the serotonin neurotransmitter system, showed widespread FC with parieto-temporal and frontal regions, consistent with the literature describing strong DR–cortex associations. Although DR is known to project toward the median raphe (MnR) via the Hypothalamus-Amygdala complex and amygdala-prefrontal circuits, no direct FC was detected with MnR. Nevertheless, statistically significant FC was found with prefrontal areas, amygdala, and thalamus, consistent with DR’s established projection system. We also confirmed direct FC with the striatum (putamen and pallidum) and insular regions.

The SC is a mesencephalic area with the role of processing different sensory inputs and generating rapid motor responses and its connectome is characterized by a highly structured pattern. As expected, SC showed strong associations with primary and secondary visual areas, including the occipital pole, middle occipital gyrus, and anterior occipital sulcus, confirming its central role in visual information processing. We also found significant FC with superior and inferior parietal areas and with the precuneus, likely reflecting SC involvement in the dorsal attention network, visuospatial orienting and saccade planning, even though no connectivity with frontal eye fields was observed. SC–brainstem interactions involved nuclei mediating multisensory and motor integration, such as CnF, mRT, ISRt, and MITg-PBG. Moreover, direct FC with the thalamus is coherent with its role in multisensory coordination.

Then, we performed PD-HC comparison using both FC methods, focusing on PCA-ρPC approach. Figure 2C shows PCA-ρPC results, revealing sparse but spatially clustered patterns of direct brainstem FC alterations, including both hypo- and hyper-connectivity spots2,5. The vertical axis indicates the seed brainstem nuclei, whereas the horizontal axis shows cortical and brainstem targets. Rectangles mark significant group differences color-coded by anatomical lobe (‘+’ for PD>CT; ‘-‘ for CT>PD).

We found focal increases and decreases in FC with frontal regions, increases in FC with temporal and occipital lobes and decreases within the brainstem. Increased FC observed in frontal, temporal, and parietal areas from various brainstem nuclei (raphe nuclei - DR, Rob, RPa - and other arousal/motor nuclei such as CnF and LDTg-CGPn) may reflect compensatory mechanisms and network reorganization involving neuromodulatory nuclei. Conversely, most of the PD-HC differences for SN show decreases in FC, consistent with striatal dopamine loss, as nigrostriatal degeneration is a hallmark of PD1. For the RN, a motor nucleus involved in limb and gait control, we found hypoconnectivity with key subcortical motor-circuit regions (pallidum, mRT and SN), as well as alterations with cortical regions involved in planning, control and execution of voluntary movements (primary motor cortex and pars Triangularis).

Conclusion

We designed a pipeline to identify FC alterations originating from mediated or direct interactions at the brainstem level, a region that represents a major locus of neurodegeneration in PD. Specifically, we analyzed rs-fMRI data from HC and PD patients using both ρP and ρPC and exploited the advantages of 7T acquisitions to assess brainstem FC.

By combining ρPC with PCA regularization, we performed FC analysis while removing shared variance among brainstem nuclei, thereby reducing multicollinearity and noise overfitting in ρPC coefficients estimates3. Moreover, compared to ρP, PCA-ρPC reveal sparser and anatomically more specific patterns of brainstem connectivity, reducing widespread indirect correlations.

We evaluated the reliability of our connectomes against anatomo-functional evidence. Interestingly, the DR connectivity pattern was consistent with its central role in arousal, emotion, sleep–wake regulation, attention, and cognition according to which we expected widespread connections with the cortex, even when using a direct FC method. The SC connectome exhibited a highly specialized architecture focused on visual and visuospatial processing, supplemented by selective interactions with multisensory and premotor nuclei (CnF, mRT, ISRt). Note that the low FC observed with temporal regions could be explained by hierarchical processing mediated by the inferior colliculus, a critical node in the auditory information processing pathways.

Moreover, PCA-ρPC analysis of the PD-HC contrast characterized direct FC disruptions, potentially highlighting distinct aspects of PD-related network reconfiguration and supporting the notion of a complex interplay between degenerative and compensatory mechanisms.
 Hypoconnectivity between RN, SN and motor-related cortical nodes suggests disruption of brainstem–cortical loops involved in gait, posture and movement. Reduced brainstem–brainstem FC further supports impairment of intrinsic premotor coordination and dysregulation in autonomic functions. Conversely, hyperconnectivity from raphe nuclei, CnF, and LDTg–CGPn may indicate compensatory effects in response to brain neuroplasticity, as may enhanced VTA–brainstem and VTA-cortex interactions, also potentially influenced by L-DOPA effects.

Our preliminary findings, in line with previous studies, provide in vivo evidence of distributed brainstem FC alterations in PD, affecting motor, neuromodulatory and sensorimotor systems. This supports the view of PD as a multisystem network disorder rather than one confined to cortico-striatal loops1. 

Overall, by combining 7T fMRI with dual-method FC, we can reveal both direct and mediated brainstem-cortical disruptions in PD. Importantly, PCA-ρPC isolates FC alterations that cannot be attributed to mediated pathways by brainstem nuclei. The methodological choice to consider only brainstem nuclei as covariates in our MLR models is justified by the brainstem’s strong structural organization, dense topological coupling, and extensive neuromodulatory roles, although indirect correlations mediated by cortical or subcortical structures likely remain present3.

Finally, while here we focused on PCA-ρPC-driven findings, a dual interpretation exploiting both direct and mediated FC may help to determine whether alterations result from intrinsic changes or secondary network effects—critical in a disease, like PD, characterized by functional compensation and network-wide reorganization. In fact, direct and mediated FC provide complementary perspectives with potential applications for early diagnosis and marker identification. For instance, the proposed approach, potentially integrated with clinical or behavioral measures, may support feature extraction for predictive modeling of disease progression and characterize prodromal populations (such as RBD patients) in order to identify early markers of neurodegeneration and potential pharmacological targets.
