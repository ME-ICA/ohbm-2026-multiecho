Introduction

Cerebellar anatomy is difficult to characterize due to the small size of its sub-structures and the highly folded geometry of the cerebellar cortex. Although recent high-field MRI scanners provide sufficient resolution for detailed morphological studies, most existing methods still rely on volumetric metrics and do not explicitly model foliation patterns¹. In contrast, cortical folding analysis has been a thriving field of research for the last decades, leading to the development of multiple tools for sulci extraction, such as Morphologist pipeline², allowing automatic skeletization of individual sulcus as well as labeling following a predefined atlas, and deep-learning based analysis methods for rare folding patterns detection³ or cortical folding patterns characterization. Given the methodological maturity of these tools and the lack of dedicated approaches for cerebellar foliation, adapting cortical-folding pipelines to the cerebellum represents a promising avenue. In this work, we propose a preprocessing pipeline that extracts cerebellar white-matter foliation skeletons from T1w MRI using curvature-based features that are used to optimize and train a beta-VAE to encode vermian morphological variability in an unsupervised manner into a "morpohological space" that we evaluate using a sex classification task.

Methods

Preprocessing pipeline

To tackle the difficulty in harmonizing MRIs from multiple sites and to focus on the morphological aspects of cerebellar substructures, we developed a pipeline aiming to convert MRI images into a binary volume containing a simplified representation of cerebellar foliation. This step removes intensity- and contrast-related variability while isolating geometry as the primary feature and reducing domain shifts from multi-site MRIs.

The first step of the preprocessing pipeline consists of a linear MRI registration in the MNI152-ICBM2009c space with a resampling of the images using a cubic-spline interpolation at a 0.5mm isotropic resolution using Pyaims tools.

The second step is the computation of the mean curvature⁴ of each voxel by computing the partial derivatives of the intensities using a 3D Gaussian kernel convolution with 1 mm standard deviation. These computed values are used as ”crevasse and bumps detectors”. Following this step, we retained curvature values in the lowest and highest deciles, which empirically capture white-matter ridges and sulcal boundaries while suppressing noise and small-scale curvature fluctuations.

To retrieve cerebellar structures in the MNI152-ICBM2009c space, a set of 27 masks of healthy subjects segmented using a combination of SUIT and CERES with manual correction have been used. The vermis was chosen as an initial target due to its compact extent, enabling a controlled first evaluation before scaling to the whole cerebellum. Those masks have been registered to MNI152-ICBM2009c and summed to obtain a probability map of the vermian structure’s location in this space. An encompassing mask has then been produced by taking all the non-null voxels of this primary mask, followed by a 5-voxel dilation to ensure capturing all targeted structures and tackle inter-subject anatomical variability.

The final output is obtained by applying this mask to the thresholded mean-curvature volume to obtain a binary volume containing the vermian morphological structure of white-matter. The complete pipeline is described in Figure 1A.

Datasets 

A subset of the UKBiobank cohort including 21,045 3T T1w-MRIs was used for training our beta-VAE. To evaluate the resulting model, we used a dataset from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Developmental study (ABCD) including 9,985 3T T1w-MRIs. Each dataset comprises multiple metadata corresponding to sex or site, and some computed internally such as the total intracranial volume by using cat12.

Model architecture

In this study, a beta-VAE is used to encode the morphological structures of the vermis. It provides a principled way to learn low-dimensional, disentangled latent variables while enforcing smoothness on the embedding space. This property is particularly relevant for morphological modeling, where gradual anatomical variations are expected across subjects. The chosen architecture is the one used in Guillon et al.³ and detailed in Figure 1B.

Predictive tasks

To assess whether the representations obtained from the model are informative of the vermian morphology, we used a predictive task consisting of a binary sex classification. It has been shown that there is a sexual dimorphism regarding vermian size⁵ , therefore, this task should be relevant to prove that morphological information is encoded by the model. The process is detailed in Figure 2B. We used a Logistic Regression model selected via a 5-fold cross-validation grid search. To control for confounding, residualization is performed for age, total intracranial volume (TIV), and site (for ABCD). The metric used for evaluation is AUC score.

Results

Figures 1B and 2A present examples of volume extracted from the developed preprocessing pipeline. The cropped T1w MRI from Figure 2A shows that our cropping strategy is able to extract vermian structures that are effectively extracted in the corresponding pipeline output. From this input, the corresponding reconstruction from the beta-VAE model shows structural similarity with its reconstruction. A voxel-wise reconstruction error has been used to evalutate similarity between inputs and reconstructions. On the UKBioBank dataset, voxel-wise reconstruction errors have a mean of 9 ± 1%.On ABCD, voxel-wise reconstruction errors are 10 ± 1%.

Looking at classification results (Figure 2C), the model achieved a mean AUC of 0.7231 ± 0.0044 on validation folds. On ABCD, the model achieved a mean AUC of 0.6966 ± 0.0126 on validation folds. Despite this drop, the ABCD performance remains reasonably aligned with the UK Biobank test AUC, suggesting that the latent space encodes transferable—but partially domain-sensitive—morphological features. This highlights the effectiveness of removing confounding effects using a residualization method, yielding similar results despite domain shifts (site, demography, localization).

Conclusion

The results obtained in this work illustrate both the feasibility and the current limitations of adapting cortical-folding–oriented preprocessing tools to cerebellar structures. The proposed pipeline, derived from the Morphologist framework, successfully extracts binary morphological representations of the vermis and enables the β-VAE to encode meaningful structural variability. The evaluation results are, at first glance, encouraging, given that residualization over TIV may cause a loss of most of the signal, since part of the difference between male and female should reflect differences in brain volume. This shows that the model can retrieve fine-grained information in the vermis, enabling sex discrimination. However, several factors likely contribute to the variability observed in downstream performance.

First, the curvature-based extraction procedure relies on empirically selected, fixed thresholds. While sufficient for large-scale processing, this strategy may introduce inconsistencies across subjects or datasets, particularly when acquisition characteristics differ. This might explain a decrease in performance regarding the classification task between ABCD and UKB. Similarly, the vermis masks used to constrain the extraction originate from a relatively small set of manually curated segmentations. Although adequate for an initial proof of concept, this may introduce bias when the method is applied to broader cohorts. 

A second limitation lies in the exclusive focus on the vermis. This choice was driven by computational considerations and the availability of reliable masks, but it restricts the method’s ability to capture the full diversity of cerebellar foliation. Cerebellar morphology varies substantially across lobules and hemispheres, and extending the pipeline to larger subregions would likely enrich the learned latent representations.

Another limitation of our masking approach lies in the choice of using a dilated summation of the 27 masks. This introduces information of external structures (e.g. tentorium cerebelli) that are kept in the resulting binary volume and which may add perturbation in the training process of the deep learning model.

The behavior of the β-VAE also reflects some intrinsic constraints. While the learned embeddings achieve moderate classification performance, reconstruction quality remains limited due to the sparsity and binarization of the input volumes—properties known to challenge VAE training stability. Alternative generative models or architectures may help mitigate these effects and improve the fidelity of the encoded morphological information.

Finally, the decrease in performance observed on the ABCD dataset indicates the presence of domain shift (albeit small), likely driven by differences in age distribution, acquisition sites, and scanner variability. This highlights the need for harmonization or domain-adaptation strategies to ensure robustness across heterogeneous cohorts.

While the current implementation focuses on the vermis and relies on threshold-based feature extraction, the results establish a solid proof of concept for generative modeling of cerebellar morphology. Future work should extend this framework to full cerebellar foliation, incorporate more robust, data-driven extraction strategies, and integrate domain-adaptation techniques to mitigate multi-site variability. Overall, our findings highlight the promise of combining geometric feature extraction with generative latent models to advance cerebellar morphometry at scale.
