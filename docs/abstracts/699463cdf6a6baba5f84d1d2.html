Introduction

Perivascular spaces serve as conduits for the glymphatic system, the brain's primary waste clearance pathway responsible for removing metabolic byproducts and toxic proteins.1 Enlarged perivascular spaces (ePVS) are visible on conventional MRI. Accumulation of ePVS in white matter (WM) reflects cerebrovascular dysfunction and impaired glymphatic clearance, processes implicated in neurodegenerative disease pathogenesis. However, the relationship between ePVS burden and cerebrovascular hemodynamics remains poorly understood.

Intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) imaging offers non-invasive assessment of cerebrovascular perfusion by separating tissue diffusion from microvascular perfusion dynamics through multi-shell diffusion-weighted imaging (Fig.1). IVIM derives three parameters: tissue diffusion coefficient (D), pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*, reflecting perfusion), and perfusion fraction (f). However, whether these parameters are associated with ePVS burden—and whether such associations differ between ePVS-laden regions and normal white matter—remains unexplored.

Sleep plays a critical role in glymphatic function, with observational studies demonstrating associations between poor sleep quality and increased ePVS burden.2 Preclinical studies suggest that sleep facilitates glymphatic clearance through increased interstitial fluid flow and waste removal. However, whether sleep disturbances interact with cerebrovascular dysfunction to exacerbate perivascular pathology in humans has not been characterized.

This study examined whether IVIM-derived perfusion parameters differ between ePVS clusters and normal WM, and whether these metrics correlate with total ePVS burden in older adults spanning the Alzheimer's disease (AD) spectrum. Additionally, we investigated whether sleep problems moderate the relationship between IVIM parameters and ePVS burden, hypothesizing that greater sleep disturbances would amplify associations between abnormal perfusion and ePVS accumulation. 

Methods

Participants

Data were acquired from 68 older adults enrolled in the Stanford Alzheimer's Disease Research Center, including 30 Aβ-positive (centiloid ≥18) and 38 Aβ-negative individuals ranging from cognitively normal to various stages along the AD spectrum. All participants underwent comprehensive clinical evaluation, neuropsychological assessment, and multimodal neuroimaging. 

Image Acquisition

Neuroimaging data were acquired on a 3-Tesla PET/MRI scanner (GE SIGNA). The imaging protocol included: (1) High-resolution 3D T1-weighted structural images (gradient-recalled inversion recovery pulse sequence: TR/TE = 7.7/3.1 ms, inversion time = 400 ms, flip angle = 11°) were acquired with two reconstruction matrices yielding voxel sizes of 1×1×1 mm³ and 0.5×0.5×1 mm³, (2) multi-shell diffusion-weighted imaging with 9 b-values (0, 25, 50, 75, 100, 150, 250, 500, 1000 s/mm²) to enable IVIM modeling, with 12 diffusion directions per b-value shell (TR/TE = 7000/80.3 ms, 2 mm slice thickness, 4 mm interslice spacing), and (3) Aβ PET images using 8.1 mCi injection of 18F-florbetaben ligand and acquired data between 90-110 minutes. PET data were reconstructed into 5-minute frames using standard methods.

Amyloid Status

Participants with centiloid values (derived using Aβ PET) ≥18 were classified as Aβ-positive; those with centiloid <18 were classified as Aβ-negative.

ePVS Segmentation

White matter ePVS were identified using an unsupervised learning algorithm.3 Since native T2-weighted (T2w) images were not acquired, T1w images were inverted to generate pseudo-T2w images, as the algorithm requires both contrasts to enhance ePVS detection. Pseudo-T2w and T1w images were concatenated into 4D volumes for participant-specific voxel-wise regression, generating ventricular signal beta maps. Frangi filtering produced vesselness maps emphasizing tubular structures, which were thresholded via density-based clustering to detect ePVS clusters within a WM mask. Two expert raters performed quality control. ePVS burden was calculated as ePVS volume/WM volume to account for brain atrophy.

IVIM Parameter Estimation

Multi-shell diffusion data were processed using an in-house developed pipeline and utilized the IAR_LU_biexp fitting model, part of the IVIM task force 2.4,4 to calculate three key IVIM metrics, D, D*, and f. Mean IVIM metrics were extracted from ePVS clusters identified by the segmentation algorithm and normalized by total WM volume (Fig. 2). For comparison, identical metrics were calculated within randomly sampled non-ePVS WM voxels (matched for total volume) to establish normal WM reference values.

Data Harmonization:

ComBat harmonization was applied to all ePVS and IVIM parameters to account for T1w voxel size differences (1×1×1 mm³ vs. 0.5×0.5×1 mm³). Age, sex, and centiloid status were preserved as biological covariates.

Sleep Assessment

Self-reported sleep problems were assessed using a validated single-item questionnaire asking participants  (n=62) to rate their overall sleep quality on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = no problems, 7 = severe problems). Higher scores indicate worse sleep quality.

Statistical Analysis

Paired t-tests assessed differences in IVIM metrics between ePVS clusters and normal white matter, with correction for multiple comparisons. Partial correlation analyses, controlling for age and sex, examined associations between IVIM parameters (D, D*, f) and total ePVS burden. Moderation analyses using hierarchical linear regression determined whether sleep problems moderated the relationship between IVIM parameters and ePVS burden, controlling for age. Interaction terms were created by centering predictor variables and computing cross-products. Exploratory stratified analyses by amyloid status (Aβ-positive vs. Aβ-negative) were conducted to examine whether associations differed by AD pathology. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Sample Characteristics

The sample included 68 older adults (mean age 73.1 ± 8.2 years, 62% male). Aβ-positive individuals (n=30) were slightly older (74.3 ± 8.9 years) than Aβ-negative individuals (n=38, 72.2 ± 7.5 years, p=0.14). Self-reported sleep problems averaged 2.5 ± 1.4 on the 7-point scale.

IVIM Metrics in ePVS Clusters vs. Normal White Matter

IVIM metrics within ePVS clusters were significantly elevated compared to normal WM (D, D*, f; all p_corrected < 0.01), indicating increased diffusion and perfusion dynamics within perivascular spaces.

IVIM-ePVS Burden Correlations in Total Sample

Correlation analyses revealed significant positive associations between ePVS burden and IVIM perfusion parameters in the total sample. Tissue diffusion coefficient (D) correlated positively with ePVS burden (r = 0.37, p = 0.002), indicating that greater tissue diffusivity corresponds with increased perivascular pathology. Pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*) showed a similarly strong correlation with ePVS burden (r = 0.39, p < 0.001), suggesting that elevated microvascular perfusion dynamics are associated with greater ePVS burden. Perfusion fraction (f) showed a trend toward positive correlation (r = 0.22, p = 0.07). These correlations remained significant after controlling for age and sex.

Amyloid Stratification of IVIM-ePVS Correlations

Exploratory stratification by amyloid status revealed markedly differential patterns of association. In Aβ-negative individuals (n=38), correlations between IVIM parameters and ePVS burden were substantially stronger: D showed a robust correlation (r = 0.51, p = 0.001), and D* demonstrated similarly strong association (r = 0.48, p = 0.002). In contrast, Aβ-positive individuals (n=30) exhibited much weaker, non-significant correlations: D (r = 0.25, p = 0.18) and D* (r = 0.30, p = 0.11). These findings suggest that amyloid pathology may decouple vascular-perivascular relationships through independent pathophysiological mechanisms or measurement confounds.

Sleep Moderation of IVIM-ePVS Relationships

Moderation analyses in the total sample demonstrated that self-reported sleep problems significantly moderated the relationship between D* and ePVS burden (interaction β = +0.31, p = 0.046, R² = 0.24). The positive interaction coefficient revealed that this relationship strengthened as sleep problems increased. At low sleep problem levels (-1 standard deviation), the slope of the D*→ePVS relationship was +0.13, whereas at high sleep problem levels (+1 standard deviation), the slope increased to +0.75, a 5.8-fold amplification. This pattern suggests that sleep problems substantially enhance the association between elevated D* and greater ePVS burden.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that IVIM-derived perfusion metrics are elevated within ePVS clusters and positively correlate with ePVS burden, indicating that non-invasive diffusion-perfusion imaging can characterize cerebrovascular abnormalities within perivascular spaces. Three key findings emerged: (1) all IVIM parameters (D, D*, f) were significantly elevated in ePVS clusters versus normal WM; (2) IVIM metrics correlated positively with total ePVS burden; and (3) these correlations were substantially stronger in amyloid-negative individuals, suggesting amyloid pathology decouples vascular-perivascular relationships.

Sleep problems significantly moderated the relationship between microvascular perfusion and ePVS burden. Poor sleep quality amplified the effects of heightened cerebrovascular perfusion on perivascular pathology, revealing a sleep-vascular interaction. This aligns with mechanistic animal models proposing that sleep facilitates glymphatic clearance through increased interstitial fluid convection and waste removal.5 Sleep disruption may impair these clearance mechanisms, allowing abnormal vascular dynamics to exacerbate perivascular fluid accumulation. 

The substantially stronger IVIM-ePVS correlations in amyloid-negative individuals (r = 0.48-0.51) versus amyloid-positive individuals (r = 0.25-0.30) suggest that amyloid pathology may obscure vascular-perivascular coupling. Several mechanisms may explain this decoupling. First, amyloid deposition in vessel walls (cerebral amyloid angiopathy) may alter vascular compliance and perfusion dynamics. Second, amyloid pathology may drive ePVS enlargement through independent mechanisms (e.g., neuroinflammation). Third, amyloid-related tissue alterations may affect IVIM parameter estimation. These possibilities warrant investigation in longitudinal studies with histopathological validation.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, reliance on self-reported sleep measures may introduce recall bias and lacks precision of objective assessments such as polysomnography or actigraphy. Second, use of pseudo-T2-weighted images for ePVS segmentation may not fully replicate native T2-weighted contrast. Third, D* estimates in IVIM modeling exhibit high variability and lower test-retest reliability. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding directionality and temporal dynamics. Fifth, the relatively small sample size, particularly in stratified analyses, may limit statistical power.

Despite these limitations, our findings enhance mechanistic understanding of brain waste clearance by demonstrating that cerebrovascular perfusion abnormalities are spatially and quantitatively associated with perivascular pathology, modulated by sleep quality and altered by amyloid pathology. These results support the potential utility of IVIM imaging as a non-invasive biomarker of glymphatic dysfunction and highlight the importance of considering sleep-vascular interactions in models of neurodegenerative disease. Future longitudinal studies with objective sleep measures, native T2-w imaging, and larger samples are warranted to establish temporal relationships and evaluate whether interventions targeting sleep or vascular health can modify ePVS progression and downstream neurodegeneration.
