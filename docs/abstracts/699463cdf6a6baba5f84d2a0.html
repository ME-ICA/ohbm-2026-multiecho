Introduction

Oxytocin (OXT) is a key neuromodulator of complex socio-affective behaviors. Most research to date has focused on circuit-level dissections aimed at defining the contribution of distinct oxytocinergic projections to specific behavioral domains. These studies have revealed that OXT has multifaceted effects on multiple behavioural domains, either inducing pro or antisocial behaviour, and either increasing or decreasing stress, seemingly dependent on context. However, how the relatively focal expression of OXT in the brain can modulate such complex behavioral domains remains unclear.

OXT is synthesized mainly in the hypothalamus, from which it is released either into the bloodstream or centrally through axonal fibers to act on oxytocin receptors (OXTRs). Interestingly, a dissociation exists between patterns of OXT projection and receptor distribution. While the functional role of this dissociation remains unclear, the widespread anatomical distribution of the OXT-R suggests that endogenous OXT release may recruit and modulate macroscale network substrates above and beyond directly innervated regions. Human and animal studies upon intranasal administration of exogenous OXT suggest this may be the case1. However, the large-scale functional effects produced by endogenous OXT release remain unknown. To address this question, we combined intersectional genetic and chemogenetic-fMRI2 to causally map the large-scale functional effects of endogenous OXT release in the mouse brain.

Methods

We generated an oxytocin-specific chemogenetic mouse line by crossing OXT-Cre mice with a Cre-dependent hM3Dq DREADD reporter, yielding animals expressing the excitatory DREADD selectively in OXT-producing neurons (OXT-hM3Dq). Expression specificity was validated histologically by colocalization of reporter and OXT in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN), and functionally by electrophysiological recordings showing increased firing of OXT neurons following DREADD ligand (J60) administration, as well as a physiologically relevant rise in circulating OXT levels.

All imaging experiments were conducted under light halothane sedation. Changes in cerebral blood volume (rCBV) were mapped using a blood-pool contrast agent (Molday Ion, Biopal) and aFLASH sequence, as previously described1. Resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) data were acquired using a gradient-echo EPI sequence, and voxelwise as well as region-of-interest analyses were used to assess functional connectivity changes. To obtain a mechanistic readout of underlying neural activity, we also performed in vivo electrophysiology with single-shank electrodes implanted in medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), dorsal hippocampus (dHPC), and mediodorsal thalamus (mdThal), quantifying local field potential (LFP) spectral modulation and interregional coherence. Dynamic rsfMRI activity was characterized by frame-wise clustering of BOLD time series to extract recurring co-activation modes (“C-modes”)3, followed by analysis of their occurrence, transition probabilities, and persistence to probe how OXT release alters the temporal landscape of network states.

Results

To enable stimulation of OXT-releasing neurons, we crossed mice harboring a double-floxed DREADD activator hM3Dq with OXT-specific Cre-recombinase mice. We next verified that  hM3Dq expression was limited to OXT+ cells,  activation of hM3Dq increased firing rate of OXT-releasing cells in the PVN, and this activation resulted in physiologically-relevant increases in circulating  OXT  levels of OXT.

We then examined how endogenous OXT release affects brain activity and connectivity. Chemogenetic activation of OXT neurons induced widespread increases in rCBV across most brain regions. Interestingly, these effects did not correlate with OXT-receptor distribution, or axonal connectivity of the oxytocin system, suggesting modulation beyond direct projection targets. We next used rsfMRI to investigate whether endogenous OXT affected systems-level network activity. We found that OXT can induce robust fMRI connectivity changes between areas of the rodent social brain. Importantly, the rsfMRI findings were corroborated by in vivo electrophysiology, showing parallel modulations in LFP spectral power and coherence consistent with a neuronal basis for the observed connectivity changes.

Given the diverse behavioral effects of OXT, we hypothesized that its endogenous release could also alter the dynamical structure of brain networks, fine-tuning large-scale activity toward specific functional states. To test this, we decomposed rsfMRI timeseries into recurring co-activation modes3. As predicted, OXT stimulation induced a marked reconfiguration of network dynamics, with dominant co-activation modes engaging salience-related structures emerging as attractor states. This result  suggests that OXT shapes brain dynamics by enhancing the engagement of salience-related networks, a mechanism that could underlie its diverse influences on social and emotional processing.

Conclusion

In summary, our chemogenetic-fMRI approach enables causal mapping of endogenous neuropeptide and neuromodulator effects at the whole-brain scale. Using this method, we find that endogenous OXT release drives a large-scale reorganization of brain network dynamics, increasing coordination among social brain regions and promoting salience-related communication across systems.

These findings offer a mechanistic account of how a focal hypothalamic neuromodulatory system can exert broad influence on macroscale functional architecture, helping to reconcile the diverse behavioural effects of OXT with its anatomical and receptor distribution. More broadly, the work illustrates how combining cell-type-specific chemogenetics, fMRI, and electrophysiology can be used to dissect the contribution of neuromodulatory systems to large-scale network dynamics, with direct relevance for understanding and targeting socio-affective dysfunction in neuropsychiatric conditions.
