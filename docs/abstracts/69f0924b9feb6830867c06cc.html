Introduction

Depressive phenotypes, ranging from subclinical depressive symptoms in the general population to clinical depression, and sleep health, spanning several components of sleep duration and quality, represent heterogeneous and multifaceted constructs that are intricately interrelated.1 However, the underlying factors that shape the interrelated association between depressive phenotypes and sleep health remain unclear. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the influencing factors and neurobiological underpinnings of sleep-related depressive (SRD) symptoms, which reflect symptoms of depression that are maximally linked to sleep health. 

Lifestyle and socioeconomic factors are modifiable conditions that can influence an individual’s risk for depressive phenotype and particularly SRD.2 However, existing evidence has overlooked the substantial interindividual variability in SRD and the role of these factors in predicting SRD at the individual level. Using the UK Biobank dataset, we calculated SRD scores for each participant using latent variables derived from multivariate mapping between sleep health and the depressive phenotype. Subsequently, we evaluated whether baseline lifestyle and socioeconomic factors can predict both baseline and longitudinal SRD scores in the general population and baseline SRD scores in clinical depression samples using predictive models. We further examined the correlation and mediating roles of brain structures in the relationships between the top lifestyle/socioeconomic predictors and SRD scores.

Methods

Participants from the UK Biobank with complete baseline data on 1) lifestyle factors including physical activity, diet, smoking, alcohol consumption, digital use, and sun exposure; 2) socioeconomic variables (i.e., social support, working status, and socioeconomic deprivation; Figure 1 B); 3) sleep health (duration, chronotype, snoring, insomnia, daytime dozing, nap during day, and getting up in morning); and 4) depressive symptoms (recent depressive symptoms-4 and neuroticism-12) were included in this study. We divided them into four datasets (Figure 1 A): a cross-sectional general population dataset (GP 1; n = 87,405); a longitudinal general population dataset with baseline (GP 2 baseline; n = 5,187) and follow-up assessments (GP 2 follow-up); and a cross-sectional clinical depression dataset (CD; n = 7,454). Participants from CD either had a written diagnosis from ICD-10 or were taking antidepressants at the time of assessment.

We first applied regularized canonical correlation analysis (rCCA) to identify the multivariate association between sleep health and depressive symptoms in GP 1. The first component of rCCA was used to identify SRD scores for each individual. Subsequently, we used the aforementioned lifestyle and socioeconomic factors to predict SRD in GP1 using several linear and non-linear predictive models. To avoid data leakage, rCCA and predictive models were integrated within a nested cross-validation framework (Figure 1 C). In particular, GP 1 was randomly split into five outer folds. In each iteration, one outer fold (20%) served as the CCA fold for training the rCCA model, and the remaining four (80%) served as the ML fold for training predictive models. Each CCA fold was further divided for hyperparameter tuning (80%) and model validation (20%). While in each ML fold, a nested cross-validation was performed, and 80% of the ML data were used as the inner fold for model training, and 20% as the outer fold for testing. Within the inner training set, it was split internally again for hyperparameter tuning (80%) and validation (20%). Multiple trained predictive models were finally evaluated based on the averaged performance across all test sets in ML folds. This procedure was repeated across all five outer folds so that each outer fold served once for rCCA training set. To enhance stability, model training and estimation in the ML folds were repeated twice. To evaluate the significance of the association between sleep health and depressive symptoms, the rows of depressive symptoms were shuffled for 1,000 iterations in both CCA folds and ML folds. Thereafter, the rCCA model was refit on the shuffled CCA folds, and the shuffled ML folds were projected onto the model to estimate statistical significance. The best-performing predictive model identified in GP1 was validated across three independent datasets to assess its generalizability: GP 2 at baseline, GP 2 at follow-up, and the CD at baseline. Age and sex were regressed out in both rCCA and predictive models.

To investigate neural correlates of the main predictors in the predictive model, we first conducted partial correlation analyses between the top four predictors and gray matter volume (GMV) across seven cortical networks and 34 subcortical regions in GP 1, adjusting for age, sex, and total intracranial volume. Significant regions were identified using false discovery rate (FDR) correction (P < 0.05). Subsequently, mediation analyses were performed to assess whether these significant brain regions mediated the association between the main predictors and SRD (Figure 1 D).

Results

To capture the maximum shared variance between sleep health and depressive symptoms, we focused on the first latent dimension identified by the rCCA in GP1. The sleep health canonical variate was positively related to the depressive symptoms canonical variate, with a canonical correlation of 0.42 (Figure 2 A). Higher canonical loadings for insomnia and difficulty getting up in the morning were associated with higher canonical loadings for frequency of tiredness or lethargy over the past two weeks. Moreover, shorter sleep duration loadings corresponded to higher depressive symptoms canonical variate scores (Figure 2 B).  

Among all applied predictive models in GP 1, the best performance was achieved by eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost; r = 0.25, R² = 0.06, rMSE = 1.03, Figure 2 C). For out-of-sample validation, XGBoost model in the GP1 was applied to GP2_baseline (r = 0.23, R² = 0.05, rMSE = 1.04), GP2_followup (r = 0.22, R² = 0.05, rMSE = 1.02), and CD (r = 0.33, R² = 0.11, rMSE = 1.17) respectively. The top four lifestyle and socioeconomic factors that predicted higher SRD in GP1 were lower frequency of confiding in others, longer hours of sedentary television viewing, more exposure to secondhand (passive) smoking outside the home, and fewer days of weekly vigorous physical activity (lasting ≥10 minutes) (Figure 2 D). 

Partial correlation analyses revealed that the frequency of confiding in others was associated with the left anterior putamen (ß = 0.046, PFDR= 0.049), hours of sedentary television viewing with the visual network (ß = 0.052, PFDR= 0.025), and frequency of vigorous physical activity with the left posterior globus pallidus (ß = -0.050, PFDR = 0.025, Figure 2 E). Mediation analyses identified that the left anterior putamen (ß = 0.0013, CI: [0.0002, 0.004]) and left posterior globus pallidus (ß = -0.0015, CI: [-0.0033, -0.0002]) mediated the relationships between frequency of confiding and vigorous physical activity with the SRD, respectively (Figure 2 F).

Conclusion

This work identified a robust association between sleep health and depressive symptoms, particularly between insomnia symptoms and somatic depressive symptoms, consistent with prior findings.1 In addition, we demonstrated that socioeconomic factors, such as the frequency of confiding in others and lifestyle factors including hours of sedentary television viewing, secondhand smoke exposure, and frequency of vigorous physical activities, contributed to individual-level SRD variability in both the general population and clinical depression participants. Notably, these factors at baseline also predicted SRD variability a decade later (mean follow-up 9.2 years), underscoring the importance of poor lifestyle and socioeconomic factors in predicting the severity of depressive symptoms that are most strongly linked to sleep disturbances. The predictive findings were robustly validated across three independent datasets, highlighting the generalizability of our predictive models. 

We identified a mediating role of the basal ganglia in the associations between key lifestyle and socioeconomic factors and SRD, suggesting a potential neural substrate underlying the complex interplay between them. The putamen is involved in reward processing and has been linked to social affirmation and emotional support, which may help to buffer stress responses.3 Unexpectedly, we identified an indirect pathway in which greater putamen volume slightly attenuated the protective association between more frequent confiding and lower SRD. However, this pattern may reflect an early compensatory response within neural systems involved in regulating negative emotion, which is similar to prior study.4 In addition, globus pallidus stimulation demonstrated concurrent improvements in motor function and reductions in depressive symptoms,5 further underscoring the important role of globus pallidus in linking physical activity and depressive symptoms. 

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that poor lifestyle and unfavorable socioeconomic conditions are associated with higher current and future interindividual SRD in the general population and current SRD in the clinical depression participants. Moreover, the putamen and  globus pallidus may constitute a central neural substrate through which these modifiable factors exert their effects on SRD across both populations.
