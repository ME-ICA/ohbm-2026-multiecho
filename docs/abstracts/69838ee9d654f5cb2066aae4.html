Introduction

Successful episodic memory relies on the ability to distinguish highly similar experiences, a process known as mnemonic discrimination (MD). MD is one of the first cognitive functions to decline in aging, driven by degradation within the medial temporal lobe (MTL). Critically, the MTL is thought to be organized into category-selective processing pathways, where the perirhinal cortex (PRC) preferentially processes item-specific object information, while the parahippocampal cortex (PHC) processes context-rich scene information. These inputs converge on hippocampal subfields (DG/CA3) to support pattern separation

While cognitive training may attenuate memory decline, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying these gains remain elusive. Specifically, it is unknown whether training in one domain (e.g., objects) can induce plasticity in specific cortical nodes (e.g., PRC) and facilitate transfer to untrained categories (e.g., scenes). Investigating this requires high-resolution imaging; standard 3T MRI often lacks the spatial resolution to detect subtle microstructural changes within small, functionally distinct subfields. Consequently, the link between subfield-specific plasticity and behavioral transfer remains unestablished in humans.

Furthermore, the aging brain is not uniform. Underlying Alzheimer’s pathology, such as phosphorylated tau (pTau), may fundamentally alter intervention responsiveness. It remains unclear whether pathology simply limits the magnitude of improvement or if it forces the aging brain to adopt compensatory strategies—shifting from precise mnemonic discrimination to associative generalization. To address these gaps, we leveraged 7T MRI and plasma pTau217 biomarkers in cognitively healthy older adults. We investigated whether an 8-week MD training program elicits behavioral transfer across object-scene pathways, whether gains are supported by subfield-specific structural plasticity, and how baseline pathology affects these outcomes.

Methods

Participants and Study Design: A total of 151 cognitively healthy older adults (age: M = 69.71 years, SD = 4.17) were recruited for an 8-week randomized controlled trial. Participants were assigned to one of three groups: Object-based Mnemonic Discrimination Training (OG), Scene-based Mnemonic Discrimination Training (SG), or an Active Control (AC) group. A subset of the Object-trained group (n = 33) and Control group (n = 28) underwent high-resolution 7T MRI at baseline (pre-training) and follow-up (post-training).
 
Intervention: The intervention was a web-based home training program administered 3 times per week (approx. 45 minutes per session) for 8 weeks. The training utilized an adaptive n-back Mnemonic Discrimination (MD) paradigm. Participants viewed continuous streams of stimuli (objects for OG, scenes for SG) and were required to discriminate between "Repeats" (exact repetitions) and "Lures" (highly similar but non-identical items). The task load was adaptive: it commenced as a 2-back task and progressively increased in difficulty (up to 16-back) contingent on performance, thereby continuously taxing pattern separation and working memory maintenance. The AC group performed low-load perceptual tasks without mnemonic demands.

Behavioral Assessment: Pre- and post-training assessments included the following tasks:

  1. Trained Task (MD-OS): We administered the Mnemonic Discrimination Object-Scene task (MD-OS; Berron et al., 2018), a continuous recognition paradigm comprising an equal ratio of object and scene trials (50% each). In this task, participants viewed a stream of stimuli and discriminated between exact repetitions ('repeats') and highly similar items ('lures'), allowing for the simultaneous assessment of trained modality performance and near-transfer to the untrained stimulus category (e.g., scene discrimination in the object-trained group).
  2. Near Transfer (MST): he Mnemonic Similarity Task (Stark et al., 2019) was used to assess generalization to an independent mnemonic discrimination paradigm utilizing novel object stimuli.
  3. Associative Transfer (Object-in-Place): An associative memory task requiring the retrieval of object locations within scenes, used to assess compensatory strategy shifts.

7T MRI Acquisition and Processing: Neuroimaging data were acquired on a 7T Siemens Terra scanner. The protocol included:

  1. MP2RAGE: A high-resolution whole-brain T1-weighted sequence (0.6 mm isotropic voxel size) for anatomical registration.
  2. High-Res T2-weighted TSE: A slab sequence acquired perpendicular to the long axis of the hippocampus (resolution: 0.4 x 0.4 x 1.0 mm) to visualize internal MTL structures.

MTL areas segmentation: To address the specific contributions of MTL subregions, we utilized the Automated Segmentation of Hippocampal Subfields (ASHS) algorithm. T2-weighted images were segmented into specific subfields including the perirhinal cortex (Area 35/36), parahippocampal cortex, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampal subfields (CA1, CA2/3, DG, Subiculum). All segmentations underwent rigorous manual quality control to correct for segmentation errors; subjects with severe motion artifacts were excluded from the MRI analysis.


Biomarkers and Statistical Analysis: Baseline plasma pTau217 levels were quantified to assess underlying Alzheimer’s pathology. Statistical analyses focused on training-related changes (Delta = Post - Pre) in behavior and subfield volumes. We utilized linear models to examine: 1) Group differences in behavioral transfer, 2) The relationship between Area 36 volume change and behavioral gains, and 3) The modulation of these gains by baseline pathology (using log-transformed pTau217 values).

Results

Behavioral training and near-transfer effects:  ANOVA models revealed that the training intervention elicited robust, category-specific improvements. The Object-trained group demonstrated near-transfer showing enhanced mnemonic discrimination performance for untrained scene stimuli compared to the active control group. Importantly, gains in the trained MD-OS task performance (A prime change) were positively linked with gains in the independent Mnemonic Similarity Task (MST) only for the training group (p = .009, R2 = .08) but not the control group (p = .538). Decomposition of this effect revealed that the cross-modality transfer drove it: the near-transfer gains to the untrained stimulus modality were specifically linked to MST performance gains (p = .018, R2 = .07). 

Structural plasticity in the perirhinal cortex: Longitudinal 7T MRI analysis showed no significant group-level changes in gray matter volume across hippocampal subfields (p > .05), suggesting that training did not induce gross morphological changes at the group level. However, individual variability in structural plasticity was predictive of behavioral transfer. Increases in the left perirhinal cortex (Area 36) volume were significantly associated with performance gains in the untrained scene modality (near-transfer) observed in the training but not the control group (p = .001, R2 = .29). This suggests that microstructural integrity in the perirhinal cortex supports the generalization of memory precision gains across category-selective pathways.

Modulation by pathology and trade-off with associative memory: While structural plasticity supported precision, baseline Alzheimer’s pathology was inversely to the training trajectory. We observed a significant behavioral trade-off within the training group: gains in mnemonic precision were inversely related to gains in object-in-place associative memory (p = .002, R2 = .11). This trade-off was not observed in the control group which displayed a non-significant positive association instead (p = .157, R2= .09). 

Baseline plasma pTau217 levels (log-transformed) significantly modulated this dissociation. Higher baseline pathology was associated with lower gains in the trained mnemonic precision task (p = .029, R2 = .09) but higher gains in associative retrieval (p = .004, R2 = .10). Notably, baseline pTau217 did not significantly predict associative performance at baseline (p = .116), indicating that this divergence was a specific, training-induced response. Together, these data suggest that individuals with elevated pathology undergo a compensatory shift, relying on associative generalization strategies when precise mnemonic discrimination mechanisms are compromised.

Conclusion

This study provides robust evidence that high-fidelity mnemonic discrimination training can elicit category-specific behavioral improvements and near-transfer effects in cognitively healthy older adults. By leveraging high-resolution 7T MRI, we identified the structural underpinnings of this transfer: individual plasticity within the left perirhinal cortex (Area 36) predicted the generalization of mnemonic precision to the untrained scene modality.

Theoretical Implications: Challenging MTL Modularity.

Our finding that perirhinal (Area 36) plasticity supports transfer to scene stimuli challenges the strict dichotomy of the "Two-Streams" hypothesis, which typically segregates object processing to the perirhinal cortex (PRC) and scene processing to the parahippocampal cortex (PHC). The fact that structural integrity in an "object-selective" region facilitated performance gains in the scene domain suggests that the PRC may support a generalized computational mechanism for pattern separation that transcends rigid category boundaries. This highlights the unique utility of 7T MRI in revealing how specific subfields contribute to cross-modal cognitive flexibility, a nuance likely missed by lower-resolution imaging.

The Strategic Shift: A New Model of Intervention Response.

Most notably, our results demonstrate that the aging brain does not respond to training uniformly. We identified a fundamental dissociation driven by underlying Alzheimer’s pathology. While individuals with low pathology showed a “canonical” precision memory improvement, those with elevated pTau217 levels exhibited a compensatory shift in strategy. The observation that high pathology constrained mnemonic precision gains but enhanced associative retrieval gains suggests that when specific neural circuits (e.g., the trans-entorhinal precision pathway) are compromised, the brain adaptively recruits alternative mechanisms (e.g., associative generalization) to solve the task. Crucially, this "trade-off" was only visible through the lens of the intervention, as baseline performance did not reveal these distinct profiles. This suggests that cognitive training can act as a "stress test" to reveal latent compensatory phenotypes.

Clinical and Future Directions. 

These findings have important implications for the design of non-pharmacological interventions. First, they argue for precision medicine in cognitive training: The efficacy and mechanism of training may depend on biomarker status. Future trials should stratify by pathology, as "at-risk" individuals may require different training targets (e.g., boosting compensatory associative strategies). Second, they highlight the utility of biomarkers as predictors: plasma pTau217 may be a sensitive modulator of training responsiveness, suggesting that blood-based biomarkers could effectively predict not just risk but the specific cognitive trajectory an individual will take during rehabilitation.

In conclusion, while mnemonic training is linked to structural changes in the perirhinal cortex supporting memory precision, underlying pathology may force a strategic reorganization of these mechanisms. This highlights the plasticity of the aging brain not just in terms of "improvement," but in its capacity for functional adaptation in the face of neurodegeneration.
