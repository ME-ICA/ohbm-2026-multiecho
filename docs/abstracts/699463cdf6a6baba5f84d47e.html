Introduction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) harmonization is essential for mitigating scanner-related variability and enabling reliable inference in multi-site studies. While harmonization methods for cross-sectional data are well established, substantially less attention has been given to longitudinal harmonization, even though longitudinal imaging is increasingly central to neuroscience research. ComBat,1 the most widely used tool for cross-sectional harmonization, has been extended to longitudinal settings as long-Combat.2 However, long-Combat assumes a simple random-intercept-only structure and inherits key limitations of the ComBat framework, namely a reliance on linear additive models and dependence on scanner/site ID. BARTharm,3 a Bayesian non-parametric harmonization model, has recently addressed ComBat shortcomings in cross-sectional data. It flexibly models scanner and biological effects as non-linear, data-adaptive functions, leveraging Image Quality Metrics (IQMs) as continuous proxies for scanner variation instead of scanner IDs, and does not require large site-specific sample sizes, nor a collection of voxels, vertices or ROIs as it is applied to each imaging feature individually. We have shown that BARTharm outperforms ComBat in challenging settings involving non-linearity, model misspecification, and correlated scanner-biology effects, improving accuracy but also remaining well calibrated where Combat exhibits substantial inflation.

Here we introduce BARTharm-long, the first longitudinal, non-linear, IQM-based harmonization method. BARTharm-long extends BARTharm by incorporating subject-specific random intercepts and slopes, thereby explicitly modelling within-subject correlation and accommodating diverse longitudinal trajectories. Using simulations and real-world imaging data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study,4 we show that BARTharm-long improves removal of scanner-related variance over long-Combat while preserving biological signal. 

Methods

Figure 1 presents our modeling approach. BARTharm-long maintains the core structure of BARTharm and explicitly separates the effect of biological covariates (i.e., covariates of interest) from scanner-related variation captured through IQMs. These two components are modelled using independent Bayesian Additive Regression Tree (BART) ensembles,3 allowing the method to flexibly capture additive, non-linear relationships and high-dimensional interactions. To model repeated measurements and account for within-individual correlation, BARTharm-long includes hierarchical random effects, individual-specific intercept and time-slope, with a sparsity-inducing horseshoe prior that shrinks negligible deviations while preserving meaningful subject-level variation.

For each imaging feature, the observed measurement yij for participant i at visit j is therefore decomposed into three components: a scanner/IQM effect μ, a biological effect τ, and individual-specific deviations (Eq. 1, Fig. 1). After fitting the model via a three-stage Gibbs sampler, we obtain posterior estimates for all of these components. The harmonised, “clean” measurement at each visit is then obtained by subtracting the estimated scanner effect from the original data (Eq. 2, Fig. 1). This formulation enables BARTharm-long to fully disentangle scanner/IQM-driven variability from true biological longitudinal signal, while adapting flexibly to complex non-linear relationships and heterogeneous visit schedules.

To evaluate BARTharm-long and benchmark it over long-Combat, we design a semi-synthetic simulation study. We extract longitudinal data for a subset of 500 participants over 5 visits from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study.4 We used real biological covariates, such as age, sex, education and cognitive tests; the actual IQM covariates extracted from each visit using MRIQC5; and the scanner IDs. We then simulate a clean outcome, influenced by biological signals and random effects, with random intercepts and slopes sampled per subject, setting 25% near zero to induce some level of sparsity; and a noisy, observed outcome incorporating scanner-related contributions. Biological and scanner effects are modelled either linearly or non-linearly.

For real data analysis, we apply BARTharm-long and long-Combat to a subset of 878 participants from the ABCD study. On average, each participant had 2.28 visits, for a total of 2002 observations. We used biological and IQM covariates and scanner IDs as described above, to harmonize 71 brain imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs), with both volumes and cortical thicknesses for each of these.

Results

Fig. 2a shows simulation results. Across all scenarios, BARTharm-long outperforms long-ComBat: it achieves lower RMSE, indicating more accurate recovery of the clean biological signal, and yields consistently weaker correlations with IQMs. This shows that long-ComBat fails to fully remove scanner-related IQM effects, even in settings that match its linear assumptions, while BARTharm-long robustly eliminates scanner contributions and preserves the underlying biology. 

On the ABCD data, Fig. 2b-1 shows the results of regressing each IDP on the NIH Toolbox Picture Sequence Memory Test, adjusting for age, sex, ethnicity, using raw, long-Combat, and BARTharm-long IDPs.  BARTharm-long reveals several associations (p-value < 0.05) that were not significant before harmonisation, or after long-Combat harmonization. Among these newly significant associations we find regions including the inferior parietal lobule, superior parietal cortex, and precuneus, which have been consistently linked to episodic memory, visuospatial integration, and sequence retrieval. The emergence of these associations after harmonisation suggests that BARTharm-long can uncover meaningful biological signals that were previously obscured by scanner-driven noise.

We then evaluate longitudinal reliability by comparing intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) before and after harmonisation. The ICC is a ratio of between-subject variation to total variation and it quantifies the proportion of total variance attributable to stable between-subject differences; higher values are desirable as they indicate more reliable distinctions between individuals. Figure 2b-2 displays the change in ICC relative to raw data for each IDP across harmonisation methods. BARTharm-long most often substantially increases ICC across regions, indicating improved longitudinal reliability through a reduction in scanner-driven within-subject variability. In contrast, long-ComBat generally shows negative ΔICC, reflecting its inability to effectively reduce within-subject noise. The strong and widespread ICC gains under BARTharm-long arise from its modelling strategy: scan-specific IQM-based correction combined with subject-specific random slopes and intercepts. This structure enables BARTharm-long to effectively separate biological trajectories from scanner artefacts, yielding harmonised measurements that are more stable over time and better suited for longitudinal inference.

Conclusion

Our results show that BARTharm-long is an effective and flexible approach for harmonising longitudinal MRI data. By modelling scanner effects directly from IQMs and using separate BART ensembles, with subject-specific random effects, it adapts to heterogeneous acquisition conditions while preserving meaningful biological structure. Across simulations and ABCD data, BARTharm-long reduces scanner-driven variance, improves reliability, and enhances detection of biologically plausible associations. Its scan-specific, IQM-driven corrections overcome the limitations of site-level methods such as long-Combat, yielding cleaner and more stable longitudinal measurements.
