Introduction

Schizophrenia is associated with stereotypical grey matter volume (GMV) changes throughout the brain which progress over the course of disease. ^1 Robust cross-sectional and longitudinal GMV reductions are observed in the frontal and temporal cortices, as well as in the limbic system and thalamus. However, GMV changes in schizophrenia arise from other processes besides just disruptions in brain structure. In particular, brain changes in schizophrenia also arise from complex interactions with cellular processes – as shaped by genetic risk factors. While a range of genome-wide association studies and next-generation sequencing techniques have identified hundreds of risk factors, the implicated genetic variants do not converge on clear mechanistic pathways and disease mechanisms remain poorly understood.

Recent work from our group and others has modelled the spatial distribution of GMV changes in schizophrenia as a function of the brain’s anatomical connectivity. Simple network spreading models, using only the structural connectivity between regions, can reliably model these GMV changes. ^1, 2 However, by construction, these models do not use gene expression data; they are therefore unable to explain the underlying molecular mechanisms behind these results.

Recently, sophisticated dynamical systems models which integrate both genomic and tractography data have been developed to describe patterns of GMV changes in neurodegenerative diseases. Here, we translate these models from neurodegenerative diseases to schizophrenia. We expand on existing models of GMV changes by integrating interactions between brain networks and genetic risk factors. Specifically, we apply an epidemiological agent-based spreading model to simulate the movement of pathological gene products along the connectome and predict the resulting spatial pattern of GMV reductions.

Methods

T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data in 857 patients (69.6% male) and 986 matched controls (61.4% male) acquired across 11 different studies and 17 sites were pooled using a linear mixed-effects model to develop a robust model of brain changes in schizophrenia. These maps were parcellated into 100 cortical and 32 subcortical regions. Structural connectivity matrices were derived from the diffusion MRI data of the Human Connectome Project, resulting in a 132×132 group-averaged connectome with 35% binary density. Gene expression profiles from microarray data across the whole brain were provided by the Allen Human Brain Atlas and processed using established recommendations. ^3 

This project employs the agent-based Susceptible-Infected-Removed (SIR) epidemic spreading model as previously described (Fig. 1) ^4. In its simplest form, the SIR model describes the dynamics of a population where susceptible agents are infected with a disease contagion and subsequently removed from the population. In this application, susceptible agents are normal proteins which are synthesised by some risk gene and removed by some clearance gene according to regional gene expression levels. These proteins are seeded with an infectious disease contagion which infects nearby proteins within a region and spreads across white matter connections to infect neighbouring regions. We consider all brain-expressed genes passing quality control metrics as candidate risk genes.

Model performance was compared with spatial null models generated using the BrainSMASH toolbox and rewired connectome nulls using the Maslov-Sneppen algorithm. Multiple comparisons were corrected for using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. A consensus set of gene pairs were selected for gene enrichment using the gene score resampling method. Associations with patient genetic data were performed by correlating SIR model gene weights with patient differentially expressed genes or genome wide association studies using the aggregate fold change method. ^5 Epicentre identification was performed by identifying the seed regions that produced the best model fit for each statistically significant gene pair.

Results

We simulated pathological processes and subsequent atrophy across all pairwise combinations of risk and clearance genes and all seed regions to identify any combinations that could recapitulate empirical GMV changes in schizophrenia. The resulting simulated atrophy maps were compared to the group consensus empirical atrophy maps to determine the maximum correlation per gene-seed combination. Across all gene pairs, the maximum model fit reached 0.72 (for gene pair [RNF144A, TCAP] Fig. 2A).

To identify significant gene pair correlations, we compared experimental performance to spatial and rewired null models. Gene pairs that were significantly associated with schizophrenia had a mean model fit of r = 0.62 (Fig. 2B). Risk genes were selected for gene enrichment analysis using the gene score resampling method. Broadly, we found that there were two types of categories identified by this analysis, genes involved in (1) synaptic, or (2) ribosomal cellular functions (Fig. 2C).

We applied PCA to our significant risk genes and correlated component gene weights with genes identified by RNA-seq or GWAS. Here we found that component 1 (associated with metabolic functions) was significantly associated with GWAS findings and component 4 (associated with synaptic functions) was associated with differentially expressed genes from RNA-seq and GWAS.

Finally, we identified the seed region for epicentre analysis which produces the highest correlation for each gene pair. These clustered into several seed regions, primarily in the temporal lobe, with an additional highly performing seed region in the orbitofrontal cortex (Fig. 2E).

Conclusion

Our agent-based model integrates genomic and connectomic data to simulate grey matter volume changes in schizophrenia. We observe that grey matter volume changes are specifically related to synaptic and ribosomal dysfunction. We validate these changes in patient datasets and identify the temporal lobe as a putative epicentre of GMV changes. Our results indicate that these processes together accumulate locally and propagate along the connectome to shape the distribution of GMV changes in psychosis.
