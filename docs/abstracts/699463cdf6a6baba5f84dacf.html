Introduction

The Hemodynamic Response Function (HRF) reflects neurovascular coupling in the brain and plays a critical role in understanding the neural activity. However, accurately modeling and predicting HRF waveforms remains challenging. In this study, we explored decoding neuronal activity from fMRI using a recently proposed Deconvolutional Unrolled Neural Learning (DUNL) framework 1. By leveraging EEG-derived spectral features and synchronization metrics for neural code extraction, our approach jointly optimised the estimation of dynamic HRF kernels and latent neural activity. This interpretable deep-learning-based framework can also reveal distinct, age-specific hemodynamic signatures.

Methods

A subset of simultaneous EEG-fMRI data, including 20 elderly and 19 young adults, were used 2. EEG was recorded using a 128-channel MR-compatible EGI system, while high-resolution anatomical MRI and fMRI were acquired using a 3T GE scanner. Artifact-corrected EEG signals were projected to the cortex via Brainstorm (https://neuroimage.usc.edu/brainstorm) and parcellated into 68 regions of interests (ROIs) using the Desikan-Killiany atlas. Corresponding ROI-wise BOLD signals were also extracted from pre-processed fMRI data. To fully capture neural dynamics, we leveraged two types of EEG features for code extraction: Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) for spectral power and Phase Locking Value (PLV) to emphasise synchronization.

For the STFT method, fMRI signals were interpolated by a factor of 20 and STFT was then applied to EEG signal using a 200 ms sliding window centered on each interpolated point. We calculated the average power in the alpha band as the features. Points with average alpha power higher than the mean + 3 std were treated as outliers, and their values were clipped to mean + 3 std to minimize their influence. The global mean and std were then updated. An interpolated point was identified as an event (code = 1) when its average power was no lower than mean +1.5 std and it was the maximum within its TR; otherwise, the code was set to 0.

For the PLV method, an interpolation factor of 5 was used. The PLV of alpha waves between different pairs of ROIs were estimated using a 500 ms sliding window, forming a 6868 matrix. The top 10% strongest connections were retained, and nodal degree was then calculated. An interpolated point was identified as an event (code = 1) when its degree was higher than 8 and it was the maximum within the corresponding TR; otherwise, the code was set to 0.

The DUNL framework was used to reconstruct fMRI signals by jointly learning dynamic convolution kernels and latent neural activity from the EEG codes. The EEG code acts as a mask to inform the model where neural activity tends to occur. A step function is used for kernel initialization for better convergence. Results

The DUNL model successfully learned ROI-wise and group-wise dynamic convolution kernels. Initialized with a step function (shown in Fig. 1), the kernels converged to the shapes of HRF. The dynamic kernels learned via two methods (STFT and PLV, displayed in different line styles) across elder group, young group, as well as elder+ young (all group) were obtained. We calculated the difference of Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) and Time-To-Peak (TTP) respectively to identify 14 ROIs that best characterized the differences between older and young adults. HRFs were also estimated used the rsHRF toolbox3. Figure 2 demonstrates the dynamic convolution kernels learned from the DUNL model across the 14 selected ROIs, highlighting the morphological variations between elders and young adults captured by our method. Across the 14 selected ROIs, hemodynamic kernels in older adults exhibited a broader FWHM compared to young adults (STFT: 10.65 s vs. 10.56 s; PLV: 10.19 s vs. 9.78 s). Additionally, we identified a counter-intuitive shorter TTP in older adults than young adults (STFT: 8.54 s vs 9.66 s; PLV: 10.23 s vs 10.57 s). A possible explanation for this phenomenon is that aging would lead to arterial stiffness. A higher stiffness would result in a higher pulse wave velocity4 for faster propagation and shorter TTP. 

Conclusion

The DUNL-based EEG-informed fMRI reconstruction framework demonstrated superior reconstruction fidelity and revealed distinct age-related differences in neurovascular coupling. By capturing broader FWHM and shorter TTP in older adults, this interpretable framework provides a promising tool modelling dynamic HRFs and distinguishing vascular aging from neural activity.
