Introduction

Advancements to Deuterium Metabolic Imaging (DMI) have greatly improved in vivo measurement of metabolic flux in humans1. Following ingestion of deuterated glucose, DMI allows the simultaneous detection of several key metabolites relevant to brain energy metabolism. The deuterated glucose (Glc) signal reflects glucose uptake, while downstream signals from glutamate/glutamine (Glx) and lactate (Lac) report glucose metabolism via the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and lactate dehydrogenase pathways, respectively. Additionally, the HDO signal provides an indirect measure of deuterated water production arising from glycolysis of deuterated glucose, serving as a global marker of metabolic activity. Activation of the visual cortex via visual stimulation is expected to increase local glucose uptake and downstream oxidative and non-oxidative glucose metabolism. Alternatively, deactivation of the default mode network would result in decreases in such processes, with no change expected in the largely unaffected frontal cortex.

Functional Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (fMRS) methods have enabled the tracking of metabolite and neurochemical dynamics in response to different stimuli or tasks2. However, such measures primarily reflect changes in metabolite pool sizes rather than direct metabolic flux. In contrast, DMI offers the potential to directly assess metabolic underpinnings associated with brain function, however work on functional DMI technique is currently limited. Ingestion of [2H7] labelled glucose (glucose-d7) allows significantly improved mapping of metabolite signals with higher signal-to-noise-ratio compared to ingestion of the commonly used [2H2] labelled glucose (glucose-d2)3. This abstract presents the first comparison of DMI following glucose-d7 and glucose-d2 ingestion in healthy human participants using a visual stimulation paradigm, to determine the feasibility of a functional DMI approach to measure brain activation-related metabolism in vivo.

Methods

Twelve healthy volunteers were enrolled for the study. Subjects were scanned on a 7T Philips Achieva scanner using 26.4cm-inner diameter, dual-tuned 1H/2H birdcage coil (Rapid Biomedical). Five subjects ingested glucose-d2, with three undergoing a visual stimulation intervention (age 24 ± 6 years; weight 64 ± 5 kg) and two experiencing no stimulation (age 22 ± 2; weight 56 ± 14 kg). Seven subjects ingested glucose-d7, with four scanned with stimulation (age 33 ± 13 years; weight 71 ± 6 kg) and three without (age 25 ± 5 years; weight 62 ± 20 kg). Subjects scanned at resting-state were instructed to keep their eyes closed, while those undergoing the stimulation intervention were instructed to keep their eyes open, experiencing a 8Hz 50s on / 10s off flickering checkerboard paradigm. Whole brain 2H 3D chemical shift images (CSI) were repeatedly acquired (FOV: 180x180x120 mm, TR: 230 ms, TE: 2.4 ms, 6 averages, 670 second acquisition time) to obtain DMI data. 1H scout and MPRAGE (1.4 mm) scans were acquired for positioning and to define brain regions. Subjects also underwent the scout scan, MPRAGE scan, and one 2H CSI before glucose ingestion to measure signal from naturally abundant HDO for use in normalisation.

Spectral peaks for HDO, glucose (Glc), glutamate/glutamine (Glx) and lactate (Lac) were fitted in MATLAB (MathWorks) using the OXSA-AMARES toolbox4. Additional peaks were included for the fitting of Glc in glucose-d7 spectra, with linked amplitudes to represent the contributions from the different H sites in the two glucose anomers8. Figure 1 displays a flowchart visualisation showing the DMI acquisition protocol and a 2H CSI spectra overlaying an anatomical MPRAGE image of one glucose-d7 subject.

Anatomical images were aligned to the CSI dimensions, and non-brain voxels were removed using FSL BET. Alignment to the MNI (Montreal Neurological Institute) 152 template was performed on the MPRAGE images using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs), creating an outline for the same reshaping to be performed on the images’ corresponding 3D-CSI acquisitions. Normalisation was performed using the HDO peaks at natural abundance as reference to account for inter-subject variance. With all DMI data in the standard space, a visual cortex region of interest was determined using the Harvard-Oxford atlas, consisting of voxels which overlaid the intracalcarine cortex, occipital pole, cuneal cortex, and lingual gyrus. The Yeo default network parcellation map was used to define voxels overlaying the default mode network5, and the frontal lobe was defined using the standard MNI frontal lobe mask. Fitted peak amplitude values were then averaged across voxels for each region of interest. A demonstration of the pipeline used for alignment of 2H CSI spectra to MNI space and region of interest definition, as well as averaged HDO, Glucose, and Glx maps across multiple CSI acquisitions and subjects are also presented in Figure 1.

Results

Peak amplitude values for HDO, glucose, Glx, and lactate were all noticeably higher following ingestion of glucose-d7 compared to glucose-d2across all regions of interest, reflecting a potentially improved mapping of metabolite signals. Unfortunately, due to poor signal to noise resolution, lactate time courses and regional maps were not progressed for further analysis. No discernible differences were observed in visual cortex HDO time courses between resting and stimulated subjects following glucose-d2 ingestion. Glucose signal time course slopes in the glucose-d2 stimulated and non-stimulated subjects appeared the same across all regions. Finally, a slightly greater increase in Glx signal was observed in the visual cortex of stimulated subjects compared to non-stimulated subjects in the glucose-d2 cohort.

Following glucose-d7 ingestion in non-stimulated subjects, HDO levels were noticeably higher in the frontal cortex and default mode network compared to that of stimulated subjects, an effect not observed in the occipital lobe. Across all regions of interest, glucose peak amplitudes appeared higher in the non-stimulated group following glucose-d7 ingestion. In contrast to findings from the glucose-d2 cohort, no stimulation-related effects on regional Glx signals were discernible from the glucose-d7 data. Statistical testing revealed no significant stimulation-related changes in these metabolite time courses across either glucose-d2 or glucose-d7 subjects. Metabolite time courses across brain regions are presented in Figure 2a. Averaged metabolite maps demonstrated higher signal intensity surrounding the occipital lobe in both stimulated and non-stimulated subjects, consistent with the high baseline metabolic demand of visual cortex, but did not reveal a clear stimulation-related signal change. Metabolite maps aligned to MNI space and averaged over subjects across the tracer and intervention conditions are also visualised in Figure 2b.

Conclusion

Ingestion of glucose-d7 did increase metabolite signals significantly more than ingestion of glucose-d2, supporting earlier findings of an increased sensitivity, but this did not translate into an improved detection of regional metabolic changes associated with brain activation. Across tracer groups, stimulation-related differences in metabolite dynamics were not statistically significant, however a number of non-significant trends were observed in metabolite time courses. Notably, stimulated glucose-d7 subjects appeared to show lower HDO signals in the deactivated default mode network regions compared to those at rest, an effect not observed in the activated occipital regions. This decreased HDO output may reflect downregulation of glycolysis in the deactivated brain. In contrast, glucose-d2 subjects showed similar HDO signals between stimulation conditions in the DMN, suggesting that functional DMI with glucose-d7 may be particularly sensitive to regional decreases in metabolic activity. Lower HDO signals were also observed in the frontal lobe time courses of stimulated compared to non-stimulated glucose-d7 subjects, an area largely expected to be unaffected by the stimulus presentation, thus raising questions regarding robustness of functional DMI using glucose-d7 despite its improved labelling efficiency. 

A higher glucose signal was observed in non-stimulated subjects compared with stimulated subjects following glucose-d7 ingestion. Regrettably, this difference is unlikely to reflect a stimulation-related effect and instead may arise from confounding between-group body weight differences, which may have affected tracer dosing and consequently influenced glucose enrichment and downstream metabolite production. Glucose-d2 time courses did show an apparently larger Glx enrichment in occipital areas in the later acquisitions, patterns which were rather surprisingly not seen in the glucose-d7 subjects, however these outcomes were statistically insignificant. A recent meta-analysis pooling numerous studies of metabolic change following visual stimulation reported no significant effect of stimulation on glutamate and Glx measures2, with the authors noting thatonly paradigms employing sufficiently high stimulus intensity may elicit detectable glutamate changes. Therefore, the visual checkerboard stimulus used in this study may have been suboptimal for eliciting measurable metabolic flux changes. The study was also limited by a relatively small sample size, which not only impacted the study’s statistical power but also resulted in increasingly small group sizes which were vulnerable to inter-subject variability, such as in the case of participant’s body weights. Challenges in reliably measuring and fitting to lactate peak amplitudes further limited the measurement of non-oxidative metabolism. Future work could also utilise a within-subject design to better detect stimulation-induced metabolite changes by reducing variability in baseline signals. While functional DMI holds considerable promise, further methodological optimisation is required before it can achieve sensitivity comparable to more conventional fMRS approaches.

Overall, this research highlighted the promise of functional DMI for measuring whole brain and regional brain metabolism, but also its current limitations in detecting activation-related changes. The use of functional DMI with alternative tracers represents a novel research approach that, to our knowledge, has not yet been explored. Preliminary findings exemplify the need for methodological refinement and improved reproducibility to exploit DMI’s capabilities for functional neuroimaging.
