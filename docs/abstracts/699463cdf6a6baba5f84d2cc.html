Introduction

Observational neuroimaging studies in healthy and clinical populations have identified associations between temporal lobe structures and language and memory. However, lesion-symptom mapping provides stronger causal inference capable of identifying areas that are critical for cognition by relating focal damage to neuropsychological deficits.  Using this framework, surgical lesions from drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy offer a natural model for identifying the temporal regions essential for language and memory.  Specifically, damage to left middle, inferior, and ventral temporal structures, and fusiform gyrus predict language decline (primarily visual naming)1,2 while damage to medial temporal structures like the hippocampus and parahippocampus predict memory decline3-5.  Through leveraging a large, single-center cohort of TLE patients, this study offers one of the largest LSM studies of temporal structures to date.

Methods

We examined 146 surgical lesions (laser ablation, resection) for unilateral drug-resistant TLE (48% left TLE, 52% right TLE) with pre and postoperative neuropsychological testing from a single comprehensive epilepsy center (Emory University). Pre and postoperative testing (approximately one year after surgery) included the Boston Naming Test (BNT, n=139), Columbia Auditory Naming (CAN, n=76), immediate and delayed recall for Logical Memory (LM, n=116), and immediate and delayed recall for the Rey Auditory Verbal Learn Test (RAVLT, n=141). Lesions were manually drawn on stable post-operative T1-weighted MRI (Figure 1), transformed into MNI 1mm space with nii_preprocess, and analyzed in NiiStat according to the Glasser atlas for region-based analysis (180 regions per hemisphere). NiiStat uses a linear model to evaluate the relationship between amount of a region lesioned and postoperative outcomes. Models controlled for preoperative performance and lesion volume when predicting postoperative outcomes. Multiple comparisons were controlled using Bonferroni correction, and all models used a one-tailed test as lesions are expected to predict worse performance. Regions were included in analyses where at least 5% of patients had damage in that area, to ensure sufficient lesion coverage for stable statistical estimation.

Results

Only left hemisphere regions predicted decline in language and memory.  Language: Damage to the left perirhinal-entorhinal cortex predicted BNT decline (z=-2.80). Damage to the left subiculum (z=-3.01) and posteromedial parahippocampal gyrus (z=-3.15) predicted decline on the CAN.  Memory: Damage to the left entorhinal cortex (z=-3.29), hippocampus (z=-3.33), anterior fusiform gyrus (z=-3.06), medial parahippocampal gyrus (z=-3.79), and perirhinal-entorhinal cortex (z=-2.92) predicted decline on LM immediate recall.  Damage to the left entorhinal cortex (z=-3.16) and hippocampus (z=-2.87) predicted decline on LM delayed recall. Damage to the left entorhinal cortex (z=-3.83), presubiculum (z=-4.15), hippocampus (z=-3.21), perirhinal-entorhinal cortex (z=-2.99), and posteromedial parahippocampal gyrus (z=-2.83) predicted decline on the RAVLT immediate recall. Damage to the left entorhinal cortex (z=-3.83), presubiculum (z=-4.18), hippocampus (z=-3.29), and perirhinal-entorhinal cortex (z=-2.85) predicted decline on the RAVLT delayed recall.

Conclusion

Using a large cohort, we demonstrate critical temporal structures supporting language and memory functions in the context of surgical lesions for the treatment of TLE.  Language: Our findings on picture naming add to previous work demonstrating that left perirhinal and entorhinal structures were associated with language decline. In addition to picture naming, we demonstrated that damage to medial temporal structures predicts deficits in auditory naming. Memory: Our findings are consistent with previous work implicating the hippocampus, parahippocampus, and entorhinal cortex in memory.  Further, we found that damage to the anterior fusiform gyrus, perirhinal cortex, and presubiculum predicted memory decline. 

This large-scale LSM study identifies critical temporal lobe regions supporting both language and memory, demonstrating that medial temporal structures are causal hubs for picture naming, auditory naming, and episodic memory. These findings refine models of temporal lobe organization and offer actionable clinical guidance by delineating regions where surgical compromise carries elevated risk of cognitive decline.
