Introduction

Schizophrenia arises from a complex interplay of genetic and environmental influences1. Stress is a major environmental risk factor and may perturb neurodevelopmental processes in heterogeneous ways shaped by individual genetic profiles1. Despite extensive evidence for such heterogeneity, few studies have examined neuroanatomical patterns linking stress to schizophrenia. Our previous work identified the caudate as a stress-mediating structure for schizophrenia in a small-putamen subgroup, motivating the need for an unbiased, whole-brain framework to delineate mediating pathways across multiple regions.

Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) is a widely used multivariate technique in neuroimaging studies. When combined with decision-tree approaches such as random forest (RF), CCA has been applied to parse heterogeneity in brain–symptom relationships in psychiatric conditions, such as autism2. Building on this strategy, we combined RF with multiset CCA (mCCA), a variant of CCA3, to identify patient subgroups exhibiting distinct volumetric patterns associated with both stress and symptom.

Methods

We analyzed a subset of participants from the IMAGEN cohort 4, the discovery sample included 975 individuals aged 19 years (mean[SD]: 18.50[0.73] years; 52.72% female). The data from a 4-year follow-up visit were used as a validation sample (N=994, aged 22.08[0.68] years; 54.83% female). In the IMAGEN cohort, stressful life events were assessed using 15 items of self-reported life events questionnaire. Psychotic-like experiences were assessed by community assessment of psychic experience. Polygenic risk score for schizophrenia (SZ-PRS) based on several p-value thresholds was calculated using Plink software. T1-weighted structural MRI images were collected using 3-T scanners and preprocessed by official procedures5. 

Pearson’s correlation was used to evaluate the association between symptom and stress and SZ-PRS. Significance was given by 1000 permutations and multiple comparison was corrected by the false discovery rate (FDR) correction (FDR q <0.05). Mediation analysis was performed by using the bruceR package.

Total 65 unaffected first-degree relatives of SZ patients (UFR-SZ, 22.58[5.08] years old with a range of 13-31 years, 58.46% female) and 54 early-onset schizophrenia (EOS) patients (16.48[1.31] years old with a range of 14-18 years, 42.59% female) were applied as clinical samples in this study. The psychosis symptom was assessed by using the Structured Interview for Prodromal Syndrome (SIPS) for UFR-SZ individuals and The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) for all EOS patients. T1-weighted MRI images were obtained from all individuals and volumes from 166 same regions were extracted after preprocessing in Freesurfer.

We integrated random forest with mCCA to identify subgroups characterized by a homogeneous stress–symptom–mediating brain pattern, referred to as RFHMCC. Individuals were split into two child nodes based on binary thresholds of 30 grouping features, including the principal components of 166 brain-region volumes and PRS-SZ measures. At each node, a random subset of grouping features was selected, and the split maximizing the homogeneity between the two child nodes was selected. Within each node, the random split-half is repeated for 10 times, the proportion in which the associations remain significant (out of 10) in second half sample was recorded as the homogeneity index (H) of this node. We repeated this process for 1000 times to collect the homogeneous nodes and two nodes were merged when they had significantly similar brain canonical components. 

Each terminal node represents a subgroup of individuals sharing a common canonical brain component that links stress to symptoms (‘stress~CC-brain~symptom’). Within each subgroup, the contribution of each regional volume to the CC-brain was quantified by its correlation coefficient. The canonical weights for each subgroup of brain regions were applied to the two clinical samples to validate the subgroups.

Results

In IMAGEN data, more stresses were associated with more psychotic symptoms (r = 0.347, p < 0.001, N = 975). No regional brain volume showed a significant mediating effect (ab p > 0.05, uncorrected) on the stress-symptom association. The homogeneous index of the whole sample was as low as H=0.1. 

Four homogeneous subgroups comprising 557 individuals with distinct stress-symptom mediating brain patterns were identified (Figure 1A-D), including subgroup 1 (G1, N=330), subgroup 2 (G2, N= 188), subgroup 3 (G3, N=102) and subgroup 4 (G4, N=110). Regions within the executive control network, auditory network, and default mode network (DMN) were involved in all subgroups, defining a tri-network pattern. Subgroup 2 uniquely engaged the salience network. Subgroup 3 exhibited a wide-spread pattern, involving additional contributions from the visual network, dorsal-attention network, and dorsal striatum. Subgroup 4 was similar to subgroup 3 but additionally involved the limbic regions. No significant group difference was identified in the age, sex ratio, stress level and psychotic symptom between subgroups. Individuals in subgroup 1 had a higher genetic risk for SZ than individuals in subgroup 2. 

Applying the canonical weights of subgroup 1 to the UFR-SZ individuals, the higher brain canonical component score was associated with the higher SIPS-D score (r=0.376, p=0.016), especially the item for the trouble with focus and attention (D3, r=0.334, p=0.033, Figure 1E). Same application in EOS patients showed that the higher brain canonical component score was associated with the higher PANSS-positive score (r=0.368, p=0.032, n=34), especially two items for delusions (P1, r=0.473, p=0.004) and suspiciousness/persecution (P6, r=0.491, p=0.007, Figure 1F).

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed a neuroimaging stratification algorithm to identify 4 subgroups of schizophrenia, each exhibiting a homogeneous stress-brain-psychotic symptom association, especially subgroup 1 had a tri-network pattern and higher genetic risks for schizophrenia. In two independent clinical samples, including the unaffected first-degree relatives of schizophrenia patients and the early-onset schizophrenia patients, we identified significant associations between the brain volumetric patterns and the symptoms in the subgroup 1. These findings underscore distinct brain neuroanatomical patterns mediating the association between the stress and psychotic symptom, highlighting the importance of parsing the heterogeneity in the neural pathways linking stress to schizophrenia.
