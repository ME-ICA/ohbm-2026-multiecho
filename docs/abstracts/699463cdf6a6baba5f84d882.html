Introduction

The pathological cascade of Alzheimer's Disease (AD) unfolds over a prolonged asymptomatic period that can last for many years, representing a clear disconnection between the onset of molecular pathology and downstream change in functional dynamics. This silent period is a critical window for intervention, yet a comprehensive understanding of the in vivo functional brain dynamics that precede overt cognitive decline remains unclear. 

While fluid and PET biomarkers have advanced significantly in identifying molecular pathology, non invasive imaging markers often lack the specificity required to map the precise topography of early functional brain alterations in preclinical disease stages. Neuropathological evidence indicates that isolated neurofibrillary tangles develop in select thalamic nuclei, as early as Braak stage I.^1 Other thalamic nuclei also demonstrate diverse connectivity profiles to cortical networks affected in AD, suggesting that subtle physiological changes in subcortical function may precede more widespread cortical dysfunction and memory impairment. 

Despite this, functional connectivity studies often focus heavily on a cortico-centric view of functional network breakdown, potentially overlooking the thalamus as an early modulator of these dynamics. We hypothesise that early AD pathology can be shown in a quantifiable disconnection in thalamocortical functional alignment, specifically within the principal gradients of brain connectivity. In this study, we adapt gradient based thalamocortical methods from developmental research, and apply connectopic gradient mapping to longitudinal resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data.^2 We aim to determine if subtle shifts in the principal axes of thalamic connectivity can measure disease progression and predict conversion to cognitive impairment before diagnosis of cognitive impairment. 

Methods

We analysed longitudinal rs-fMRI data from the PREVENT-AD (Pre-Symptomatic Evaluation of Experimental or Novel Treatments for Alzheimer's Disease) cohort, a study of pre-symptomatic individuals with a parental history of AD.^3 We identified 34 participants who later converted to Cognitive Impairment (future-CI) and matched them to 34 cognitively normal (CN) controls based on age, sex, and years of education. The groups had a mean age of 64.9 years for CN and 66.1 years for future-CI (p=0.31), resulting in a total dataset of N=204 scans acquired across three annual timepoints (Baseline, 12-months, and 24-months). 

Imaging data was pre-processed using a standardised, reproducible pipeline comprising fMRIPrep for minimal preprocessing and anatomical alignment, followed by XCP-D for further processing (including 24-parameter nuisance regressor and bandpass filtering at 0.008–0.09 Hz). To enable surface-based analysis, data was projected to the fs_LR32k surface space using Ciftify. Thalamocortical functional alignment was quantified using Connectopic Mapping.^4 We derived Connectopic Maps (CMAPs) to characterize the principal axes of functional organization across the thalamus based on the similarity of their connectivity profiles to the cortex (Figure 1B). CMAPs then were projected onto the cortical surface to generate Projection Maps (PMAPs) using a special regression approach. Specifically, we measure the similarity between the CMAP and thalamic activation at each TR, yielding a time-series of similarity coefficients (β). Then, we compute the dot product with the cortical activation to produce an average of cortical rs-fMRI maps across the TRs, weighted by β. See Figure 1A for PMAP projections on the cortical surface, and Figure 1B for volumetric CMAPs. 

To quantify longitudinal effects, we calculated Network Projection Scores (NPS). The NPS represents the subject-specific thalamic PMAPs values across cortical resting-state networks (Yeo-7 atlas template), providing a metric of thalamic alignment across several networks. Mass univariate analysis was performed on NPS values for each resting-state network independently. For univariate assessments, pairwise group differences (CN vs. future-CI) were evaluated at each cross-sectional timepoint using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Rank-Biserial Correlation (rrb) was calculated to evaluate effect size for each comparison. Longitudinal trajectories of these NPS values were modelled using Linear Mixed-Effects (LME) models, specifying a Group × Time interaction to detect divergent rates of change between future-CI and CN groups. We examined the relationship between changes in thalamocortical connectivity NPS and cognitive performance by correlating the longitudinal change scores against corresponding deltas for each of the major RBANS cognitive index scores using Pearson's correlation coefficient. The predictive utility of these gradient features was assessed using a Random Forest classification framework with 10-fold cross-validation. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed to interpret feature importance. Finally, Cox proportional hazards models were employed to determine if the rate of NPS decline (slope) could predict the time-to-conversion, providing a hazard ratio for clinical risk stratification. All results are controlled with Bonferroni corrections, with a significance threshold of p=0.01.

Results

Mass univariate analysis of the projection maps revealed a progressive, highly specific functional disconnection between the thalamus and DMN. This disruption was primarily observed in the first principal thalamic-DMN NPS, which captures the dominant axis of thalamic functional organization. Notably, this alteration was absent at baseline but emerged significantly at the 24-month follow-up, on average 4.3 years ahead of a conversion to cognitive impairment, and showing a large effect size (p<0.01 rrb =−0.61). 

LME modelling confirmed a significant divergent trajectory in the future-CI group compared to controls. As illustrated in Figure 2A, the future-CI group exhibited a progressive, linear decline in thalamic-DMN alignment (β=−0.006, p<0.01), whereas the control group remained stable over the two-year period. Figure 2B demonstrates the separation of individual data points at month 24, highlighting the specificity of this metric. Critically, the magnitude of this longitudinal connectivity decline was strongly correlated with the concurrent decline in delayed memory assessment scores (r=0.64, p<0.01), directly linking the alterations in DMN-NPS to the emergence of symptoms.

Random Forest analysis demonstrated 24-month DMN-NPS features could distinguish future converters from stable controls with high accuracy (AUC=0.84), with SHAP highlighting how the primary DMN-NPS act as the strongest predictors. Most significantly, Cox proportional hazards models revealed that the rate of thalamic-DMN NPS decline was a strong predictor of conversion risk. A one standard deviation preservation in functional connectivity was associated with a 54% reduction in the hazard of conversion (HR=0.46, 95% CI [0.32, 0.67], p<0.01). 

Conclusion

We present a novel gradient-based mapping approach that identifies progressive thalamic-DMN functional alterations as an early, clinically relevant candidate marker of preclinical AD. These findings challenge purely cortical models of network failure, highlighting the thalamus as a critical, early mediator of DMN vulnerability. By linking measurable functional change to memory decline and conversion risk years before overt symptoms, we offer a sensitive, non-invasive tool for tracking preclinical disease progression and stratifying risk of vulnerable individuals. The association between this metric and the trajectory of memory decline positions the NPS as a promising functional candidate marker for identifying and tracking the preclinical disease process with high sensitivity. Furthermore, these results highlight a need to further evaluate the complexity of thalamic function in early AD models. 

Beyond the traditional view of the thalamus as a simple passive relay, our findings align with the perspective that it functions as a critical integrative hub for memory and cognition (i.e. in the wider Papez circuit).^5 The progressive nature of this functional decline shown may also coincide with an active, contributory pathological process within the thalamus, rather than isolated cortical amyloid pathology. With neuropathological evidence showing tau pathology appearing in select thalamic nuclei as early as Braak stage I, we suggest a potential modality bias, where the focus on cortical amyloid may obscure earlier subcortical tau-related pathology. Given the established role of the thalamus as a causal hub capable of modulating wider brain networks, primary thalamocortical alterations provides a plausible marker that could actively reflect DMN vulnerability.

In conclusion, our longitudinal, gradient-based analysis has identified a progressive thalamic-DMN dysconnectivity that emerges within an extended yet critical window, on average 4.3 years before the onset of cognitive impairment. The association between this metric and the trajectory of memory decline positions the NPS as a promising functional candidate marker for identifying and tracking the preclinical disease process with high sensitivity. Furthermore, the ability to detect small functional alterations years prior to clinical conversion enables options for targeted therapeutic intervention. This systems-level approach may also hold potential for identifying distinct neurophysiological subtypes of cognitive decline and could be extended to measure thalamocortical signals across a range of other degenerative conditions. Ultimately, our findings show the significant involvement of subcortical hubs in AD pathology and point towards a need for more integrated, systems level view of the functional brain dynamics that precede cognitive decline.
