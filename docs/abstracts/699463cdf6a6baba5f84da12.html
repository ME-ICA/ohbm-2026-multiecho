Introduction

Research to date on the neural correlates of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) has identified heightened activity in cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuitry involving orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), and dorsal striatum, at rest and in response to feared stimuli.1 OCD has also been associated with fear learning deficits,2 including blunted vmPFC activity during safety learning.3,4 However, little is known about the neural basis of compulsions, the hallmark behavioral patterns of OCD. In the present study, we employed a novel functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) paradigm to elicit compulsion-like behavior. 

Methods

Adults (n=31) recruited to represent a range of obsessive-compulsive traits (Mean Dimensional Obsessive-Compulsive Scale [DOCS]5 score=11.06, SD=10.86, clinical cutoff5=18) completed the Tap-To-Safety (TTS) Task, a novel experimental paradigm designed to elicit compulsion-like behavior (Figure 1A) during fMRI. A subset of participants (n=20) repeated the TTS Task at a 2-3 week interval. The task began with Pavlovian conditioning to a threat cue (CS+) paired with mild electric shock, safety cues never paired with shock (CS-), and safe generalization stimuli never paired with shock (GSs). On subsequent choice trials, participants could repeatedly tap a button to neutralize the risk of shock, but tapping interfered with their accrual of points. Finally, in an extinction phase, no stimuli were paired with shock. Linear mixed-effects models (LMEs) were conducted to assess effects of pre-neutralization neural activity on neutralization behavior. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were computed to assess test-retest reliability of task behavior and neural activity. Finally we assessed whether individual differences in obsessive-compulsive traits were linked with differences in generalization and extinction of task behavior and neural activity. 

Results

Elevated pre-neutralization activity to CS+ vs. CS- was observed in dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), lateral orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), intraparietal sulcus (IPS), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), and dorsal striatum (ps<.012; whole-brain results: Figure 1B), consistent with hypotheses. Analyses of brain-behavior relationships (Figure 1C) indicated that pre-neutralization neural activity was correlated with neutralization behavior across stimuli in dACC (main effect of neural activity: F[1,144]=20.24, ηp2=.14, p<.001), anterior insula (F[1,144]=7.81, ηp2=.06, p=.006), dlPFC (F[1,144]=13.4, ηp2=.10, p<0.001), and putamen (F[1,144]=19.9, ηp2=.16, p<0.001). We identified good test-retest reliability of neutralization behavior to CS+ (ICC=.73); moderate test-retest reliability of threat reactivity in anterior insula, dmPFC, dlPFC, and IPS (ICCs=.56–.70); and moderate test-retest reliability of pre-neutralization activity in dmPFC (ICC=.68), dACC (ICC=.54), lateral OFC (ICCs>.50), right putamen (ICC=.44), IPS (ICCs>.62), and left dlPFC (ICC=.55), indicating suitability for between-subjects analyses. In the full sample, obsessive-compulsive traits moderated change in neurobehavioral measures across trials of the Extinction phase. Specifically, those with higher DOCS scores showed: a trend of decreasing vmPFC activity to the former CS+ (F[1,142]=3.61, ηp2=.02, p=.060; Figure 1D); a trend of persistent right putamen pre-neutralization activity to the former CS+ (F[1,142]=3.67, p=.057, ηp2=.03; Figure 1E); and more RTN behavior to the former CS+ (F[1,148]=4.84, p=.029, ηp2=.03; Figure 1F).

Conclusion

Findings indicate that the TTS Task reliably activates the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuitry relevant for compulsive behavior in OCD. Impaired vmPFC safety signaling and persistent striatal activity may underlie excessive compulsion-like behavior. Future research with clinical samples can further establish the diagnostic and predictive validity of the task.
