Introduction

Clinical integration of neuroimaging methods remains limited despite major advances in research pipelines. Hospital computing environments rarely support modern neuroimaging tools, and installation hurdles, dependency conflicts, and inconsistent system configurations prevent clinicians from accessing emerging quantitative methods and integrating them into their everyday practice. Beyond installation challenges, programming analyses requires technical knowledge and time not always available to clinicians. This translation gap is particularly problematic in acquired brain injury (ABI) rehabilitation, where lesion characteristics, atrophy patterns, and network disruption have well-established links to symptoms and recovery but are not routinely available to clinicians.

Neurodesk is a portable, containerised neuroimaging platform that eliminates installation and compatibility barriers by providing fully reproducible, self-contained environments that run on personal computers, hospital workstations, and cloud systems. This infrastructure solves most of the installation challenges mentioned. We developed clinical workflows within Neurodesk using a co-design process with clinicians and clinical researchers working with stroke and neurodegenerative communication disorders. These workflows aim to make quantitative neuroimaging accessible in routine clinical practice through linking open datasets, automated lesion segmentation tools, interactive visualisation tools, and automatically generated clinician-facing and patient-facing reports.

Methods

We used a structured co-design approach to translate research-grade neuroimaging pipelines into clinically usable workflows. Clinicians from speech-language pathology and rehabilitation medicine, along with informatics infrastructure staff, participated in iterative design sessions focused on workflow requirements, usability constraints, and clinical reporting needs. Prototype interfaces were evaluated through informal feedback and prioritised revisions across several rounds of refinement to ensure alignment with real-world clinical workflows, documentation practices, and time constraints inherent to ABI rehabilitation settings.

All workflows were implemented within the Neurodesk ecosystem to ensure reproducibility, version control, and hardware-agnostic deployment. Each workflow runs without installation, dependency management, or system-level configuration, allowing clinical teams to execute the pipelines on standard hospital machines and across restricted health networks with limited administrative privileges. This design principle is central to the platform’s ability to operate within stringent hospital IT governance frameworks without requiring exceptions or bespoke configurations.

Three prototype workflows were developed: (1) Acute Lesion Segmentation Workflow, (2) Chronic Lesion Segmentation Workflow, and (3) Atrophy Mapping Workflow. In the first workflow, the inputs include diffusion-weighted images (DWI). The automated lesion segmentation is performed using Acute-stroke detection segmentation (ADS)^1. In the second workflow, the inputs are T1 images, and the lesion segmentation is performed with the LINDA package^2. In each case, the lesion segmentation is followed by the extraction of the normalised lesion location, volume, and atlas-based regional involvement. Several atlases are included in the workflow to allow clinicians to select the option that best fits their field. For both of the stroke workflows, outputs include an interactive 3D brain viewer for qualitative interpretation, a clinician-facing PDF report detailing lesion metrics and implicated networks and a patient-facing visual summary supporting education and goal-setting. The report structure was developed iteratively to balance clinical precision with accessibility, ensuring that outputs could be used both during multidisciplinary case discussions and in patient consultations.

In the third workflow, structural MRI is processed using FreeSurfer’s^3 morphometric pipeline to generate whole-brain and region-specific atrophy maps, which will then be compared to normative data^4. The workflow produces interactive overlays highlighting atrophic regions, quantitative tables aligned with clinical interpretability, and a patient-adapted visual summary.

Workflows are co-designed with clinicians with experience in ABI rehabilitation. Feedback focused on interpretability, relevance to clinical decision-making, and the usefulness of patient-facing materials. Additional feedback addressed workflow transparency, preferred terminology, level of quantitative detail, and the need for explicit linkage between imaging findings and functional communication outcomes. These insights guided refinements to colour schemes, lesion overlays, atlas selections, and text descriptors used in the generated reports. Deployment feasibility is assessed in hospital environments using standard Windows machines without administrative privileges.

Results

The Neurodesk workflows substantially reduced clinicians’ barriers to accessing quantitative neuroimaging results. The absence of installation steps or system configuration is a key advantage in hospital settings, where restrictive IT governance typically prevents the use of research pipelines. The interactive 3D viewer enhances understanding of lesion extent, structural changes, and potential network involvement. Clinician-facing reports are clear, concise, and clinically actionable, supporting case formulation and communication with multidisciplinary teams. Patient-facing reports are particularly valuable for educating individuals with ABI and for facilitating collaborative goal setting by providing simplified, anatomy-informed explanations that clinicians reported as improving patient engagement during sessions.

Informal feedback indicates that the workflows help bridge the gap between research methods and clinical interpretation, especially for clinicians without specialised neuroimaging training. Participants emphasised that consistent, reproducible processing across systems supports integration into team-based care and reduces reliance on subjective interpretation of imaging. Several clinicians noted that the lesion overlays and atlas-derived summaries improved their ability to contextualise neurological symptoms, particularly in cases where conventional radiology reports provided limited functional detail. In addition, usability testing highlighted the value of rapid turnaround time, allowing imaging-based insights to be incorporated into early rehabilitation planning.

Although not yet evaluated in a formal study, clinicians anticipated that access to lesion and atrophy information could improve confidence in interpreting neuroanatomical findings and enhance the precision of rehabilitation planning. Feedback also suggested that structured imaging outputs may facilitate more consistent mapping between neurological injury and tailored therapy approaches, offering a foundation for future clinical decision-support tools. These expectations will inform the design of upcoming formal evaluation studies assessing confidence, interpretative accuracy, and downstream clinical decision-making. These insights informed refinements of the interface, report templates, and result visualisation and continue to shape the next development cycle as workflows expand to additional ABI subpopulations.

Conclusion

Neurodesk enables clinically usable neuroimaging workflows by removing installation barriers and providing reproducible, portable, containerised pipelines that are compatible with restrictive hospital computing environments. Co-designed workflows for lesion segmentation and atrophy mapping demonstrate the feasibility of translating research-grade neuroimaging tools into formats that clinicians in ABI rehabilitation find interpretable, actionable, and aligned with routine clinical workflows. By integrating automated processing with interactive visualisation and structured clinician-facing and patient-facing reports, these workflows address longstanding challenges in accessibility, interpretability, and relevance of quantitative neuroimaging.

Preliminary feedback indicates strong potential to improve clinicians’ understanding of neuroanatomical features relevant to recovery, enhance communication within multidisciplinary teams, and support patient education and engagement. Importantly, the co-design process ensured that outputs prioritised clinical questions, functional relevance, and usability rather than solely technical optimisation. This approach highlights the value of clinician-centred design in bridging the persistent gap between neuroimaging research innovation and real-world application.

Beyond individual workflows, this work illustrates a scalable framework for embedding reproducible neuroimaging methods into clinical and clinical-research contexts. The use of containerised infrastructure ensures consistent execution across systems, reduces reliance on local technical expertise, and supports transparent versioning and governance. These features are critical for future integration of quantitative imaging into longitudinal care pathways, training environments, and collaborative research embedded within healthcare systems.

Immediate next steps focus on operationalising Neurodesk within secure infrastructure to facilitate clinical evaluation. This includes: (1) integration with Open Recon to enable on-scanner execution of lesion and atrophy workflows on Siemens MRI systems, allowing quantitative outputs to be generated at the point of acquisition; (2) deployment within a tertiary hospital environment under strict data governance protocols; (3) leveraging the secure infrastructure to scale the workflow collection to additional neurological and psychiatric conditions as well as more varied analyses to support broader clinical and translational use; and (4) conducting formal evaluation, assessing the impact of Neurodesk workflows on clinicians’ confidence, interpretative accuracy, and decision-making processes. Together, these steps aim to move beyond feasibility toward sustained clinical adoption.

Overall, Neurodesk provides a practical path toward embedding reproducible neuroimaging methods directly into clinical environments, supporting both current clinical decision-making and future data-driven rehabilitation research, while expanding the reach of advanced neuroimaging beyond traditional research settings.
