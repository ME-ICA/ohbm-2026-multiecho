Introduction

Misophonia is characterized by strong negative emotional and physiological reactions to specific trigger sounds, often distinct from responses to generally aversive stimuli. Although behavioral and clinical studies have documented the severity and specificity of these reactions, the neural mechanisms that differentiate trigger responses from general aversive processing remain poorly understood. Traditional univariate approaches, which focus on mean activation differences, may overlook distributed neural patterns that encode fine-grained distinctions between similar affective states. In contrast, multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) and machine learning approaches can reveal subtle, spatially distributed representational differences that are not detectable through univariate contrasts alone. Moreover, these methods also enable explicit predictive tests of whether neural activation patterns reliably distinguish trigger from aversive stimuli, which is especially important for conditions like misophonia, where idiosyncratic but robust neural signatures may underlie the intense subjective reactions to everyday sounds. Our prior work shows that individualized triggers can be classified based on physiological responses measured from facial muscle movement, sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems,1 suggesting that similarly discriminable patterns may exist in the brain. 

In this study, we applied whole-brain searchlight MVPA and ROI-based machine learning analyses to identify the neural patterns that discriminate Trigger from Aversive stimuli across three sensory modalities (audio, video, and imagery). By examining modality-specific representational profiles and testing their predictive utility, we sought to pinpoint the brain regions encoding misophonia-specific trigger responses.

Methods

Forty participants with clinical or subclinical misophonia were recruited based on the Duke-Vanderbilt Misophonia Screening Questionnaire (DVMSQ),2 along with 26 age-, biological sex-, and handedness-matched controls.  

To investigate the neural correlates of triggers across different sensory modalities, we used a 3 × 3 factorial design crossing Modality (hearing sounds, watching silent videos, generating auditory imagery) with Stimulus Condition (Trigger, Aversive, Non-aversive) using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) in a block design. Trigger stimuli (e.g., chewing) were individualized for each participant to ensure effectiveness.3 Aversive (e.g., infant crying) and Non-aversive (e.g., wind chimes) stimuli were validated in prior behavioral rating studies to equate arousal and vary in valence.  

Data for each modality were collected in separate fMRI sessions. Each block was 15 seconds. The same stimuli were presented in an identical order to matched misophonia-control pairs.  

FMRI data were acquired using a T2*-weighted multiband EPI sequence (TR = 1.5). Images were de-identified with PyDeface and preprocessed using the state-of-the-art fMRIPrep v25.1.4 pipeline. For each participant, voxel-wise activation patterns were estimated with a first-level GLM implemented in SPM, including 108 stimulus-block regressors (3 modalities × 3 conditions × 12 blocks), along with 48 nuisance regressors (16 per run: 12 head-motion parameters, mean white-matter and CSF signal, framewise displacement, and derivative of RMS variance over voxels). To obtain activation estimates for individual stimulus blocks, we generated a binary contrast for each block, resulting in 108 t-statistic maps per participant. These t-maps were used as input for subsequent analyses. 

For each sensory modality, we conducted whole-brain searchlight MVPA separately for participants in the misophonia group (Fig.1A). A spherical searchlight (6-mm radius) was centered on every voxel within a gray-matter mask. To identify brain regions that encode information distinguishing Trigger from Aversive stimuli, we performed cross-validated classification within each sphere. In each fold, a Naive Bayes classifier was trained on neural patterns from 11 Trigger and 11 Aversive blocks and tested on the left-out Trigger and Aversive blocks (leave-one-group-out cross-validation). Within each searchlight and each fold, we implemented univariate ANOVA-based feature selection to improve signal-to-noise ratio and reduce dimensionality. Voxels in the searchlight were ranked according to their F-statistic for the Trigger–Aversive comparison, and only the top 80% of voxels were retained. Classification accuracy was computed for each fold and averaged across folds. The resulting mean accuracy was assigned to the center voxel of the sphere, producing one accuracy map per participant per modality. Participant-level accuracy maps were submitted to group-level inference to identify regions reliably encoding Trigger vs. Aversive information across participants. Statistical maps were thresholded using non-parametric permutation testing (5,000 iterations), with family-wise-error (FWE) correction at p < .05. To validate the predictive utility, we conducted ROI-based MVPA using features defined by the thresholded group-level searchlight maps described above (Fig.1B). For each participant, a Naive Bayes classifier was trained to distinguish Trigger from Aversive blocks, with leave-one-group-out cross-validation. Classification accuracy was computed for each participant. At the group level, one-sample t-tests were used to determine whether accuracy exceeded chance (50%) within the misophonia and control groups. This analysis helps to validate if we can use the information from the brain regions identified as encoding Trigger vs. Aversive to predict triggers in misophonia participants.

Results

Searchlight MVPA revealed robust neural discrimination between Trigger and Aversive stimuli in the audio modality. Significant decoding emerged broadly across bilateral superior temporal gyrus (STG) and insula, with additional voxels in medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), supplementary motor area (SMA), supramarginal gyrus (SMG), and parahippocampal gyrus (PHG). For the video modality, discrimination was observed across extended regions of bilateral occipital cortex, precuneus (PreCun), superior frontal gyrus (SFG), and PHG, as well as scattered voxels in bilateral mPFC, dACC, SMA, and SMG. In contrast, the imagery modality yielded only several voxels surviving FWE correction, mainly in occipital cortex, precuneus, dACC, and PHG.

In a validation analysis, when training MVPA models using voxels identified by the searchlight analyses from misophonia participants, classification accuracy for distinguishing Trigger from Aversive blocks was significantly above chance in both groups. In the misophonia group, accuracy reached 73.33% for audio (p < .001), 81.25% for video (p < .001), and 54.79% for imagery (p = .019). In the control group, accuracy was likewise significantly above chance for audio (66.99%, p < .001), video (76.12%, p < .001), and imagery (56.57%, p < .005). 

Conclusion

Using MVPA and machine learning, we identified neural systems that reliably distinguish individualized misophonia triggers from general aversive stimuli across three sensory modalities. The regions identified (bilateral insula, SMA, SMG, and dACC) align with prior GLM-based findings,4 reinforcing their role in misophonia-specific processing. Sensory cortices also carried discriminative information for different modalities, including auditory cortex for audio, visual cortex for video, and precuneus for imagery. MVPA classifiers trained on misophonia participants’ neural data successfully distinguished Trigger from Aversive blocks in both groups, with significantly higher accuracy in misophonia participants for audio and video. Interestingly, control participants also showed above-chance classification, suggesting that trigger stimuli differ from aversive stimuli not only in emotional meaning but also in lower-level sensory features (e.g., pitch, temporal structure, visual contour) or semantic content. Considering that we combined both the sensory processing regions and other regions engaged in salience detection, affective integration, and contextual reflection to classify Trigger vs. Aversive, the results of high classification accuracy should be interpreted with caution, because it could be entirely driven by stimuli properties rather than misophonic responses. Despite these limitations, our preliminary analyses offer new insights into the neural mechanisms underlying misophonia and demonstrate the utility of machine learning approaches for identifying misophonia-specific neural signatures. This work also lays the foundation for future efforts to develop brain-based markers for trigger identification and misophonia diagnosis.
