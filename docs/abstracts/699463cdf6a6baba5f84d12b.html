Introduction

Adolescent-onset major depressive disorder (MDD) is a recurrent disorder that, in addition to being characterized by substantial behavioral symptoms, is hypothesized to adversely impact brain maturation. Typical brain development in adolescence is marked by a refinement of intrinsic functional brain networks (Edde et al., 2021; Sanders et al., 2023). Depressed adolescents, however, appear to have aberrant functional connectivity in the default mode (DMN), frontoparietal attentional, and limbic networks. In prior cross-sectional studies, intra-network DMN hyperconnectivity and inter-network DMN hypoconnectivity differentiated depressed adolescents from psychiatrically healthy controls (CTL), with anticorrelations between DMN and frontoparietal networks (as well as anticorrelations between frontoparietal and limbic networks) that were linked with greater depression severity (Tse et al., 2024). 

Methods

To better understand whether early-onset depression might alter brain maturation, we analyzed resting-state fMRI data from four independent cohorts. The first cohort included longitudinal data from 78 youth (57 MDD; 13.2-18.4 years) scanned on 3T MRIs using echo planner image (EPI) sequences, and the second cohort included cross-sectional data from 49 youth (35 MDD; 14.2-22.0) scanned on a 7T scanner using an EPI sequence. The third and fourth cohorts included CTL participants and were sourced from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, including longitudinal data (1113 scans) from 100 CTL youth (9.0-14.9) scanned on 3T scanners using axial planar sequences across three bi-annually collected waves, and the Human Connectome Project (HCP), including cross-sectional data from 479 young adults (22.0-26.0) scanned on 3T scanners using gradient-echo EPI sequences. We calculated whole-brain functional connectomes for all participants using the Shen atlas ( 268 regions-of-interest). To assess age-related differences in functional connectivity (FC) strength between depressed and non-depressed adolescents, we compared average whole-brain and network-level FC for CTLs and MDDs at every age for which data were available. We then iteratively compared whole-brain and network-level FC strength in each MDD participant with that of CTL participants at each year of age spanning from the MDD participant’s age of depression onset to their chronological age plus the corresponding age difference between these values (a proxy of burden of illness), using Pearson’s r correlations. The mean was computed for all correlations between a given MDD participant and each CTL participant that matched the age criteria for that iteration and then compared to the mean correlation for other age criterion iterations. This approach allowed us to determine whether depressed adolescents have FC patterns reflecting accelerated, delayed, or normative development compared with their non-depressed peers. 

Results

When examining whole-brain FC, we found that the age of best fit for MDD participants was an average of 3.52 months greater than their chronological age, reflecting a modest acceleration in maturation (Figure 1). However, there was considerable variability in the difference between actual age and age of best fit: 32 participants had a delay in maturation averaging 12.61 months, and 29 participants had an acceleration in maturation averaging 21.33 months. Chronological ages (accelerated group mean = 193.84 months; delayed group mean = 213.23 months; t = -3.69, p < 0.001) and burden of illness (accelerated group mean = 38.25 months; delayed group mean = 52.73 months; t= -2.86, p = 0.005) differed significantly between the accelerated and delayed maturation groups. We additionally identified network- and edge-level differences between the groups, finding that the accelerated maturation group had broadly stronger FC compared with the delayed group, and that accelerated maturation was most apparent in the frontoparietal network for the accelerated group (15.13 months ahead), while delayed maturation was most apparent in secondary visual cortex (V2) (12.61 months behind), as well as the medial frontal (8.86 months behind) and DMN (8.12 months behind).

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that early-onset MDD may lead to both delayed and accelerated maturation of functional brain networks. These results have important implications for understanding the neurobiological sequelae of adolescent MDD and may inform effective intervention strategies for rescuing behaviors compromised by such neural alterations.
