Introduction

Performing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at mid-field strengths (0.5T) offers reduced radiofrequency heating and fewer susceptibility artifacts when compared to higher, standard clinical field strength magnets – this presents unique opportunities for safely visualizing implants and neuromodulation devices. For example, imaging deep brain stimulation (DBS) implants can be used to confirm electrode placement after surgery. Additional MRI information (structural, functional, and diffusion imaging) from DBS patients may also assist with understanding the mechanisms behind treatment responses in Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, and other neurological disorders. For DBS patients, a pre-surgical MRI (1.5T or 3T) is performed for surgical planning; however, the choice of imaging modality after surgery is often restricted to computed tomography (CT) imaging due to associated safety concerns with electrical devices in an MRI scanner. Although post-operative CT scans can be used to visualize haemorrhage, these images suffer from metal streaking artifacts and lack the anatomical detail needed to determine electrode placement relative to the target structure. In addition, potential brain shift due to a loss of cerebrospinal fluid can lead to inaccuracies when trying to localize DBS probes after registration to pre-operative MR scans.1 At lower field strengths, less heating allows for safer imaging of metal implants, while decreased signal drop out at metal-tissue interfaces1,2 facilitates DBS lead localization. Therefore, 0.5T MRI may be a suitable alternative for routine post-operative imaging of DBS patients given that sufficient contrast can be obtained in deep brain regions. Our aim is to quantify deep brain contrast at 0.5T to determine optimal sequence parameters needed for the delineation of structures relevant to DBS targeting (e.g., thalamus, subthalamic nucleus, substantia nigra).3

Methods

For pre-operative visualization of target structures with 3T MRI, a Fast Gray Matter Acquisition T1 Inversion Recovery (FGATIR) sequence4 may be used, which is an optimized magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) technique for subcortical structures. The same imaging parameters cannot be directly translated to 0.5T because tissue properties and relaxation times change at different magnetic field strengths. Thus, we aim to collect axial T1-weighted MPRAGE scans across 10 healthy subjects with obtained REB approval on a Synaptive Medical head-only 0.5T MRI scanner. For a single subject, brain scans were acquired at different inversion times (TIs) ranging from 50 to 750ms with all other parameters fixed (𝛼=8.5°, ETL=100, FOV=25cm, slab thickness=18cm, TR/TE/IR-IR TR: 5.452/2.1805/1600ms, BW=333Hz/pixel, 1.5mm isotropic resolution) to determine the optimal TI to maximize contrast in deep brain structures (see Figure 1); the scan time for one 3D acquisition was 6:07min.

Anatomical MRI data for 3 initial subjects was processed with the FSL/FMRIB software library v6.0 [FMRIB Centre, University of Oxford, UK]. Skull-stripping was done with the Brain Extraction Tool (BET) using a fractional intensity threshold of 0.3-0.5 to avoid removing brain tissue at some contrast levels. Registration was performed on an intra-subject basis with FMRIB’s Linear Image Registration Tool (FLIRT) using DOF=6 and correlation ratio as the cost function. Tissue segmentations for white matter (WM), gray matter (GM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) were generated by voxel intensity thresholding and bias field correction via Gaussian and Markov Random Field modelling in FMRIB's Automated Segmentation Tool (FAST). For subcortical structures, segmentation was done with the Integrated Registration and Segmentation Tool (FIRST) which registers the brain to a non-linear MNI152 template and applies a Bayesian-model based approach to produce boundary corrected meshes and volume estimates for various regions of interest (ROIs) such as the accumbens, amygdala, caudate, hippocampus, pallidum, putamen, thalamus, and brain stem. Manual segmentation was also performed to identify the substantia nigra (SN) and surrounding tissues since these smaller subcortical structures were not included in the standard atlas. Mean signal intensities for each ROI were extracted by applying the binary masks from the aforementioned segmentation steps. These values were then used to compare the absolute difference between normalized signal intensities at the various inversion times. The contrast-to-noise ratio was also calculated using CNR=|S1-S2|/σ, where S1 and S2 are average signal intensities for ROI 1 and ROI 2, and σ is the standard deviation of background noise.

Results

The images obtained from the initial inversion time sweep experiments demonstrate changes in contrast for different tissues and subcortical structures with greater nulling of most tissue types at intermediate time points. Looking at the SN and surrounding tissues (Figure 2), a maximum absolute normalized contrast difference of 0.14 can be achieved with an inversion time of 250ms with a maximum CNR of 51.32 at the same TI (compared to a minimum CNR of 5.25 at TI=700ms). Better gray and white matter contrast occurred at longer inversion times, akin to a typical structural T1-weighted sequence, with an absolute normalized signal difference around 0.15. In addition to increased contrast in the SN at TI=250ms, an absolute difference in GM-WM contrast of 0.20 was seen with increased white matter nulling, as opposed to gray matter nulling at TI=550ms with a signal difference of 0.16. The maximum CNR between GM and WM was 114.90 at TI=650ms (CNR=105.47 at TI=550ms), and a minimum CNR of 17.80 at TI=50ms due to greater GM-WM signal magnetization caused by the inversion pulse. 

Decreased intensity differences between gray and white matter were observed at intermediate inversion times around 400ms, coinciding with the null point of these tissue types. Around the same inversion time, bounce point artifacts (seen as dark outlines between tissues) can be observed in the MPRAGE scans due to phase cancellation in boundary voxels containing a mix of tissue types with different recovery times. A shift in contrast can also be seen across inversion times for subcortical structures with greater nulling of some regions, such as the thalamus, putamen, and hippocampus, with longer TIs (i.e., transitioning from positive to negative contrast in these areas). 

To visualize smaller structures needed in targeting, millimeter or even submillimeter resolution is necessary. While our current 1.5mm isotropic scan takes approximately 6 min, increasing the matrix size to get 1mm isotropic voxels results in a scan time of 14:57min. By reducing the repetition time of our MPRAGE sequence to ~920ms, a 1mm isotropic scan with similar contrast can be acquired at 0.5T in 11:04min, which is comparable to a 3T FGATIR with 1mm in-plane resolution and 2mm slice thickness4. Using the inversion recovery curves, average T1s for the SN, GM, and WM were calculated to be 660ms, 953ms, and 557ms, respectively. Since the T1 relaxation times for most tissues increase with greater field strength, leveraging lower field magnets may help decrease the required repetition time, thus improving overall scan time. 

Conclusion

Sufficient contrast of deep brain structures, along with reduced heating and fewer metal artifacts, motivate the use of 0.5T MRI for routine pre-/post-operative imaging for DBS patients and other neuromodulation interventions. So far, we have demonstrated that an MPRAGE protocol with an inversion time of 250ms can be used for white matter nulling and delineation of the substantia nigra. On the other hand, using a longer inversion time of 550ms offers improved gray matter and white matter contrast, with negative contrast in most subcortical structures. Having an optimized inversion recovery sequence facilitates the use of 0.5T MRI as a pre-operative imaging modality for DBS targeting. When coupled with the enhanced safety of imaging neuromodulation devices, 0.5T MRI also provides a compelling option for routine post-operative imaging to confirm electrode placement and to monitor patient outcomes in the long-term. 

Further work is being done to compare contrast differences across a larger range of volunteers to confirm whether these results can be generalized to a wider patient population, as well as looking into other DBS targets for Parkinson’s patients (e.g., globus pallidus internus and subthalamic nucleus) with more specialized brain atlases5. We also plan to analyze potential T1 contrast changes due to age and how that affects the optimal protocol parameters.
