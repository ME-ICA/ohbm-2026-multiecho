Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) neurodegeneration manifests from macrostructural atrophy to large-scale functional network dysconnectivity, exhibiting marked inter-individual heterogeneity. Clinical1, biomarker, and structural MRI2 studies suggest distinct AD subtypes, but functional neuroimaging in the preclinical (asymptomatic) stage remains underexplored. In particular, we lack understanding of how early amyloid-beta (Aβ)-associated atrophy patterns perturb brain network connectivity and cognitive domains. To address this gap, we propose an open-source computational framework for disentangling the heterogeneity of functional affectations inferred from network mapping based on gray matter atrophy patterns. This data-driven approach identifies latent functional dysconnectivity phenotypes without imposing a priori network constraints. To enhance interpretability, we incorporated a cognitive inference step based on meta-analytic functional mapping (COGNET-Stroke3), enabling us to identify the cognitive processes most consistently associated with each subtype’s dysconnectivity profile.

Methods

We analyzed a group of 69 amyloid-positive (A+), and 174 amyloid and tau negative (A-T-) cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals (65.60±6.67 years) included in the Gipuzkoa Alzheimer Project (GAP) and the DEBA project, both from the Basque Country in Northern Spain. Each participant underwent a comprehensive clinical, neurological and neuropsychological assessment, blood sampling, structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) collection. Cognitive status was determined in consensus meetings between neurologists and neuropsychologists. CU participants showed no objective cognitive impairment in any domain and preserved functional independence. CSF Aβ, phosphorylated tau and total tau biomarkers were interpreted according to established cut-off values to derive amyloid (A), tau pathology (T) and neurodegeneration (N) status, respectively. Participants with significant vascular burden defined by Fazekas ≥ 2 or ≥4 microbleeds were excluded.

For each participant, a T1-weighted structural MRI scan at 1mm isotropic resolution (3T) was acquired, then bias-field corrected using the N4BiasFieldCorrection algorithm available in ANTs, ACPC line-aligned, and coregistered to the MNI152NLin6Asym template using FSL. No images were discarded after visual inspection for coregistration errors.

Voxel Based Morphometry (VBM) was then performed and spatially smoothed with a 3D Gaussian filter at a FWHM of 6mm to estimate the voxel-level gray matter (GM) probability maps in the two groups (A-T- and A+). A normative General Linear Model was built in the CU A-T- GM space, including age, age-squared, sex, and total intracranial volume as covariates. Then W-score maps were computed in CU A+ group to locate significant GM deviations from the reference CU A-T- population, (atrophy seeds), binarized at W≤1.96.

To map the atrophy seeds to their respective functional dysconnectivity patterns, resting-state functional MRI data from 1000 healthy controls was obtained from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) dataset. The functional connectivity maps for each CU A+ participant were generated through seed-based connectivity (SBC) analysis following the previously described pipeline4. Summarizing, the atrophy seed of each CU A+ participant in the MNI152 space was used as the seed region for SBC, which was conducted separately for each HCP subject. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were computed between the mean time-series of the seed region (obtained by averaging voxel-wise time-series within each atrophy seed) and the time-series of all other brain voxels. To improve Gaussianity in the data, the r-values were Fisher-transformed using the inverse hyperbolic tangent function. This results in a 3D brain map of z-values (Z) for each atrophy seed, generated independently for each HCP subject. A one-sample t-test across the normative cohort yielded individual atrophy-induced functional dysconnectivity (AIFD) maps. The code is available at https://github.com/compneurobilbao/lnm.

Highly coherent spatially overlapping (HCSO) regions were defined as voxels with Z > 5 present in >90% of the participants. Considering that identified HCSO were not extensive, we searched for more coherent underlying subgroups using consensus clustering5 derived from AIFD similarity matrices. To compute these, we divided the brain using the AAL parcellation, and calculated the cross-participant similarity for each parcellated ROI. The matrices were then fed to the consensus clustering algorithm, returning distinct AIFD subtypes. The HCSO binary masks of the subtypes are shown in Figure 1. The subgroup AIFD maps were compared at the voxel level with a significance level of 0.05, correcting for cluster size. To enhance interpretability, we incorporated a cognitive inference step based on meta-analytic functional mapping (COGNET-Stroke tool)3, enabling us to identify the cognitive processes most consistently associated with each subtype’s dysconnectivity profile.

Results

The consensus clustering algorithm identified 4 robust distinct AIFD subtypes among CU A+ individuals. A voxel-wise ANOVA test across all individual AIFD maps revealed widespread group differences (p<0.05) covering the whole GM, and the subsequent pairwise analyses comparing subtypes confirmed that the differences between individual AIFD maps visible in the subtype masks extend to all pairs along the HCSO regions for each subtype (p<0.05), similar to the ones seen in the network masks in Figure 1. This further shows that the subtypes have consistent connectivity in the HCSO

The cognitive decoding analysis demonstrated that each subtype exhibited a characteristic cognitive association pattern. Subtype 1 showed a predominant involvement of auditory and somatosensory-related networks, where 15 out of 25 participants had AIFD maps overlapping with those concept metamaps. This reinforces the notion that early sensory-disconnection phenotypes may index vulnerability in preclinical AD.

Subtype 2 showed highly consistent associations with motor, action-related, and tactile processing domains (17, 16, and 15 out of 19 participants, respectively) with AIFD maps statistically significantly associated with the concept metamaps. This can be seen in the subtype 2 network mask, where the postcentral gyrus, an area well known for its contribution to locomotive functions, is fully covered. This pattern suggests that sensorimotor dysconnectivity, traditionally considered a late feature of AD, may emerge earlier in a subset of individuals.

Although subtypes 3 and 4 exhibited partially overlapping cortical territories, their subcortical signature markedly diverged. Subtype 3 demonstrated extensive basal ganglia involvement and was primarily associated with working memory and cognitive control (8/15 participants with significant associations), consistent with the participation of striatal–frontoparietal circuits in early executive vulnerability. In contrast, subtype 4 showed limbic-hippocampal disconectivity. For subtype 4, memory and social cognition were the most represented domains, with 9 out of 10 participants showing statistically significant associations with the metamaps.

Conclusion

We present an open-source, data-driven framework bridging gray matter atrophy patterns to normative functional connectomes. Applied to cognitively unimpaired individuals with biomarker evidence of AD pathology, this approach identified four distinct functional subtypes, each with dissociable cognitive correlates, demonstrating that amyloid pathology does not produce uniform functional consequences. These findings indicate that early amyloid pathology does not perturb brain networks uniformly, but instead gives rise to distinct patterns of functional vulnerability that may precede domain-specific decline. 

By linking subject-level structural variations to population-level functional dysconnectivity templates (not solely explained by regional atrophy variations), we create a quantitative bridge between individual anatomy and canonical functional systems, enabling discovery of coherent functional subtypes. This may potentially serve as a sensitive biomarker of early network dysfunction. In turn, the presence of distinct AIFD phenotypes in the preclinical stage of AD raises the possibility that individuals follow multiple, partially independent trajectories of network disruption. If confirmed longitudinally, these phenotypes may index differential progression risks or reflect early compensatory mechanisms within large-scale circuits. This framework, providing capacity to phenotype early functional alterations, is generalizable beyond AD and applicable to any neurodegenerative condition characterized by focal atrophy and multimodal heterogeneity.

Ongoing work integrates these functional subtypes with multimodal biomarkers including blood-based biomarkers of neuroaxonal injury, amyloid and tau pathologies and neuroinflammation and neuroimaging markers of cerebral small-vessel disease (Fazekas score and the presence of microbleeds). These analyses will help determine whether functional subtypes reflect distinct pathobiological pathways and enable prospective stratification. In the future, AIFD-based stratification may offer a conceptual framework for future preclinical trial design, particularly if subsequent studies demonstrate that specific dysconnectivity phenotypes relate to differential risk trajectories.
