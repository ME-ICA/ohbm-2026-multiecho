Introduction

Hoarding disorder (HD) is an under-recognized condition which belongs to ‘Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders’ (DSM-5). It is an inability to get rid of objects as a result of a perceived need to save the items and distress associated with discarding them, causing debilitating clutter. It is a prevalent condition, estimated to affect 2-5% of the population. HD poses a serious public health concern, as it puts people with HD and their families at risk of fire, injury, emotional trauma and living in a dysfunctional house. HD is challenging to treat, and this difficulty is compounded by the fact that, unlike individuals with other conditions such as Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder, those with HD very rarely seek treatment. Research underscored genetic, biological, affective, behavioural and cognitive vulnerabilities linked to HD. Neuropathological mechanisms underlying HD are unknown. This review of neuroimaging publications on HD has two aims. First to uncover the endophenotypes of brain systems affected by HD and second to gain insights from neuroimaging publications into current available treatments for HD.

Methods

Bibliographic searches of the PubMed and Web of Science databases were conducted to identify papers published before September ‘25. The search terms included: ‘hoarding disorder’ and either ‘neuroimaging’, ‘EEG’, ‘TMS’, ‘cathodal HD-tDCS’, ‘DW-MRI’, ‘DTI’, ‘fMRI’, ‘MRS’, ‘VBM’, ‘MRI’, ‘SPECT’, ‘MEG’, ‘PET’. A total of 23 studies met the inclusion criteria (participants with HD (HDPs) who fulfilled the DSM-5 criteria for HD or well-defined probable HD, usage of at least one neuroimaging technique and peer-reviewed studies published in English). 

Results

4 object decision fMRI studies showed HDPs had heightened insula, ACC, and frontal‑limbic BOLD signal for possession‑related decisions, reduced or abnormal BOLD signal responses for non‑personal possession decisions, and connectivity patterns predicting treatment engagement. 3 Go/No‑Go fMRI studies showed participants with OCD (OCDPs) had normal performance but hyperactive error‑monitoring (OFC/ACC), whereas HDPs displayed impulsive performance with reduced or atypical activation (OFC, precentral gyrus, DLPFC), indicating distinct deficits. 3 fMRI connectivity studies revealed HDPs exhibited disrupted brain connectivity at rest and during object discarding task, with weakened cognitive‑control and insula-frontal/temporal connections and emotion‑linked connectivity abnormalities tied to sadness, anxiety and perceived importance of possessions. 3 visual flanker EEG publications (all results relative to non-HDPs) showed: a) HDPs exhibited significantly lower ERN amplitude, suggesting deficient error monitoring; b) HDPs exhibited an enhanced ERP amplitude at N1, no differences at P1/N2 and an attenuated amplitude in LNSW, indicating abnormalities in mid- and late-stage VOTC processing; c) HDPs showed reduced phase locking in theta and alpha, suggesting impaired neural synchronisation. 2 Go/No-Go EEG studies showed: a) HDPs (all results below relative to control participants (CPs)) exhibited blunted indices of error-detection outside possession-related decision-making, underscoring abnormal error-monitoring; b) HDPs and CPs showed predicted frontal error‑related negativity, but no ERN differences, suggesting simulated discarding may not reliably elicit group effects. A resting EEG study found that, despite higher self‑reported hyperactivity and inattention, HDPs showed no neurophysiological marker of impaired attention. An VBM study found increased GM volume in BA10 and BA11 in HDPs. Results from DTI study revealed HDPs exhibited increased radial diffusivity and decreased fractional anisotropy in white matter tracts. 5 publications focused on treatment. One case study showed rTMS reduced hoarding symptoms and improved frontal‑lobe function, while another study found accelerated cathodal HD‑tDCS over R DLPFC improved acquisition and depression but not anxiety, clutter or discarding. A pilot fMRI study revealed abnormal BOLD signal in HDPs during acquiring and discarding decisions, but after CBT most of the differences normalized. An experimental fMRI study (simulated discarding and acquiring tasks) showed decreases in BOLD signal after CBT, but no CBT‑related change in dACC, despite that pre‑treatment dACC BOLD signal predicted the outcome. An fMRI study found reductions in hoarding‑related beliefs due to CBT corresponded to distinct BOLD signal changes. 

Conclusion

The review of the neuroimaging studies on HD shows that this is a complex disorder with widespread functional and structural brain abnormalities and the results from treatment studies are promising. However, the majority of the reviewed studies, have shortcomings, e.g., most used small and unrepresentative samples. Some important confounds were not controlled for, e.g. cooccurring disorders and medication. Some studies did not correct for multiple comparisons. As a result, numerous conclusions remain preliminary. There is an urgent need to undertake neuroimaging studies which will address these shortcomings.
