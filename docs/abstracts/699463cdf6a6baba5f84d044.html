Introduction

White matter development is shaped by a wide range of environmental exposures, including air pollution, socioeconomic status, educational opportunity, and early life stress. However, studying these environmental influences in isolation may fail to capture the interactive and additive effects of these exposures. To address this, we instead study the exposome—defined as the total set of an individual’s co-occurring environmental exposures and experiences—to move beyond studying single environmental exposures in isolation. In parallel, recent technical advances have enabled the characterization of person-specific white matter properties across major anatomical tracts, including their microstructural properties and macrostructural shape. How the exposome might relate to person-specific white matter development remains largely unknown. Despite strong evidence that the exposome is tightly linked with cognition in youth, it remains unknown whether these effects may be related to differences in white matter properties shaped by the environment. To address these gaps, we used state-of-the-art tractometry to derive person-specific white matter micro- and macrostructural features. In a large sample of youth, we tested whether the exposome was associated with white matter properties at the individual level using univariate analyses, and whether white matter features could collectively predict exposome scores in held-out data using multivariate modeling. Finally, we examined the degree to which the exposome accounts for relationships between white matter properties and cognition.

Methods

Dataset and data preparation: We analyzed cross-sectional data from 8,428 children (ages 9–10) from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study. Diffusion MRI data were downloaded from NDA FastTrack and processed with QSIPrep and QSIRecon1. To obtain person-specific white matter features, we used DSI Studio’s AutoTrack functionality to segment 62 major white matter tracts per participant. For each tract, we extracted three microstructure measures from Neurite Orientation Dispersion and Density Imaging (NODDI) models. We also estimated 18 macrostructural measures for each tract, including volume, length, trunk diameter, elongation, surface area, and end surface geometry. This yielded 1,302 tract-level features per participant. To assess the environment, we leveraged a previously validated exposome score (“General Exposome”), a data-driven factor score derived from 354 environmental measures and which primarily reflects family and neighborhood socioeconomic status (SES)2. A higher exposome score corresponds to higher SES in general. To mitigate site-related differences, imaging features were harmonized across sites using CovBat3. We used ABCD Reproducible Matched Samples to define a discovery sample (n=4,243) and a demographically matched replication sample (n=4,185). Mass-Univariate Models: First, we pursued a mass-univariate approach using  linear models to examine the associations between individual tract-wise metrics and  exposome scores, while co-varying for age, sex, and image quality (1,302 models in total). We calculated the partial correlation (partial r) for the exposome in each model. Multiple comparisons were accounted for using the False Discovery Rate. Multivariate Models: Next, using linear ridge regression with nested cross‐validation, we trained models to predict exposome scores from neuroimaging features. Within each subsample, we regressed age, sex, and image quality from the data. Model performance was evaluated by Pearson correlation (r) between observed and predicted factor scores in the unseen half of the data. Significance was evaluated with permutation testing. Sensitivity Analyses: We ran a large number of sensitivity analyses including: repeating analyses using diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) metrics instead of NODDI metrics, adding total brain volume as a covariate, and using an alternative machine learning approach (partial least squares [PLS] instead of ridge regression). We also tested for sex-by-exposome interaction effects to examine whether white matter-exposome associations differed between sexes. Cognition Models: Finally, we examined the extent to which white matter features could predict cognition in held-out data and how these predictions change when the exposome was taken into account. We use previously validated cognition scores derived from principal component analysis of nine neurocognitive assessments administered to the ABCD participants4. In particular, we performed analogous ridge regressions with cognition as the target. We reran these models with and without accounting for each participant’s exposome score to identify shared variance across the exposome, white matter features, and cognition. 

Results

Mass-univariate Associations: The exposome showed associations with the majority of white matter properties after FDR correction (pFDR<0.05) in both halves of the data. The general exposome score (with higher scores corresponding to higher childhood SES) had significant negative associations with microstructural measures Intracellular Volume Fraction (ICVF, reflects neurite/axon density) and Orientation Dispersion (OD, reflects fiber coherence), but not Isotropic Volume Fraction (ISOVF, reflects free water content). The exposome score also had strong, positive, significant associations with many macrostructural features, with particularly strong effects for the length of white matter tracts. We found no significant sex-by-exposome interaction effects on white matter features. Multivariate Predictive Performance and Robustness: The ridge models significantly predicted the exposome using white matter features, with a held-out performance of r=0.51 and r=0.52 across split halves, indicating high predictive accuracy (Figure 1). Results were robust across analytical variations, with similar predictive accuracy when using DKI-derived diffusion metrics in place of NODDI (r=0.52), controlling for total brain volume (r=0.41), or employing a PLS regression model (r=0.50). Furthermore, using identical ridge models and cross-validation, brain features predicted the General Exposome score substantially better than isolated SES exposure measures including household income (r=0.36) or parental education (r=0.30) across split halves. Haufe transformed model weights revealed broad contributions across numerous white matter tracts rather than a single tract driving the effect. This diffuse, whole-brain pattern aligns with the finding of widespread effects from mass-univariate models. Cognition Models: White-matter–based prediction of cognition was more modest (r=0.38, r=0.37 across split-halves) and performance dropped substantially when exposome scores were regressed from the data prior to model fitting (r=0.16, r=0.15 across split-halves). In contrast, prediction of exposome scores from the same imaging features was considerably stronger (r=0.51 and r=0.52 across split halves, as described above), and this association remained robust when cognition was regressed out beforehand (r=0.33, r=0.31). Together, these results suggest that much of the brain–cognition association captured by diffusion features may reflect shared variance between cognition and the childhood environment. When total brain volume was added as a covariate, a similar pattern emerged but with attenuated effect sizes: cognition prediction decreased to r=0.24 across split halves, and regressing out exposome scores reduced this further (r=0.09). 

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that children’s environmental exposures are reflected in their white matter architecture. We found that the childhood environment was strongly associated with tract shape properties, with longer and larger tracts associated with higher exposome (higher SES). Our multivariate analysis revealed that person-specific white matter properties could predict an individual’s exposome with high accuracy, underscoring the value of modeling complexity in both the environment and in neurodevelopmental features. Finally, we found that while white matter structure was moderately associated with cognition, this relationship was largely explained by variance shared with the exposome. Together, our findings underscore the importance of capturing the influence of individuals’ multifactorial environmental exposures on person-specific brain development, with implications for elucidating variability in cognition. These robust associations between the childhood environment, white matter organization, and cognition provide a foundation for future longitudinal studies examining how early-life adversity  impacts neurodevelopment.
