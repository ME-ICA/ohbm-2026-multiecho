Introduction

The human brain does not permit its dynamics to be fully probed through controlled experiments. As a result, neuroscience relies on advanced data analysis of observational recordings. Functional Connectivity (FC) describes the statistical dependence between remote neurophysiological events and has played a key role in the study of spontaneous brain activity. Numerous FC measures have been proposed, ranging from Pearson’s correlation coefficient, capturing linear relationships, to mutual information (MI), an entropy-based measure sensitive to arbitrary dependencies. MI has been widely applied to modalities such as EEG and fMRI, and it has been linked to disease-related changes.1

Despite its theoretical generality, MI entails practical trade-offs in terms of estimation accuracy, data requirements, and computational cost. While nonlinear dependencies are theoretically well established, their empirical relevance for FC analysis across modalities and spatial scales remains unclear. Here, we extend a principled framework2 for quantifying nonlinearity across neuroimaging modalities from fMRI to scalp and intracranial EEG and single-unit recordings, and provide an open implementation to support future research.

Methods

We compared the total mutual information (TMI) of empirical time series with that of surrogate data, which preserves only linear relationships. Nonlinearity was defined as extra-Gaussian information, i.e., the information shared beyond that explained by linear correlation. Among distributions with Gaussian marginals, the multivariate Gaussian minimizes MI for a given covariance; thus, higher MI indicates non-Gaussian, nonlinear dependence.

For each subject and dataset, we computed MI on the original data and on 99 Fourier-transform surrogates. If empirical MI exceeded surrogate MI, this was taken as evidence of nonlinearity. A control analysis using linear shadow datasets was performed to account for potential biases in surrogate generation.2

Session-wise averages were used to estimate the relative nonlinearity (RNL), defined as the relative difference between empirical TMI and surrogate-based Gaussian MI. Significant deviations from shadow datasets indicated nonlinear contributions. We assessed the spatial localization3 and test–retest reliability across sessions of nonlinearity.

We distinguish two primary sources of apparent non-linearity in neural time series: intrinsic non-Gaussian dependencies and non-stationarities, such as state changes, transient bursts, or slow drifts, as well as acquisition and preprocessing artifacts. Potential non-intrinsic sources of nonlinearity were explored across modalities, including EEG band-limited power, seizure dynamics in iEEG, and temporal segmentation of single-unit spike trains.

Analyses were conducted on five open datasets spanning fMRI (ESO245 dataset,4 245 hc, 10 min ec rs fMRI 3T, TR/TE = 2,000/30 ms, Craddock 10-950 regions and AAL90 atlases; LEMON, 14 hc, 3 sessions, 15 min eo rs fMRI 3T, TR/TE = 1,400/30 ms, AAL90 atlas), EEG (LEMON, 150 hc, 54 channels from 10-10 system, 8 min ec rs, 5 frequency bands), iEEG (SWEC Inselspital Bern, 18 subjects with pharmaco-resistant epilepsies, 24 electrodes, 3 daily sessions, 124 s each), and single-unit recordings (Allen Brain Observatory Neuropixel, 26 specimens, 30 min rs).

Results

In resting-state fMRI, non-linearity was consistently negligible, at around a few percent, across spatial scales (Fig. 1A), and only slightly above the estimator bias estimated from shadow data. The EEG showed statistically significant, yet negligible, non-linearity, generally below a few percent, with modest band-specific differences (Fig. 1B). In contrast, iEEG exhibited more pronounced non-linearity, exceeding several percent across frequency bands (Fig. 1D), partly attributable to non-stationarities during natural activity. Single-unit spike data showed the most potent effects, with non-linearity exceeding 40% and decreasing with spatial averaging (Fig. 1E). The bias observed in the shadow dataset depends on the effective number of samples in the series (through duration, sampling rate, and autocorrelation).

Alternative connectivity measures did not show greater sensitivity to non-linearity than MI. Spatial localization analyses revealed weak and preprocessing-sensitive patterns in fMRI, suggesting artifactual origins, whereas EEG exhibited heterogeneous, band-dependent spatial distributions, partly attributable to muscle artifacts.

Across modalities, non-linearity was unreliable across sessions, with linear correlations exhibiting systematically higher test–retest reliability. Moreover, accounting for non-linearities can harm the classification of subjects with schizophrenia and HC.5 Processing choices (band-limited power, Fig. 1C) and non-stationarities (such as epileptic seizures and drift in single-unit recordings) can account for a tenfold increase in non-linearity.

Conclusion

Neural activity is intrinsically nonlinear; however, the amount of observable nonlinear information decreases significantly as the recording scale increases. While single-unit spike data exhibit substantial nonlinearity, this contribution is progressively reduced in iEEG and EEG and is minimal in fMRI. This effect is likely driven by spatial and temporal averaging, which involves rapidly growing numbers of neurons and leads to effective linearization consistent with the Central Limit Theorem. Simple oscillator models reproduce this decay and accurately reflect the empirical differences between modalities.

In human recordings, iEEG shows the most significant nonlinear component, partly due to non-stationarities such as interictal discharges and natural, non-rs activity. Although this makes iEEG a promising modality for detecting nonlinear interactions, improved control of non-stationarities is required. In non-invasive modalities, nonlinearity is weak, variable, and unreliable at the individual level, which limits localization and favors group-level analyses. Observed spatial heterogeneity may reflect artifacts rather than genuine nonlinear interactions.

Overall, while nonlinear dependencies are present at all scales, their practical impact is often negligible. Routine use of nonlinear connectivity measures, such as mutual information, is therefore not justified without clear evidence of robust nonlinearity and careful exclusion of spurious sources. Linear methods remain appropriate in most cases, with targeted nonlinear analyses reserved for specific contexts.
