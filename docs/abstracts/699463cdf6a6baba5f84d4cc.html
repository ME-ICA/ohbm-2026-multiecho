Introduction

The well-known century-old cortical parcellations of Korbinian Brodmann and Oscar and Cecile Vogt divide the human cortex into discrete areas based on its layered cytoarchitecture and myeloarchitecture. Early on, the Vogts already used the layered structure of intracortical fibers to define clearly distinguished myeloarchitectonic cortical types (myelo-types), grouping similar cortical areas together. The presence of the bands of Baillarger (horizontal myelin bands) classifies the cortex into bistriate (two bands), unistriate (only the outer band), unitostriate (both bands fused into one), and astriate (no bands) cortex. The transition between different myeloarchitectonic areas is often abrupt and distinct areas can have rather small spatial extent. For example, Sanides described the existence of a bistriate fringe-area (‘Randsaum’) and a border-tuft area (‘Grenzbüschel’) in the transition zone from unistriate V1 to astriate V2 1,2. Most knowledge of human cortical cyto- and myeloarchitecture comes from ex-vivo histology 3,4, which has recently seen advances in sectioning, microscopy, and digital analysis, e.g. 5–7. In parallel, extensive work with ex-vivo MRI on small tissue samples, e.g. 8–11, often combined with correlated histology, has provided the concept of MRI ‘virtual histology’ biomarkers 12,13. These include (quantitative) T1 and T2* contrasts which are correlated to microscopic myelin (and iron) content, typical of myeloarchitecture. Here, we used pTx in a bespoke 9.4T RF-coil allowing homogenous T2* contrast over the whole ex-vivo human brain and pushed the resolution to 75μm (Fig.1A), twice that achieved before in whole ex-vivo human brain 14. We characterized T2* depth-profiles of all 4 myelo-types, sampled cortical-depth-profiles along a mesh-surface and used  a peak-finding algorithm to create a data-driven parcellation of the early visual cortex into unistriate V1, a bistriate fringe-area in the transition zone, and astriate V2.

Methods

We imaged a formalin-fixed, whole human brain, obtained from the University of Rostock body donor program (post-mortem-interval-to-fixation: 32h, fixation-to-scanning: 25 months). Experiments were done on a 9.4T 820mm bore human MR scanner (Magnetom 9.4T, Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany) with an 80mT/m, 330T/m/s head gradient and 8-channel multiple-transmit system. We used a custom-built 9.4T/400MHz conformal parallel transmit (8Ch) phased-array receive (24Ch) RF-coil 15 holding a 3D printed conformal container (Somos Watershed) for post-mortem human brains (Fig.1B,C). For the pTx pulse design, transmit (B1+) maps were acquired with DREAM 15–19. High resolution whole brain imaging was performed with a 3D gradient echo (GRE) pulse sequence with a full pTx kT-points composite excitation pulse optimized for B1+ homogeneity18–20. We acquired at 75µm isotropic resolution with T2*-weighted contrast (matrix 2160x2046x1920, FA=30deg, TR=51ms, TE=23ms, readout-bandwidth=30Hz/px, TA=43h42m), and reconstructed offline in MATLAB.

We bias-field corrected the data using the N4-algorithm 21. We then manually segmented 3 orthoslices into gray-matter (GM), white-matter (WM), background, and a subcortical class (Fig.1E), and trained a 3D Res-Enc U-Net using nnUNet 22, treating other slices and subcortex as missing data. We then iteratively chose new, imperfectly predicted regions in the output, manually corrected, and re-trained the U-Net until a good segmentation was achieved throughout (Fig.1F-H). We also used nn-Interactive to semi-automatically segment subcortical areas 23 (Fig.1I). We then applied smoothing of the cortical gray matter borders (LN2_RIM_POLISH implemented in LayNii, iter_smooth=20), while preserving sharp bends and separation of nearby cortical walls such as narrow sulci. We then defined voxel-wise equivolumetric cortical-depth (LN2_LAYERS in LayNii, iter_smooth=1000). We applied median-smoothing of the cortex using a spatially varying structuring element that locally conforms to the folding of the cortex (LN2_UVD_FILTER implemented in LayNii, width=0.5mm tangential to the cortex, height=3.33% of equivolumetrically defined cortical depth). We then extracted columnar regions-of-interest (ROIs) at 4 locations on the cortex, roughly corresponding to locations displaying the 4 distinct myelo-types (Fig.2A-F) and digitized their depth-profiles into 50 equally spaced depth-bins according to the equi-volume metric. We smoothed these profiles with a gaussian kernel (std=1 depth-bin) and automatically extracted peaks (corresponding to decreased myelin) and troughs (increased myelin) in the intensity of these profiles, as well as the maximum slope between supra- and infra-granular regions (Fig.2G). 

Finally, for a ROI at the V1-V2 boundary, we generated a mesh conforming to the mid gray-matter, and defined vertex-wise streamlines along the gradient of the cortical-depth metric (Fig.2H). We then sampled the depth metric and the T2*-intensities along the points of each streamline and extracted cortical-depth-profiles by digitizing and minimally smoothing as described above (Fig.2I). We then parcellated the ROI based on simple peak-counting: 1 peak = astriate (one peak in supragranular cortex, putative V2 and ‘Grenzbüschel’), 2 peaks = unistriate (two peaks including outer band of Baillarger, putative V1), and 3 peaks = bistriate (three peaks including inner band of Baillarger, putative ‘Randsaum’) (Fig.2J,M). After classification into myelo-types, vertex classifications were smoothed with a mesh-based mode-filter (neighbourhood=2 steps, iterations=4).

Results

Figure 1D shows the broad homogeneous coverage, resolution, and strong WM/GM and intra-cortical contrast enabled by the bespoke pTx RF-coil, pulse sequence and reconstruction. The iterative expert-guided and deep-learning-assisted segmentation (Fig.1E-G) resulted in high-accuracy and high-detail WM/GM and pial surfaces (Fig.1H) necessary for accurate layer ordinates, as well as extensive sub-cortex segmentation (Fig.1I). Accurate layer depth-ordinates in an equivolume coordinate system are important to accurately analyze laminar contrast in curved cortex, and to smooth along these ordinates to emphasize layer contrast (Fig.2C-F,L,M). We selected four cortex-locations in our data representing unitostriate, unistriate, bistriate, and astriate (Fig.2C-F) according to the Vogt&Vogt delineations, atlased by Foit et al. 24. Each shows its characteristic banded patterns over depth, in histogram and filtered data (and often raw data by eye): One band of Baillarger (unistriate, Fig.2D), two bands (bistriate, Fig.2E), two bands merged (unitostriate, Fig.2C) and no bands (astriate, Fig.2F). We selected a ROI at the V1-V2 boundary for which Sanides described a bistriate fringe-area located adjacent to unistriate V1 and near astriate V2 (Fig.2K)1. Using an advanced sampling technique to aggregate depth-profiles into a mesh, combined with depth profile peak-counting, we parcellated the region into its known myelo-types (Fig.2G-M). A further area can be identified in the ROI, adjacent to V2 and marked by comparatively lower intensity (putatively more myelinated), likely corresponding to the radiate-type border-tuft area (Fig.2L). Finally, sampling along vertex-wise streamlines which follow the gradient of the equivolume depth-metric has multiple benefits: Being derived from the smooth equivolume metric, these streamlines naturally run normal to the cortical layers at all depths. Further, this approach could be extended, and the gradient of the depth-metric could also be used as a target to project local fiber-directions estimated from diffusion-weighted imaging, to gain a high-fidelity metric of fiber radiality or tangentiality.

Conclusion

Our 9.4T pTX acquisition methods resulted in a 75μm T2* dataset providing intracortical depth contrast detailed enough to characterize myeloarchitectonic layers and identify myeloarchitectural types. The homogenous whole brain coverage through pTx opens up the opportunity of a complete brain MRI atlas correlated to myeloarchitecture, even in parts left uncovered by Vogt&Vogt. We exemplified this using our new analysis pipeline, resulting in a high-fidelity, and highly interpretable parcellation.

Lower resolution (quantitative) T2* as well as diffusion-weighted datasets were recorded, which could provide complementary aspects. The full-brain segmentation could be used to compare our approach to other methods for myeloarchitectonic parcellation e.g.25,26 and to translate to in vivo MRI myeloarchitecture imaging.
