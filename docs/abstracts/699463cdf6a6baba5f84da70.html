Introduction

Dementia affects 7% of older people, impacting many families.1 In addition to its human impact, dementia causes a large financial burden on society, with a total cost for the UK of £34.7 billions in 2019, projected to increase to £94.1 billion in 2040. Early diagnosis is key to both improving prognosis and achieving positive results with therapeutic interventions. Currently, diagnoses are being carried out by clinicians through medical history evaluation and cognitive tests, assisted occasionally by additional tests such as neuro-imaging.2 However, dementia is defined by cognitive decline, and thus diagnosed only after the development of symptoms. A survey from the UK Alzheimer Society uncovered that 56% of the respondents delayed their medical visit for a dementia diagnosis for a year or more.3 It is, therefore, crucial to develop methods to diagnose dementia earlier, such as predicting its onset using neuro-images. This study aims to explore the performance of standard machine learning (ML) compared to deep learning (DL) models to predict the onset of dementia, leveraging the largest dataset of clinically collected brain images (to our knowledge).

Methods

We used 42K structural brain magnetic resonance images (T1-, T2-weighted (w), and FLAIR) from 15.5K patients from the Scottish Medical Imaging (SMI) archive.4 The scans were routinely collected from over 35 hospitals, between 2010 and 2018, using 27 different types of scanners from 3 manufacturers. The scans were recorded between one to thirteen years before the diagnosis of dementia. The cohort was evenly split between dementia cases and healthy controls, matched by age at time of scan and sex. After having been reoriented, the scans went through two different processing pipelines described in Figure 1. In one of them (SVM Pipeline), T1w images were registered to a common template before applying tissue segmentation, non-linear normalisation and scalar momentum smoothing. In the other (DL / LR Pipeline), all three sequences were co-registered, their bias field inhomogeneities were corrected, the skull and extracranial tissues were removed, and the intracranial contents were then normalised. Three ML and DL models were used. First, a support vector machine (SVM) was used on the T1W volume processed through the first pipeline. Second, a late-fusion-based DL framework that used two popular backbone architectures of convolutional neural networks and vision transformers was applied to the output of the second pipeline. Third, a logistic regression (LR) model was trained using the 101 tissue volume features from the brain parcellation carried out after the skull-stripping in the second pipeline. Another LR model was used as a baseline and to explore the impact of demographic variables. It was trained using the age, sex, urban/rural separation, and the Scottish index of multiple deprivation (SIMD)).

Results

As seen in Figure 2. b), the baseline model obtained average balanced accuracy with low specificity, over-predicting dementia cases. The three models outperformed the baseline and obtained balanced accuracy between 61% and 64.4%. With a reported 70% sensitivity, the DL model was the best model at predicting dementia cases, however, its average specificity indicates a tendency to over-classify positive cases. The SVM model covered 87% of all patients, since it only uses T1w, and obtained higher balanced accuracy than the DL model. The higher specificity but lower sensitivity indicates fewer errors in the prediction, while not finding as many dementia cases. The T1w scans covered slightly less healthy (85.6%) than dementia (88.4%) cases. The LR model obtained the highest balanced accuracy with 64.4% and a sensitivity almost as good as the DL model (i.e., 69%). It also obtained the highest F1 score, showing the best predictive capabilities. The volume of the brain regions where atrophy is commonly associated with dementia was smaller within the dementia cases. The LR showed a tendency to classify with dementia individuals with less hippocampus and amygdala volumes, while the two other models did not, with an example given in Figure 2. d). However, the three models favoured increased ventricular size for predicting dementia cases, as seen in Figure 2. a). Half of the samples received the same prediction from all three models, among which two-thirds were correct predictions. The overlap in prediction is given in Figure 2. c). The prevalence of dementia reduces as the time between the scans and diagnosis increases, as expected due to the mortality of the disease. Additionally, it is anticipated that signs of future development of dementia will be less visible in scans captured several years before the diagnosis. Figure 2. e) shows a decrease in the sensitivity as the years between scans and diagnosis increase, illustrating the complexity in predicting dementia several years before its onset

Conclusion

The three ML models obtained decent prediction results on highly heterogeneous clinical data. The limited overlap in the model’s prediction demonstrates that no unknown bias, such as demographic variable, was responsible for the closely similar metrics. On balance, the LR trained on anatomical volumetric information yielded the best metrics. However, both the SVM and the DL, despite not being given the tissue volumes as features, predicted samples with increased ventricular size as having dementia, demonstrating that the features learned by the models were similar to what is used in standard ML models. Training DL models on clinically collected images remains a challenge, due to the high complexity involved with colloquially referred to as "real-life" data. The traditional LR model obtained better results, leveraging feature selection. With the availability of a large dataset such as this, we hope to further develop more accurate predictive models to detect the onset of dementia, for example, by focusing on the difference between the scans with short and long time frames between the recording and the onset of the disease.
