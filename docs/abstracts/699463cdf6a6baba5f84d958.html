Introduction

Implicit forms of self-control operate without conscious regulatory intent and have been proposed as an adaptive mechanism for modulating emotional responses with minimal cognitive cost. Previous neuroimaging work has shown that implicit self-control priming can alter neural responses to negative stimuli and engage control-related brain networks 1,2,3. However, it remains unclear whether such effects depend on individual differences in anxiety. Anxiety is known to impact perceptual sensitivity, emotional reactivity, and control allocation, raising the question of whether implicit self-control remains effective under heightened anxiety. The present study investigated how implicit self-control priming influences affective experience and neural processing of emotional stimuli, and whether these effects are modulated by general anxiety proneness.

Methods

Fifty-five participants (36F, 19M; M age = 25.3) completed an implicit self-control priming task in which scrambled sentences contained either self-control-related (implicit regulation) or neutral content (no-regulation). Participants subsequently viewed negative and neutral images while providing valence and arousal ratings. Trait, state, and social anxiety were assessed (STAI-T, STAI-S, LSAS) and a composite General Anxiety Proneness score was derived. MEG data were analyzed using cluster-based permutation tests and hierarchical linear mixed-effects modelling. Brain–behavior interactions were explored using correlational analyses.

Results

At the behavioral level, implicit self-control priming reliably altered affective evaluations of negative stimuli. For valence ratings, the influence of implicit regulation increased with higher general anxiety proneness, indicating that individuals with elevated anxiety showed stronger positive shifts in stimulus evaluation under implicit regulation. In contrast, arousal ratings were influenced by the priming manipulation but did not show systematic modulation by anxiety.

MEG analyses revealed two dissociable neural patterns associated with implicit self-control. An early occipital response showed enhanced activity during implicit regulation relative to no-regulation when viewing negative images, suggesting modulation at the level of visual-perceptual processing. In contrast, a later parietal response was more strongly expressed during no-regulation and was attenuated following implicit regulation, consistent with reduced neural responses associated with emotional arousal.

Model comparisons indicated that these neural effects were not affected by general anxiety proneness. However, follow-up analyses revealed that the occipital modulation associated with implicit self-control increased with higher levels of state anxiety, whereas no comparable effects were observed for trait or social anxiety. Brain–behavior analyses further showed that occipital activity was associated with more positive valence ratings, while parietal activity tracked subjective arousal, mirroring the direction of the condition effects at the neural level.

Conclusion

Together, these findings suggest that implicit self-control engages multiple levels of emotional processing. Early visual-perceptual modulation appears sensitive to situational anxiety, becoming more pronounced under heightened state anxiety, while later parietal responses track reductions in emotional arousal under implicit regulation. Behaviorally, implicit self-control remains effective across anxiety levels and may even exert stronger evaluative effects in individuals with higher general anxiety.

Rather than being disrupted by anxiety, implicit self-control may capitalize on heightened affective states, selectively modulating perceptual and arousal-related processes without requiring explicit regulatory effort. These results support the view of implicit self-control as a flexible and anxiety-tolerant mechanism for emotion regulation.

Ongoing analyses will examine whether implicit self-control similarly modulates directed functional connectivity within frontoparietal and cingulo-opercular networks, extending prior EEG work showing enhanced theta-band control-network coupling during implicit regulation1,3.
