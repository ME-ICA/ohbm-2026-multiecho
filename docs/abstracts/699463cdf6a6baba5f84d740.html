Introduction

Transcranial high-intensity MR-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) targeting the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) of the thalamus is an effective, incisionless treatment for essential tremor (ET). Outcomes remain variable, partly due to limitations in patient-specific anatomical targeting.  Since the VIM is not directly visualized on standard brain MRI, current targeting strategies rely often on indirect methods such as anatomical landmarks, atlas-based coordinates and tractography. Advanced MRI sequences—including fast gray matter acquisition T1 inversion recovery ^1 and white-matter-nulled (WMn) MPRAGE ^2 have been developed to improve visualization of thalamic nuclei, by enhancing intrathalamic contrast while suppressing surrounding white-matter signal. These sequences reveal a characteristic hypointense region in the posterolateral ventral thalamus—believed to represent the terminal projection of dentato-rubro-thalamic (DRT) tract into VIM, making it a promising direct anatomical target for stereotaxic surgery^3. However, this hypointensity is not consistently detectable in routine clinical imaging, often degraded by motion artifacts or low signal-to-noise ratio, limiting direct targeting. Group-specific MRI templates may reduce registration error relative to general atlases by minimizing population mismatch during atlas-to-subject alignment, while providing a higher signal-to-noise representation of average anatomy. Yet, an ET-specific WMn template to support Vim targeting is lacking. We hypothesized that constructing such a template and registering it to individual subjects would facilitate targeting, revealing the Vim-related hypointensity in subject space, particularly in scans of suboptimal quality. To test this, we constructed a population WMn template from ET patients scans, identified a consensus Vim hypointensity, and evaluated whether template-to-subject registration enhances Vim localization in retrospective MRgFUS candidates.

Methods

To construct a group-specific template, we gathered preoperative T1-weighted and WMnMPRAGE scans from 30 ET patients who subsequently underwent MRgFUS (mean age ± SD: 72.4 ± 8.1 years). Patients had an average 90% tremor improvement on the treated hand and no persistent side effects. All images were skull-stripped using HD-BET, and a multimodal template was generated with ANTs MultivariateTemplateConstruction2 with 5 iterations. The Vim-related hypointensity, considered the optimal lesion target, was identified and marked on the WMn template (Fig. 1). For anatomical visualization, a T1 template was also created alongside, for facilitation of registrations and integration with brain atlases. 

To evaluate the utility of the template for patient-specific Vim localization, we performed a retrospective analysis of 10 MRgFUS-treated ET patients whose images were not included in template construction. These patients were selected to span a range of image quality, including those exhibiting motion-related artefacts.   

Our regular targeting strategy combined: anatomical landmarks (Guiot parallelogram), YeB atlas ^4, dentato-rubro-thalamic (DRT) tract reconstruction, and WMn VIM hypointensity identification. For patients with poor-quality WMn images, targeting relied more heavily on these indirect methods and was refined during sonications to ensure an optimal clinical target. 

Postoperative images were rigidly aligned (7 DOF) to preoperative acquisitions to determine the location of the thermal lesion. For each patient, the group template was nonlinearly registered to the preoperative WMn scan using ANTs SyNQuick. Registration quality was assessed qualitatively by visual inspection, confirming consistent, anatomically appropriate alignment across all subjects. Finally, to test our hypothesis, we inspected images in individual space to determine whether the Vim-related hypointensity became consistently identifiable and corresponds to the clinical treatment target. 

Results

Following nonlinear registration of the group-specific WMn template into each patient’s native space, a distinct hypointense region was consistently observed at the location corresponding to the clinically selected optimal target and the final thermal lesion (Fig. 2). Importantly, this evaluation was performed in a separate, independent cohort of patients whose images were not included in template construction, providing an out-of-sample assessment of the template’s performance.

In patients ,whose native WMnMPRAGE images already allowed direct visualization of the VIM-related hypointensity, the template-derived hypointensity co-localized with the same anatomical location, confirming consistency between the group template and individual anatomy. In contrast, for patients with suboptimal native WMn image quality—most commonly affected by motion-related artifacts or low signal-to-noise ratio—the VIM-related hypointensity was not reliably identifiable on the original scans. In these cases, registration of the ET-specific WMn template revealed a clear hypointense feature in patient space that corresponded spatially to the clinically selected target. 

Across the independent cohort, the ET-specific WMn group template enabled consistent visualization of the VIM-related hypointensity regardless of native image quality. Postoperative imaging demonstrated spatial correspondence between the thermal lesion and the template-derived hypointensity. Clinical outcomes in this cohort showed substantial tremor reduction with minimal persistent adverse effects. 

Clinical outcomes further supported the precision of targeting achieved using this approach. Patients demonstrated substantial tremor reduction with no persistent adverse effects, consistent with accurate VIM lesion placement. Together, these findings indicate that the WMn group template enhances anatomical targeting for MRgFUS by improving confidence in VIM localization, particularly in cases where direct visualization is limited. 

Conclusion


We present an ET-group specific WM-null MRI template as an imaging tool that can facilitate patient-specific direct targeting of the VIM of the thalamus. This template enhances the visualization of the VIM across a range of image qualities, including those compromised by motion artifacts, thus serving as a potential anatomical landmark for thalamic interventions. Our findings indicate that the WMn template aids in the reliable identification of the VIM region, critical for targeting MRgFUS treatments. This approach could serve as a tool to increase confidence in target localization, thereby facilitating the procedure and potentially reducing the number of sonications required, which may in turn lower the risk of treatment-related oedema and associated side effects. Looking forward, we aim to expand the number of patients' scans included in the template, to refine and enhance the template further, improving its stability and representativeness for the group. We also aim to evaluate its performance in a larger cohort. Future studies are also needed to focus on prospective validation of this approach, integrating the template into clinical workflows to optimize MRgFUS treatment planning and improve patient outcomes.
