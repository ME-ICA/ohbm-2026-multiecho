Introduction

Cognitive control is a multi-scale, dynamic, context-dependent process [1]. Efforts to understand how cognitive control emerges from the brain via task fMRI have been, however, limited to single scale, static, context-agnostic approaches. This epistemological mismatch has significantly limited our ontological understanding of cognitive control. Here, we introduce a new computational method, inspired by processes in architecture [2], that matches the ontological characteristics of cognitive control and respects the way tasks are designed in cognitive neuroimaging. 

Methods

Our approach integrates across methods from statistical physics, specifically Iterative Coarse Graining (ICG) [3], and routine neuroimaging, including Multiplication of Temporal Derivatives (MTD) [4] and the General Linear Model (GLM), into a unified pipeline. The method iteratively clusters pairs of brain regions based on the strength of their task-related dynamic functional connectivity. 

First, dynamic functional connectivity is computed for each region pair via MTD. Next, a contrast-based GLM is fit to each edge to quantify how task context (e.g., NoGo vs Go trials in a Go NoGo task) modulates the dynamic coupling between regions. The resulting GLM-derived betas are then ranked in descending order based on their absolute magnitude. These rankings are then used to determine which regions are clustered at each iteration, with clustering performed by summing regional activity in time. Through this process, the algorithm generates progressively coarser assemblies of brain regions, whose beta coefficients constitute the main output for subsequent analyses (Figure 1A). 

After developing the method, we evaluated its validity in silico using simulated timeseries with prespecified modular structure and different task designs. We compared the ability of our approach to detect dynamic, context-dependent connectivity signatures, against commonly used task fMRI methods, including Pearson’s correlation and background connectivity (Figure 1B). Next, we applied the method to openly available 3T task fMRI data collected in 48 healthy young adults, while completing three cognitive control tasks (Go/No-Go, Task Switch, N-back) [5]. Following standard preprocessing and denoising, data for each task were parcellated using a 1024-region cortico-subcortical parcellation (i.e., 1000-17 Schaefer cortical combined with the 24 Tian subcortical) and submitted to our ICG. 

For each task, the ICG method generated 11 spatial scales per task (scale 1: 1024 regional betas, scale 2: 512 assembly betas, scale 3: 256 assembly betas, [...], scale 11: 1 beta). Here, we focused on tracking regional beta coefficients across scales to test whether cognitive control relies on multiscale neural processes. To this end, assemblies from scales 2-11 were mapped back to the original 1024-region space (1024 beta values x 11 scales) for each task. These matrices were then concatenated across tasks (1024 regions*3 tasks x 11 scales) and submitted to Principal Component Analysis (PCA). PCA allowed us to identify axes of maximal variation across tasks and scales. To ensure PCA results were not driven by the hierarchical nested nature of the ICG method, we constructed two null models, as shown in Figure 2A. Finally, PC components were anchored to existing literature by correlating them with the first three cortical gradients of functional organisation.

Results

On simulated data, our approach isolated dynamic, context-dependent functional connectivity, outperforming traditional methods (Figure 1B). On empirical fMRI data, our method revealed both scale-independent and scale-dependent signatures of cognitive control that were not reproduced by our null models (Figure 2B). Specifically, PC1 (55% variance explained) showed sustained loadings across scales, while PC2 (18% variance explained) and PC3 (9% variance explained) respectively peaked at later and intermediate scales (Figure 2B). Scale-independent and dependent components were furthermore found to be distributed across the brain, as they significantly (all p<0.001) recapitulated two distinct gradients of functional organisation: the primary gradient unfolding from sensorimotor to higher-order regions (PC1 r = -0.46; PC2 r=0.32; PC3 r=0.27) and the third gradient separating task positive and task negative regions (PC1 r=0.34; PC3 r=-0.15). These correlations survived Bonferroni correction.

Conclusion

Our new computational framework enabled an enriched conceptualisation and formalisation of human cognitive control as a distributed, multiscale process that evolves in time, context and space. By moving beyond spatially and temporally constrained analyses, this approach reveals new organizing principles of cognitive control that could pave the path for richer cognitive investigations in neuroimaging at large.
