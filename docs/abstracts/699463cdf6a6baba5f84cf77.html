Introduction

Human minds contain the social cognitive capacity to maintain rich, multidimensional mental models of the people they know—representations that guide perception, prediction, and action in social contexts (Amodio et al., 2019). Mental representations are thus central to cognition in social contexts: they allow us to anticipate others’ actions, infer intentions, and navigate cooperation or conflict (Hassabis et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2017). Importantly, mental representations of other individuals are theorized to be dynamic (Thornton & Tamir, 2018). In order for representations to help in adaptively navigating our social worlds, they must be necessarily be updated as a function of experience. In turn, newly sculpted mental representations should better facilitate future navigation.  

Surprisingly, remarkably little work has been devoted to investigating how mental representations of others evolve across time with accumulated social experience. Instead, most prior work has treated representations as relatively static snapshots, choosing instead to chart out where mental representational content is stored in the brain. While this foundational work was necessary to establish a bedrock of knowledge, it leaves open fundamental questions about how mental representations change over time, especially as a function of social experiences. 

Recent methodological advances have opened the door to studying these questions with greater temporal precision (Flournoy et al., 2024). Intensive longitudinal fMRI—repeated scanning of the same individuals over short timescales—allows researchers to capture fine-grained fluctuations in neural phenotypes as they unfold in everyday life. When paired with experience sampling techniques, these designs allow researchers to model fluctuations in session-to-session neural activity as a function of daily behavior. This combination offers a powerful framework for identifying putative mechanisms that drive representational change in vivo.

Methods

In the current study, we used intensive longitudinal task-fMRI and daily checklists to understand how daily experiences with known, close others sculpt mental representations of them. 
 
 We recruited N = 5 undergraduate students (5 Female, mean age = 21.2) to participate in an eight-week fMRI study. Prior to scanning, we used a standard procedure (Rachlin & Jones, 2008) to have participants nominate two close friends. Friend nominations were not allowed to be romantic partners nor family. 
 
Thereafter, participants completed n = 20 sessions of a task-based fMRI protocol that was comprised of two tasks: a (i) trait judgment task and (ii) autobiographical memory playback task. In the trait judgment task, participants were presented a series of trait statements (e.g., ‘fears public speaking’, ‘would enjoy living in a big city’) and were asked to rate how well each statement described their friend. In the memory playback task, participants read back memories that they provided about each upon enrolling in the study. Trait items were optimized to load uniquely on one of the Big 5 personality traits (Guassi Moreira et al., 2025). Memories were obtained by having participants provide written responses to 30 total prompts tapping unique aspects of mental representations, each validated in an independent sample of undergraduates (N = 300) to reliably predict core relationship characteristics (e.g., frequency of contact, relationship quality). 
 
Scan sessions were scheduled based on each participant’s availability. The minimum duration between scans was one day (e.g., scanned on back-to-back days), the maximum was four days (e.g., scanned on the 10th of the month and then again on the 14th). 
 
During this period, participants completed daily checklists that tapped social experiences with each of the two nominated friends. Participants were asked to report on (i) how much time they spent with each friend, (ii) experiences of friendship cohesion (e.g., how close they felt to their friend, whether they lent or received support to/from each friend, etc.), and (iii) how susceptible they were to their friends’ influence (e.g., doing something their friend wanted to do but they did not). Checklists falling on the same day as a scan session were assigned to correspond to the following scan sessions, as scans took place earlier in the day.
 
To link daily social experiences with neural dynamics, we conducted three complementary analyses: mass-univariate models, representational similarity analysis (RSA; Kriegeskorte et al., 2008), and voxel-wise encoding models. Mass-univariate analyses tested whether fluctuations in activation magnitude for each friend varied as a function of the three daily behavioral measures. RSA assessed whether fine-grained multivoxel patterns distinguishing the two friends changed in tandem with day-to-day variations in social experience. Finally, voxel-wise encoding models (Naselaris et al., 2011) examined whether the semantic feature spaces underlying each friend’s mental representation—derived from semantic atlases (Huth et al., 2016)—were modulated by recent interactions, allowing us to characterize how representational content itself shifts with experience.

Results

Mass-univariate analyses revealed that medial prefrontal regions canonically implicated in social cognition—including ventromedial, medial, and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex—tracked all three daily behavioral metrics (time spent together, cohesion, and susceptibility to peer influence). More daily interaction, stronger feelings of cohesion, and heightened susceptibility were each associated with increased recruitment of these regions when participants accessed mental representations of their friends.

Representational similarity analyses yielded convergent results, while also implicating additional neural hubs of the person-knowledge and social cognition. Day-to-day variation in social experience predicted changes in multivoxel patterns within anterior temporal cortex (ATC) as well as superior temporal sulcus (STS) and temporoparietal junction (TPJ), core nodes supporting theory-of-mind and social semantic processing.

Finally, semantic atlas maps derived from voxel-wise encoding models indicated that experiences of friendship cohesion were robustly encoded in TPJ, STS and ATC. These findings suggest that these regions dynamically update the semanticized content of close-other representations as a function of subjective relational experiences.

Conclusion

Together, these findings provide some of the first direct evidence that mental representations of close others fluctuate on a day-to-day basis in ways that mirror ongoing social experience. By leveraging intensive longitudinal fMRI, we were able to observe representational dynamics that would be invisible to conventional cross-sectional designs. Across analytic approaches—from activation-based models to multivoxel pattern analysis to voxel-wise encoding—daily behaviors consistently shaped neural signatures of person knowledge in medial prefrontal cortex, anterior temporal cortex, superior temporal sulcus, and temporoparietal junction.

These results support the longstanding hypothesis that mental representations are not static cognitive structures but are continuously molded by lived experience. They further suggest that regions traditionally implicated in social cognition do not merely store abstract person knowledge; they actively update representational content as individuals navigate their relationships.

More broadly, this work demonstrates the utility of combining dense-sampling neuroimaging with ecologically-based behavioral measures to capture the temporal dynamics of social cognition in vivo. As methodological tools for within-person neuroimaging continue to advance, such designs will be critical for identifying the mechanisms through which everyday social interactions reshape the neural architectures that support our understanding of the people in our lives.
