Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) are the two most common neurodegenerative disorders affecting the elderly population. AD is primarily characterized by progressive cognitive decline, with amyloid-β plaque deposition and tau neurofibrillary tangles as its core neuropathological features. In contrast, PD is clinically defined by motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, and resting tremor, and pathologically by dopaminergic neuronal loss in the substantia nigra and the accumulation of Lewy bodies.^1

Traditionally, AD and PD have been considered distinct and independent disease entities. However, accumulating evidence indicates clinical and pathological overlap between these two diseases. For example, in advanced stages of PD, amyloid-β and tau pathology has been identified in approximately 50% of patients with Alzheimer’s disease–like dementia.^2

In recent years, many studies investigating biomarker progression have applied the Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) model to capture disease heterogeneity and temporal trajectories.^3 However, to date, the application of SuStaIn has been largely restricted to a single neuroimaging modality and a single disease spectrum—either Alzheimer’s disease or Parkinson’s disease.

Therefore, this study aims to integrate multi-modality neuroimaging data—including structural MRI, amyloid-PET, and DAT-SPECT—by applying independent component analysis (ICA) to investigate biomarker spatial distribution patterns, and a SuStaIn Z-score model to explore the progression of these patterns, targeting at improving the differential diagnosis of AD, DLB, and PDD and advancing mechanistic understanding. 

 

Methods

We included 193 older adults from the Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s Disease Dimensional Neuroimaging Initiative (PADNI) cohort. All participants completed DAT-SPECT, Aβ-PET, and sMRI, together with comprehensive demographic and clinical assessments. Participants were further stratified into four motor–cognitive profiles: MI−CI− (without motor or cognitive impairment, n=81), MI−CI+ (with cognitive impairment, n=59), MI+CI− (with motor impairment, n=43), and MI+CI+ (with motor and cognitive impairment, n=45). MI+ was defined as UPDRS Part III ≥ 9, and CI+ was defined as CDR-sum of box (CDR-sob) > 0.

Images were preprocessed and co-registered using standard pipelines. To improve registration accuracy between DAT-SPECT and sMRI, a manipulated sMRI was generated based on FreeSurfer segmentation, in which striatal regions were assigned higher intensities to resemble the DAT-SPECT signal pattern.^4 DAT-SPECT SBR maps and Aβ-PET SUVR maps were then computed using the cerebellar cortex as the reference region. All images were spatially normalized to MNI space, and inter-subject effects related to age, sex, and scanning site were harmonized using ComBat.

After preprocessing, spatial independent component analysis was performed separately for DAT-SPECT and Aβ-PET data using the GIFT toolbox. Independent components were estimated using the Infomax algorithm, and component stability was assessed with ICASSO. Artifact-related ICs were identified by visual inspection and excluded. An “minimum Redundancy - Maximum Relevance (mRMR)” model (https://github.com/smazzanti/mrmr) was then applied to further select informative spatial ICs.

Feature weights derived from the selected ICs were entered into a SuStaIn Z-score model to investigate biomarker progression trajectories across diagnostic groups. Maximum likelihood-stage (Ml-stage) information was extracted from the model output, and correlations between ML-stage and clinical measures, including CDR-sob and UPDRS Part III scores, were examined to facilitate interpretation of the SuStaIn results.

Results

After selected by mRMR, ICA extracted 7 characteristic spatial components from Aβ-PET data and 5 from DAT-SPECT data. Based on their spatial distributions, these were labeled as follows: Aβ-frontal, Aβ-parietal, Aβ-default mode network (DMN), Aβ-occipital, and Aβ-caudate for amyloid imaging; and DAT-parietal, DAT-occipital, DAT-putamen, DAT-caudate1, and DAT-caudate2 (potentially indicating hemispheric asymmetry) for dopaminergic imaging.

Comparison of mean feature weights across the four motor-cognitive groups revealed that the MI-CI+ group showed significantly lower feature weights in the DAT-caudate components compared to the MI-CI− group. Additionally, the MI+CI+ group exhibited elevated feature weights in posterior Aβ-related components (Aβ-DMN, Aβ-parietal, and Aβ-occipital) relative to the other groups.

The SuStaIn Z-score model identified four progression subtypes, potentially corresponding to a pure AD-like subtype, a PD-dominant subtype, and two mixed subtypes. Within the model, subtype 3 showed an earlier progression of the DAT-parietal component, which was corroborated by the group-average images showing lower DAT-specific binding ratio (SBR) in the parietal region for this subtype compared to others. Analysis of the model-derived maximum-likelihood stages for each subject revealed that the disease stage in subtype 2 correlated with CDR-sob scores (p<0.001), supporting its reflection of AD progression. In contrast, the disease stage in subtype 3 correlated with both UPDRS-III (p=0.001) and CDR-sob (p<0.001) scores, suggesting it may represent the progression of a mixed spectrum condition, such as PDD or DLB. In addition, the model identified a potential mixed-type subtype (Subtype 4), characterized by early amyloid-β accumulation in the frontal and occipital region, together with decreased dopamine transporter availability in the caudate, although the sample size for this subtype was small (n = 12).

Conclusion

By integrating multi-modal neuroimaging biomarkers, specifically Aβ-PET and DAT-SPECT, within a data-driven SuStaIn framework, this study characterizes distinct and progressive neural signatures across the AD/PD dementia spectrum. The SuStaIn model delineated four progression subtypes, including trajectories consistent with AD-like progression as well as subtypes indicative of mixed pathologies such as PDD and DLB. ICA study revealed that amyloid accumulation exhibits spatial patterns similar to the typical functional brain networks like DMN, while SuStaIn further identified parietal dopamine transporter decline as a potentially important neuroimaging biomarker for PD spectrum dementia.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that biomarkers of Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease do not evolve independently, but instead undergo substantial and concurrent changes within the AD/PD dementia spectrum, highlighting the intertwined nature of amyloid and dopaminergic neurodegeneration.
