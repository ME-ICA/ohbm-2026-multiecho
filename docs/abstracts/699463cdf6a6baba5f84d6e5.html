Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is characterized by progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (SN) and decreased density of dopamine transporters in the striatum (Str). A novel rat model has recently been developed to study this specific vulnerability by injecting a viral vector (AAV) expressing human tyrosinase (hTyr) into the SN, inducing neuromelanin accumulation, reduced dopaminergic activity, motor deficits, neurodegeneration, and Lewy Body formation in the rats1.

Neuromelanin generates T1-weighted hyperintensity, providing a non-invasive MRI marker to track dopaminergic neuron integrity. Longitudinal studies in AAV-hTyr rats showed that T1 contrast captures both neuromelanin accumulation and subsequent neurodegeneration2. Reductions in T2* were also observed, consistent with iron accumulation, although interpretation is complicated by multiple factors and lacks information on microstructural changes.

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) complements relaxometry by using biophysical models to estimate microstructure parameters. Diffusion Kurtosis Imaging (DKI) quantifies non-Gaussian water diffusion, providing kurtosis as an empirical marker of voxel-level tissue complexity, and offering wide applicability across all brain tissues. Neurite Exchange Imaging (NEXI) estimates neurite density, intra- and extra-neurite diffusivity and inter-compartment exchange time – a proxy for myelination – and is thus particularly valuable to study long-range myelinated projections in the Str. Soma and Neurite Density Imaging (SANDI) alternatively estimates soma density and size, neglecting exchange. SANDI is thus best suited for assessing microstructure in soma-rich areas, such as the SN.

In this study, we acquired multiparametric imaging in AAV-hTyr rats and controls, combining T1 contrast, T2* relaxometry, and advanced dMRI to investigate neuromelanin accumulation and microstructural changes in the SN and Str.

Methods

Surgery: Sprague Dawley rats were injected with AAV-hTyr (N=8 females and  6 males) or empty vehicle AAV (Sham, N=7 females and 8 males) bilaterally in the SN at the age of 2 months.

MRI: One month after injection, rats were scanned on a 9.4T Bruker MRI system equipped with a cryoprobe under medetomidine sedation. A T2-weighted image was acquired for anatomical reference followed by a multishell, multi-diffusion time (Δ) diffusion MRI protocol (dw-SE-EPI, TR/TE=2400/52ms, Res=0.25x0.25x1 mm3, 122 directions, 5 b-values, 3Δ, 1h, Figure 1). For each diffusion time, 4 b=0 images were acquired with both forward and reverse phase encoding. This was followed by T1-weighted FLASH for visualization of neuromelanin contrast (TR/TE=300/5ms, Res=0.2x0.2x0.4 mm3, 16 slices, 5min) and T2* mapping (multi-gradient echo, TR=42ms, TE=2.2:2.2:35.2ms, Res = 0.2x0.2x0.2 mm3, whole brain coverage, 11 min). Acquisition sequence parameters are summarized in Figure 1A.

Processing: T2-w anatomical image was used for brain extraction using RATS toolbox. Preprocessing of dMRI included MP-PCA denoising, Gibbs unringing, distortion and motion corrections. DKI was fit using a constrained weighted least squares algorithm to the dMRI images with the intermediate diffusion time (Δ=26ms) up to bmax=3.5 ms/um2 using the Designer2 toolbox. DMRI images with the shortest diffusion time (Δ=15ms) were used to fit the SANDI model using the SwissKnife toolbox. All dMRI data (multi-shell, multi-Δ) were used to fit the NEXI model using SwissKnife. 

Quantitative T2* maps were computed by voxel-wise fitting of the multi-echo data to a mono-exponential decay, S(TE)=S0 e-TE/T2*, where S0 is the extrapolated signal at TE=0. 

Segmentation: A multivariate template was generated from 12 animals (3 of each sex and group) using T2-weighted images and b=0 images. All native images as well as the Waxholm-space (WHS) atlas were registered to the multivariate template using AntsRegistration. WHS segmentation of the striatum was propagated from multivariate space to all subjects in native space. The substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) was manually delineated bilaterally using T1-weighted FLASH contrast in hTyr rats. SNpc segmentations from all subjects were registered to multivariate template, averaged, and binarized with a threshold of 0.15. The resulting segmentation was then propagated to native space of all contrasts and all animals.

In order to mitigate the interscan variability, particularly the effect of sex, we reported all metrics as a contrast between the SN (or Str) and the medial lemniscus, used as a control region. The medial lemniscus was manually segmented and registered similarly than the SN. This region was chosen because it is close to the SN, visible for manual segmentation on T1-w images, and has not been reported to be involved in the pathology. Manual segmentation of the SN and control region are shown on Figure 1B.

Statistics: Mean parameter estimates were computed per ROI in native space for each contrast and each rat. SANDI model was used in the SN as we expected the neuromelanin aggregation to have a stronger effect on the diffusion in the soma than on the membrane permeability in this region. In the striatum, we used NEXI to evaluate the effect of neuromelanin aggregation on the projections of dopaminergic neurons of the SNpc. For each parameter λ, we computed the contrast between the region of interest (SN or Str) and the control region using the formula Contrast = ( λ(ROI) - λ(Control)) / λ(Control).

The effects of Group and Sex were assessed for each parameter using a linear model (parameter ~ Sex * Group), accounting for the interaction Group x Sex (R, r-project.org). To evaluate correlations between parameters independently of the strong effect of Sex, we first removed the variance explained by Sex by extracting the residuals of a linear model (parameter ~ Sex). Spearman’s ρ correlations were then computed on these residualized values.

Results

Group differences:

T1-weighted contrast in the SN showed a significant main effect of Group, but neither the main effect of Sex nor the Sex × Group interaction reached significance (Figure 2A). Compared to the hTyr group, the Sham group exhibited significantly lower T1-w contrast (β= −0.292, SE= 0.021, t= −13.94, p= 2.7×10⁻¹³, FDR-p= 5.7×10⁻¹²). In addition, a small Group effect was detected for Axial Kurtosis in the substantia nigra (β= 0.053 ± 0.025, t= 2.13, p= 0.043), but this effect did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. No other metrics showed any significant group difference in the SN, including T2*, SANDI parameters and other DKI parameters. We did not find any significant group difference in the striatum. Notably, some parameters, such as extracellular diffusivity derived from NEXI, showed a consistent trend of higher values in the hTyr group for both sexes, while other metrics, like T2*, displayed inverted tendencies when comparing male and female cohorts.

Correlations: Within the SN, using T1-weighted contrast as a proxy for neuromelanin accumulation, we found that it was negatively correlated with axial kurtosis (Figure 2B, Spearman’s ρ= –0.37, p= 0.045). Interestingly, several correlations were found between nigral neuromelanin accumulation and striatal parameters. T1-weighted contrast in the SN was positively correlated with striatal T2* (ρ= 0.38, p= 0.042) and extra-cellular diffusivity (ρ = 0.46, p = 0.012). Axial kurtosis in the substantia nigra was negatively correlated with striatal intra-neurite diffusivity (ρ= –0.49, p= 0.007). Finally, nigral T2* was negatively correlated with striatal mean kurtosis (ρ= –0.40, p= 0.034) and intra-neurite diffusivity (ρ= –0.57, p= 0.001). However, none of these correlations remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons.

Conclusion

Conclusions:

Neuromelanin accumulation in the SNpc was confirmed by a significant Group effect observed on the T1-weighted contrast in this region. Apart from this finding, no other metric (e.g., dMRI or relaxometry parameters) showed a significant group difference in the SN or the Str at this 1-month follow-up. This preliminary result suggests that the microstructure of the SN and Str remains largely preserved one-month post-injection, despite the confirmed neuromelanin accumulation. Longitudinal evaluation of these microstructure metrics in the second imaging session (acquired four months post-injection) is essential. This complementary data will not only increase the statistical power of the study but also potentially reveal the subsequent progression of microstructural pathology and evidence any emerging sex-specific changes.

Despite the limited statistical power, we observed a notable tendency toward decreased axial kurtosis in the SN. Generally, a reduction in kurtosis metrics indicates decreased microstructural complexity. However, axial kurtosis lacks specificity when interpreting complex gray matter microstructure. Intriguingly, correlations (as shown in Figure 2) reveal that neuromelanin accumulation in the SN is associated with an increase in extracellular diffusivity as estimated by the SANDI biophysical model. This finding, when considered alongside the tendency for decreased axial kurtosis, may reflect underlying neurodegenerative processes. This pattern aligns with observations in other neurodegenerative contexts. For instance, reduced axial kurtosis has been reported in both cortical3 and subcortical4 gray matter nuclei in Parkinson’s disease (PD). Furthermore, an increased fraction of extracellular space was detected in subcortical gray matter using the NODDI model3. Similarly, the SANDI model revealed that elevated extracellular diffusivity in the striatum correlated with motor symptom severity in Huntington’s disease5.

In a previous longitudinal study2, male hTyr rats showed a rapid T1-weighted contrast increase followed by a slower decline, where the early rise reflected neuromelanin accumulation and the subsequent decrease corresponded to neurodegenerative changes (histologically confirmed). Building upon this context, the analysis of the second imaging session (four months post-injection) is crucial. This longitudinal data will allow us to evaluate the temporal dynamics of neuromelanin accumulation and microstructural degradation, notably by capturing the potential delayed microstructural breakdown currently absent at one month. Critically, integrating all diffusion (NEXI, SANDI, DKI) and relaxometry metrics across both sexes will fully characterize the microstructural alterations in this model, providing essential insight into how neuromelanin-related changes impact tissue integrity and connectivity over time, and confirming or refuting the emergence of sex-specific vulnerabilities in the chronic phase.
