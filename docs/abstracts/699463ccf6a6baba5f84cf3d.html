Introduction

Long-range white matter (WM) tracts are thought to enable communication between distant brain regions that gives rise to complex cognition1. Yet, how the connectivity profiles of WM tracts to cortical regions support unique aspects of cognition remains poorly understood. Notably, higher-order cognitive functions rely on spatially distributed regions embedded across a cortical hierarchy defined by the sensorimotor-to-association (S-A) axis2,3, a major axis of brain organization. Here, we sought to delineate how anatomically defined, canonical WM tracts are situated within the broader context of the cortical hierarchy to support diverse aspects of cognition.

This study addresses three major gaps in our knowledge. First, structure-function relationships have been widely explored using connectome approaches, but structural connectomes suffer from high false positive rates and unclear relations to key anatomical tracts. Second, efforts to map canonical WM tracts directly to distinct cognitive functions remain sparse. Third, we lack a clear understanding of how WM tracts align with cortical organization. In the context of these limitations, recent methodological advances have enabled the mapping of WM tracts onto the cortical surface in individual brains, yielding population-level maps that link tracts to cortex4. Importantly, this tract-to-region mapping represents an anatomically grounded alternative to conventional inter-regional structural connectomes that are agnostic to anatomical WM tracts, and provides the foundation for annotating tracts with the cognitive functions and biological properties of their connected regions (i.e., tract cortical endpoints). Here, we leveraged this tract-to-region mapping to delineate how anatomical WM tracts are situated within the cortical hierarchy to support cognition.

Methods

Population-level data: We leveraged three population-level resources. First, we used the tract-to-region mappings from Human Connectome Project Young Adult tractography (N=1,065). These mappings describe the connections of 52 tracts to 360 cortical regions of the HCP-MMP parcellation, yielding a 360-by-52 matrix (Figure 1a). Second, we leveraged Neurosynth task-based fMRI meta-analyses to characterize tract cognitive profiles across 125 Cognitive Atlas terms, producing a 360-by-125 cognitive-term matrix (Figure 1b). Cognitive term contributions of each tract were computed as the normalized average activation across a tract’s connected regions for each cognitive term. Third, to delineate the biological properties of regions connected to each tract, we used 28 cortical feature maps from Neuromaps and BigBrain (e.g., intracortical myelination, gene expression, laminar differentiation, and neurotransmitter receptor density), forming a 360-by-28 biological property matrix (Figure 1c). 

Tract annotations: A key goal of the study was to assess relationships among different tract annotations, which we quantified using Spearman correlations and tested for significance with 10,000 permutations. We began by describing tracts’ spatial embedding in the cortical hierarchy. To assess spatial distance spanned by regions connected to each tract, we calculated mean Euclidean distance across connected region pairs. To determine the hierarchical positioning, we used the S-A axis and evaluated the degree to which each tract spanned the cortical hierarchy by computing the tract's S-A range (max–min S-A rank of its connected regions). We used partial least squares (PLS) to determine whether WM tracts and cognitive functions align along a shared latent spatial axis. To more precisely characterize the cognitive profiles of individual tracts, we averaged cognitive term contributions across connected regions and grouped these into 11 Cognitive Atlas categories. We next quantified tract cognitive diversity using the Gini coefficient, indexing how evenly term contributions were distributed across cognitive terms, with higher values indicating greater specialization and lower values indicating greater diversity. To further contextualize how tracts support cognition, we examined biological properties of their connected cortical regions by computing a region-region similarity matrix across all biological properties and extracting the mean similarity of connected region pairs for each tract. 

Sensitivity analyses: We tested the robustness of findings across different methodological choices. We replaced Euclidean distance with geodesic distance, repeated all analyses separately for association vs. projection tracts, and implemented a tract-rewiring null that randomized connected regions while preserving the number of connections, allowing us to determine whether observed associations exceeded those expected from size-matched but biologically random white-matter systems.

Individual-level analysis: Finally, we tested whether the position of a tract in the cortical hierarchy captures meaningful variation in brain development and individual differences in cognition using the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC, N=1,145, ages 8-23 years). We modeled age- and cognition-related associations in tract fractional anisotropy (FA) using generalized additive models (GAMs). For age models, a separate GAM was fitted for each tract with mean FA as the dependent variable, modeling age as a smooth term with penalized splines and including sex and data quality (quantified as mean framewise displacement) as covariates. For cognition models, the GAM included an additional term for executive efficiency. Effect sizes were quantified using partial R² comparing the full model to a reduced model without the predictor of interest. In each set of analyses, multiple comparisons were controlled for using the False Discovery Rate (FDR, q < 0.05). 

Results

We found that WM tracts vary in the degree to which they span the hierarchical S-A axis. Euclidean distance was positively associated with tract S-A range (rs=0.688, pperm<0.001, 10,000 iterations, two-sided), indicating that while some tracts connect proximal regions of similar hierarchical ranking, others extend across long distances to bridge the cortical hierarchy. We next evaluated whether this specific spatial organization was linked to different aspects of cognition using PLS, revealing that cognitive functions and WM tracts spatially co-vary along a common spatial axis (p=0.002, 10,000 iterations). Broadly, this analysis revealed that WM tracts connecting dorsolateral regions were associated with action-related functions, whereas ventromedial connecting tracts were linked to affective functions. This result demonstrates that tracts are associated with specific types of cognition based on their spatial layout within the brain. Furthermore, WM tracts each showed distinctive cognitive profiles, varying in both the nature and range of cognitive functions they support. Our quantification of tracts’ Gini coefficients revealed that WM tracts systematically varied in their degree of cognitive diversity: tracts with the greatest cognitive specialization (high Gini) included the optic radiation, the fornix, and the corticobulbar tract. In contrast, the most cognitively diverse tracts (low Gini) included the inferior fronto-occipital, the middle longitudinal, and the inferior longitudinal fasciculi (Figure 2). Notably, the Gini coefficient of tract diversity was negatively associated with tract hierarchical span (S-A range) (rs=-0.7, pperm<0.001), indicating that tracts connecting regions with a similar position in the cortical hierarchy are specialized, while tracts that span the cortical hierarchy support diverse cognitive functions. 

We next examined the neurobiological similarity of regions connected to long-range anatomically-defined tracts. We found that tract hierarchical span was negatively correlated with tract mean cortical similarity (rs=-0.649, pperm<0.001), and that tract Gini coefficient of functional diversity was positively correlated with tract mean cortical similarity (rs=-0.599, pperm<0.001). These findings suggest that tracts linking adjacent levels of the cortical hierarchy tend to connect regions with similar biological properties, whereas tracts crossing the cortical hierarchy connect regions with more heterogeneous properties. These biologically diverse connections support greater functional diversity, suggesting that these long-range tracts serve as anatomical bridges across distinct cortical environments to facilitate complex cognition. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that all results were robust to differing methodological choices. 

Finally, individual-level analyses in the PNC indicated that the effect size of developmental associations was positively correlated with tract S-A axis range (rs=0.708,  pperm<0.001) and negatively correlated with tract Gini coefficient (rs=-0.445, pperm=0.016). Similarly, controlling for age, the effect size of individual differences in executive efficiency was positively associated with the S-A axis range of tracts (rs=0.495, pperm=0.009), and negatively associated with tract Gini coefficient  (rs=-0.377, pperm=0.037). These results demonstrate that tracts which span the cortical hierarchy show the most pronounced developmental changes in youth and are also most strongly linked to individual variation in executive function. 

Conclusion

This study provided a unified framework linking anatomical white matter tracts to the cognitive and neurobiological properties of their cortical endpoints. We found that tracts are differentially positioned along the cortical hierarchy to support distinct cognitive functions. Examining the biological properties of connected regions, we furthermore observed that tracts serve as anatomical bridges to connect distinct biological environments. Finally, we found that the position of a tract in the cortical hierarchy captures meaningful variation in brain development and individual differences in cognition. Together, these results highlight the importance of understanding WM architecture within the organization of the cortical hierarchy.  This framework  moves beyond conventional categories of projection, association, and commissural tracts  toward a cortex-anchored understanding of how anatomical WM tracts support the diverse cognitive repertoire of the human brain.
