Introduction

Early psychosis and depression show high diagnostic heterogeneity, complicating prognosis and treatment. Transdiagnostic neurobiologically-informed subgroups may refine differential diagnosis, aligning with the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework.

Previous data-driven clustering studies have identified distinct gray matter volume-based transdiagnostic subgroups, consistently revealing an anatomically impaired subgroup with reduced gray matter, poorer functioning, and worse outcomes compared with preserved subgroups.¹ However, resting-state fMRI captures complementary information about large-scale network dynamics.

Both psychosis-spectrum and mood disorders show dysregulation of the default mode, frontoparietal, and salience networks. Within-diagnosis rs-fMRI subtypes have been linked to symptom trajectories and treatment response. Yet no study has investigated transdiagnostic rs-fMRI biotypes across early psychosis and depression using semi-supervised clustering.

We aimed to identify rs-fMRI-based transdiagnostic subtypes in individuals with clinical high-risk for psychosis (CHR), recent-onset psychosis (ROP), and recent-onset depression (ROD) from the multi-site PRONIA consortium.² We compared Hierarchical Bayesian Normative Modeling (HBNM)³ versus ComBat harmonization for site-effect correction, hypothesizing HBNM would improve clustering stability.

Methods

Participants were recruited across 10 European sites as part of the PRONIA study (Prognostic Tools for Early Psychosis Management).² Following quality control, we included N=1,296 participants (mean age 25.0±5.9 years; 51.6% female): 419 healthy controls (HC), 289 CHR individuals, 270 ROD patients, and 318 ROP patients.

Resting-state fMRI preprocessing used SPM12 and REST toolbox: slice-time correction, realignment, T1 co-registration, MNI normalization, nuisance regression (white matter, CSF, Friston-24 motion parameters), smoothing, despiking, and detrending. Voxel-wise fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF) was computed. Using all 1,296 participants, we estimated 30 large-scale Probabilistic Functional Modes (PFMs) with the PROFUMO variational Bayesian framework.⁴

We compared two site-correction approaches: (1) ComBat harmonization trained on HC, and (2) Hierarchical Bayesian Normative Modeling (HBNM) trained on HC, yielding deviation z-scores quantifying departures from healthy norms while modeling hierarchical site structure (Figure 1).³ HBNM effectiveness was validated using Random Forest site classification trained on pre-normalization HC data and applied to post-normalization data.

Heterogeneity through Discriminative Analysis (HYDRA) semi-supervised clustering identified patient subgroups (N=877) by fitting a convex polytope of linear SVMs maximally separating patient clusters from HC.⁵ We tested K=1-10 clusters within 10-fold cross-validation. Optimal K was selected via Adjusted Rand Index (ARI). Hyperparameter sweep (C=0.01-1.0) identified C=0.01 as optimal. Final HBNM model stability was assessed using 1,000 cross-validation repetitions.

Subgroup characterization included PANSS, SANS, BDI, cognitive assessments, polygenic risk scores for cognitive performance, and psychosocial functioning (GAF, GF scales) at baseline and 9-18 month follow-up.

Results

HBNM validation confirmed effective site harmonization: site classification accuracy dropped from 41.5% to 17.9% post-normalization (chance=12.5%), representing 81.4% reduction in above-chance site discriminability. No site pairs remained significantly discriminable after Bonferroni correction.

Both harmonization approaches yielded an optimal two-subgroup solution. In K-selection (K=1-10), HBNM achieved superior clustering stability (ARI=0.731) compared to ComBat (ARI=0.650). Validation across 1000 resamples at K=2 confirmed robustness (mean±SD): ARI=0.690±0.20, Silhouette=0.066±0.006 (typical for high-dimensional clustering), Calinski-Harabasz=62.1±6.3. C-parameter sweep confirmed all values (0.01-1.0) converged on K=2, with lower C yielding higher ARI.

Clinical characterization from ComBat-derived assignments (Subgroup 1 "Impaired": N=369; Subgroup 2 "Preserved": N=508) showed the impaired subgroup had higher ROP and fewer ROD patients.

Robust cognitive differences emerged: the impaired subgroup exhibited significantly lower verbal learning and global cognitive ability (FDR-corrected p<.05). Correspondingly, this subgroup showed significantly lower polygenic loading for cognitive performance.

At baseline, the impaired subgroup showed lower GAF Disability and GF Role scores. Critically, biotype membership predicted persistent poor functioning at 9-18 month follow-up: impaired subgroup patients had higher likelihood of persistent low GAF Symptoms (χ²=8.8, p=.003) and poor GF Role (χ²=6.4, p=.01).

The impaired subgroup showed lower amplitudes across most PFMs relative to the preserved subgroup and HC, except for increased activation in salience and somatomotor networks (Figure 2).

Conclusion

We identify two transdiagnostic rs-fMRI biotypes in early psychosis and depression with distinct cognitive, symptom, and outcome profiles, supporting biologically-informed alternatives to categorical diagnosis.

Hierarchical Bayesian Normative Modeling achieved 81.4% site effect reduction and improved clustering stability (K-selection ARI=0.731; 1000-resample ARI=0.690±0.20) over standard ComBat (ARI=0.650), demonstrating the critical importance of appropriate harmonization in multi-site subtyping.³ Convergence on K=2 across validity metrics and regularization parameters indicates robust transdiagnostic structure.

The impaired biotype, characterized by reduced functional network amplitudes except in salience and somatomotor regions, showed lower cognitive ability and cognitive PRS loading, extending previous structural MRI findings to the functional domain.¹

Clinically, baseline rs-fMRI biotype membership predicted persistent poor functioning over 18 months, suggesting utility for early prognostic stratification beyond traditional diagnostic boundaries.
