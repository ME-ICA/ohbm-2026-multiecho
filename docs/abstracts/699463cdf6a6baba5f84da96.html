Introduction

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) is the only non-invasive technique for imaging brain pathways in vivo, but it provides only indirect measurements of axonal orientations based on water diffusion, and it has limited resolution. Thus it is critical to validate it against direct measurements of axonal orientations that can only be obtained ex vivo1. Polarization-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PS-OCT) provides such measurements at the microscale, while also measuring the apparent birefringence of the tissue, which represents myelin concentration in the brain. The advantages of PS-OCT include being label-free, as it relies on intrinsic tissue contrast, and being free of the spatial distortions that are typical of other microscopy techniques, as it images the tissue blockface before sectioning the tissue. However, its main limitation has been that a single PS-OCT measurement captures only 2D axonal orientations, in the plane perpendicular to the light incidence angle. In prior work, we performed systematic validation of dMRI orientation estimates against such 2D axonal orientations from PS-OCT2-3. While that work offered important insight into the fiber configurations that are most challenging for dMRI, and into the accuracy of different dMRI acquisition and analysis strategies, it was limited to validating dMRI against 2D fiber orientations within the PS-OCT imaging plane. More recently, the ability to reconstruct through-plane orientations by combining PS-OCT measurements from multiple light incidence angles was demonstrated in human samples4. In this study, we demonstrate the first comparison of fully 3D axonal orientations from a brain sample serially imaged with PS-OCT against dMRI previously acquired from the same sample. Our results show the promise of PS-OCT as a technique for imaging fiber pathways in 3D at microscopic resolutions in large brain volumes, opening up new possibilities for validating and improving dMRI.

Methods

We imaged a 3.5 x 1.5 x 8.3 cm tissue block from a formalin-fixed left human hemisphere (60 year-old female, cause of death: adenocarcinoma of pancreas, postmortem interval: 2 hours). The block was extracted from a coronal slab in the frontal lobe and contained segments of the anterior limb of the internal capsule and anterior segments of the superior longitudinal fasciculus5. 

dMRI: Scans were performed in a small-bore 4.7 T Bruker BioSpin MRI system equipped with maximum gradient strength of 660 mT/m. The dMRI data were collected using a two-shot, 3D echo-planar imaging sequence with: TE/TR=43/500 ms, δ/Δ=15/19 ms, 0.5 mm isotropic resolution, bmax = 40K, Cartesian grid q-space sampling scheme (515 volumes on an 113 cubic lattice enclosed in a sphere). Image preprocessing included denoising to improve signal-to-noise-ratio, and correction for distortions due to eddy currents and bias fields5. The data were resampled from a Cartesian grid to shells in q-space, as demonstrated previously2-3. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) was used to extract a fractional anisotropy (FA) map and multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (MSMT-CSD) was used to extract the three peak orientations of the fiber orientation distribution (FOD) at each voxel.

PS-OCT: A custom-built automatic serial sectioning PS-OCT system was used to acquire optical data for 3D axis mapping4. The system consists of a spectral domain PS-OCT centered at 1300 nm, motorized xyz translational stages and rotational stage to provide normal and 15° tilted illuminations, and a vibratome for tissue sectioning. The brain block was sectioned with 500 μm thickness and imaged via tile scan automatically until the whole volume was imaged. En face orientation data and apparent birefringence were obtained from 150 μm of tissue volume per physical slice. A computational algorithm retrieved in-plane and through-plane orientations based on polarization based contrasts from the normal and tilted illuminations which were used to generate 3D axis maps at 10 μm resolution4. As images were acquired in the blockface before sectioning, no section-to-section registration was required, thus the data collected from 19 sections were stacked into a volume.

Figure 1 shows a photograph of the human brain sample, and a slice of the volume as imaged with each modality (FA volume from the dMRI scan, and intensity and retardance volumes from the PS-OCT scan). The major fiber bundles that travel through the sample are marked on the retardance image: anterior commissure (AC), external capsule (EC), extreme capsule (EmC), internal capsule (IC), and superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF).

We used the retardance volume from PS-OCT and the FA volume from DTI to guide manual affine registration between the PS-OCT and dMRI data. In each PS-OCT voxel, the angular error in the dMRI orientations was computed as the minimum of the angles between the 3D optic axis measurement in that PS-OCT voxel and each of the three peak orientations of the FOD in the corresponding dMRI voxel.

Results

As seen in Figure 1, the bundles that contain fibers most closely aligned with the imaging plane, the AC (left-right) and the EC (inferior-superior), exhibit the highest retardance. The claustrum, a thin band of gray matter separating the EC from the EmC, can be resolved both in the dMRI and in the PS-OCT data, and is visible as a dark band in both the FA and retardance maps. The superficial white matter area that surrounds the SLF has uniformly low FA but more variable retardance levels, suggesting a complex pattern of in-plane and through-plane fibers that may confound dMRI.

Figure 2 shows the comparison between 3D axonal orientation estimates from the two modalities: the three most prominent peaks of the FODs estimated from the dMRI data, and the optic axis orientations estimated from the two PS-OCT measurements are all shown color-coded based on 3D orientation (red: left-right; green: anterior-posterior; blue: superior-inferior). The angular error map is shown as a heat map, thresholded to highlight areas with errors greater than 10°. Four locations in the sample where the angular error between dMRI and PS-OCT orientations was large (60-80°) are shown in magnification (a-d). In the case of a) and b), there is a fiber orientation that is prominent in the PS-OCT orientation maps but is not exactly captured by any of the dMRI peaks. In c) and d), the PS-OCT orientation maps show far greater complexity than what can be captured by a handful of peak orientations in dMRI. 

Conclusion

We present the first side-by-side comparison of 3D axonal orientation estimates from PS-OCT and dMRI in a large subvolume of the ex vivo human brain. Our prior validation studies compared dMRI to 2D (in-plane) orientation estimates from PS-OCT2-3, thus were limited to assessing the ability of dMRI to estimate orientations parallel to the PS-OCT imaging plane. The present study shows the feasibility of reconstructing both in-plane and through-plane fibers using PS-OCT measurements with multiple light incidence angles, in a large volume that has also undergone extensive dMRI scanning. This is a major step forward for PS-OCT as a technique that can access the ground truth of fiber pathways in the brain, and opens new avenues for validating, and potentially improving, dMRI in 3D. Our preliminary results highlight areas where dMRI, even at a high resolution of 0.5 mm isotropic, is confounded by complex fiber configurations. Future work will include a deeper characterization of these 3D fiber configurations that lead to errors in dMRI, as well as a direct comparison of tractography across scales using dMRI vs. PS-OCT.
