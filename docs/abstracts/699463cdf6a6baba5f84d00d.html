Introduction

Adolescents' mental health is jointly shaped by both family environment and neurodevelopment, and their influences may be bidirectional. The transactional model1 proposes that family contexts can regulate brain plasticity and shape neural architecture,2 while neural features may reciprocally elicit corresponding parenting behaviors. Furthermore, despite a shared objective environment, parents and youths often differ in their perceptions, and such perceptual gaps may affect neurodevelopment and psychopathology.3 Therefore, leveraging 5-year longitudinal data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, we examined bidirectional links between parent- and child-perceived family environment, multimodal neuroimaging markers, and youth psychopathology.

Methods

We used longitudinal data from the ABCD Study (release 5.1; https://abcdstudy.org) from 8,667 unrelated children (with siblings randomly excluded) aged 9–10 at baseline across 21 sites. Data access application documents were approved by the National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive. Relationships among family environment, mental health, and neuroimaging metrics across 5 annual time points (Baseline [T0] through Year 4 [T4]) were examined (Fig. 1). Family environment latent factors were constructed using the Child Report of Parent Behavior Inventory, Family Environment Scale, Parental Monitoring Survey, and parent-child relationship quality assessments.4 Parent- and child-reported environments were examined separately, with parent reports available at all 5 time points (T0–T4) and child reports collected at T0, T1, T3, and T4. Both factor models showed longitudinal invariance. Youth psychopathology was indexed by the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) scores measured at all waves. Structural and resting-state functional MRI data were collected at T0, T2, and T4 using harmonized protocols on 3T scanners. After standard preprocessing, we extracted cortical area for 148 regions (Destrieux Atlas) and functional connectivity (FC) within 13 networks defined by the Gordon Atlas,5 including the Visual (VIS), Auditory (AUD), Sensorimotor Hand (SMH) and Mouth (SMM), Dorsal/Ventral Attention (DAN/VAN), Salience (SN), Cingulo-Opercular (CO), Fronto-Parietal (FPN), Default Mode (DMN), Cingulo-Parietal (CP), Retrosplenial Temporal (RST), and "None" networks.

Baseline associations between neural features (i.e., surface area in regions, FC) and parent- and child-reported family environments were identified separately using general linear models (FDR-corrected P < .05). Bidirectional links were then tested using cross-lagged panel models, run separately for parent- and child-reported environments, integrating latent factors, baseline-identified neural features, and CBCL scores. All models used Full Information Maximum Likelihood for missing data and applied FDR correction to all paths (P < .05).

Results

Distinct neurobiological patterns emerged for parent versus child perspectives. In the Parent-Reported Model, a better family environment predicted reduced sensorimotor-hand (SMH) connectivity and increased surface area in superior frontal, inferior occipital, and precentral gyri (Fig. 2A, |β| = .017–.069, PFDR < .05). Reciprocal negative loops linked mental health and brain function: higher CBCL scores predicted altered SMH connectivity, while reduced SMH connectivity conversely predicted future symptom escalation (Fig. 2A, |β| = .019–.077, PFDR < .05). In contrast, the Child-Reported Model mapped onto higher-order cognitive  networks. Better environment predicted reduced connectivity among CO, DMN, and SN (Fig. 2B, |β| = .022–.073, PFDR < .05). Structurally, the insula exhibited dynamic bidirectional effects: larger baseline insular/supramarginal area predicted better T1 family perception (subsequently increasing T2 area), whereas larger T2 area predicted worse T3 perception (Fig. 2B, |β| = .017–.037, PFDR < .05). Behaviorally, CBCL scores showed complex bidirectional links with CO and DMN connectivity (Fig. 2B, |β| = .021–.035, PFDR < .05).

Conclusion

These findings support the transactional view that family environment, brain organization, and youth psychopathology mutually shape one another over time, highlighting reciprocal FC-psychopathology feedback loops and distinct neural markers across parent and child perspectives.
