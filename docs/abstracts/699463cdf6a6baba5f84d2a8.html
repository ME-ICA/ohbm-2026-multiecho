Introduction

Repetitive sensory-motor (RSM) behaviors, such as hand flapping, are considered "lower-order" restricted and repetitive behaviors (RRBs) in autism. These behaviors emerge in early childhood and have been linked to later functional outcomes.1 However, their neural mechanisms are unknown. The sensorimotor cortico-striatal circuit—comprising the dorsal putamen and its connections with motor and sensory cortices—may be relevant for RSM behaviors, given its role in motor learning and habit formation.2 Sensorimotor networks are among the earliest brain systems to develop, suggesting that their maturation may coincide with the emergence of RSM behaviors in early childhood. However, the relationship between the sensorimotor cortico-striatal connectivity and RSM behaviors remains poorly characterized during this critical developmental period when these symptoms are most prominent. To address this gap, we examined the relationship between RSM severity and sensorimotor cortico-striatal iFC in a well characterized sample of autistic preschoolers, including main effects of RSM and age as well as their interaction. 

Methods

Ninety autistic children aged 18-69 months (M=33.87, SD=8.49) with T1-weighted MRI, resting-state fMRI (>10 minutes, median FD<0.3mm), and Repetitive Behavior Scale-Revised (RBS-R) data were included. RSM severity was assessed using the RBS-R Stereotypic Behavior subscale. MRI data were preprocessed using C-PAC v1.8.7 with the RBC pipeline and 36-parameter nuisance regression. FreeSurfer and Ciftify were used for surface extraction and volume-to-surface mapping (~10k vertices per hemisphere) A study-specific anatomical template was generated using iterative, non-linear registration.3 Intrinsic functional connectivity (iFC) was computed between striatal seed regions and whole-brain cortical vertices. We focused on two dorsal putamen subregions, dorsal caudal putamen (DCP) and dorsal rostral putamen (DRP) [Di Martino et al4] which are both implicated in sensorimotor functions. Surface-based statistical analyses using BrainStat tested whole-brain vertex-wise associations between cortico-striatal iFC and (1) RSM severity, (2) age, and (3) RSM-by-age interaction, controlling for sex, median FD, and scan–RBS-R time difference. Multiple comparisons were corrected using random field theory with vertex-wise significance at FWER-corrected p < 0.05 and cluster-wise significance using a cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.01 with cluster-level FWER p < 0.05. We used the Yeo-7 network parcellation for interpreting the results. For regions showing significant interaction effects, we applied Johnson-Neyman curves5 to determine the age at which the RRB-iFC effect switches direction. 

Results

RSM main effects: For DRP, higher RSM severity was associated with reduced DRP-cortical iFC in two clusters: one in the posterior insula comprising sensorimotor and salience/ventral attention functional networks, and another in the central sulcus region included in the sensorimotor network. For DCP, no significant clusters survived correction at p<0.01. 

Age main effects: For DRP, no significant age effects were observed. For DCP, age was negatively associated with cortico-striatal iFC across multiple cortical regions including clusters in the visual and sensorimotor regions, as well as medial prefrontal regions in the salience, frontoparietal and default mode networks. 

RSM-by-Age interaction effects: Significant positive interactions effects were found for both DRP and DCP. Analyses revealed that a negative relationship between RSM severity and cortico-striatal iFC weakens with increasing age. For DRP, one cluster was identified in the visual cortex, where younger children (<36 months, n=29) exhibited a negative association (higher RSM → weaker connectivity; r=-0.49, p=0.007). This relationship weakened in middle-aged children (36-46 months, n=44; r=0.12, p=0.43) and showed a trend toward positive association in the oldest children (>46 months, n=20; r=0.41, p=0.07). For DCP, one cluster was found in the posterior insula, comprising primarily of sensorimotor and to a lesser degree salience networks. Younger children (<42 months, n=53; r=-0.34, p=0.01) showed a negative association between RSM severity and connectivity, whereas older children (≥42 months, n=40; r=-0.09, p=0.60) showed no significant relationship, indicating the negative association disappeared with age.

Conclusion

Higher RSM severity in autistic preschoolers was associated with reduced functional coupling between the DRP and cortical regions, including the posterior insula and central sulcus, implicating disrupted integration across sensorimotor and salience/ventral attention networks. Increasing age showed reduced iFC between the DCP and multiple cortical regions spanning the visual, sensorimotor, salience/ventral attention, frontoparietal and default mode networks, suggesting functional specialization across age. Critically, RSM-by-age interactions revealed two distinct developmental patterns. For DCP-posterior insula connectivity, higher RSM severity predicted weaker coupling in younger children, an effect that was absent in older children, suggesting developmental decoupling between RSM severity and DCP-sensorimotor connectivity. For DRP-visual cortex connectivity, higher RSM severity was associated with weaker coupling in younger children, implicating disrupted visual-motor integration. 

This study provides preliminary evidence that the neural correlates of RSM behaviors in autistic children undergo distinct patterns of developmental reorganization during early childhood. These findings highlight important implications for understanding heterogeneity in developmental trajectories and for detecting sensitive periods for targeting interventions. Future longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether early connectivity profiles predict symptom trajectories.
