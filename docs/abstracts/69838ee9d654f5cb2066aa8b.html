Introduction

Speech signals convey rich multidimensional information, encompassing not only linguistic content but also speaker identity. Accurate perception of speaker identity is crucial for social interaction. Previous neuroimaging studies have established that the bilateral middle and anterior superior temporal cortex, often referred to as the “temporal voice areas” (TVAs), show selective activation to human voices and play a key role in recognizing speakers.^1 However, most prior work has treated speaker identity as a unitary construct, focusing on who is speaking rather than on the specific traits that define that identity. Consequently, the neural mechanisms underlying the processing of distinct speaker traits remain underexplored.

Yet, the accurate perception of core traits, such as gender, age, and accent (region) is fundamental for communication. Critically, these traits are expressed through partially dissociable acoustic and phonological cues. For instance, gender is strongly associated with fundamental frequency (F0) and formant structures^2; age is linked to temporal and spectral features reflecting vocal tract maturity^3; and accent is defined by systematic phonological and prosodic deviations.^4 These acoustic divergences suggest that the neural encoding of speaker traits is likely non-uniform. To clarify how the cortex encodes these core identity attributes, the present fMRI study recorded participants listening to pseudowords produced by speakers differing in gender, age, and accent. Neural responses were analysed to identify brain regions supporting the specific encoding of these distinct speaker traits.

Methods

Ninety-six pseudo-English words were generated using ChatGPT-4, distributed equally across gender, age, and accent conditions (32 words each). Auditory stimuli were synthesized using Text to Speech OpenAI. The gender condition utilized two adult male and two adult female speakers (8 words per speaker). The age condition included two children (a boy and a girl) and two adults (a male and a female). The accent condition featured two British and two Indian English speakers (one male and one female per accent). Additionally, a level tone and a rising tone (196 Hz, 570 ms) were synthesized for a baseline tone identification task. All speech stimuli were normalized to 70 dB.

The experiment followed a blocked design controlled by E-Prime, consisting of four task conditions: gender, age, accent, and tone. These conditions were randomized across subjects and divided into two functional runs, with 16 trials per condition in each run. Task blocks were separated by four to six silence trials, during which only two visual asterisks were presented without auditory input. Each active trial began with a 500 ms fixation, followed by binaural audio presentation via MRI-compatible headphones. Simultaneously, a visual prompt displayed two choices (e.g., Male / Female), and participants were instructed to indicate their choice via a left or right button press within a 3-second window.

MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma scanner using a 32-channel head coil. High-resolution anatomical T1-weighted images were collected first (FOV = 220 mm, TR = 2500 ms, TE = 2.16 ms). Subsequently, two runs of functional images were acquired using a multiband T2*-weighted echo-planar sequence (TR = 1000 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3.5 mm³, FOV = 240 × 240 mm²).

Results

Participants achieved high accuracy in identifying gender (97.70% ± 3.71%), age (95.40% ± 3.29%), and accent (86.76% ± 7.10%). Accent proved to be the most difficult trait to identify (ps < 0.001), while gender was the easiest (ps < 0.05). Response times were generally rapid (around 1 second). Accent elicited the slowest responses (ps <0.001) and gender the fastest (ps < 0.001).

Structural and functional MRI data were preprocessed using DeepPrep (v25.1.0). ^5 A univariate GLM, contrasting each speaker trait against the tone baseline, identified robust bilateral activation within the auditory cortex. A conjunction analysis across all three conditions defined a shared “voice core” centered on the bilateral STG, STS, and Heschl’s gyrus, (Figure 1. b). Within the conjunction-defined ROI, one-way repeated-measure ANOVAs revealed significant differences in cortical activation across the three conditions in both hemispheres (left: F (2, 66) = 5.21, p < 0.001; right: F (2, 66) = 4.0301, p = 0.011). Post-hoc analyses indicated that accent processing elicited significantly stronger activation in bilateral posterior and middle STG/ STS compared to age (ps < 0.05) and gender (ps < 0.01), but that the age and gender showed no significant differences, indicating that neural intensity within the auditory cortex is modulated by the complexity of the acoustic-phonetic analysis.

Beyond this shared temporal network, distinct speaker traits recruited specific additional regions. Age identification engaged the left sensorimotor cortex, specifically the postcentral and precentral gyri, extending into the premotor cortex. This suggests that listeners may utilize sensorimotor integration to internally simulate vocal tract maturation, relying on motor resonance to map perceived age-related vocal changes onto their own articulatory motor plans. Accent processing heavily engages the inferior frontal gyrus (Broca’s area and its right homologue) and dorsal frontal regions (MFG/SFG), suggesting a high demand on top-down cognitive control, phonological working memory, and prosodic analysis. In contrast, gender identification did not recruit regions beyond the auditory cortex, consistent with the idea that gender judgments primarily rely on static acoustic features such as F0 and formant patterns that are mainly processed within early auditory stages. 

Conclusion

By deconstructing speaker identity into its constituent dimensions, gender, age, and accent, the present study provides compelling evidence that the neural perception of speaker identity is not a unitary operation, but rather a hierarchical process involving both shared auditory mechanisms and distinct, trait-specific cortical networks. First, our results confirm the existence of a fundamental “voice core” centered on the bilateral STG and STS. This region was universally active across all conditions, reinforcing the role of TVA in the initial extraction of vocal information. However, activity within this core was not uniform. Accent processing, which was behaviorally the most difficult, elicited significantly stronger responses in this area than age or gender. Beyond this core, we observed a clear dissociation in higher-order recruitment. Gender identification relied exclusively on early auditory regions, suggesting a bottom-up process driven by robust acoustic cues like F0 and formants. In contrast, age identification engaged the left sensorimotor cortex, implying a “motor resonance” mechanism where listeners internally simulate vocal tract maturation. Finally, accent processing recruited executive and phonological regions in the frontal lobe (IFG, MFG/SFG), reflecting the heavy top-down cognitive load required to analyze prosodic and phonological deviations. Collectively, these results support a “core-plus-extension” model of voice perception.
