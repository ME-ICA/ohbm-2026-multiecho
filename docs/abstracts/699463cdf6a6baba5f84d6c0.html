Introduction

Early life is characterized by rapid brain growth that is accompanied by profound changes in tissue microstructure, such as myelination of both gray and white matter. This developmental myelin growth is essential as myelin supports efficient neural communication, and atypical myelination is linked to a plethora of developmental and cognitive disorders. While previous work has characterized cortical and white matter myelination separately, it remains unclear whether myelin growth in these tissues proceeds independently or in a coordinated fashion during infancy. To close this gap in knowledge, here we combined two neonatal data sets with different myelin-sensitive neuroimaging metrics - a large-scale  T1w/T2w dataset and a quantitative R1 dataset - and tested whether the development of these myelin-sensitive metrics is coupled between white matter bundles and their respective cortical targets.

Methods

We evaluate two neonatal data sets: First, we leveraged data shared by the Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP), which included anatomical MRI, diffusion MRI, and T1w/T2w maps from 273 infants scanned between 29.29 and 44.71 weeks post-menstrual age. Second, we evaluated a locally collected dataset from the Stanford VPNL Baby Project (SVBP), which included anatomical MRI, diffusion MRI, and quantitative R1 maps from 21 infants scanned between 39.10 and 48.10 weeks post-menstrual age. In both datasets, diffusion MRI was processed using infant-specific pipelines, and 26 white matter bundles were automatically segmented in each infant’s native space using pyBabyAFQ. Each bundle was resampled to 100 nodes, and myelin-sensitive metrics (T1w/T2w in dHCP, R1 in SVBP) were assessed along their length. In addition, each bundle’s endpoints were mapped to the cortical surface, and myelin-sensitive metrics (T1w/T2w in dHCP, R1 in SVBP) were assessed at these cortical terminations for each bundle (Fig. 1, Fig. 2f). Finally, the myelin-sensitivity metrics along the bundle were related to those at the cortical terminations to assess if myelination in white matter bundles is linked with myelination of their connected cortical regions during early infancy. In addition to evaluating a potential link of the mean values of the metrics, we also assessed if their developmental slopes are linked across tissues. We further explored inter-individual variability and assessed whether the gray matter-white matter coupling varies with factors such as birth age or sex, and how early patterns of coupled myelination relate to later cognitive, language, and motor outcomes. Finally, we assessed the impact of prematurity on these developmental relationships using a longitudinal subsample followed from birth to term-equivalent age. 

Results

Our analyses revealed five key findings: (i) T1w/T2w and R1 values in white matter bundles are positively correlated with values in their cortical targets, both at the group level (Fig. 2a,b) and within individual infants. (ii) For both metrics, developmental slopes are also correlated between white and gray matter (Fig. 2d,e), indicating coordinated developmental trajectories of T1w/T2w and R1 across tissues. (iii) The strength of the gray matter-white matter coupling varies substantially across individuals. (iv) Inter-individual variability in T1w/T2w coupling is related to gestational age and stronger coupling is linked to better motor skills (Fig. 2c), but not cognitive or language performance at 17-25 months of age. (v) The T1w/T2w coupling across tissues was weaker in infants born prematurely compared to those born full-term, and these differences persisted even when infants were scanned at term-equivalent age.

Conclusion

Overall, our analyses suggest that cortical and white matter myelination develop in a tightly coordinated manner during early infancy. The degree of coupling of gray matter and white matter myelination is impacted by gestational age and premature birth and hence likely reflects brain maturity. Moreover, the degree of coupling differs between individuals and relates to later behavioral outcomes, suggesting that it has functional significance beyond the individual maturity of each tissue. Together, these findings argue that assessments of infant brain development and vulnerability should consider gray and white matter myelination jointly.
