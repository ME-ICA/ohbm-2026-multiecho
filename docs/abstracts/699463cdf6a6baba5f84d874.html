Introduction

In persons with drug-resistant epilepsy, epilepsy surgery may represent the only curative option for achieving seizure freedom. The goal is to remove the Epileptogenic Zone (EZ), namely the brain region indispensable for the generation of epileptic seizures.1 In complex scenarios, the EZ consists of a network of multiple brain structures, and its analysis requires invasive diagnostic techniques, among which Stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) is the most effective solution. This latter enables the direct recording of brain signals via intracranial electrodes, with the highest temporal and spatial resolution possible in humans.

Nowadays, SEEG interpretation is mostly based on visual analysis, focusing on the period associated with seizure generation. However, SEEG signals may include hundreds of channels, each associated with a specific brain location: in the case of large SEEG implants, studying channel characteristics and defining the EZ becomes particularly challenging. In the last few years, many tools have been proposed to improve SEEG analysis. However, most solutions exploit local channel features, while epileptogenic networks involve complex relationships2 that cannot be captured by considering a single channel at a time. In this context, the use of higher-order connectivity3 represents a promising but relatively unexplored methodology.

In this work, we compare several higher-order information-theoretic interaction measures on SEEG recordings from 24 patients who underwent intracranial exploration. We focus on the period of the earliest ictal discharge and analyze how the epileptogenic channels behave before and after the onset of the seizure. Our results denote that higher-order connectivity, in particular the Dual Total Correlation (DTC), characterizes the transition toward seizure more significantly than pairwise measures. This opens the definition of new EZ biomarkers, improving our understanding of the dynamics behind seizure generation.

Methods

We consider a dataset of N=24 consecutive patients who underwent SEEG monitoring at the IRCCS Institute of Neurological Science of Bologna, Italy. For each patient, we select a 200-second epoch encompassing the transition from the inter-ictal to the ictal phase, with the latter denoting the period associated with the clinical manifestation of seizure symptoms. When multiple seizure recordings are available per patient, we retain the first event, ensuring that the inter-ictal period reflects physiological brain activity and is not altered by prior seizures.

During the pre-processing stage, we align the data to a common temporal reference, ensuring that the seizure onset takes place after 175 seconds. Then, we segment each epoch into 1-second windows interspersed by 0.25 seconds, for a total of 797 windows per epoch. We remove channels associated with artifacts and implement a comb filter centered at 50 Hz to suppress powerline interference. As the next step, we apply the Hilbert transform to obtain the analytic representation of the signal and compute the instantaneous phase.4 Hence, we estimate the empirical distribution of the phase associated with each channel and window. In this stage, we adopt the Sturges rule to determine the number of bins per window, which, given the sampling frequency fs=1000 Hz, yields k=1+log(1000)≈11 bins. 

We model the SEEG data associated with each window as a hypergraph with hyperedges of up to order 3, where each channel corresponds to a node and synchronization between groups of channels determines the hyperedges. We compute the following information-theoretic measures: (i) the Shannon entropy (H) of the instantaneous phase to characterize channels at a local level, (ii) the Mutual Information (MI) to characterize pairwise relationships between channel pairs, (iii) the Total Correlation (TC) and (iv) the Dual Total Correlation (DTC) to characterize higher-order relationships among triads of channels. Furthermore, we also compute (iv) the Interaction Information (II)  as the difference between DTC and TC. In doing so, we distinguish between triads with positive (P-II) and negative (N-II) interaction information, discriminating the higher-order effects associated with information redundancy and synergy.5

To compare across measures fairly, we project pairwise and higher-order measures onto individual brain locations, aggregating the values of MI, TC, DTC, P-II, and N-II across all the hyper-edges incident to the same node. To quantify how node-level features are affected by the ictal phase, we define a baseline period as the 50 seconds preceding the seizure onset. For each measure and node, we compute the mean and standard deviation during the baseline, providing a reference for the node's physiological activity. Subsequently, we consider the 5 seconds following seizure onset and normalize the node-level features by the corresponding baseline, yielding the relative shift of each measure during the early ictal phase.

For group-level analysis, we differentiate between nodes in the Epileptogenic Zone (EZ) and those part of the physiological network (NON-EZ). In our dataset, channels were labeled as EZ and NON-EZ based on the decision taken by the medical doctor at the end of the recording, when the EZ is thermocoagulated. We aggregate the results for the two classes, obtaining a distribution of N shift values for each class and measure. To assess whether the different measures can discriminate between these two classes of nodes, we compare the shifts in H, MI, TC, DTC, P-II, and N-II via a two-sample t-test. For each patient and measure, we also compute the difference between the normalized values associated with the EZ and the NON-EZ areas, respectively, obtaining a distribution of N difference values for each measure. Hence, we conduct an additional two-sample t-test to assess whether the difference between EZ and NON-EZ is significantly larger when using DTC than MI. 

Results

We first analyze the temporal evolution of node-level features, distinguishing channels in the Epileptogenic Zone (EZ) from those in the physiological brain network (NON-EZ), and averaging the results across all patients. Figs. 2a-2f show that all measures exhibit an abrupt variation at seizure onset, indicating that the ictal phase strongly alters signal dynamics not only within the EZ but also across the physiological brain network. Notably, before seizure onset (t<175 s), the Shannon entropy (H) is consistently lower in the epileptogenic channels compared to the physiological ones. Since entropy is an indicator of the amount of information contained in a variable, this finding suggests that the EZ presents less diversified waveform patterns. A possible interpretation is that the EZ produces pathological oscillations that are relatively independent and not modulated by other brain areas. This reduced influence limits the richness of oscillations and, consequently, decreases the randomness of the instantaneous phase, quantified by H.

When considering connectivity measures, both pairwise (MI) and higher-order (TC and DTC) features indicate that, during the inter-ictal phase, the EZ exhibits stronger information flow compared to physiological nodes. This relationship reverses after the seizure onset, as the EZ undergoes an abrupt drop in connectivity. We can hypothesize that, upon initiating the generation of epileptic discharges, the EZ adopts a pathological regime and becomes desynchronized from the rest of the brain network. Interestingly, as the brain progresses into the core of the ictal phase, connectivity increases again in both epileptogenic and physiological channels. This rebound suggests that the propagation of epileptic discharges leads to widespread synchronization of the brain region, albeit in a pathological state.

When we take the positive (P-II) and negative interaction information (N-II) into account, we observe that the latter measure better differentiates epileptogenic and non-epileptogenic channels. Before seizure onset, the EZ presents lower N-II values, indicating that physiological brain regions present stronger synergistic interactions and, consequently, more complex functional organization than pathological ones. At the beginning of the ictal phase, synergistic interactions abruptly increase in both the EZ and NON-EZ, reflecting a rapid transition of the brain into a new regime. In contrast, P-II does not appear to be a discriminative indicator, as the EZ and NON-EZ areas show comparable values across all time windows. This suggests that information redundancy is less sensitive to seizure-related alterations than synergistic interactions.

In Fig. 2g, we compare the shifts of the different measures during the first 5 seconds of the ictal stage. This period is characterized by an increase in entropy (H) and a decrease in pairwise (MI) and higher-order (TC and DTC) connectivity. Notably, higher-order measures exhibit stronger variation than both MI and H. This observation is quantitatively supported by a two-sample t-test comparing the distributions of the normalized node-level features. In particular, the shift in DTC is significantly larger than that observed for MI (t-score=2.626, p-value=0.006) and H (t-score=5.61, p-value<0.001). Finally, in Fig. 2h, we report the distribution of the differences between the normalized values associated with the EZ and NON-EZ (see methods) for each node-level feature. By comparing the distributions obtained with MI and DTC via a two-sample t-test, we confirm that these measures exhibit a significant performance difference (t-score=2.4, p-value=0.011), and the second is more effective in discriminating epileptogenic channels. These findings indicate that DTC is particularly sensitive to ictal-related changes, suggesting its potential for developing novel epileptogenic biomarkers.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first application of higher-order information-theoretic interactions for modeling Stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) data. We developed a processing pipeline to estimate the empirical distribution of the instantaneous phase of SEEG signals. We quantified signal randomness using a range of pairwise and higher-order quantities. The results, obtained from a cohort of 24 patients, demonstrate that higher-order measures, particularly DTC, enable an improved characterization of epileptogenic networks during the ictal transition, and that they are more effective in discriminating epileptogenic from physiological channels.

Our findings align with results from similar studies using MRI and other neuroimaging modalities, and reinforce the need to extend connectivity analyses beyond pairwise interactions to higher-dimensional representations of brain dynamics. This perspective is particularly relevant in epilepsy as epileptogenic networks are inherently defined by the complex interactions among multiple brain structures. A proper evaluation of how distinct brain regions cooperate and disengage during the pre-ictal phase is fundamental for understanding the dynamics that lead to seizure onset. In this regard, the ability to differentiate between synergistic and redundant information exchange may open unexplored avenues for characterizing the Epileptogenic Zone (EZ) and elucidating the mechanisms that yield seizure outbreaks.

Notably, the application of higher-order measures to SEEG data remains very limited in the current literature. This may be due to the high complexity of such data, which often includes days of continuous recordings and poses severe computational challenges, even greater than those encountered in other neuroimaging modalities. Furthermore, SEEG recordings are highly patient-specific, as each implant targets particular brain regions based on the clinical evaluation and surgical hypothesis for that individual. As a result, the sampling of brain regions is irregular, and the number of nodes involved in epileptogenic activity can vary considerably across patients. These factors make the interpretation of higher-order measures in SEEG particularly challenging and raise important questions about how to compare or generalize such measures across individuals.

In the future, we plan to validate the proposed pipeline in larger patient cohorts, potentially involving multi-center datasets. Furthermore, exploring alternative processing strategies beyond those used in this initial study may yield diverse outcomes. Notably, the results presented here were based on broadband SEEG data, without applying any band-pass filtering. Repeating the analysis across different frequency bands may provide additional insights into how higher-order interactions vary across distinct oscillatory components. Moreover, our pipeline can be applied to different stages of SEEG recordings, enabling the characterization of brain dynamics during the inter-ictal as well as post-ictal periods. This will yield new insights to improve the interpretation of SEEG data and, more generally, our understanding of the mechanisms behind epileptogenic networks.
