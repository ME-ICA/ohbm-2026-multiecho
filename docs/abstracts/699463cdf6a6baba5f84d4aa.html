Introduction

Puberty represents rapid physical, emotional and neurocognitive changes, marking the transition from childhood to adulthood. Neurocognitive development across adolescence is well documented, involving neuroanatomical changes associated with the development of inhibitory control, working memory, and other executive functions that shape decision-making. Typically, research investigating  neurocognitive development in adolescence is focused on age-related changes. However, with puberty starting at younger ages, especially for females, it raises the question whether age-related benchmarks are the most effective way to understand this developmental period. There remains a significant gap in our understanding of the relative importance of pubertal maturation versus chronological age in driving neurocognitive development. Moreover, given sex differences in pubertal timing, sex-specific models are needed to examine the independent contributions of chronological age versus pubertal stage on neurocognitive development. Using a large longitudinal dataset, the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, we investigated the extent to which age and pubertal stage influence brain and cognitive development across adolescence (9-16yrs). 

Methods

Data were collected from the ABCD Study 6.0 release at baseline (ages 9-10 years), 2-, 4-, and 6-year follow-ups. Variables included age, pubertal development (Pubertal Development Scale), three fluid cognition tasks from the NIH Toolbox: Flanker task (inhibitory control), Picture Sequence Memory Task (episodic memory) and Pattern Comparison Task (processing speed), and anatomical metrics (thickness, surface area, and gray matter volume) from frontal regions based on the Desikan-Killiany parcellation. These regions included: the superior frontal, rostral middle frontal, caudal middle frontal, pars opercularis, pars triangularis, pars orbitalis, lateral orbitofrontal, medial orbitofrontal, precentral gyrus, and the paracentral lobule. Frontal regions were selected based on prior research highlighting their developmental changes across adolescence and critical role in cognitive functioning. 

We tested the relationship between age, pubertal stage and brain and cognition, longitudinally, across four time points. Sex-specific linear mixed-effects models were implemented for each cognitive task and region of interest. All models included subject ID as a random intercept and age as a random slope, with age and pubertal development stage as fixed effects. For cortical models, site was additionally included as a random intercept and scanner model as a fixed-effect covariate. All models were corrected for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate (FDR) and Variance Inflation Factor values were checked to rule out multicollinearity. Surface area and volume were both adjusted for intracranial volume before they were entered into each model. The sample size for each model varied given availability of cortical and cognitive data ranging from n=4,966-4,976. 

Results

In males, cognitive models showed that age significantly (p < 0.001) positively predicted performance on all three cognitive tasks across adolescence (Flanker: β = 2.33; Picture Sequence Memory Task: β = 5.92; Pattern Comparison Task: β = 5.93). When adjusting for age, higher pubertal scores were associated with lower Flanker performance. Pubertal development did not significantly predict Picture Sequence Memory or Pattern Comparison Task performance. In females, age significantly (p < 0.001) positively predicted performance on all three cognitive tasks across adolescence (Flanker: β = 1.99; Picture Sequence Memory Task: β = 5.86; Pattern Comparison Task: β = 5.85). When adjusting for age, higher pubertal development was associated with higher Picture Sequence Memory Task scores (β =0.71, p = 0.016). Pubertal development did not significantly predict Flanker or Pattern Comparison Task performance. 

In males, cortical models (Figure 1) showed that age significantly (p < 0.01) negatively predicted cortical thickness, surface area, and volume in all regions of interest. When adjusting for age, higher pubertal scores were significantly (p < 0.01) associated with less cortical thickness in six of the ten regions of interest (rostral middle frontal, caudal middle frontal, pars triangularis, pars orbitalis, lateral orbitofrontal, and paracentral lobule) and less surface area and volume in all regions of interest (p < 0.01). In females (Figure 1), age was negatively associated with cortical thickness in all regions of interest and volume for nine of the ten regions (excluding the precentral gyrus). However, an older age was significantly (p < 0.01) associated with larger surface area in eight of the ten frontal regions (excluding the rostral middle frontal and pars triangularis). When adjusting for age, higher pubertal scores were significantly (p < 0.01) associated with less cortical thickness in eight of the ten frontal regions (excluding pars opercularis and paracentral gyrus) and less surface area and volume in all regions of interest. When comparing the effect size of age versus pubertal stage in each model patterns emerge (Figure 1). Age was the strongest predictor of cortical thickness in both sexes while pubertal stage was the strongest predictor of surface area in both sexes (albeit in opposite directions). While age and pubertal stage showed comparable effects in males, pubertal stage more strongly predicted volume in females. This suggests that during adolescence, pubertal stage may primarily drive maturation of surface area and volume of frontal regions, whilst chronological age predominantly drives maturation of cortical thickness. 

Conclusion

Taken together, our findings reveal distinct sex-specific patterns in how age versus pubertal maturation drive neurocognitive development during adolescence. While age consistently predicted cognitive improvements across all domains in both sexes, pubertal development showed divergent effects: impaired inhibitory control development in males and enhanced episodic memory in females. This suggests, depending upon the cognitive domain in question, earlier pubertal development may be beneficial for females but potentially problematic for males. Notably, our finding that males who were more pubertally developed for their age showed poorer inhibitory control may contribute to the risky decision-making behaviors commonly observed in some adolescent males. 

These cognitive patterns align with the neuroanatomical findings, where age was the strongest predictor of cortical thickness in both sexes, while pubertal stage more strongly influenced surface area and volume. Notably, females showed a unique pattern of age-surface area relationships, in which girls that reached the same pubertal stage but at an older age showed larger surface area. This may indicate less pubertally-driven cortical maturation for girls who hit pubertal milestones later in adolescence. 

The stronger influence of pubertal stage on brain volume changes, particularly in females, coupled with its positive effects on episodic memory performance, suggests that hormonal and biological aspects of puberty may be more critical than age for understanding some aspects of brain development and cognitive domains during adolescence. Our findings challenge age-based developmental benchmarks and highlight the importance of considering pubertal timing, for understanding individual differences in adolescent brain and cognitive development. This is especially important for individuals with atypical pubertal development, as deviations in this process may affect the maturation of neurocognitive functions. Accounting for pubertal development in addition to chronological age may enhance the accuracy of mental health assessments and could inform tailored academic support for children who develop at different rates.
