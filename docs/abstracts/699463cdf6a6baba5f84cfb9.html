Introduction

Efficient communication relies on the integration of information across multiple sensory modalities, enabling a coherent and unified perception of the environment. In particular, audiovisual (AV) integration plays a key role in constructing meaning during speech comprehension1. Receptive vocabulary, an indicator of lexical and semantic knowledge, further supports this process and constitutes a fundamental component of language development2. At the neural level, audiovisual integration and narration comprehension depend on coordinated interactions of multiple brain regions. Functional connectivity (FC) analyses provide insights into how distributed brain regions interact as coordinated networks to support such complex cognitive functions. These networks further show extensive development throughout childhood, reflecting the maturation of higher-order processes such as language, attention, and conflict resolution. Yet, the behavioral and neural mechanisms underlying AV narration processing, and conflict detection and their developmental trajectories in children remain poorly understood, partly due to methodological challenges when testing younger populations.

Movie-based fMRI paradigms offer a naturalistic and engaging way to study these mechanisms, capturing neural processes during realistic multisensory experiences. In the present study, we examined both brain activation and FC within corresponding networks during AV congruent and incongruent narration in children, using a manipulated fMRI movie paradigm. We further assessed how age and receptive vocabulary skills modulate these neural responses. This work provides new insights into how individual language abilities shape the functional organization of multisensory language networks and audiovisual integration and conflict processing in the developing brain.

Methods

159 German-speaking children (mean age = 7.8y [5.6 - 13y], 75 female) participated in an fMRI movie-watching paradigm consisting of blocks of visual and audiovisual scenes. Visuals were based on cartoon movie sequences from “The Snail and the Whale” and the narration was recorded in German and manipulated to create 4 different conditions: (1) audiovisual congruent (AVcon), in which the narration matched the visual content; (2) AV incongruent (AVinc), in which the narration did not match the visuals; (3) visual-only scenes presented without narration; and (4) audiovisual scenes featuring time-reversed speech as narration. The conditions were presented in 15-second blocks in a pseudo-randomized order. Each condition was presented 8 times, resulting in a total movie duration of 8 minutes. Stimuli were presented via MR-compatible goggles and headphones.

Functional MRI data were acquired on a Philips Achieva 3 Tesla scanner using a 32-channel coil. T2* weighted functional images were recorded with a whole-brain gradient echoplanar image sequence (repetition time TR = 1395 ms, echo time TE = 35 ms, 44 slices, voxel size = 3.0 x 3.0 x 3.0 mm3). High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were obtained using a magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo sequence (TR = 6.96 ms, TE = 3.2 ms, 176 slices, voxel size = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm³). Standard preprocessing included slice-time correction, realignment, coregistration and segmentation and was implemented in MATLAB (version R2022b) toolbox SPM12 (7771). After preprocessing, scans with excessive motion were repaired with ArtRepair and unrepaired scans surrounded by scans with motion were flagged. Children with more than 15% repaired and flagged volumes were excluded from analyses.

The first-level analysis was conducted using a general linear model including the onsets and durations (15 seconds) of the 4 conditions convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function. The model also incorporated the six realignment parameters and an additional regressor of no interest for each flagged scan.

Whole-brain activation during AV incongruent scenes was contrasted with activation during AV congruent scenes (AVinc > AVcon) using one-sample t-tests and controlling for age and sex, applying a cluster-level family-wise error corrected threshold (FWEcorr) of p < .05 with a cluster-defining threshold of p < .001. In addition, age-related differences in brain activation during AV incongruency processing (AVinc > AVcon) on whole-brain level were examined.  

FC analyses were performed using the MATLAB toolbox CONN with its default analysis pipeline3. Seed-to-voxel FC analyses were performed using the left and right inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and the left and right posterior superior temporal gyrus (pSTG) as seeds of the language network, defined according to CONN’s HCP-ICA networks atlas. Condition-related differences in FC across the whole sample were assessed using the contrasts AVinc > AVcon and AVcon > AVinc. Further, age was included as a second-level covariate and age-related effects on FC using the same seeds were examined by testing the contrast associated with the age regressor.

Receptive vocabulary skills were measured with the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test, 4th edition (PPVT-4), and converted to age-normed percentile ranks. To examine whether vocabulary proficiency modulated functional connectivity during audiovisual narration processing, second-level regression analyses were performed between PPVT-4 percentile ranks and seed-to-voxel connectivity for each seed and contrast. All FC analyses used a cluster-level family-wise error correction (FWEcorr) threshold of p < .05 with a cluster-defining threshold of p < .001.

Results

One-sample t-tests across the full sample showed stronger bilateral activation in the middle frontal gyrus, angular gyrus, anterior insula, and precuneus during the processing of AV incongruent compared to AV congruent scenes (AVinc > AVcon). No region showed greater activation for congruent scenes. 

Age was positively associated with activation in the right supplementary motor cortex, right middle frontal gyrus, left superior and inferior frontal gyrus, as well as with bilateral activation in the anterior insula, precuneus, superior temporal gyrus, angular gyrus, and middle cingulate gyrus during AV incongruency vs congruency processing.

Seed-to-voxel FC analyses, controlling for age and sex, indicated condition-specific connectivity patterns. Using the left IFG as a seed, stronger FC with the bilateral medial frontal cortex emerged for AVinc > AVcon. The right IFG showed stronger FC with the bilateral inferior occipital gyrus and right fusiform gyrus for AVinc > AVcon, and with a cluster spanning the left posterior cingulate gyrus, left cuneus, and left lingual gyrus for AVcon > AVinc. The left pSTG seed exhibited stronger FC with the left IFG and with a cluster encompassing the left inferior occipital and middle temporal gyrus for AVinc > AVcon, and increased FC with the left lingual gyrus and right calcarine cortex for AVcon > AVinc. The right pSTG showed increased FC with a cluster spanning the right inferior occipital and temporal gyrus and the left supramarginal gyrus for AVinc > AVcon, and with the left precuneus for AVcon > AVinc.

Regarding age-related effects on functional connectivity during AV processing, the AVinc > AVcon contrast showed that age was positively associated with FC between the right IFG and a cluster spanning the left cuneus and left posterior cingulate gyrus. The opposite contrast AVcon > AVinc revealed a positive association between age and FC between the left IFG and left pSTG (seed regions) and the left precentral gyrus, as well as between the right pSTG (seed) and a cluster spanning the right inferior occipital and temporal gyri. 

Finally, higher receptive vocabulary scores were associated with stronger FC between the left IFG and the bilateral precuneus for AVcon > AVinc, whereas children with lower scores showed stronger FC between the right pSTG and the bilateral superior parietal lobule, left middle occipital gyrus, and right inferior temporal gyrus during AV congruent scenes.

Conclusion

Children engaged partially distinct neural systems when listening to congruent versus incongruent audiovisual narration during movie watching. Incongruent scenes elicited stronger activation in frontal, parietal, temporal, and insular regions typically involved in conflict monitoring, salience detection, and semantic processing, indicating that mismatching narration imposes higher cognitive demands on control and integrative mechanisms as children orient to unexpected narration and attempt to reconcile discrepant information4. 

These congruency-related differences in neural activation also show systematic age-related variation, highlighting how the underlying processes mature across childhood. Older children showed greater recruitment of frontal, temporal, and insular regions when processing incongruent as compared with congruent narration, suggesting increasingly efficient engagement of higher-order systems for detecting and interpreting audiovisual conflicts5. 

Functional connectivity results extend these findings by showing that interactions between regions of the language network and visual, frontal, and parietal areas depend on the audiovisual context. Age effects on FC suggest continued refinement of the network organization. Older children showed stronger connectivity between language seeds and regions involved in visual processing and audiovisual integration during congruent scenes, as well as stronger connectivity between language seeds and regions supporting the processing of incongruent information and attention-modulated visual processing during AV incongruent scenes. Together, these age-related connectivity patterns parallel the activation findings, pointing to increasing coordination within distributed networks and suggesting that children gradually develop more specialized and efficient neural strategies for processing both congruent and incongruent audiovisual information.

Beyond age, individual differences in receptive vocabulary further shaped network organization. Children with higher vocabulary showed stronger coupling between language seeds and regions implicated in semantic integration and mental imagery during congruent scenes, suggesting that richer lexical knowledge supports more efficient construction and integration of narrative meaning. In contrast, children with lower vocabulary proficiency showed increased reliance on parietal, occipital, and temporal regions during congruent scenes, potentially consistent with greater reliance on perceptual and attentional mechanisms when lexical resources are limited.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that audiovisual congruency, age, and the size of the vocabulary shape the neural systems supporting audiovisual narration processing in childhood. The naturalistic movie-based paradigm allowed us to examine neural network organization under ecologically valid conditions, revealing variability in how children process audiovisual information. Together, this work contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how multisensory language networks develop and how linguistic abilities modulate their functional organization.

In summary, these findings suggest that the brain regions supporting conflict detection and resolution during audiovisual comprehension continue to develop throughout childhood and are language skill dependent.
