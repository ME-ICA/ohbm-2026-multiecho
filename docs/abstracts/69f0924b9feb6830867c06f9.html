Introduction

Why do we sleep? While substantial evidence establishes the role of sleep in memory consolidation and learning, ^1, ^2 a more intriguing question remains. Does sleep merely serve the replication of old knowledge, or does it actively fuel the creation of new ideas? Recent studies suggest the latter, indicating that sleep may enhance creativity and novel idea generation. ^3 However, the behavioural and neural mechanisms by which creativity is generated remain unknown.

We focus here on the transition from wakefulness to Stage 1 Non-REM sleep (N1). This hypnagogic state is often accompanied by vivid visual imagery and represents a unique window of reduced cognitive control that may facilitate the escape from conventional thought patterns. ^4 Theoretically, this aligns with the mechanism of divergent thinking, a core component of creativity. Divergent thinking relies on associative recombination, which refers to the ability to reorganize existing memory elements into novel configurations. ^5 To test whether sleep promotes this specific mechanism, an objective measure that assesses divergent thinking via associative recombination is required. To this end, we developed a novel behavioural paradigm: the Associative Imagination Task (AIT). The AIT specifically evaluates the capacity to generate numerous novel ideas by combining distinct visual modalities, such as color and texture. Based on this framework, we hypothesized that the Wake-to-N1 transition facilitates novel idea generation by enabling the flexible recombination of recently experienced elements into new combinations. To empirically test this hypothesis and elucidate the underlying neural substrates, we conducted a simultaneous electroencephalography (EEG) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) recording during the sleep following the experience of AIT.

Methods

Participants

A total of 62 healthy adults (mean age = 24 years, SD = 4.53 years, 29 female) participated in the study and were randomly assigned to either a sleep group (N = 30) or an awake group (N = 32). 

Behavioral task

Participants in the sleep group completed the AIT, designed to quantify novel idea generation, before (Pre-sleep) and after (Post-sleep) a 2-2.5 hour interval. Within this interval, participants were allowed to take naps of up to 90 minutes while undergoing simultaneous EEG–fMRI recording. In contrast, the wake group remained awake during the interval and engaged in unrelated cognitive tasks. In this AIT, participants were asked to generate as many ideas as possible based on combinations of two elements from different visual modalities (e.g. color and texture, shape and motion, etc.). For instance, as shown in Fig. 1, when the combination of a circle and outward motion was presented, participants typically responded with “fireworks,” “shower,” or “fountain.” The Post-sleep task comprised three conditions defined relative to the pairs experienced during Pre-sleep: identical pairs (SM-SC; Same Modality, Same Combination), recombined pairs of pre-exposed elements (SM-DC; Same Modality, Different Combination), and pairs consisting of entirely new elements (SM-NE; Same Modality, Novel Elements).

EEG–fMRI acquisition 

Simultaneous EEG-fMRI recordings were performed using a 32-channel BrainAmp MRplus system (sampling rate: 5 kHz) following the 10–20 system, with additional ECG, EOG, and EMG channels. MRI data were collected on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner (64-channel coil). Functional images used a T2*-weighted EPI sequence (TR=1250ms, TE=30ms, voxel size=2.4mm³), and structural images used a high-resolution T1-weighted sequence (voxel size=0.7mm³).

EEG preprocessing and sleep scoring 

EEG data were processed using BrainVision Analyzer 2. After gradient and BCG artifact correction, data were downsampled to 250Hz and band-pass filtered (0.1–50 Hz). Sleep stages were scored in 15 s epochs according to AASM criteria. We calculated the frequency (total count) and duration (length of N1 immediately following wakefulness) of Wake-to-N1 transitions to characterize sleep onset.

Functional MRI preprocessing and EEG–fMRI coupling analysis 

fMRI data were analyzed using FSL FEAT. Preprocessing included motion/slice-timing correction, spatial smoothing (5mm), and high-pass filtering. First-level GLM analysis included regressors modelling the transition from wakefulness to N1 sleep based on EEG scoring (onset defined as N1 start). Group-level analysis used FLAME 1 (FWE cluster-level [z>3.1] corrected p<0.05). Subsequently, to examine the relationships between brain activity and behavior, we extracted BOLD signal time courses from significant clusters within a 30-s window centered on the transition onset (-15 to +15 s). Mean signals for the preceding Wake phase (-15–0 s) and the subsequent N1 phase (0–15 s) were calculated and their correlation with behavioral performance was assessed separately.

Results

Behavioral results

To test whether sleep promotes novel idea generation, we performed a mixed-model ANOVA on the number of generated ideas after the 2-hour interval of sleep or wake. This analysis revealed a significant Group (Sleep, Wake) × Condition (SM-SC, SM-DC, SM-NE) interaction (F2,120 = 3.320, p = 0.041), indicating that sleep influenced task performance. Post-hoc analyses revealed that a significantly larger number of novel ideas were generated for the SM-DC condition [N(SM-DC)] than the SM-SC condition [N(SM-SC)] in sleep group (t60 = 3.121, p = 0.008, Holm-Bonferroni corrected) but not in the wake group (t60 = 1.026, p = 0.309, Holm-Bonferroni corrected). Moreover, the novel-combination-specific gain [N(SM-DC) – N(SM-SC)] was significantly larger for the Sleep group compared to the Wake group (t60 = 2.956, p = 0.004). In contrast, no significant differences between SM-NE and SM-SC conditions were observed in either Sleep (t60 = 1.097, p = 0.277, Holm-Bonferroni corrected) or Wake group (t60 = 0.255, p = 0.799, Holm-Bonferroni corrected). Collectively, these results suggest that sleep facilitates the reorganization of existing associations, allowing distant concepts to be flexibly recombined to spark novel ideas.

EEG results

To investigate whether the wake-to-N1 transition specifically enhances the generation of novel ideas, we analyzed the relationship between transition metrics (frequency and duration) and the behavioral gain (defined as the differential number of generated ideas between novel and previous combinations). A multiple regression analysis revealed that the transition frequency was positively correlated with the novel-combination-specific gain (β = 1.371, p = 0.007) whereas the mean duration of N1 sleep after the transition was negatively correlated with the novel-combination-specific gain (β = −1.233, p = 0.014). These results suggest that frequent entries into short N1 sleep state facilitate recombinational association processes.

fMRI results

To characterize the neural basis of novel idea generation across the wake-to-N1 transition, we conducted a whole-brain fMRI analysis. Significant activation clusters in response to the wake-to-N1 transition were identified in the right anterior prefrontal cortex (R aPFC; [x,y,z]=[46,34,18], Z = 4.27, p < 0.05, cluster-level corrected [z  >  3.1]) and in the right pars opercularis of the inferior frontal gyrus (R IFG; [x,y,z]=[58,30,18], Z = 4.68, p < 0.05, cluster-level corrected [z  >  3.1]). Importantly, the sleep-driven behavioral improvement assessed by novel-combination-specific gain was predicted by the signal in both R aPFC and IFG at the time of wake-to-N1 transition. We further found that aPFC decorrelated spontaneous activities of visual cortical regions encoding the pre-exposed visual elements, which would support the flexible recombination of distant concepts. Taken together, these findings implicate the aPFC and IFG as key neural substrates supporting novel idea generation triggered by the brain state change during the wake-to-N1 transition.

Conclusion

While sleep is widely recognized for supporting memory consolidation and learning, our findings demonstrate its opposite role: facilitating the generation of novel ideas. The present study provides the first direct empirical evidence elucidating the neural mechanisms by which the transition from wakefulness to N1 sleep facilitates novel idea generation. We developed a new behavioural paradigm, the Associative Imagination Task (AIT), to quantify novel idea generation, and performed simultaneous EEG-fMRI recording during sleep. Behaviorally, we found that the sleep-induced benefit is specific to the associative recombination of pre-existing memory elements. Crucially, frequent transitions from wake to brief N1 episodes (sleep onset state) facilitates this process and enhanced activity in the aPFC and IFG was involved in this improved performance. Furthermore, aPFC activity during the transition drives the desynchronization of visual cortical areas ("neural decoupling").

Importantly, the creative boost is specific to the associative recombination of pre-exposed memory elements rather than general cognitive functions. This finding directly answers our initial research question, suggesting that the sleep onset period serves as a unique cognitive window for "creation" rather than simple "replication." Our EEG results revealed a critical dynamic underlying this process: a sleep onset state, characterized by high-frequency yet brief entries into N1, proved optimal for novel idea generation. This challenges the conventional view that N2/N3 sleep or REM sleep yields better cognitive benefits. Our fMRI results propose a possible neural mechanism to generate new ideas from old memories via neural decoupling mediated by the aPFC. These results fundamentally expand our understanding of sleep's function, highlighting its role not only in stabilizing the past but also in preparing for the future based on imagination.
