Introduction

Motor adaptation is the capacity to adjust movement in response to a change in the environment or in the body. Maintaining this capacity is important in aging as it helps preserve motor skills and independence despite physical changes over time.^1 Adaptation relies on multiple learning processes with distinct neural substrates: implicit learning, a function responsible for unconscious error minimization supported by cerebellar function, and explicit learning (i.e., conscious learning) linked with frontoparietal and visuospatial working memory systems. Older adults (OA) are reported to retain implicit recalibration but show reduced reliance on explicit strategies,^2 yet this shift occurs alongside pronounced age-related degeneration within cerebellar networks.

Although behavioral dissociations between implicit and explicit learning are well established, it is still unknown whether intrinsic resting-state (rs) neural signatures predict the ability to retain and re-engage these components over extended timescales. Rs-functional MRI (rsfMRI) offers a window into these intrinsic neural properties, but connectivity analysis show limited test-retest reliability. 

To overcome this limitation, we utilized the fractional Amplitude of Low Frequency Fluctuations (fALFF), a semi-quantitative voxel-wise metric of rsfMRI.^3 fALFF characterizes spontaneous rs neural oscillations in low frequencies [0.008-0.01 Hz], has demonstrated high repeatability, and low susceptibility to physiological confounds.^4 This motivated its use to investigate the relationship between cerebellar function with age and performance changes in implicit and explicit savings (i.e., faster relearning) in a visuomotor adaptation task over multiple sessions.

Methods

Behavior:

Younger (YA, N=25, age=21+3.5y.o.) and older adults (OA, N=25, age=72+6.6 y.o.) completed two visuomotor rotation sessions separated by an average of one week (session 1, S1; session 2, S2). Perturbation trials consisted of a 45° rotation clockwise or counterclockwise, counterbalanced across participants and across sessions. Hand movements were obscured for the duration of the experiment. A target was positioned at 90° (directly away from the participant) on the screen, 10cm from the start position. Explicit learning was measured by selecting the direction of an arrow on the screen to indicate intended movement, while overall movement was measured by the heading angle determined 3cm into the movement. Implicit learning was the angle difference between these two measurements. ‘Good trials’ were determined to be movements that were completed between 0.25 and 2 seconds. Moreover, any explicit selection duration that was +3SD from the median of a 10-trial moving window was also discarded. Participants that had <40% good trials during the training block of either session were not included in the final analysis (YA: N=1, OA: N=2). Savings was calculated by measuring the error of trials 2-6 of the perturbation portion of the task for both sessions. The error was averaged for each session, and the difference between the two values was calculated. A more negative value indicates less error during the second session, and therefore greater savings.

Imaging:

A subset of participants (OA: N=7, YA: N=7) underwent an MRI scan (3T, Siemens Skyra (N=12) or Siemens Prisma (N=2)) at most 4 weeks after the last VMR session. MRI scanning sessions included 1 rsfMRI (TR:825ms, flip angle: 50°, duration: 5.5 minutes, multi-band factor: 8, spatial resolution: 2.5mm isotropic), a T1w MPRAGE (TR: 2300ms, flip angle: 9°, spatial resolution: 1mm isotropic), and a T2w FLAIR scan (TR: 5000ms, flip angle: 9°, spatial resolution: 1mm isotropic). CONN was used for preprocessing of the rsfMRI and included functional realignment, slice-timing correction, and alignment to the T1 image. The first 5 timepoints were discarded to account for relaxation effects. 6 parameters each were used for linear regression of movement, CSF, and white matter. The denoised signal was then Fourier transformed and filtered to [0.008-0.01 Hz]. fALFF is a voxel-wise metric calculated by summing the amplitude across the frequency-filtered spectrum, then taking the square root of this metric. This value was then divided by the sum of amplitudes across the full, non-filtered spectrum and normalized to the whole brain to account for inter-individual differences. The SUIT toolbox was then used to mask each participant’s cerebellum and generate subject-specific alignment to the SUIT atlas, a cerebellar-specific atlas.^5 Average cerebellar fALFF within functional networks defined from the Yeo 17-network atlas in SUIT space was then calculated. Cerebellar temporal SNR (tSNR) was calculated and visually inspected for post-denoised fMRI maps for quality control. After quality control, YA: N=5 and OA: N=6 fALFF maps remained for correlation analysis. Cerebellar fALFF was averaged within a network and correlated with implicit and explicit savings in the VMR task, measured by the change in error across sessions for each component.

Results

YA demonstrated faster relearning during the counterbalanced perturbation block of S2, with a large relative contribution of explicit learning, indicating savings after one week. In contrast, OA relearning during S2 was similar to initial S1 and dependent on implicit processes, indicating a lack of savings. Notably, measures of same-day savings from both groups had similar contributions of explicit and implicit processes. 

Preliminary imaging findings revealed that greater fALFF in visual network (VN) cerebellar regions was associated with worse implicit savings (higher savings score) (p=0.045, R2=0.374). Greater cerebellar fALFF in the frontoparietal network (FPN) showed a negative association with explicit savings, indicating better savings (p=0.031, R2=0.417). 

Conclusion

These results provide initial evidence that spontaneous cerebellar oscillations are indicative of long-term retention of distinct motor learning processes. Because lower savings values reflect greater savings (better retention), the direction of each correlation should be interpreted carefully.

Participants with lower explicit savings scores (indicating greater explicit retention) demonstrated greater cerebellar fALFF within FPN regions. This pattern may reflect compensatory recruitment of cerebellar regions to support long-term explicit strategy use. In contrast, greater implicit savings (poorer implicit retention) was positively correlated with cerebellar VN fALFF, demonstrating that greater implicit savings was associated with lower cerebellar fALFF. This suggests that memory of implicit recalibration is less reliant on intrinsic cerebellar activity.

Together, these findings suggest that cerebellar function is important in retaining motor performance with age, and that cerebellar activity is differentially associated with implicit and explicit motor memory. Future work will involve recruitment of more participants to confirm the relationship between fALFF and long-term retention of motor recalibration.
