Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is the leading cause of disability worldwide.1 First-line treatment for MDD includes pharmacological treatment, to which approximately 50% of patients responds. The type of antidepressant is chosen based on a stepwise trial-and-error procedure because no reliable prediction can be made. For prediction of individual treatment response, biomarkers could be informative in distinguishing responders from non-responders, however, reproducible and externally validated biomarkers are currently lacking.2 The use of biomarkers may enable clinicians to make personalized, evidence-based treatment decisions for individual patients, thereby reducing reliance on a stepwise trial-and-error approach and reducing time to remission.

Electroencephalogram (EEG) is a cost-effective tool that is accessible for clinical use and is increasingly being used to develop personalized approaches.3 No sensitive and reliable EEG biomarker has been found for predicting pharmacological treatment response in depression yet, highlighting the need for more advanced predictive methodologies. Machine learning tools show promise for predicting outcomes based on multivariate inputs in psychiatry.4 A meta-analysis suggests that EEG can predict treatment response in MDD with a high balanced accuracy of 84%.5 However, most studies relied on small sample sizes and lacked external validation. Large-sample evaluations are crucial to establish biomarker generalizability.

In this study, we used data from the international Study to Predict Optimized Treatment in Depression (iSPOT-D). iSPOT-D was an international multicenter randomized biomarker trial in which MDD patients received first-line antidepressant treatment with escitalopram, sertraline, or venlafaxine. We investigated the added predictive value of EEG compared to clinical characteristics in predicting treatment response to escitalopram, sertraline or venlafaxine using random forest classification.

Methods

The sample consisted of 675 patients with unipolar MDD who were randomized to receive escitalopram (n=224), sertraline (n=231) or venlafaxine (n=220). Clinical characteristics and EEG were collected before the start of treatment. The Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HRSD) was repeated eight weeks later. Response was defined as ≥50% symptom reduction measured with the HRSD scale.

Our approach consisted of three models: the clinical model, the EEG model and the combined model. Clinical features as input for the clinical model were demographics, the course of depression, comorbidities and questionnaires evaluating extensive symptomatology, quality of life, personality and emotion recognition. In the iSPOT-D study, two-minute resting state EEG with eyes open was recorded with 26 channels (10-20 system) with a sampling rate of 500Hz. Preprocessing and calculation of the delta (1-4 Hz), theta (4-8 Hz), alpha (8-13 Hz) and beta (13-30 Hz) band, which were input for the EEG model, was performed according to the pre-existing iSPOT-D protocol. For the combined model, clinical and EEG features were used.

Machine learning methods for the three models were as follows. We created a pooled antidepressant model, combining all treatments, and treatment-specific models. We trained random forest classifiers to discriminate between responders and non-responders. Model validation was performed using nested 5-fold cross-validation (stratified by response). The pipeline included imputation, scaling, and random undersampling. In each inner loop, we performed grid search to find optimal model hyperparameters (number of estimators, maximum depth, minimum samples per split, minimum samples per leaf) and feature selection (K-best – with K equal to 10, 20, or 50 – or Boruta) while maximizing the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Model performance was primarily evaluated by averaging the AUC values across the outer cross-validation folds. We derived 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the AUC analytically. Secondary performance metrics included F1 score, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), sensitivity, and specificity. Statistical significance differences between performance metrics of clinical and EEG or combined models were tested using Welch’s t-test.

Results

Patients had a comparable baseline depression severity, with mean HRSD scores of 21.8 ± 4.0 for escitalopram, 21.9 ± 4.2 for sertraline, and 21.7 ± 4.1 for venlafaxine, indicating successful randomization and a sample characterized by severe depression. Treatment response rates were 60.5%, 66.4% and 59.7% for the three treatments, respectively. 

Model performance, summarized by AUC values in Figure 1, was generally poor. Across all treatments, AUC values were comparable (i.e., around chance level). The performances of the clinical model for escitalopram and sertraline were similar to the pooled antidepressant sample, while a non-significant reduction in AUC with 0.05 was found for venlafaxine. The EEG and combined model’s performances were not significantly different from the clinical model. The combined model yielded both the lowest and highest AUC values: 0.47 for venlafaxine and 0.60 for escitalopram. 

Secondary performance metrics showed negligible differences across models. F1 scores ranged from 0.52 for venlafaxine to 0.63 for pooled, escitalopram and sertraline samples, reflecting limited predictive performance. Sensitivity and specificity were slightly above 0.5, indicating limited discriminative ability. PPV values were generally higher than NPV values, averaging roughly 0.6 and 0.4. This suggests that the models were better at correctly identifying positive than negative outcomes, although overall predictive performance remained modest.

Conclusion

We evaluated whether EEG features provide added predictive value beyond clinical features for pharmacological treatment (i.e. escitalopram, sertraline and venlafaxine) response in MDD using random forest classification. We hypothesized that EEG would enhance prediction, as it captures heterogeneous neural processes. Surprisingly, mean AUC values marginally above chance level were achieved for all treatment models except venlafaxine, including the clinical prediction models. Overall, EEG band power values performed as poorly as clinical characteristics in predicting treatment response. 

A key limitation is that the EEG model relied solely on band power values, which capture only a fraction of the underlying neural dynamics. Current work applies deep learning to raw EEG epochs, which contain richer information that could include cross channel and cross frequency covariation between oscillation power values with Riemannian projection. We hypothesize that convolutional neural network models trained on raw EEG signals, rather than pre-specified band power values, will yield improved performance compared with the feature-based approach used in this research.
