Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is widely used to causally probe and modulate human brain function and for the treatment of diverse neurological and psychiatric disorders, yet stimulation outcomes vary markedly within and across individuals and cortical targets. Parts of this variability may be driven by unaccounted variance in cortical stimulation strength, i.e., the effective TMS dose delivered at the target. Current dosing strategies typically only calibrate the stimulator output strength at the motor cortex or rely on arbitrary device output strengths [1]. Critically, both approaches ignore interindividual differences in head and brain morphology, including different cortical depths of the motor hotspot and the actual stimulation target, thereby introducing substantial, uncontrolled variance in the realized stimulation strength at the cortex [2]. Electric-field-based-dosing using individual MRI and head models offers a principled, biophysically grounded alternative  ([3], [4]), but its routine use is constrained by the need for high-quality MRIs, extensive computational resources, and demanding processing pipelines. In between these approaches, the Stokes-adjustment aims to compensate for different cortical depths with a linear adjustment, thus requiring individual MRI data but ignoring non-linear E-field changes over distance [5].
The present work proposes a population-level TMS dosing atlas that aggregates large-scale E-field simulations (> 600 targets) from an anatomically diverse, healthy lifespan sample (> 600 individuals). By systematically quantifying how demographic and structural features shape cortical stimulation strength across hundreds of TMS-accessible targets, the atlas is designed to reveal previously unknown dosing landscapes: for which targets is simple, non-individualized dosing sufficient, for which targets is electric-field individualization critical, and how do these requirements differ across subpopulations such as age groups?

Methods

Preprocessing. Structural MRI data were drawn from the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN) cohort, comprising ~650 healthy individuals (age: 18-82 years) and released in a BIDS-compliant format. The dataset includes high-resolution T1-weighted and, for most participants, T2-weighted anatomical images, alongside demographic and basic physiological measures (age, sex, height, weight, head-size–related variables). Imaging data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep (version 22.0.2), including intensity non-uniformity correction, ANTs-based skull stripping, tissue segmentation with FSL FAST, spatial normalization to standard spaces, and FreeSurfer surface reconstruction to obtain high-quality cortical surfaces for subsequent modeling.

Head mesh construction, target definition and E-field calculation. Subject-specific head meshes were generated with the CHARM pipeline in SimNIBS (version 4.5), yielding multi-tissue models from the structural data and FreeSurfer-derived surfaces. Cortical stimulation targets were defined using the 1000-parcel Schaefer atlas, functionally labeled according to the seven-network Yeo parcellation, represented in fsaverage space and mapped to individual space via the SimNIBS SurfaceMorph procedure. For each parcel selected as TMS-accessible (i.e., located on the cortical convexity and in proximity to the scalp), a ROI was defined as the centroid of its pial-surface vertices in native space, and scalp-to-cortex distance (SCD) was computed. TMS-induced E-fields were simulated with the SimNIBS OnlineFEM framework using MagVenture Cool-B65 and Cool-B35 figure-of-eight coil models. For each ROI, coil position and orientation were optimized by sampling candidate placements within a predefined search space, selecting the one maximizing the E-field magnitude in gray matter within a small radius around the target. For the optimal one, normal, tangential, and magnitude at the ROI were extracted as dosing-relevant outcomes together with whole–GM field maps, and the full workflow was executed on high-performance computing infrastructure with SLURM-based workload management, requiring in total more than two million CPU core hours.

Analyses. We set up four models to put different dosing approaches to the test: 

  1. MT-based: Tests how well the same stimulator output intensities reproduces the cortical stimulation from motor cortex stimulation at the other brain regions. 

  2. MRI-free: Tests how well a linear model with predictors such as head circumference, age, and sex can predict the cortical E-field strength.

  3. MRI-free + MT: Assuming a similar cortical stimulation threshold across (healthy) subjects, the motor threshold can be used to estimate the E-field strength at the motor cortex. Model 3 includes this predictor in addition to the MRI-free parameters from Model 2. 

  4. MRI-informed: In addition to the MRI-free parameters, this model includes skin-cortex distance (SCD), SCD2 and total intracranial volume as imaging-derived covariates.

Model performance was evaluated in a cross-validation framework with held-out subjects (80/20 train/test split), and additional subgroup analyses across age ranges were performed to identify targets and demographic strata for which MRI-informed dosing is particularly critical. These analyses delineate targets where population-level dosing without individualized E-field modeling is sufficient versus targets that require subject-specific simulations. Additionally, we analysed how well E-field differences between motor cortex, and non-motor targets can be predicted to inform validity limits of motor-threshold–based dosing. 

GUI. To enable practical use, all atlas-level dosing estimates and model results are exposed through a web-based graphical user interface that allows users to select targets of interest, explore population distributions of E-field strength, and inspect how stimulation strength varies across dosing strategies.

Results

MRI-free linear models explained moderate variance in target-wise stimulation strength but showed pronounced spatial heterogeneity across the cortex (Fig. 2A, top). Parcels on gyral crowns and superficial dorsal regions were predicted relatively well, whereas deeply buried or ventrolateral regions exhibited low explained variance, indicating that demographic and simple head-size measures are insufficient in these areas. In contrast, MRI-informed models that additionally incorporated skin–cortex distance and intracranial-volume measures substantially increased explained variance across almost the entire accessible cortex, with many parcels approaching R² values close to 1, suggesting that a large fraction of interindividual dose variability can be predicted easily via linear models  (Fig. 2A, bottom).

When comparing prediction schemes against the simulated electric fields, MT-based dosing, i.e. applying the same stimulator output strength across all targets, showed almost no relationship between predicted and true field strength at non-motor targets, resulting in a nearly rectangular scatter pattern (Fig. 2B - MT-based). MRI-free regression models produced a clear positive but relatively broad relationship across parcels, indicating systematic but somewhat noisy dose estimation from non-imaging predictors alone (Fig. 2B - MRI-free model). Adding MT-based information to the MRI-free model slightly tightened this relationship but did not eliminate large residual errors (Fig. 2B - MRI-free + MT model). The MRI-informed model yielded the closest alignment with the identity line and the narrowest scatter, demonstrating that combining simple demographic variables with MRI-derived anatomical metrics enables accurate, target-specific prediction of cortical stimulation strength and helps identify parcels and subpopulations where individual E-field simulations are indispensable (Fig. 2B - MRI-informed model).

Conclusion

MT-based dosing alone performs poorly at predicting the actual electric field at non-motor targets and thus provides only a crude and often misleading proxy for cortical dose. In the simulations, motor-threshold–based prescriptions showed virtually no systematic relationship between the nominally “scaled” output and the realized field strengths across parcels, effectively flattening target-specific differences into a narrow band of intensities. This implies that conventional MT-based approaches can substantially over- or underdose many cortical regions, introducing uncontrolled variance into both experimental and clinical TMS protocols and limiting interpretability of dose–response relationships.
Against this backdrop, the population-level dosing atlas demonstrates that a large fraction of this variability can be rendered predictable once anatomy is explicitly taken into account. MRI-free models using only demographic and gross head-size measures already capture a meaningful proportion of variance but do so very unevenly across the cortex, performing relatively well for superficial dorsal targets and poorly for deeper or ventrolateral regions. MRI-informed models that incorporate skin–cortex distance and volumetric measures markedly improve prediction performance across most TMS-accessible parcels, with many regions approaching near-deterministic relationships between predicted and simulated fields. This pattern indicates that much of the “mystery” of TMS variability is rooted in macroscopic anatomical differences that can be quantified and modeled rather than treated as noise.

These results argue for a tiered, target- and population-specific view of TMS dosing. For some parcels and demographic ranges, atlas-based MRI-free predictions may be sufficiently accurate to guide approximate dosing when individual imaging is not available, offering a principled alternative to purely MT-based heuristics. For other regions—particularly those with large depth, complex folding, or strong age-related morphological change—precise dosing clearly requires MRI-informed modeling and, in some cases, full individual E-field simulations. The atlas helps delineate these regimes explicitly by mapping where MRI-free predictions are reliable, where MRI-informed models are essential, and where MT-based approaches are particularly misleading.

From a practical standpoint, the dosing atlas and the accompanying web interface provide a concrete tool for improving protocol design and reporting. Users can inspect specific targets or networks, explore how expected field strength varies with demographic and head-size measures, and quantify how far MT-based prescriptions are likely to deviate from target-specific biophysical dose in the populations they study. This enables more transparent communication of achieved stimulation strength, facilitates comparison across studies and centers, and supports better-informed safety margins. Additionally, the full dataset will be made publicly available. 

Conceptually, the work encourages a shift from one-size-fits-all dosing rules toward population-informed, anatomically grounded dose prediction. Extending this framework to additional coil types, pulse parameters, and clinical populations will further refine our understanding of when and to what extent individualization is necessary. Ultimately, systematically characterizing where MT-based dosing breaks down and where atlas- or MRI-based adjustments are required is a key step toward more reproducible, interpretable, and equitable TMS interventions across laboratories and clinical settings.
