Introduction

Physiologically informed dynamic causal modelling (P-DCM) may provide a richer account of neurovascular coupling and hemodynamics than the standard DCM for fMRI (S-DCM), yet its implementation and systematic validation within widely used software such as SPM remain limited. This gap is particularly relevant because both neural and vascular physiology are known to vary across healthy ageing and to be altered in neurodegenerative conditions such as mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease, where changes in neuronal gain, vascular compliance, transit times, and metabolic–vascular coupling are all implicated. Distinguishing whether observed fMRI differences arise from neural changes, vascular changes, or a combination of both is therefore an important methodological and translational challenge.

To address this, we developed an implementation of the P-DCM model and examined model stability, empirical model evidence, and the capacity to dissociate neural and vascular sources of individual variability. We first validated the implementation against the neuronal, hemodynamic, and BOLD-signal dynamics reported by Havlicek et al. (2015) for both single-node and five-node networks. We then applied the models to empirical task fMRI data to compare P-DCM under the priors proposed by Havlicek et al. (2015), P-DCM under revised priors proposed here, and S-DCM. Finally, motivated by scenarios in which individuals may differ in specific neural and hemodynamic properties associated with ageing or disease, we conducted three simulation experiments—focusing here on single-node models—to evaluate the identifiability of neural (σ: excitatory self-decay) and hemodynamic (tMTT: mean transit time; τ: viscoelasticity; α: Grubb’s exponent) parameters. Together, these analyses assess the methodological and computational value of P-DCM as a mechanistic tool for characterizing interindividual differences and for distinguishing neural from vascular contributions to fMRI signals.

Methods

Implementation and prior revision
 Our P-DCM implementation exactly reproduced the physiological model components of Havlicek et al. (2015). Validation revealed that their Gaussian priors on neural excitability parameters A (prior variance of 1/4) allowed unstable neuronal dynamics, yielding positive real eigenvalues in multiple subjects. We therefore narrowed the prior variance on the A-matrix to 1/64 while retaining its Gaussian form. Conversely, the viscoelastic time constant τ exhibited a physiologically narrow range of values; these were replaced with bounded uniform priors consistent with reported variability.

Empirical evaluation
We analyzed the Cam-CAN sensorimotor task (TR = 1.97 s) in 32 participants (16 young (20-30 years of age), 16 elderly (70-80 years of age)). Volumes of interest were extracted using task-thresholded anatomical definitions from the Juelich atlas for left and right M1, and bilaterally pooled visual cortex (VC), auditory cortex (AC), and supplementary motor area (SMA). We fitted S-DCM, P-DCM with Havlicek et al.’s priors (P-DCMH), and P-DCM with revised priors (P-DCMR) to single-node (VC) and five-node networks. We evaluated evidence for each model using fixed-effects Bayesian model comparison (BMC), which aggregated free energies across subjects.

Experiments 1 and 2: recovery of group assignment (single-node)
From the empirical fits, we computed a Bayesian parameter average (BPA) and constructed exemplar groups by systematically varying four key parameters: σ (sigma; self-decay of excitatory neural population), tMTT (transit; mean transit time), τ (tau; viscoelasticity), and α (alpha; Grubb’s exponent). Each parameter was assigned either a high or low value, defined as BPA ± k·SD for effect sizes k = 0.5, 1.0, or 1.5. Thus, the full factorial combination across four parameters yields 24 = 16 distinct exemplar groups for each effect size.

For each of these 16 groups, we simulated 32 virtual subjects by perturbing parameters around the exemplar and adding measurement noise, producing 512 subjects per effect size. All simulated datasets were then fitted using the revised-prior P-DCM implementation.

Experiment 1 (exemplar-based classification): each model estimated from simulated data underwent Bayesian model reduction under all 16 exemplar priors, producing a model-evidence matrix subsequently normalized via softmax to obtain posterior probabilities over groups. Predicted group assignments were obtained using a winner-take-all rule for each subject.

Experiment 2 (group-average-based classification): using the true group labels, we computed group-level BPAs (excluding the evaluated individual) and repeated the same Bayesian model-reduction and classification procedure. Experiments 1 and 2 therefore assess how well P-DCM can recover latent group identity arising from neural or vascular parameter differences.

Experiment 3 (parameter recovery)
 For each parameter, we generated a 7-point grid using k·√Varpost (k = 1,2,3; Varpost is posterior variance) and simulated 5 noisy replicates per grid point. After estimation (yielding θS), we re-simulated new replicates and re-estimated models (yielding θR). Recovery was quantified using Pearson correlations between θS and θR, together with R2 and RMSE values obtained from robust linear fits.

Results

Our implementation was able to qualitatively reproduce the single-node and five-node impulse-response dynamics reported by Havlicek et al. Refinement of the A-matrix and τ priors resolved the instability in the neuronal dynamics and produced hemodynamic transients consistent with empirical physiological profiles.

Empirically, fixed-effects model comparison strongly favoured P-DCM: for the single-node network, variational free energy for P-DCM with Havlicek et al.’s priors = 1057.6 (P-DCMH), and P-DCM with revised priors = 1197.3 (P-DCMR) relative to S-DCM; for the five-node network, P-DCMH = 5951.6, and P-DCMR = 3360.7 relative to S-DCM. Thus, P-DCM consistently outperformed S-DCM, although the preferred prior structure differed by network, likely due to the more flexible A-matrix in P-DCMH.

For Experiments 1 and 2, we assessed recovery of latent group identity arising from factorial manipulations of σ, tMTT, τ, and α. At large effect size (k = 1.5), posterior-probability structure and confusion matrices showed clear separation of the 16 groups. In Figure 1, the top-left panel displays the posterior-probability matrix from Experiment 1, and the top-right panel shows the corresponding confusion matrix. The second row mirrors this structure for Experiment 2 in the bottom-left and bottom-right panels, respectively. Group recovery was evident at smaller effect sizes (k = 0.5 and k = 1), albeit with weaker separability than at k = 1.5; the figure therefore highlights the clearest case. Together, these panels illustrate that P-DCM can robustly differentiate groups defined by neural or hemodynamic manipulations when those differences are sufficiently pronounced.

Per-parameter recovery was high in both exemplar-based (Experiment 1) and group-average (Experiment 2) settings. For Experiment 1, high/low classification accuracies (confusion-matrix diagonals) were: σ = 0.726/0.828, tMTT = 91%/99%, τ = 79%/89%, and α = 89%/75%. For Experiment 2, high/low accuracies were σ = 81%/75%, tMTT = 100%/93%, τ = 84%/89%, and α = 77%/91%. Across both experiments, manipulations of both neural and hemodynamic parameters were cleanly dissociated at this effect size, supporting a stable mapping from generative parameters to inferred group identity.

Experiment 3 quantified parameter-level recovery. Correlations between recovered parameters (θ_R) and ground-truth values (θ_S) were high: σ (r = 0.87, R2 = 76%, RMSE = 0.057), tMTT (r = 0.94, R2 = 87%, RMSE = 0.055), τ (r = 0.97, R2 = 95%, RMSE = 0.155), and α (r = 0.80, R2 = 65%, RMSE = 0.060). The results indicate that, in the model’s well-constrained operating regime, the underlying generative parameters were reproducibly recovered with high fidelity.

Conclusion

We provide a validated implementation of P-DCM in the SPM software and show that it reproduces expected physiological dynamics while delivering substantially higher model evidence than S-DCM for both single-node and five-node networks. Our refined priors enabled stable model inversion across subjects while allowing a wider range of hemodynamic transients due to viscoelasticity. Empirically, P-DCM consistently offered a better explanation of task fMRI data, with both single-node and five-node P-DCM models showing greater model evidence relative S-DCM, reflecting that P-DCM’s richer account of underlying neuronal and hemodynamic dynamics is better supported by the data.

Our simulation results demonstrated that P-DCM can successfully recover latent group identity arising from neural and hemodynamic manipulations when effect sizes are sufficiently large, whereas sensitivity is reduced for smaller between-group differences. Although not shown here, we note that group recoverability is better for the five-node than the one-node network. Experiment 3 further showed that σ, tMTT, τ, and α are recoverable with high fidelity, yielding strong correlations, high R2 values, and low RMSE, indicating robust parameter recovery when the model operates within a well-constrained regime. Taken together, these findings position P-DCM as a practical and sensitive approach for quantifying neuro-vascular contributions to fMRI signals, characterizing interindividual variability, and supporting mechanistic inference in studies of healthy ageing and neurodegenerative conditions.
