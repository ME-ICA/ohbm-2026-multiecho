Introduction

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a condition characterized by a problematic pattern of alcohol use that leads to significant distress or problems in functioning. An increasing number of studies are using resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) functional connectivity (FC) to build predictive models of human behavioral and cognitive phenotypes, and a branch of this literature links brain connectivity to AUD. Machine learning models allow for individualized prediction, but this comes with a trade-off with interpretability: multivariate models including thousands of features, sometimes from multiple imaging modalities, can partially explain the variance of behavioral phenotypes such as psychometric scales scores, but the high number of features and small sample size can lead to problems of overfitting, which implies a lack of generalization and interpretability. Transfer learning approaches such as multilayer meta-matching (MMM) 1 offer a promising solution. MMM has been shown to improve prediction accuracy relative to locally trained models, and its learned weights can be interpreted post hoc using the Haufe transform 2, which has demonstrated strong generalization performance for FC-based behavioral prediction in Chopra et al.3.  The objective of this study is to apply MMM to predict alcohol consumption from resting-state fMRI data from the TRR265 ReCoDe cohort 4 a large, multi-center sample of over 700 individuals with varying degrees of AUD severity, none of whom were actively seeking treatment. The Haufe transformed model weights will be used to generate reliable and outcome-specific relevance maps that identify which connections and regions in rs-fMRI are more closely linked to AUD, improving feature selection and interpretability of predictive models in this field to narrow the gap towards potential clinical applications of rs-fMRI-based biomarkers.

Methods

The ReCoDe cohort was collected at three sites in Germany to study underlying mechanisms and trajectories, as well as possible interventions for substance use disorders.  After excluding subjects for missing data or insufficient imaging quality, N = 739 subjects (305 females; age: 16-65) were included for this study. Details on cohort recruitment and procedures are described in Spanagel et al. (2024). A subset of subjects from UK Biobank with matched gender and drinking patterns was also analyzed for the preregistration of hypotheses (see 10.17605/OSF.IO/SJZY9) and for testing cross-cohort generalizability of the findings.

Functional MRI data were acquired on 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma and Trio scanners using multi-band T2*-weighted EPI sequences (500 volumes; TR = 869 ms; voxel size = 2.4 mm isotropic). Field maps were collected for distortion correction, and T1-weighted MPRAGE scans were used for anatomical normalization. Preprocessing included slice-timing correction, realignment, fieldmap correction, coregistration of T1 to EPI, and normalization to MNI152 space. After high-pass filtering (σ = 50 TRs), the temporal mean was restored. Denoising was carried out using ICA-AROMA followed by global signal regression, and images were resliced to 2 mm resolution.

We derived FC matrices from the resting-state sequences using an atlas comprising 400 cortical and 19 subcortical regions as used by Chen et al.1, which were then Fisher z-transformed and L2-normalized within-subject.

The main outcome variable to quantify alcohol consumption for this study is the AUDIT-C score, a psychometric score equal to the sum of the first 3 items of the AUDIT score 5. 

We compared the performance of a Kernel Ridge Regression model trained directly on the local subjects (KRR) with that of one trained on the outcome of the MMM embedding (MMM-KRR), i.e., the phenotypes of interest predicted by the pre-trained meta-matching model. The prediction accuracy was measured as the Pearson correlation between predictions and ground truths. We trained each model using 100 repetitions of an 80/20 random train–test split, in order to be able to generate distributions of performances, and use their mean r as an estimate of the model performance. For each split, we harmonized the inputs across sites using ComBat, and the outcomes were residualized for sex, age, and head movement of the participants, measured as mean framewise displacement. Within each training set, we performed a five-fold cross-validation using a random search for hyperparameter tuning. 

To derive relevance maps, we applied the Haufe transform by computing the covariance between each input feature and the model’s predictions across training subjects. We then aggregated these weights into connection-, region-, and network-level maps, separating positive and negative contributions.

We assessed the significance of the models’ performances and of the data-driven relevance maps by generating a null-distribution of 1000 samples through permutation testing, and compared the model performances with one-tailed paired t-tests on the correlation distributions, with correction for repeated splits.

We evaluated split-half reliability for each relevance map by dividing the ReCoDe cohort into two independent halves, refitting the best-performing model, and correlating the resulting relevance maps (Pearson’s r), averaged over 100 splits. We evaluated cross-cohort reliability by comparing in the same way the ReCoDe AUDIT-C maps with those generated analogously in the UK BioBank sample. 

Furthermore, we tested whether the model produces distinct connectivity patterns for different targets, since high similarity across unrelated outcomes would undermine interpretability. We therefore generated relevance maps for age - an outcome independent of AUDIT-C after deconfounding - and compared them with the AUDIT-C maps to confirm that the identified patterns are specific to alcohol consumption.

Results

Permutation testing showed that neither model achieved a statistically significant prediction of AUDIT-C. Nevertheless, the MMM-KRR model still significantly outperformed KRR in corrected paired one-tailed t-tests (average Pearson’s r = 0.064). 

The data-driven relevance maps showed moderate split-half reliability across FC edges, regions, and networks (r≈0.45), with higher values at the region and network levels. Outcome specificity was supported by consistently lower similarity between the AUDIT-C relevance maps and those derived for age across all maps. Negative relevance maps show the highest cross-cohort similarity (r≈0.5), whilst the positive associations replicated less (r≈0.2), suggesting more consistent patterns of reduced connectivity across datasets.

Figure 2a shows the brain regions most strongly associated with AUDIT-C predictions based on their functional connectivity profiles. Notably, stronger connectivity associated with higher predicted alcohol use was observed in the bilateral thalamus, basal ganglia, and precuneus, as well as in the left parietal gyrus and the middle and posterior cingulate cortex. In contrast, reduced connectivity was found in the bilateral middle temporal gyrus, left orbitofrontal gyrus, right rectus, and left inferior frontal junction. Figure 2b and 2c show the changes in connectivity on the functional network level that were significantly associated with AUD. The diagrams show that several cortical networks - including the dorsal attention network B (frontal eye fields, intraparietal sulcus), the somatomotor network (pre- and postcentral gyri), and temporal regions (middle temporal gyrus and temporal lobe) - exhibit a shift in their connectivity patterns. Specifically, these networks show reduced coupling with cortical default mode regions, namely the left default mode network (DMN) B (inferior frontal gyrus) and the right DMN C (parahippocampal gyrus), while at the same time displaying increased connectivity with non-cortical areas such as the basal ganglia and thalamus, as well as with the executive control network (ECN) C (precuneus).

Conclusion

In this study, we applied the meta-matching framework to enhance the prediction of drinking patterns in a large cohort of non-treatment-seeking individuals with mild AUD, and interpreted the best-performing models by extracting data-driven relevance maps, which provided insights into how changes in FC are associated with drinking patterns at different levels of aggregation. Despite the models' modest predictive performance, these maps demonstrated robust reliability—both in split-half and cross-cohort comparisons—and showed specificity to the outcome of interest, which shows that the predictions may not be statistically significant but are also unlikely to be false positives.

Several factors may underlie the generally modest effect sizes observed for drinking-related outcomes. First, prediction performance for AUDIT-C was moderate, which aligns with previous findings: drinking behaviors have proven difficult to predict in large datasets, with alcohol intake prediction rarely exceeding r=0.1. Second, the ReCoDe cohort comprises non-treatment-seeking individuals with mild AUD. While the use of a mild, non-clinical cohort like ReCoDe may enhance generalizability to the broader population, it also introduces limited variability in drinking severity - likely constraining model performance. Despite the small effect size and non-significant overall performance, the meta-matching framework consistently improved prediction performance for AUDIT-C, suggesting its value under the right conditions. 

Although the models did not yield empirically significant predictive performance, the data-driven relevance maps consistently captured the linear contribution of FC to the predictions. These maps showed good replicability, both across split halves of the dataset and between independent cohorts. To rule out the possibility that this reliability simply reflects a constant, non-specific relevance pattern, we also examined outcome specificity. 

While the observed reliabilities were moderate, several connections involving key regions and functional networks were consistently associated with drinking behaviors. Specifically, we observed an association between increased alcohol consumption and increased centrality of regions belonging to the dopaminergic pathway, such as the nucleus accumbens, which is responsible for regulating feelings of reward. On the other hand, DMN regions responsible for inhibition and memory processing, such as the left inferior frontal and the right parahippocampal gyri, seem to have the opposite tendency, with decreased connectivity associated with increased consumption.

Despite providing some promising insights into AUD, this study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design prevents us from drawing conclusions about the causal direction of the observed associations. For consistency, we used the same parcellation in the local KRR models as was used to train the meta-matching models. However, reducing the feature space may help mitigate the curse of dimensionality, for example, by employing a coarser atlas, dimensionality reduction, or feature selection. In future work, we plan to test whether applying our relevance maps as a feature selection strategy in independent cohorts can improve prediction performance. At the same time, the low predictive accuracy of the models and overall modest reliability of the relevance maps highlight that they are not yet suitable for clinical applications. While we remain confident that resting-state fMRI can advance our understanding of how brain connectivity is altered in AUD, predicting disorder severity - or specific symptom dimensions within this heterogeneous condition - will likely require multimodal approaches that integrate the most informative features from different modalities. We view the present work as an incremental step toward such integrative predictive models.
