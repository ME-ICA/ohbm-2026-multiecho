Introduction

BOLD-fMRI is widely employed to probe brain function in both humans and animal models. Conventionally, the BOLD contrast is interpreted as reflecting hemodynamic changes secondary to neural firing through neurovascular coupling (NVC), which is a complex interplay involving neurons, blood vessels, and glial cells. However, within this conceptual framework, the specific contributions of astrocytes, i.e., star-shaped glial cells that are integral to tripartite synapses, have been significantly underestimated. 

A pivotal study by Wang et al.1 shed light on this issue by combining BOLD-fMRI with calcium fiber photometry in anesthetized rats. Their findings revealed that sensory evoked astrocytic calcium signals correlated with positive BOLD responses confined in the sensory pathway, whereas state-specific astrocytic calcium signals were associated with negative BOLD responses across multiple cortical regions1. These observations suggested that both evoked and state-dependent astrocytic calcium signaling contributed to BOLD signal dynamics via distinct NVC mechanisms1. Nevertheless, causal evidence directly linking astrocytic calcium activity to BOLD signals remains elusive.

To address this gap, we combined awake mouse fMRI with 1) simultaneous neuronal and astrocytic calcium recordings in the barrel field of the mouse somatosensory cortex (S1BF), 2) sensory stimulation, 3) behavioral state monitoring via video recording, and 4) targeted astrocytic manipulation. Our results provided causal evidence that evoked astrocytic calcium transients were coupled with positive BOLD signals, while state-specific astrocytic calcium fluctuations were associated with negative BOLD signals, respectively. In conclusion, we demonstrated that astrocytes exert a potent, state-dependent, and bidirectional influence on neurovascular coupling, fundamentally shaping the fMRI signals widely used to infer brain activity.

Methods

All mouse experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Neuroscience, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China. Mice were group-housed (5–6 per cage) under a 12-h light/dark cycle (light on from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m.) with food and water ad libitum. Experiments were conducted at 8-10 weeks of ages, weighted 18-30 g. Seven mice were used in each awake mouse fMRI experiment. Virus for labeling S1BF neuronal and astrocytic signals or for activating/inhibiting S1BF astrocytes are depicted in the Main Figures (Fig.1A-B and 2A, 2E), respectively. Optical fiber and head holder implantation were performed one week after virus injection (Fig. 1A). Following a 1-week recovery from the surgery, the mice underwent a 7-day habituation for awake fMRI scanning. During this period, mice were head-fixed on the animal bed while being exposed to recorded acoustic MRI scanning noise, following protocols detailed in our previous studies2,3. No reward was given during or after the habituation training.

For simultaneous fiber photometry recording during fMRI, two 7-m optical fibers were connected to the bilateral S1BF with sleeves (Fig. 1C). Each photometry setup employed 470 nm and 530 nm LEDs (Doric Lens) as excitation sources, with excitation power at the fiber tip maintained at approximately 20 μW. The fluorescence detection was done by fluorescence optical mini cubesand the visible femtowatt photoreceivers (Doric Lens). A software-controlled lock-in detection algorithm was implemented using the TDT RZ2 system, employing the fiber photometry 'Gizmo' of the Synapse software (https://www.tdt.com/). Fiber photometry signals were recorded at a sampling rate of 1017 Hz.

Functional MRI data were collected using a multiband EPI sequence with following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 750 ms, echo time (TE) = 15 ms, flip angle=38.8°, bandwidth = 300 kHz, field of view (FOV) = 15×10.5 mm2, matrix size = 100×70, slice thickness = 0.48 mm, 1200 volumes per scan, and 38 axial slices per volumes. A T2-weighted structural image was acquired for co-registration with following parameters: TR = 3400 ms, TE = 33 ms, FOV = 16×16 mm2, matrix size = 256×256, slice thickness = 0.5 mm, 30 axial slices, RARE factor = 8, and number of averages = 2. Each simultaneous fiber photometry and fMRI session included 5 or 8 EPI scans with whisker stimulations. In addition, a custom-made MR-compatible video camera (25 fps) was placed inside the bore to record the mouse spontaneous body movement.

After the image format conversion, the mouse brain was extracted manually utilizing ITK-SNAP (http://www.itksnap.org/). Subsequent procedures were performed utilizing custom scripts in MATLAB 2020a (MathWorks, Natick, MA) and SPM12 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). Images of each EPI scan were registered to the scan-specific structural image utilizing the rigid body transformation, and the scan-specific structure image was nonlinearly transformed to the 3D Allen Mouse Brain Common Coordinate Framework, v3 (CCFv3, http://atlas.brain-map.org/) for group analysis. A light spatial smoothing (0.4 mm isotropic Gaussian kernel) was performed but no band-pass filter was applied to the BOLD time series. Finally, denoising approach was performed based on the “6 rp + 6 Δrp + 10 PCs” nuisance signal regression, which has been effectively applied to various fMRI datasets in rodents, non-human primates and humans in our previous studies2,3 to minimize effects of scanner drift, motion and other non-neural physiological noises. The “6 rp + 6 Δrp” represented 6 head motion parameters and their 1st order first derivatives, and “10 PCs” were the first 10 principal components (PCs) derived from the BOLD signals of non-brain tissue, e.g., the muscles. The global signal was deliberately preserved in our analysis, based on established evidence that it contains valuable information about subjects’ internal state, such as its arousal4 and consciousness dynamics.

Results

The experimental timeline for the simultaneous fiber photometry and fMRI acquisition in awake, head-fixed mice was illustrated in Fig. 1A-C. This methodological framework enabled us to capture neuronal and astrocytic activities with hemodynamic signals across different behavioral states. Combined with spontaneous behavioral monitoring by MR-compatible cameras during scanning (Fig. 1D-F), we observed that whisker stimulation accompanied by body movement elicited significant larger astrocytic calcium signals compared to whisker stimulation only (Fig. 1G-H). These enhanced astrocytic calcium signals were consistently coupled with delayed negative BOLD responses (Fig. 1H-I). Furthermore, during resting state awake mouse fMRI (Fig. 1J), spontaneous calcium spikes in both astrocytes and neurons were detected in S1BF region during mouse’s body movement (Fig. 1K). The corresponding BOLD signal in S1BF exhibited a biphasic pattern, characterized by an initial rise followed by a decline to negative levels (Fig. 1L). The peak of positive BOLD signal lagged approximately 2 seconds behind the neuronal calcium signal (Fig. 1L-N), closely matching the time-to-peak time of mouse hemodynamic function (HRF). Notably, the astrocytic calcium peak occurred approximately 2 seconds after the positive BOLD maximum, yet preceded the negative BOLD trough by ~4.5 seconds (Fig. 1M–N). Moreover, the amplitude of neuronal and astrocytic signals were strongly correlated with the positive and negative BOLD responses, respectively (Fig. 1O).

Based on these findings, we hypothesized that whisker stimulation evoked astrocytic calcium signals primarily drive positive BOLD responses, while spontaneous state-specific astrocytic calcium signals contribute to negative BOLD responses (Fig. 1P).

To further explore such relationship causally, we first employed a chemogenetic gain-of-function approach, selectively activating S1BF astrocytes by expressing the engineered receptor hM3Dq under the GfaABC1D promoter (Fig. 2A), followed by systemic administration of deschloroclozapine (DCZ) (Fig. 2B). In the task-only condition, chemogenetic stimulation enhanced both evoked astrocytic calcium responses and the concomitant positive BOLD signals. However, under concurrent conditions (combining task-aligned and spontaneous events), astrocytic activation led to more pronounced increases in ipsilateral neuronal and astrocytic responses, without a corresponding significant increase in ipsilateral positive BOLD signals (Fig. 2C). During the resting state, heightened spontaneous astrocytic calcium fluctuations resulted in increased ipsilateral neuronal activity but suppressed ipsilateral BOLD dynamics, affecting both positive and delayed-negative components (Fig. 2D-E). We further validated these effects through a loss-of-function experiment by selectively suppressing calcium elevation in S1BF astrocytes using the IP3R2flox mice (Fig. 2F-G). Injection of GfaABC1D-Cre into the S1BF of the IP3R2flox mice effectively suppressed astrocyte calcium transients. Consequently, whisker stimulation evoked weaker astrocytic and BOLD responses under both task-aligned only and concurrent conditions (Fig. 2H). Moreover, astrocytic inhibition during the resting state significantly reduced the amplitude of spontaneous BOLD signal dynamics in the ipsilateral hemisphere (Fig. 2I–J). 

Collectively, our findings demonstrated that whisker stimulation-evoked astrocytic calcium activity primarily supports positive BOLD signals, whereas state-specific astrocytic calcium activity underlies negative BOLD signals. We provided causal evidence that astrocytes bidirectionally modulate BOLD responses. These findings challenge the conventional view that the contribution of neuronal and astrocytic activity on positive BOLD signals, underscoring the crucial role of arousal-modulated astrocytic signaling in shaping neurovascular coupling.

Conclusion

Our study bridges this critical gap by integrating simultaneous fiber photometry, awake fMRI, and bidirectional astrocytic manipulation, providing direct causal evidence that astrocytic calcium activity serves as a pivotal and bidirectional modulator of BOLD signals, with its effect being fundamentally dependent on the behavioral or internal state of subjects. We robustly demonstrated a dualistic and opposing role for astrocytes in hemodynamic signaling, in which sensory-evoked astrocytic calcium transients facilitate the canonical positive BOLD response, while spontaneous state-specific astrocytic calcium fluctuations act as a primary driver of the negative BOLD signal. Chemogenetic activation and knockout-based suppression of astrocytes led to corresponding enhancements and reductions, respectively, in both evoked calcium and BOLD responses, confirming that astrocytic activity is not merely an epiphenomenon but a determinant of the BOLD signals.

The sequential signal dynamics (Fig. 1L-N) uncovered the complex sequence of neuro-glio-vascular events. The initial positive BOLD response is likely driven by rapid neuronal signaling and subsequent vascular dilation. The subsequent rise in astrocytic calcium may then initiate slower, modulatory processes, potentially involving the release of vasoactive agents like prostaglandins or epoxyeicosatrienoic acids, which could subsequently modulate vascular tone and contribute to the negative BOLD phase. This sequence positions astrocytes not as passive followers of neural activity, but as active integrators that shape the temporal evolution of the hemodynamic response. A critical and nuanced finding from our chemogenetic activation experiments is the dissociation between local cellular activity and the hemodynamic output under certain conditions. The observation that heightened astrocytic and neuronal calcium responses during concurrent stimulation did not translate into stronger positive BOLD signals (Fig. 2C). Similarly, increased spontaneous astrocytic activity elevated neuronal calcium but suppressed BOLD dynamics (Fig. 2D-E). This suggests that astrocytic influence is not uniformly facilitatory for BOLD dynamics. Instead, astrocytes can exert suppressive effects on the vasculature, possibly through the release of constrictive agents, thereby contributing to observed negative BOLD signals. This bidirectional control, potentiating positive BOLD in response to discrete stimuli while simultaneously promoting negative BOLD during spontaneous states, indicated a sophisticated regulatory system for balancing metabolic supply and demand.

Methodologically, our decision to conduct these experiments in awake animals, while deliberately preserving the global signal, was crucial. Anesthesia is known to suppress neural activity and alter neurovascular coupling, while global signal regression risks removing biologically meaningful variance linked to arousal⁴. Our approach therefore captures a more physiologically relevant picture of brain function, where sensory processing is continuously interleaved with internally generated behavioral states. The global negative correlations between state-specific astrocytic calcium and BOLD signals underscore that astrocytic networks operate on a broad scale to modulate brain-wide hemodynamics.

Furthermore, our study has profound implications for the interpretation of fMRI studies. It necessitates a shift from a purely neuron-centric view to a more integrated "tripartite" perspective that explicitly includes astrocytic computation. Ignoring this astrocytic contribution risks misattributing the origins of BOLD signal changes, particularly the often-overlooked negative BOLD components. Understanding this astrocyte-mediated mechanism is not only essential for basic neuroscience but also opens new avenues for investigating neurological disorders where astrocytic function and neurovascular coupling are implicated, such as Alzheimer's disease and epilepsy.
