Introduction

New information is more likely to be remembered when it aligns with prior knowledge. While this so-called congruency effect has often been used as a marker of schema-dependent learning, the underlying neural dynamics remain unclear (Packard et al., 2017; van Kesteren et al., 2012). A plausible neurochemical contributor to this facilitation is dopamine, which influences interactions between prefrontal control and mesolimbic learning signals that support memory encoding. Importantly, dopaminergic effects on cognition can vary nonlinearly across individuals and pharmacological states (Yousuf et al., 2025; Cools & D’Esposito, 2011). In our recent fMRI study using the same pharmacological manipulation, administration of the dopamine D2 receptor agonist bromocriptine and the D2 receptor antagonist sulpiride, we observed behavioral and functional effects consistent with dopaminergic modulation of semantic memory benefits under placebo conditions (Yousuf et al., 2025).

Here, we examined whether the same pharmacological and behavioral manipulations leave detectable signatures in the EEG. Standard event-related potential (ERP) and time-frequency analyses may conflate narrowband oscillatory activity with broader background spectral changes. Therefore, we used spectral parameterization with FOOOF (fitting oscillations & one-over f) to model the power spectrum as oscillatory peaks overlaid on aperiodic components (Donoghue et al., 2020). This approach enabled us to determine if dopaminergic manipulations are most strongly reflected in aperiodic parameters during recognition-related memory effects, beyond conventional ERP and time–frequency measures.

Methods

Design and Participants: A double-blind, between-subjects pharmacological EEG study was conducted with 89 participants (Sulpiride = 30, Placebo = 30, Bromocriptine = 29). They received the dopamine D2 receptor agonist bromocriptine (1.25 mg), the dopamine D2 receptor antagonist sulpiride (400 mg), or a placebo prior to the encoding phase of the paradigm.

Task: A two-stage paradigm (see Figure 1), consisting of an encoding phase (day 1, under drug or placebo) and a recognition phase (day 2, no drug), was performed. EEG recordings were only taken during the encoding phase. Stimuli comprised 66 semantic categories (e.g., furniture), each with six typical instances (e.g., chair), for a total of 396 words. The encoding phase comprised 396 trials: fixation (2000–3000 ms), category cue (1500 ms), fixation (2000 ms), and target word (1000 ms). Targets were either congruent (category-consistent) or incongruent (category-inconsistent); participants indicated congruency by pressing a button. The recognition phase included 792 trials (396 old words from the encoding phase and 396 new words): a fixation (1500 ms) followed by a word displayed for 4000 ms. Participants responded with “sure old," "guess old," "guess new," or "sure new” within 4000 ms.

Behavioral analyses: Encoding response times were analyzed using a mixed-design ANOVA with group (bromocriptine, sulpiride, placebo) as the between-subjects factor and congruency (congruent, incongruent) as the within-subjects factor. Recognition performance was measured using corrected high-confidence (i.e., sure) hit rates, calculated as the proportion of sure-old responses to old words minus the proportion of sure-old responses to new words (i.e., false alarms), and analyzed using the same ANOVA structure.

EEG analyses: This involved evaluating ERP and time–frequency effects using cluster-based permutation testing with family-wise error correction. Subsequent memory was assessed with “difference due to memory” (DM) contrasts calculated separately for congruent and incongruent words, defined as the difference between high-confidence hits (sure old) vs forgotten trials within each condition. Power spectra were analyzed using FOOOF (1–30 Hz), separating aperiodic components (offset and exponent) and periodic components (peak power, center frequency, and bandwidth).

Results

Behavioral results: During the encoding phase, responses were faster for congruent than for incongruent trials (F(1,83)=53.12, p<.001), but there were no main effects of group or interaction (p> 0.1). During the recognition phase, corrected high-confidence hit rates were higher for congruent than for incongruent words (F(1,83)=452.133, p<.001). However, neither the main effect of group nor the group × congruency interaction was significant (p > 0.1). When combining the EEG and prior fMRI samples, corrected high-confidence hit rates in the recognition phase showed a strong main effect of congruence (F(1,170)=994.80, p<.001) and a significant Congruence × Group interaction (F(2,170)=4.30, p=.015), with no main effect of group (F(2,170)=0.332, p=.718). Additionally, the congruency benefit (congruent–incongruent) varied by group (F(2,170)=4.30, p=.015) and followed a significant quadratic trend (t=-2.93, p=.004). This was driven by a larger congruency benefit in the placebo group than in both drug groups, whereas bromocriptine and sulpiride did not differ from each other (Figure 2A). 

Conventional EEG: Encoding ERPs showed differences between congruent and incongruent trials across a broad time window (~200–1500 ms). Across analyses, congruency-related effects were topographically widespread, involving nearly all electrodes across the scalp. Time–frequency analyses during encoding revealed higher power for congruent than incongruent trials in the beta range and higher power for incongruent than for congruent trials in the theta–alpha/low-beta range. DM congruency effects appeared in ERPs, with congruent DM exceeding incongruent DM in three positive clusters (252–512 ms, p=.005; 636–808 ms, p=.017; 816–956 ms, p=.023). A similar DM congruency effect was observed in time–frequency power (7–30 Hz, 428–1496 ms; p = .0135). No significant main effects of group or group-related interactions were detected in ERP or time–frequency cluster analyses (p> 0.1). 

Spectral parameterization: FOOOF revealed pharmacological effects not visible in traditional EEG measures. During encoding, congruency was associated with notable changes in aperiodic parameters and alpha peak features, including a congruency × group interaction for alpha bandwidth. During recognition, DM aperiodic offset (F(2,64)=5.61, p=.006) and DM aperiodic exponent (F(2,64)=3.75, p=.029) exhibited significant main effects of group. Post hoc analyses revealed that these group effects were driven by lower aperiodic offset (Figure 2B) and exponent (Figure 2C) under bromocriptine compared with sulpiride, with placebo showing intermediate values that did not differ significantly from either drug condition.

Conclusion

Schema congruency yields significant behavioral benefits, as evidenced by higher recognition performance for congruent compared to incongruent words. When combining the EEG data with previous fMRI data, we observed the expected nonlinear (quadratic) dopaminergic modulation of the behavioral congruency advantage, such that the congruency benefit was maximal under placebo and reduced under both bromocriptine and sulpiride. Spectral parameterization indicates that dopaminergic manipulation influences memory-related processing by altering the cortical excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance, as reflected in drug-related differences in aperiodic offset and exponent during recognition. Notably, these effects were not detectable with conventional EEG analyses, highlighting the importance of FOOOF-based decomposition for isolating neuromodulatory effects on cortical state.
