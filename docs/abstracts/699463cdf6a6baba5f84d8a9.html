Introduction

Muscle proprioception, often referred to as the "sixth sense", enables the perception of one’s body position and movement in space. It relies on afferent fibers wrapped around the intrafusal fibers of muscle spindles, which detect muscle stretch and are essential for perceiving movement, also known as kinesthesia. These perceptions can be manipulated through mechanical tendon vibration, which selectively stimulates proprioceptive Ia afferents, giving rise to kinesthetic illusions of muscle elongation without actual movement.  

While central proprioceptive integration has been well studied at the brain level (Kavounoudias et al., 2008), its mechanisms at the spinal level are less understood. The complex network of sensory inputs, interneurons, and motor neurons within the spinal cord has been anatomically demonstrated by cadaver studies, and its functional organization has been explored through clinical cases, invasive epidural electrical stimulation, and, to a lesser extent, electrophysiology. Over the past decade, spinal functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) has overcome significant technical challenges and has since emerged as a reliable, noninvasive tool, opening new possibilities for spinal research, where many questions remain.  

We conducted two innovative studies in healthy adults to explore proprioceptive circuits using spinal fMRI in combination with muscle tendon vibration, to provide new insights into spinal sensorimotor circuits and show that they can be studied noninvasively (Schlienger et al. 2025). 

Methods

Participants 

Healthy young adults participated in cervical (n=24, 27.1 ±2.6 years, 13 females) and lumbar (n=29, 37.7 ±11 years, 13 females) spinal fMRI studies. All but one were right-handed/footed and provided written informed consent (Ethics Committee Sud-Est VI, n°2021-A01733-38). 

Proprioceptive Stimulation 

Mechanical vibration at 50-75 Hz activated Ia afferents in muscle spindles, eliciting movement illusions in immobile participants who rated clarity of the illusory movement (0-4 scale). MR-compatible pneumatic vibrators (small: 53×26mm, large: 102×40mm) were positioned bilaterally on six muscle sites per participant:  

  • Cervical study: wrist flexors, biceps, and anterior deltoids;
  • Lumbar study: anterior tibialis, quadriceps, and hip flexors. 

Vibration was induced by regulated airflow spinning an asymmetric ceramic mass, and a custom LabVIEW-based system synchronized stimulation onsets with MR acquisition. FSR polymer sensors (25mm diameter, 1000Hz) monitored vibration frequency in real-time, confirming delivery within the optimal 50-75Hz range for muscle spindle recruitment. 

MRI Protocol 

Acquisitions were performed on a 3T MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (Siemens) with 64-channel head/neck and 32-channel spinal coils (cervical) or 32-channel body/spine coils (lumbar). A T2-weighted SPACE anatomical scan (0.8×0.8×0.8mm³) was acquired first, followed by 4 (cervical) or 5 (lumbar) functional runs using gradient-echo EPI ZOOMit sequences (TR=2s, TE=30ms, FOV=128×48mm², 1×1mm² in-plane, 4mm slice thickness, 30 or 28 slices). Each run comprised pseudo-randomized 8-second trials of six muscle stimulation conditions plus a no-vibration control, separated by variable inter-stimulus intervals (1.5-2.5s) to prevent post-stimulation effects and enable random subsampling. Participants closed their eyes and focused on movement illusions, rating clarity after each run. Physiological signals (respiration, cardiac pulse) were recorded throughout. 

Image Preprocessing 

Data was bidsified and preprocessed using Spinal Cord Toolbox (v5.8-7.0), FSL (v6.0), and AFNI. A critical step in spinal fMRI preprocessing is motion correction, which was performed using slice-wise realignment with translations regularized along the z-axis using polynomial functions. Runs were co-registered via nonlinear registration, and further on the cord and CSF were segmented from mean functional images and anatomical scans to enable functional-to-anatomical registration. Normalization to PAM50 template space was guided by spinal rootlet landmarks for the cervical study, through a novel automatic tool developed in collaboration with the SCT team in Montreal (Valosek et al., 2024). For the lumbar part, the lumbosacral enlargement and conus medullaris positions were preferred. All warping fields were concatenated and applied with spline interpolation and lastly, anisotropic Gaussian smoothing (1.5×1.5×6mm FWHM) was applied within the cord mask. Volume outliers were identified using DVARS (75th percentile+1.5×IQR). Temporal SNR was computed in the gray matter and used at the individual scale to exclude low-quality data. 

Denoising & Statistical Analysis 

Physiological noise correction used CompCor (15 CSF principal components) and RETROICOR (cardiac/respiratory regressors and their interactions). Univariate analyses employed FSL FEAT with high-pass filtering (128s), in which stimulation and no-vibration conditions were modeled using boxcar functions convolved with Gaussian HRF, alongside motion parameters and outlier volumes. Fixed-effects models combined runs within participants. Group-level analyses used nonparametric permutation testing (Randomise, 10,000 permutations) with Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement. Activation maps were either left uncorrected (lumbar, puncorr<0.005) or corrected for multiple comparisons (pFWE≤0.05).  For more robust ROI analyses, mean activity was extracted from gray-matter subdivisions at each spinal level without thresholding.

Results

Movement Illusion Ratings 

All participants consistently reported illusions of movement corresponding to elongation of vibrated muscles. In the cervical study, wrist and biceps illusions occurred in all trials, whereas anterior deltoid illusions were less frequent (84%) and significantly less salient (p<0.001). Similarly, for the lumbar study, a clear proximal-distal gradient emerged with ankle stimulation, eliciting the strongest illusions, followed by knee and hip stimulation. Hip illusions were less consistent and often subtle. In both studies, vibration frequencies remained within the optimal 55-75Hz range across muscles and sessions.  

Spinal Level Variability 

In the cervical study, individual spinal level mapping using deep-learning-based rootlet identification revealed interparticipant variability in spinal anatomy. This approach was not feasible for the lumbar level due to limited rootlet visibility in MR imaging. The C7 spinal level was significantly shorter than the PAM50 template reference (p=0.007), while other cervical segments showed consistent lengths. A population-specific probabilistic spinal level map (C4-C8) was generated to account for anatomical variability in all activation localization analyses. In the lumbar study, spinal level localization relied on the lumbosacral enlargement and the conus tip, two well-defined and reliable neuroanatomical landmarks.

Spatial Organization of Proprioceptive Activity 

Group-level analyses revealed clear lateralization and dorso-ventral organization. Cervical vibration-versus-rest contrasts showed predominant ipsilateral activation (laterality indices: -0.37 for left-limb stimuli and 1.0 for right) with strong ventral horn localization. Activations were almost exclusively ipsilateral and ventral. Lumbar data similarly demonstrated ipsilateral tendencies (left: -0.34, right: 0.93) with both ventral and dorsal activation patterns.  

Rostro-Caudal Somatotopy 

Stimulation of different muscle groups revealed systematic rostro-caudal gradients. In the cervical cord (Figure 2, Top Left Panel), left-versus-right contrasts showed FWE-corrected activations at C5 (anterior deltoid), C7 (biceps), and C8 (wrist flexors), primarily in ventral horns. Individual-level laterality indices confirmed significant ipsilateral lateralization at expected spinal levels (C5-C6 for deltoid p<0.05; C6-C8 for wrist p<0.05). 

In the lumbar cord (Figure 2, Bottom Left Panel), peak activations appeared at L1, L4, and L5 (knees), L4-S1 (ankles), with hip stimulation recruiting more rostral segments. However, lumbar group-level activations did not survive FWE correction due to greater acquisition and processing challenges in this region. 

ROI-Based Individual Analysis 

To overcome spatial normalization limitations, particularly in the lumbar cord, ROI analyses extracted unthresholded activity within anatomical subdivisions at each spinal level across participants. Maps showed that most-activated levels corresponded to expected myotomal entries: T12-L1 and L1&S1 (left and right hips), L1-L4 and L2-L3 (knees), L4-L5-S1 (ankles). Five levels showed significantly stronger ipsilateral versus contralateral activity for ankle and hip stimulation, with fewer differences along the dorso-ventral axis. This approach revealed robust rostro-caudal organization and ipsilateral preferences at the participant level, confirming somatotopic organization despite limited statistical power at the group voxel-wise level. 

In the cervical study, individual-level density maps projected participant activations onto the probabilistic spinal cord map, revealing consistency with group-level peaks while also showing more distributed activation patterns along the rostro-caudal axis. Density maps demonstrated maximal participant cluster prevalence at C4-C6 for anterior deltoid, C6-C8 for biceps, and C8 for wrist flexors, paralleling the group activation patterns and confirming inter-individual reproducibility of the somatotopic organization.

Conclusion

By combining spinal fMRI with controlled proprioceptive stimulation, we investigated the functional organization of human spinal sensorimotor circuits at both cervical and lumbar levels. Using muscle tendon vibration, a motionless task that preferentially activates muscle spindle afferents, we were able to probe proprioceptive pathways while minimizing motion-related artifacts that have limited earlier spinal fMRI studies relying on passive or active movements. Across both studies, vibration consistently induced movement illusions aligned with muscle elongation, confirming effective and reproducible recruitment of proprioceptive afferents. 

By stimulating multiple muscle groups in the upper and lower limbs, we identified distinct patterns of in-plane (lateralized and ventro-dorsal) and rostrocaudal organization depending on the stimulated muscle and body side. Spinal activations were predominantly ipsilateral, in line with the anatomical organization of primary afferent projections, although contralateral involvement was also observed, likely reflecting commissural interneuronal pathways supporting interlimb coordination. When analyses were focused on expected myotomal levels, activations showed a preferential ventral distribution, consistent with synaptic processing within proprio-motor loops. Dorsal activations were also present and may reflect cutaneous input induced by vibration at the skin surface, or proprioceptive processing in intermediate lumbar pathways, including Clarke’s column. 

Rostrocaudally, activation peaks aligned with established myotome maps in both the cervical and lumbosacral cord, with proximal muscles engaging more rostral segments and distal muscles recruiting more caudal levels. At the same time, activity often extended across adjacent levels, consistent with polysynaptic integration within longitudinal and propriospinal networks. Importantly, substantial inter-individual variability was observed across participants, echoing clinical and intraoperative evidence that muscle innervation is overlapping, asymmetric, and variable between individuals. 

At the cervical level, this variability was addressed by developing a deep-learning-based method to identify individual nerve rootlets, enabling individualized spinal level mapping and the generation of a population-specific probabilistic map to improve group-level alignment. This approach revealed deviations from standard templates, underscoring the limitations of atlas-based normalization for functional spinal analyses. In the lumbar cord, where rootlet identification was not feasible, ROI-based analyses were used to mitigate variability and preserve spatial specificity. 

Together, these findings demonstrate that spinal fMRI can noninvasively probe the organization and modulation of human spinal sensorimotor circuits. By accounting for individual anatomical variability and leveraging controlled proprioceptive stimulation, this approach opens promising avenues for studying spinal plasticity and for developing clinically relevant biomarkers of sensorimotor function and recovery.
