Introduction

Auditory processing disorder (APD) is characterized by impaired analysis and interpretation of auditory information despite normal peripheral hearing and affects approximately 0.2–0.5% of school-aged children1. APD can significantly impact speech perception, language development, attention, and academic performance2. Children with APD typically experience difficulties in auditory discrimination, binaural and temporal processing, and speech comprehension in noisy or competitive environments, even when standard audiometric thresholds are normal2. The clinical heterogeneity of APD has long complicated its definition and diagnosis, with two main clinical profiles commonly reported: children with isolated listening difficulties, and those with associated comorbidities such as attention deficit disorder, specific language impairment, or cognitive delay.

The aim of this study is to use a multimodal MRI approach to investigate central auditory processing and to improve understanding of the neural structures and mechanisms involved in children with APD. Functional MRI (fMRI) using auditory stimuli has been widely used to characterize cortical and subcortical auditory networks while arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI enables non-invasive quantification of cerebral blood flow (CBF) at rest and provides complementary information to fMRI by reflecting baseline cerebral perfusion. Combining ASL and fMRI may improve the characterization of neural mechanisms underlying APD, particularly in children with isolated listening difficulties.

Methods

This prospective single-center study was conducted at Necker–Enfants Malades Hospital (Paris, France). Fourteen children diagnosed with APD were included (10 females, mean age 11.02 ± 2.88 years). Within this group, a subgroup of seven children met criteria for isolated APD, without any associated comorbidities (5 females, mean age 10.35 ± 2.22 years). Eleven normally hearing control children recruited via clinical advertisement (8 females; mean age 12.34 ± 2.36 years). All prospectively enrolled children underwent structural 3D T1-weighted, ASL and functional MRI (fMRI). In addition, ASL images from fifteen age-matched control children from our database was also used for comparison.

All MRI examinations were performed on a 3 Tesla scanner (Discovery MR750, GE Healthcare). During fMRI, children passively listened to binaurally presented vocal and non-vocal auditory stimuli3. Stimuli were delivered using E-Prime 3.0 software. Two auditory paradigms were implemented. The first paradigm (10 minutes) consisted of 60 blocks equally divided into voice, non-voice, and silence conditions. The second paradigm (5 minutes) included 30 blocks comprising binaural listening and silence conditions. Each block lasted 10 seconds and consisted of 2 seconds of silence followed by 8 seconds of auditory stimulation (or silence)3. Block order was pseudo-randomized, identical across participants, and synchronized with image acquisition.

Structural 3D T1-weighted images were segmented into gray matter using CAT12/SPM12, normalized to a pediatric MNI template, and smoothed with a 6-mm Gaussian kernel. ASL images were co-registered to native gray matter, normalized to MNI space, brain-masked, and smoothed (6 mm). Task-based fMRI preprocessing (DPARSF/DPABI) included slice-timing correction, realignment, co-registration to 3D T1-weighted images, spatial normalization (3-mm isotropic), and smoothing (6 mm).

Voxel-wise group comparison of CBF were performed using two-sample t-test within a general linear model (SPM12), including age and global CBF as covariates. Analyses were restricted to cortical gray matter. Significance was set at p < 0.05, FWE-corrected. For fMRI analyses, participants with excessive motion (>3 mm translation or >3° rotation) were excluded. First-level analyses were conducted using voxel-wise general linear models including task regressors convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function and motion parameters as nuisance regressors. A high-pass filter (128 s) was applied. Group-level random-effects analyses included one-sample t-tests for the contrasts voice > silence, non-voice > silence, voice > non-voice, and binaural > silence. Statistical significance was assessed using a voxel-level threshold of p < 0.001 (uncorrected) and a cluster-level FWE correction at p < 0.05. Laterality index (LI) were also computed4.

Results

Individual 3D T1-weighted MRI examinations did not reveal any structural brain abnormalities.

Whole-brain voxel-based comparison of CBF at rest between the subgroup of seven children with isolated APD and 26 controls revealed a significant hyperperfusion predominantly in right-hemisphere temporal and frontal regions. Specifically, increased CBF was observed in the right middle temporal gyrus (t = 7.70, z = 5.60, p < 0.001, FWE-corrected), right superior temporal gyrus (t = 5.94, z = 4.74, p = 0.043, FWE-corrected), and right inferior frontal gyrus (t = 6.81, z = 5.19, p = 0.006, FWE-corrected). A significant cluster was also identified at the junction of the left inferior temporal and middle occipital gyri (t = 6.42, z = 4.99, p = 0.015, FWE-corrected). When considering the entire APD group (n = 14), voxel-wise comparisons with controls did not reveal any significant differences at the voxel peak level after FWE correction (p < 0.05). However, using a voxel-level threshold of p < 0.001 (uncorrected) combined with a cluster-level FWE correction at p < 0.05, similar patterns of increased resting CBF were observed in right-hemisphere temporal and frontal regions. No regions of decreased resting CBF were observed in either the isolated APD subgroup or the entire APD group compared to controls.

Whole-brain second-level random-effects analyses of task-based fMRI were performed separately for each group (controls, children with isolated APD, and the entire patient group). For the voice > silence and non-voice > silence contrasts, control children showed significant bilateral activation of the transverse and superior temporal gyri, planum polare, and planum temporale (LI between −0.20 and 0.20). In contrast, both the isolated APD subgroup and the entire APD group exhibited a more right-lateralized activation pattern (LI < −0.20). For the voice > non-voice contrast, control children showed significant bilateral activation in the superior temporal gyrus and planum polare (LI between −0.20 and 0.20). The entire APD group also showed significant bilateral activation in the middle and superior temporal gyri, with a peak that was predominantly left-lateralized. However, the isolated APD subgroup exhibited a strongly left-lateralized cluster (LI > 0.20). Finally, for the binaural > silence contrast, results revealed a bilateral activation pattern in the transverse and superior temporal gyri, planum temporale, and central operculum across all groups, with a left-predominance at the cluster-level (LI > 0.20). Both children with isolated APD and the entire patient group exhibited more widespread activation clusters extending into the right postcentral gyrus and the parietal operculum (LI between −0.20 and 0.20).

Conclusion

Our preliminary results suggest that APD is associated with altered central auditory processing. None of the children diagnosed with APD showed structural brain abnormalities on 3D T1-weighted MRI. However, children with APD appeared to rely predominantly on a right-hemisphere network to process both voice and non-voice sounds, with reduced hemispheric specialization and an attenuated contribution of the left hemisphere, compared with controls. This persistent right-sided activation, potentially associated with baseline hyperperfusion observed on ASL MRI, may hinder the emergence of a distinct voice-selective network. In cases of isolated APD, perfusion abnormalities appeared to be even more pronounced, suggesting a clearer disruption of hemispheric organization.

These findings support the hypothesis of an altered hemispheric balance during auditory processing in children with APD. Overall, multimodal MRI appears to be a promising tool for revealing subtle functional alterations that remain undetected on conventional structural MRI and for refining the functional characterization and diagnosis of APD. Furthermore, the identification of such subtle cerebral changes seems to require the study of homogeneous patient groups. From a clinical perspective, therapeutic strategies that may benefit these children include minimizing background noise exposure and the use of assistive listening devices incorporating noise reduction and remote microphones to capture auditory information at the source before it is degraded by competing sounds.
