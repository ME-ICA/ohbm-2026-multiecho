Introduction

Focal cortical dysplasias (FCDs) are a type of malformation of cortical development characterized by disorganized cortical layers and intracortical connectivity. They represent the leading cause of drug-resistant focal epilepsy in children and the second most common cause in adults.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is currently the main non-invasive tool used to identify FCDs. However, not all lesions are visually apparent at the macroscopic level, and it is estimated that 30-40% of FCDs remain undetected on conventional T1- and T2-weighted MRI.1 This reduces the opportunity for surgical treatment, which can significantly improve seizure outcomes.

The main abnormalities of FCD occur at a mesoscopic level. Diffusion-sensitive MRI (dMRI) allows characterization of the brain’s microarchitecture through quantitative water-diffusion parameters, thereby representing a viable method for the detection of FCD. In a rodent model of subtle FCD, dMRI analysis using Multi-Resolution Discrete-Search (MRDS) method2 to estimate the diffusion parameters of a multi-tensor model, has proven useful for detecting cortical myeloarchitectural disorganization.3 This work represents a first step toward translating these advanced diffusion MRI methods to human FCD by providing an initial description of lesion-related diffusion abnormalities in individual patients.

Methods

Diffusion parameters were analyzed in five patients with suspected FCD and one patient with histopathologically confirmed FCD. All patients had drug-resistant focal epilepsy and ranged in age from 20 to 57 years (3 females, 3 males). Control subjects were matched to each patient by age and sex.

MRI data were acquired on a Siemens 3T scanner at the Peter S. Allen MRI Research Centre in Edmonton, Canada, using a 64-channel head coil. Whole brain volumetric T1- and FLAIR images were obtained with 1 mm isotropic resolution. dMRI was collected using a whole brain multi-shell acquisition with 100 slices, 1.2 mm isotropic voxels, and b-values of 500, 800, 1500, and 2500 s/mm² with 6, 16, 32, and 64 diffusion directions, respectively, resulting in a total acquisition time of 17 minutes. All MRI data were preprocessed to minimize artifacts. Preprocessing included noise and Gibbs-ringing reduction, and correction of motion, susceptibility, and eddy-current distortions. Diffusion parameters from the multi-tensor model were estimated using the MRDS method.

The pial surface was estimated using FreeSurfer’s recon-all, and from this surface, virtual trajectories (streamlines) were propagated inward through a Laplacian potential field toward the ventricles, approximating the inverse path of neuronal migration (Fig. 1A). Diffusion metrics were sampled at equidistant 0.5 mm points along 10 mm streamlines, enabling characterization of cortical and juxtacortical microstructure. When the MRDS method identified multiple tensors within a voxel, each tensor was classified as radial or tangential according to its orientation relative to cortical fibers (Fig. 1B), allowing the separation of projection/afferent pathways from short-range fibers (i.e., U-fibers).

Descriptive diffusion profiles were generated for FCDs visible on T1/FLAIR MRI. After a neuroradiologist identified each lesion, FLAIR was co-registered to diffusion space using SynthSeg and antsRegistration tools (Fig. 1C). Lesion masks and contralateral homologous masks were manually delineated (Fig. 1D). Diffusion values were sampled along the streamlines within each mask, and the same sampling procedure was performed in homologous regions of an age- and sex-matched control subject. Intra-voxel bundle-wise (i.e., per-tensor) Fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) were quantified for all relevant voxels, categorized into radial (rad) and tangential (tan) orientations. An interhemispheric asymmetry index (AI) was calculated for each metric and fiber orientation as:
AI=(mvrh-mvlh)/(mvrh+mvlh), where mvrh and mvlh denote the metric value in the right and left hemisphere, respectively.

Results

Our proof-of-concept analysis revealed heterogeneous diffusion patterns across patients. Figure 2 shows one of the six patients analyzed, who exhibited markedly reduced per-bundle FA at the lesion site in the occipital lobe, particularly in juxtacortical white matter (WM), and in both radial and tangential orientation, along with elevated MD values in both orientations. On another hand, we found that two patients showed the opposite pattern, exhibiting higher FA-rad and FA-tan values at the lesion site (located in the frontal lobe) compared with both their contralateral region and the matched control, resulting in an increased AI. 

Three patients (with FCDs in frontal and two in parietal lobes) demonstrated per-bundle FA and per-bundle MD asymmetry values similar to their matched control, however, they presented overall higher FA ranges in both orientations compared with the control subject. Interestingly, in two patients per-bundle MD appeared to be the metric with greater sensitivity to lesion-related alterations. 

Regarding sampling depth, we found that the cortex was the region with the greatest asymmetry in both diffusion metrics for most of the patients. However, in some cases asymmetry also extended into the subsequent WM. Notably, in one patient, we observed an inversion in the direction of the FA asymmetry when transitioning from the cortex into the juxtacortical WM.

Conclusion

As previously reported in pediatric epilepsy studies using conventional  Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI),4 diffusion abnormalities in FCD can vary markedly between individuals, likely reflecting differences in lesion type or the degree of tissue disruption. In our sample, per-bundle FA and MD differed both within patients and relative to matched controls, supporting this notion of heterogeneous diffusion signatures.

The use of a multi-tensor analysis allowed us to probe diffusion properties along radial and tangential orientations across cortical and subcortical depths, providing greater anatomical specificity than conventional DTI. This approach may better capture subtle microstructural alterations, particularly in cases where standard DTI metrics appear normal or inconclusive.⁵

Only one patient in our cohort currently has histopathological confirmation, highlighting the need for additional validation. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that the multi-tensor MRDS method is a sensitive approach for characterizing diverse forms of FCD and may help detect subtle lesions that remain invisible on conventional MRI. Ongoing work includes acquiring MRI data from additional patients with suspected FCD and analyzing a control cohort to build normative diffusion profiles and enable group-level comparisons.
