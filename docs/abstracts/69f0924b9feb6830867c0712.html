Introduction

Clinical neuroscience studies rely on the assumption that brain measures are valid correlates of self-reported measures of behavior. Construct validity, which reflects the extent to which a measure(s) reflects a person’s true score on an underlying theoretical construct, requires that both behavioral and brain measures exhibit similar longitudinal stability. For instance, a person’s self-report of negative affect over the past day will fluctuate from day to day while their genome is constant through time. Therefore, it would not be expected that a state, such as a person’s negative affect on any given day, could be reliably predicted from their genome. Instead, it is likely that a temporally stable measure (e.g., propensity to experience negative affect) would be associated with one’s genes. This example demonstrates that two measures must exhibit similar temporal stability to observe valid relationships between them.

One of the most widely used measures in clinical neuroscience research is resting-state functional connectivity (FC) computed as the correlation between BOLD fMRI signals. Past work has demonstrated that replicable associations between FC and self-report measures of psychopathology are small and require thousands of participants to observe (Marek et al., 2022). One possible reason for the poor reproducibility of brain-behavior correlations is that FC is highly stable over time within individuals (e.g., Gratton et al., 2018). In addition to high stability, FC has also contains enough sources of stable variance to discriminate individuals from each other across time (Finn et al., 2015).

Here, we examined which aspects of self-report measures affect the reproducibility of brain-behavior correlations. First, we demonstrate that the consistency of brain-behavior correlations decreases as the interval between self-report measures increases. Then, we compare the univariate and multivariate longitudinal stability of self-report psychopathology data versus FC.

Methods

We used the BrainMAPD dataset (N = 274), which longitudinally followed young adults for 3 years from ages 18-21. Participants completed annual psychopathology assessments and fMRI scans at baseline and 3 years post-baseline. These analyses were replicated using annually collected self-report data (baseline through 3-year follow-up) and fMRI (baseline and 2-year follow-up) data from the ABCD study (N = 11,875). Only participants with >13 minutes of fMRI data after censoring at each timepoint were analyzed.

For the BrainMAPD study, at baseline and the 3-year follow-up visits 150 participants completed approximately 65 minutes of resting-state and task fMRI scans. The data from these scans was processed using fMRIPrep (Esteban et al., 2019). FC processing was performed using custom scripts which included nuisance regression and censoring high motion frames (fFD > .1), and mapping functional data to surface space. Only participants with >20 minutes of fMRI data after censoring at each timepoint were analyzed.

For the ABCD study, behavioral data for the first 3 timepoints were obtained from the ABCD 4.0 release. The fMRI data for the ABCD study analyzed here comes from the ABCD-BIDS Community Collection (ABCC; Feczko et al., 2021), which contains FC-processed data that has been mapped to surface space. In both studies, functional data were parcellated using the Gordon 333 atlas (Gordon et al., 2016).

Test-retest reliability was computed for all self-report and FC measures using Pearson correlations. To quantify the amount of stable variance in self-report and FC data, subject identifiability (“fingerprinting”) at different longitudinal time points was performed using different features of the data: either psychopathology scales (ABCD = 26, BrainMAPD = 16), psychopathology items (ABCD = 161, BrainMAPD = 153), or fMRI FC (55,278 ROI-to-ROI edges). Gower’s distance was used to calculate similarity between all participants based on each set of features. If a given participant had the closest distance between their data at different timepoints, they were classified as being successfully fingerprinted.

To evaluate the reproducibility of brain-behavior correlations, a correlation matrix was created for all pairwise correlations between each FC edge at a given fMRI scan and each self-report item and scale (see Figure 1A). In the ABCD study, for example, this resulted in 8 correlation matrices (4 self-report assessment time points x 2 fMRI scan time points; see Figure 1B). The consistency of correlations at each edge was computed by calculating the similarity of brain-behavior correlations for the same edge across fMRI scans (Figure 1C). Conversely, the consistency of correlations for each self-report feature (item or scale) was calculated by correlating the brain-behavior correlations for the same self-report feature across fMRI scans (Figure 1D).

Results

We found that the reproducibility of brain-behavior correlations was affected by the assessment interval between self-report measures (see Figure 2A). For self-report items in the BrainMAPD study, the shortest interval between assessments (3 weeks; r = .36) showed significantly greater consistency of brain-behavior correlations versus the same assessments at 1 year (r = .27, t(152) = 12.6, p < .001, Cohen’s dz = 1.02), 2 years (r = .25, t(152)= 16.1, p < .001, Cohen’s dz = 1.3), or 3 years (r = .24, t(152) = 17.9, p < .001, Cohen’s dz = 1.4). A similar result was also observed in the ABCD data, with the shortest assessment interval (1 year; r = .13) demonstrating greater consistency of brain-behavior correlations versus the same assessments at 2 years (r = .11, t(154)= 4.2, p < .001, Cohen’s dz = .34) and 3 years (r = .09, t(154)= 8, p < .001, Cohen’s dz = .64). The same pattern of results was also observed for scales in both studies.

To evaluate the longitudinal stability of self-report data versus fMRI FC, we quantified whether these measures had similar test-retest reliability over time (see Figure 2B). In the BrainMAPD dataset, self-report items (r = .43) had significantly lower test-retest reliability than FC edges (r = .53) 3 years apart, t(55429) = 6.8, p < .001, Cohen’s ds = .06. In the ABCD data, FC edges (r = .35) also exhibited significantly greater longitudinal stability 2 years apart versus self-report items (r = .28), t(55432) = 5.1, p < .001, Cohen’s ds = .04. These results suggest that FC data has greater univariate test-retest reliability than items over a multi-year time window. Scale test-retest reliability did not significantly differ from FC test-retest reliability in the BrainMAPD data, t(55292) = .67, p = .5, Cohen’s ds = .01. However, scales did exhibit greater test-retest reliability than FC edges on average in the ABCD data, t(55299) = 3.7, p < .001, Cohen’s ds = .03.

The fact that FC data shows greater reliability for individual edges than self-report items suggests that they contain more stable variance on a per-feature basis. Next, we evaluated whether the amount of stable variance across all features differed between self-report data and FC (see Figure 2C). In both datasets, longitudinal FC identifiability (ABCD = 92%, BrainMAPD = 98%) was greater than psychopathology items (ABCD = 24%, BrainMAPD = 65%; BrainMAPD: t(3999) = 2115, p < .001, Cohen’s dz = 33.4, ABCD: t(2998) = 479, p < .001, Cohen’s ds = 17.5) and scale scores (ABCD = 16%, BrainMAPD = 33%; BrainMAPD: t(3999) = 1668, p < .001, Cohen’s dz = 26.3, ABCD: t(2998) = 683, p < .001, Cohen’s ds = 24.9). Though here FC edges showed greater multivariate stability than self-report items or scales, this could be due to the substantially greater number of FC edges versus self-report features. However, after controlling for the number of features, FC edges still showed significantly greater fingerprinting accuracy in both studies (BrainMAPD: F(4,15995) = 21575, p < .001, η2 = .84; ABCD: F(3,6996) = 34205, p < .001, η2 = .94).

While FC edges showed greater multivariate stability than self-report data, self-report items also exhibited greater stability than scales across time (see Figure 2D). In both studies, items show greater identifiability than scales (BrainMAPD: 67% vs 41% fingerprinting accuracy, F(1,29992) = 137370, p < .001, η2 = .32; ABCD: 18% vs 26% fingerprinting accuracy, F(1,11994) = 9158, p < .001, η2 = .21). Notably, this was the case despite items having significantly lower univariate stability on average (see Figure 2B) in BrainMAPD (Items r = .35 versus Scale r = .59; t(167) = 5.2, p < .001, Cohen’s ds = .8) and ABCD (Items r = .38 versus Scale r = .49; t(177) = 5.8, p <. 001, Cohen’s ds = .87). This suggests that aggregating items into scales increases their univariate test-retest reliability, but reduces the amount of overall stable variance in self-report data.

Conclusion

Previous work has demonstrated that reproducible brain-behavior correlations are small and require thousands of subjects (Marek et al., 2022). Here, we show that a likely reason for this is that the consistency of brain-behavior correlations decreases as the interval between an fMRI scan and a given self-report assessment increases. Notably, in these analyses the FC data used to generate the observed brain-behavior correlations were held constant, suggesting that one of the causes of poor reproducibility in brain-behavior correlations is that true scores on self-report data become less temporally stable as time between assessments increases. This result implies that many self-report measures may lack validity as correlates of FC due to the true value of an individual’s scores being less stable over time.

One plausible explanation for why self-report measures exhibit poor temporal stability is that they exhibit lower test-retest reliability for univariate features than FC edges. However, while this was true for self-report items, self-report scales actually exhibited greater reliability than FC edges. Critically, although scales demonstrated greater test-retest reliability versus FC edges and items, the reproducibility of observed brain-behavior correlations still decreased as the time between scale administrations increased. This suggests that while scales demonstrate more stable observed scores than FC edges over time, they may still be an inadequate measure of someone’s true score on a given construct.

Another possible contributor to the poor reproducibility in brain-behavior correlations is that self-report measures lack enough stable “trait-like” (versus state-related) variance to produce replicable correlations with FC. In other words, the amount of stable information in self-report measures may provide a description of behavior that does not contain enough sources of “trait-like” variability to adequately map onto the complex biology of the brain. Using fingerprinting accuracy as a measure of multivariate stability, we observed that FC edges contain substantially more stable information than self-report measures. This effect remained significant after controlling for the number of features, suggesting that individual FC edges on average contain more stable information than items or scales. Interestingly, despite scales having greater univariate test-retest reliability than items or FC edges, they also had significantly lower multivariate stability across both datasets. This suggests that a significant amount of stable “trait-like” variance in self-report data is being compressed when items are averaged into scales. One potential explanation for this result is that although averaging items into scales improves the observed univariate test-retest reliability of self-report measures, aggregated scales may do a worse job of defining constructs that are valid correlates of biology.

An important caveat to the results reported here is that that test-retest reliability of fMRI FC data is affected by the total amount of low-motion data in each scan. This explains why the univariate test-retest reliability of FC data is different between the two datasets as they have different amounts of fMRI data in each scan. However, despite the differences in scan time, the multivariate test-retest reliability of FC data was still much greater than self-report data in both datasets. Overall, these results suggest that the lack of stable variability in psychopathology measures reduces our ability to observe reproducible brain-behavior correlations.
