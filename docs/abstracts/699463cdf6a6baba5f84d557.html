Introduction

Increasing age has commonly been associated with decline in working memory, yet substantial individual variability is observed among healthy older adults. Resting-state EEG (RS-EEG) has been proposed as a sensitive marker of intrinsic large-scale neural dynamics that may help explain these differences1. Traditional RS-EEG metrics, such as spectral power, individual alpha frequency (IAF), or alpha reactivity, capture oscillatory properties that often show age-related slowing2. However, evidence linking these spectral features to cognitive performance in healthy aging remains limited.

A complementary framework conceptualizes RS-EEG not only in terms of oscillatory activity but as a sequence of rapidly changing topographies known as EEG microstates.3 Microstates reflect coordinated activity within large-scale cortical networks and offer temporally precise markers of network stability and switching. Although microstate alterations have been described across the adult lifespan4, their relevance for cognitive functioning in healthy older adults is still unclear3.

In addition, task-evoked event-related potentials (ERPs), such as the P300 in the context of memory tasks, provide additional insight into cognitive processes by indexing attentional allocation of working-memory updating during stimulation. Relating these ERPs to intrinsic RS-EEG organization may reveal how RS-networks influence neural efficiency and behavioral performance during cognitive demands.

Here, we combined RS- and task-based EEG in a multimodal paradigm to directly compare the predictive value of traditional RS-EEG spectral features and RS microstate dynamics in a sample of healthy older adults. We examined whether (a) RS-EEG metrics predict individual differences in ERP metrics during N-back performance, (b) RS-dynamics relate to task-evoked P300 amplitude across load levels, and (c) RS-microstates explain variance in behavioral and neural responses beyond that captured by traditional spectral markers.

Methods

Thirty healthy older adults (Mage = 69, SDAge = 6.2, RangeAge = 60-83, 63% female; recruited from the community), completed an RS-EEG session followed by an EEG-based N-back working memory task (0-, 1-, and 2-back). Behavioral performance was quantified using sensitivity (d’) and rection time (RT). EEG was recorded with 32 Ag/AgCl scalp active electrodes (BrainVision, Brain Products GmbH, Gilching, Germany) during both RS- and task performance. 

Traditional RS-EEG metrics were computed from 5-minute (eyes-open and closed) recordings. Data were filtered, segmented, and transformed into power spectral density estimates using Welch’s method. For each participant, relative spectral power was extracted in frequency bands estimated by their individual alpha frequency and across predefined clusters (frontal, fronto-central, parietal). IAF was estimated from the parieto-occipital spectrum, and alpha reactivity was computed as the relative reduction in alpha power from eyes-closed to eyes-open recording. These spectral measures served as traditional RS predictors of behavior and P300 amplitude during the N-back task. For microstate analysis, global field power (GFP) peaks from RS-EEG (eyes closed) were extracted, and GFP peak maps were clustered using a modified k-means algorithm (PyCrostates5), yielding four canonical microstate templates (A-D). These templates were back-fitted to each participant’s continuous RS-EEG to compute microstate parameters (mean duration, occurrence rate, temporal coverage). Event-related potentials were extracted from task-evoked EEG, focusing on P300 amplitude at Fz, Cz, and Pz individually within each N-back level.

Linear mixed-effects models were used to assess whether RS spectral features and RS microstate metrics predicted behavioral performance (d’, RT) and P300 amplitude across N-back levels.

Results

Analysis of traditional RS-EEG metrics showed minimal explanatory value. Across all bands, clusters, and metrics, none of the spectral RS features predicted N-back behavioral performance, or P300 amplitude, and these variables also did not moderate P300 reactivity to working memory load. Thus, traditional RS-EEG power markers did not account for individual variability in performance or neural correlates of working memory in our sample of older adults. 

In contrast, RS microstates revealed significant behavioral and neural associations. Microstate B, reflective of early sensory processing, was negatively associated with performance, with longer duration predicting lower d’ (β = -0.38, p = .002); further duration of microstate B predicted faster RT at high working memory load. Microstate C, reflective of attentional control and salience, moreover, showed a positive effect: higher occurrence was associated with greater d’ (β = 0.25, p = .041), and longer duration predicted faster RT in the 2-back condition. Finally, microstate D, mostly associated with cognitive control, showed mixed behavioral relationships, but critically, higher RS microstate D occurrence predicted larger parietal P300 amplitudes during the working memory task (β = 0.77, p = .014), independent of load.

Conclusion

Together, our findings highlight resting-state microstate dynamics as a promising window into intrinsic neural organization that supports cognitive processing and engagement in healthy aging. Whereas traditional spectral RS-EEG markers showed little relevance to behavioral or neural variability in our dataset, microstate characteristics captured meaningful differences in both working-memory performance and P300 amplitude. Microstate B has been linked to visual- and early sensory processing and microstates C and D to higher cognitive functions, top-down modulation, and executive- and cognitive control.3 The here reported behavioral and neural patters, particularly the positive association between microstates C and D with N-back accuracy and P300 amplitude, suggest that older adults with more stable executive-control-related resting-state networks may recruit attentional and updating resources more efficiently during a working memory task. These results underscore the value of microstate analyses as a complementary approach for characterizing neural functioning and individual variability in older adults and might serve as a sensitive biomarker for brain health in aging.
