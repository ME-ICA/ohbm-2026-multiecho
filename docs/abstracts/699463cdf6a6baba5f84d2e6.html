Introduction

An increasing amount of literature suggests that arousal fluctuations dynamically shape large-scale brain organization, influencing cognition and emotional state even in the absence of external tasks. A domain-general arousal network has been suggested1 as a potential “coordinator” of other brain networks, affecting the function of the entire cortex. The noradrenergic locus coeruleus (LC), in the brainstem, has been suggested as a central hub of this arousal-modulated architecture1,2. This paves the way to study this mechanism in cases of aberrant and varying arousal levels, such as those potentially occurring in Insomnia Disorder (ID). ID is primarily characterized by impaired subjective sleep quality and has been associated with a general state of hyperarousal and altered mood states (including depression). More recently, ID has also been connected with inadequate silencing of the LC3. The role of the latter is undisputed in the regulation of arousal during wakefulness and sleep, especially REM (Rapid Eye Movement)4. Notably, temporal fluctuations in network structure have been suggested to be driven by ongoing dynamic alterations in ascending neuromodulatory input to the cortex and subcortex2. The LC is suggested as a central hub, potentially modulating fluctuations in global network topology, through phasic alterations in neural gain. Despite accumulating evidence for the arousal-mediating role of the LC in sleep and mood on the one hand, and LC-driven noradrenergic influences on whole-brain functional connectivity on the other, the relationship between LC activity, resting-state network configuration and sleep quality and/or mood states remains greatly understudied. In the current exploratory analysis, we used a dynamic functional connectivity (DFC) and LC BOLD activity, from resting-state fMRI data to explore whether network integration and LC coupling associate with sleep quality and depression in a sample of healthy individuals. 

Methods

24 healthy adults (14 women, 39.1±18.7y) underwent MRI in a MAGNETOM Terra 7T MRI system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a single-channel transmit coil and a 32-receiving channel head coil (1TX/32RX Head Coil, Nova Medical). They further completed the Insomnia severity Index (ISI), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). 

An overview of the analysis steps is shown in Figure 1. In particular, blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI data were acquired during rest, using a multiband gradient-recalled echo–echo-planar imaging (GRE-EPI) sequence: repetition time (TR) = 2,340 ms, echo time (TE) = 24 ms, flip angle = 90°, MB acceleration factor = 2, voxel size = 1.4 × 1.4 × 1.4 mm, 120 volumes, acquisition time = 4.68min. A Magnetization-Prepared with 2 RApid Gradient Echoes (MP2RAGE) sequence was used to acquire T1 anatomical images: TR = 4,300 ms, TE = 1.98 ms, FA = 5°/6°, TI = 940 ms/2,830 ms, bandwidth = 240 Hz/pixel, voxel size = 0.75 × 0.75 × 0.75 mm, acquisition time = 9:03min. The LC was localized using a 3D neuromelanin-sensitive high-resolution magnetization transfer–weighted turbo-flash (MT-TFL) sequence with the following parameters: TR = 400 ms, TE = 2.55 ms, FA = 8°, bandwidth = 300 Hz/pixel, number of averages = 2, turbo factor = 54, magnetization transfer contrast (MTC) pulses = 20, MTC FA = 260°, MTC RF duration = 10,000 μs, MTC inter-RF delay = 4,000 μs, MTC offset = 2,000 Hz, voxel size = 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.5 mm, acquisition time = 8:13min. Sixty axial slices were acquired and centered for the acquisitions perpendicularly to the rhomboid fossa.

Individual LC masks were manually delineated by 2 experts (AG and NJS) based on MT-TFL LC-specific images5 after being coregistered to each subject’s upsampled T1 image. Mean LC timeseries were calculated as the mean of all voxel timeseries from the BOLD resting-state fMRI images of voxels overlapping between both raters. Notably, LC timeseries were extracted from subject-space data, to avoid any distortions and alterations due to MNI or group-space normalization. Typical preprocessing steps can be found in previous publications from our center5. After nuisance regression/denoising (WM, CSF, movement) and bandpass filtering (0.008-0.09Hz), the 1mm granularity AAL atlas was used for ROI parcellation, excluding cerebellar and LC, 148 brain areas remained. For these areas, we calculated whole-brain ROI-ROI Dynamic Functional Connectivity (DFC) for every timepoint (TR) using instantaneous phase coherence, i.e. the cosine of the difference of instantaneous phases of ROI pairs, obtained via Hilbert transform of their average timeseries. The resulting 120 temporally-specific DFC networks where individually “filtered” via OMST (Orthogonal Minimum Spanning Trees) to reveal only the meaningful underlying connectome, as fully-weighted networks are considered suboptimal and problematic. Subsequently, global efficiency (GE) was calculated for each timepoint, characterizing integration tendencies of each temporally-specific network. The mean LC BOLD signal served as an index of arousal fluctuations. Directed coupling between LC activity and DFC transitions was quantified using the Granger causality metric, specifically, the log/likelihood ratio as described in the MVGC toolbox (github.com/lcbarnett/MVGC1) and p-values showing the significance of the causal relationship. 

MRI data were preprocessed and analyzed using SPM12, ANTs, FSL, SynthStrip (Freesurfer) and custom MATLAB scripts (version 2023a). The brain connectivity toolbox (https://sites.google.com/site/bctnet/) was used for the calculation of graph-theoretic measures (global efficiency) and the OMST implementation was used for graph-reduction (github.com/stdimitr/topological_filtering_networks). Spearman correlations were used to assess the relationship between LC-DFC coupling and our metrics of interest (PSQI and BDI scores) in this exploratory investigation. 

Results

Our sample consisted of “neurotypical” control subjects, without subjectively reported or objectively confirmed sleep disturbances or diagnosed mental disorders. The sample was however characterized by varying ISI (3.8±3.32, max=12), PSQI (3.42±1.6, max=7) and BDI (5±4.74, max=16) scores. As mentioned above, the granger causality metric was used to evaluate the coupling between LC-BOLD fluctuation (used as a proxy of arousal modulation) and the temporal fluctuation of global network integration. LC BOLD fluctuations were individually extracted, after the two raters manually segmented LC masks allowing for a conservative and subject-specific average LC timeseries calculation. Global neural integration was quantified by GE calculated on OMST-reduced temporally resolved fMRI DFC networks. The Granger causality log-ratios and p-values were used to evaluate causal strength and significance as described in the MVGC toolbox. Delay parameters of 1 and 2 TRs were evaluated, higher values were deemed unrealistic for the mechanism under study given the relatively high TR value of our acquisitions (2.34s). For delay values of 1TR, 7/24 participants showed significant (Granger) causal relationships from LC->DFC-GE, indicated by Granger causality p-values<0.05. Spearman correlations were used for LC->DFC-GE coupling and behavior correlations, as both Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Anderson-Darling tests confirmed non-normally distributed data. LC->DFC-GE causality correlated with PSQI (ρ=0.41, p<0.0472) and with BDI scores (ρ=0.41, p<0.0471), Figure 2a. The same analysis was repeated using delay values of 2TRs (9/24 participants showed significant (Granger) causal relationships from LC->DFC-GE, Granger causality p-values<0.05). With this delay, LC->DFC-GE causality correlated with ISI (ρ=0.36, p<0.08; non-significant trend) and with BDI scores (ρ=0.52, p<0.0095), Figure 2b. Finally, it is important to mention that for the causality tests mentioned above, the opposite direction of causality (DFC-GE->LC) was not significant and did not correlate with the behavioral measures. 

Conclusion

Although the specifics of the underlying mechanisms still require further investigation, these results work towards delineating the causal temporal relationship between LC-mediated arousal fluctuations and global neural topology. The relationship between task-related LC (tonic) activity during wakefulness and REM sleep quality have been demonstrated in previous studies from our center5. We consider the current results as extensions of this relationship via the connection of LC’s activity during rest and subjective sleep quality. This could also be seen as further evidence on the broader effects of the LC-NA system, beyond exploitation-exploration regulation, decision making, attentional and information filtering, as a crucial hub, regulating the theorized1 domain-general arousal network as a configuration “coordinator”, complexly affecting the entire cortex and subcortex2. 

Our results support the hypothesis that the causal relationship of LC->DFC-GE is relevant for inter-individual variability in mood, such that higher depression scores were associated with higher LC->DFC-GE metrics. This finding indicates once more that the LC is potentially influencing more systems than merely those concerned in arousal and sleep-wake regulation. Notably, only a subset of participants (7 or 9 out of 24; depending on time delay) significantly follows the causal pattern of LC->DFC-GE (Granger causality p-values<0.05). This could potentially be evidence that the coupling of LC and global integration is only present, or at least strong enough to be measured, only in cases of increased trait or state arousal, lower/negative emotional affect overall and/or worse subjective sleep quality. This indicates the need for further investigation in cases with aberrant arousal or emotional profiles, either by trait or state manipulation. This would show whether the demonstrated relationships become stronger in clinical populations or the mechanism is more complex than indicated by the present results. Insomnia disorder clearly poses an excellent example for the latter, as sufferers are expected to have increased arousal, anxiety and diminished mood/higher negative affect. Furthermore, increasing the delay factor of the granger causality measure from 1 to 2 TRs seemingly strengthened the relationship between LC->DFC-GE and BDI scores (ρ=0.52 for delay=2 vs ρ=0.41 for delay=1) and also leaded to a significant LC->DFC-GE causal relationship in more individuals (9; delay=2 vs 7; delay=1). The increase in causal relationships and stronger association with mood when using higher delay, could be evidence of our approach’s ability to mainly quantify the slower, diffuse neuromodulatory, tonic effects of the LC-NA system, influenced by the NA release that operates in the scale of seconds, as opposed to the LC’s direct axonal projections which should be much quicker4. Lastly, only significant relationships in the LC->DFC-GE causal direction exist in our data and none towards the opposite direction both in terms of Granger causality significance and the correlations with behavioral data, strengthening our suggested mechanism and interpretation. 

Even though exploratory in nature and based on a relatively reduced sample size of healthy individuals, our results provide first evidence for a relationship between LC-mediated arousal, global brain organization and sleep- and mood-related metrics. They pave the way to further explore how spontaneous arousal fluctuations during rest causally influence dynamic brain-state organization in typical wakefulness. We believe that establishing this normative LC–connectivity relationship provides a foundation for investigating altered arousal–network coupling in insomnia and other clinical conditions. To further explore this, a larger sample is needed to confirm and solidify these results, as well as a more variant one in terms of mood and/or sleep disruptive states.
