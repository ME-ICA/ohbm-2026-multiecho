Introduction

Fine motor skills rely on widespread neural networks that support motor coordination and control, as well as, sensorimotor integration. Impairments of these fine motor skills have been noted across multiple mental health disorders1,2, yet the neural structures supporting these impairments across disorder states remain poorly understood, warranting comparative analysis. Grey matter volume provides an important index of structural brain integrity as it is essential for information processing3. Thus, it may clarify why similar behavioral impairments may result from distinct neural structures across different disorder states.

Methods

We compared fine motor skill performance across 529 participants: 170 with schizophrenia, 78 with current depression, 87 with remitted depression and 190 controls using the coin rotation task4. This task requires the participants to rotate a coin (the size is equivalent to a dime coin) through consecutive 180° turns using their thumb, index and middle finger for 30 seconds. If the coin was dropped, it was recorded and included in the final score calculation using the following formula: (rotations − [(coin drops × .10) × rotations])4. Differences in coin rotations across groups were assessed using linear regression, while differences in grey matter volume were examined using voxel-based morphometry in CAT12. Groups differed significantly in age and sex distribution. We specified a full-factorial model with Group (4-levels) and coin rotation performance (entered with group-specific slopes), controlling for age, sex, and total intracranial volume (cluster-level FWE-Corrected p<0.05). A grey matter mask and an absolute threshold of 0.2 was applied.

Results

Schizophrenia and remitted depression had worse fine motor skill abilities than controls, while current depression had trend-level differences. In contrast, no differences in fine motor skill abilities were observed between disorders. Controls demonstrated a widespread positive association between coin rotation performance and grey matter volume, encompassing both cortical and subcortical regions, indicating that better task performance corresponded to greater grey matter volume across a broad neural network. In contrast, the current depression group showed more restricted associations, primarily involving the ventral striatum, putamen, precuneus, and cerebellum. Whereas, Individuals with remitted depression and schizophrenia exhibited no significant associations between coin rotation performance and grey matter volume. The factorial ANOVA revealed a significant interaction effect localized to bilateral orbitofrontal cortex. Post hoc t-tests showed that current depression participants had stronger grey matter volume performance relationships than individuals with schizophrenia in the putamen and ventral striatum, whereas controls exhibited greater right middle frontal gyrus grey matter volume performance than the remitted depression group. Additionally, controls showed higher grey matter volume performance associations than schizophrenia in orbitofrontal cortex and the cerebellum.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrates that coin rotation performance relates to grey matter volume in distinct ways across controls and individuals with mental health disorders. The broad and robust associations observed in controls suggest that coin rotation performance reflects coordinated engagement of widespread cortical and subcortical networks. In contrast, current depression associations were centred on striatal and cerebellar regions, potentially reflecting disorder specific alterations in motor and reward-related circuitry. The absence of significant associations in both remitted depression and schizophrenia suggests functional disturbances might explain the observed behavioural impairments rather than grey matter volume differences. The group interaction analysis further highlight orbitofrontal, striatal, and cerebellar regions as key sites where diagnostic groups differ, underscoring their relevance in differentiating neurobiological signatures across disorders. Together, our results indicate that while enhanced coin rotation task performance engages a consistent set of neural systems in controls, psychiatric disorders show behavioural impairments that disrupt these systems. These findings elucidate how sensorimotor and visuospatial control functioning is altered in mental health disorders and may inform future work aimed at identifying biomarkers of disease state, remission, and chronicity5.
