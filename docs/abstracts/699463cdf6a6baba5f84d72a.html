Introduction

Brain age models estimate the difference between predicted and chronological age, offering a simple measure of accelerated or resilient brain aging.1 Since the concept was introduced, a wide range of machine-learning and deep-learning approaches have been developed to estimate brain age from neuroimaging data.2 Most work has focused on predicting brain age and linking the resulting brain age gap to cognition or the stage of cognitive impairment.3 Here, we test whether brain age can serve as an early, noninvasive marker for predicting cognitive decline and progression toward dementia. Our goals were to evaluate brain age as an early marker of cognitive decline, characterize longitudinal changes in brain age across cognitive status groups, and assess its predictive value, alone and together with other widely available measures, for multiple dementia-related outcomes. Because the brain age scores are derived from MRI, we specifically asked whether they provide additional predictive information beyond structural MRI features.

We evaluated these questions in Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (http://adni.loni.usc.edu) participants across three tasks: predicting conversion from cognitively normal (CN) to mild cognitive impairment (MCI)/dementia, estimating the rate of memory decline,4 and predicting amyloid-β levels measured by CSF and PET. We aimed to determine whether brain age offers added value beyond demographic, cognitive, and MRI measures, and when brain age differences first emerge relative to clinical symptoms.

Methods

We calculated global and regional brain age scores for Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) participants using the MRI-based BrainAGE framework developed by Kalc et al.,1 which derives gray- and white-matter tissue segments from T1-weighted MRI using CAT12 preprocessing5 and applies Gaussian process regression to predict brain age. The brain age score, defined as predicted minus chronological age, served as our main marker. We compared brain age trajectories across diagnostic groups and examined longitudinal differences in global and regional brain age scores. We also assessed when shifts in brain age could be detected relative to clinical or biomarker changes. Group differences were tested using multiple linear regression, with diagnostic group as the main predictor and age and APOE4 as covariates. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Predictive performance was evaluated using elastic-net models (Fig. 1): logistic regression for CN-to-MCI/dementia conversion prediction (192 stable-CN, 92 progressive-CN) and linear regression for predicting memory-decline rates (n = 1431) and amyloid-β levels (n = 1260 CSF-based; n = 896 PET-based) in individuals without dementia. Predictors included age, APOE4, cognitive scores (memory, executive function, language), MRI features (volumetric and cortical thickness measures), and brain age measures. Four models were compared: Basic (age, APOE4, cognitive measures), MRI (Basic + MRI features), GlobalBA (MRI + global brain age), and RegionalBA (GlobalBA + regional brain age scores). Model performance was estimated using nested, stratified cross-validation.

Results

For predicting progression to MCI/dementia in CN individuals, the Basic model reached an AUC of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.66–0.78). Adding MRI features increased performance to 0.76 (95% CI: 0.70–0.81). Including the global brain-age increased the AUC to 0.78 (95% CI: 0.72–0.83), while regional brain age scores provided no additional improvement. DeLong tests confirmed significant gains from the Basic to MRI and from the Basic to both brain age models, but not between MRI and brain age models. 

We then examined short-term (within 4 years) and long-term (after 4 years) progressors. Both groups showed the same pattern. For short-term progressors, AUCs were 0.82 (Basic), 0.83 (MRI), and 0.84 (GlobalBA/RegionalBA). For long-term progressors, AUCs were lower but improved significantly with brain age features (0.62 (Basic) → 0.66 (MRI) → 0.70 (GlobalBA/RegionalBA), indicating a clear benefit for long-term prediction (Fig. 2b).

To assess changes over time, we compared progressive-CN and stable-CN individuals at 0, 2, and 4 years before conversion. Progressive-CN individuals showed higher global brain age at all time points, with significant differences detectable four years prior (Fig. 2a). Brain age carried a predictive signal well before symptoms and increased more steeply over time. Regional results followed the same pattern, with the strongest effects in temporal regions.

For predicting memory-decline rates, adding global and regional brain ages improved performance beyond MRI (correlation increasing from R = 0.48 for the MRI model to R = 0.49 with GlobalBA and R = 0.50 with RegionalBA). In contrast, brain age did not improve the prediction of continuous amyloid-β levels beyond MRI (R = 0.54 CSF; R = 0.55 PET). Longitudinal analyses showed accelerated brain aging in individuals with high-rate memory decline (Fig. 2c) and in amyloid-positive individuals, with the clearest effects in CSF-defined groups.

Conclusion

Brain age scores detect subtle neurobiological vulnerability several years before clinical progression and improve the prediction of memory-decline trajectories. Although brain age reflects amyloid-related brain changes, it does not improve continuous amyloid-β prediction beyond structural MRI. Importantly, despite being derived from MRI, brain age scores provide complementary information that enhances early identification of cognitively normal individuals who later progress to MCI or dementia, particularly those with long presymptomatic periods. These findings support brain age as a scalable early marker for cognitive risk stratification, particularly for predicting clinical progression and the rate of cognitive decline.
