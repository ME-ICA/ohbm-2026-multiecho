Introduction

Resting state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is one of the most widely used techniques for non-invasive investigation of the brain’s functional organization. Functional relationships between brain regions are typically mapped using undirected, zero-lag temporal correlations of their resting state fMRI signals, a measure known as functional connectivity. This approach has provided valuable insights into brain organization in both health and disease. However, because of the way it is computed, functional connectivity cannot reveal the directionality of interactions between regions, limiting our understanding of brain communication. To overcome this limitation, here we implement and validate the use of a directed functional connectivity measure based on  Transfer Entropy (TE)1 to characterize the directional structure of resting state fMRI data in the mammalian brain. We validate this approach in the mouse, a physiologically accessible species in which high-resolution directed connectome data are available, and where direct manipulations of neural activity allow for a biologically grounded validation of this metric.

Methods

TE quantifies the directional flow of information between two signals. In this work, we adopted an implementation of TE for discrete time series applied to resting state fMRI data in lightly sedated (N = 58 wild-type, N = 20 Tsc2+/- 2) and awake (N = 26 sham, N = 14 basal forebrain-ablated) mice. All time series were preprocessed as described in3 and subsequently discretized into equally populated bins. Directed connectivity between any two regions/voxels was then estimated using TE with a lag of 2 repetition times. The statistical significance of the exchanged information was assessed non-parametrically by comparing empirical TE estimates against surrogate estimates generated via circular time-shifting. Finally, we derived a sender-to-receiver axis of brain organization by comparing the total amount of information sent and received by each region or voxel.

Results

Using TE, we revealed the presence of significant, spatially structured, and asymmetric information flow in the resting mouse brain, organized along a robust sender-to-receiver architecture that flexibly reconfigures across different brain states. In lightly sedated mice (representing a state of low arousal), cortical regions such as the retrosplenial, anterior cingulate, and somatosensory cortices predominantly act as information senders, whereas the motor cortex and insula serve as primary receivers (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, this functional organization does not trivially mirror the sender-to-receiver architecture of the underlying monosynaptic axonal connectome, suggesting a polysynaptic embedding of this sender-to-receiver axis (Spearman’s ρ = - 0.30, p = 0.09). Furthermore, we found that TE was altered between sensory and polymodal cortical regions of a mouse model of autism, in a pattern that was independent of functional connectivity strength (Fig. 1B), and in the absence of major white matter reorganization in this model2. Taken together, these findings indicate that TE captures polysynaptic interareal interactions beyond what undirected functional connectivity can measure.

We next asked whether this sender-to-receiver axis reflects a stable property of the mouse brain or whether it is modulated by higher arousal states. To address this question, we applied the same analytic pipeline to a cohort of awake mice that underwent resting state imaging following an extensive habituation protocol. Remarkably, in awake mice, regions involved in arousal, including the basal forebrain, emerged as prominent information senders, whereas the retrosplenial cortex shifted to acting as an information receiver (Fig. 1C). These findings indicate that the sender-to-receiver axis undergoes substantial reconfiguration across arousal states. To further corroborate the validity of our analytical framework, we genetically ablated one key information sender in the awake state, the basal forebrain, via genetic expression of diphtheria toxin. Target lesioning of basal forebrain neurons led to a significant change in the region’s functional role as an information sender, as evidenced by a significant direction-by-group interaction (Fig. 1D). These results suggest our metric is sensitive to changes in local brain activity and brain state, and it is not a simple reflection of underlying anatomy.

Conclusion

Our study advances our understanding of the intrinsic organization of resting state fMRI networks by characterizing the directed exchange of information between brain regions in the mammalian brain. Our analytical framework revealed the existence of a flexible sender-to-receiver axis along which regions are organized as a function of brain state. Specifically, cortical regions within the mouse default mode network emerge as senders of information under light sedation, whereas arousal related areas become prominent senders in the awake state. Importantly, applying this framework to mouse data enabled us to empirically validate the ability of our directed connectivity metric to detect changes in neuronal activity though direct causal manipulations. This approach could be extended to human fMRI data, where identifying upstream hubs of altered connectivity may help uncover drivers of widespread network disruptions.
