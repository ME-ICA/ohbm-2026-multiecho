Introduction

When individuals share experiences or jointly attend to stimuli, their brains often show synchronized activity.1,2 This inter-brain synchrony reflects alignment in attention, interpretation, and mental state, facilitating communication and social learning. For example, friends tend to exhibit similar neural responses while watching the same narrative compared to strangers, and this neural similarity declines with increasing social distance.3 This suggests that social closeness is mirrored in neural alignment. However, it remains unclear whether such synchrony emerges naturally or can be actively shaped through shared learning or feedback.

Researchers commonly use the naturalistic fMRI paradigm, where participants watch or listen to a continuous stimuli such as movies or stories, to examine how brain regions respond synchronously across individuals. Hasson et al., (2004)1 showed widespread cortical synchrony when participants watched the same movie, extending beyond visual and sensory regions. Intersubject correlation (ISC) analysis detects these shared patterns without relying on predefined models as in task-based GLM approaches,2 especially in higher-order regions like the DMN. Large-scale datasets such as The Narratives collection4 further demonstrate the reliability of ISC for modeling narrative comprehension and shared cognitive processes.

In this study, we asked whether a shared learning experience can enhance intersubject neural synchrony. Participants underwent fMRI scans before and after completing a 12-week skill-learning program (Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, BJJ), during which they watched one short video related to the learned skill and two unrelated control videos. ISC was used to measure brain synchrony across participants in each session and condition. We found that synchrony was modulated after learning, specifically for the video related to the learned skill.

Methods

PARTICIPANTS

A total of 150 subjects (mean age = 30.9 years, SD = 8.3, range = 18-53) participated in a 12-week learning program, during which they were scanned before and after learning a new skill (BJJ). All participants provided informed consent before participation. 

EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

Each participant underwent two fMRI sessions, one before and one after a ~12-week training program (“pre” and “post”; mean interval = 15.2 weeks). In each session, participants were scanned on a 3T Siemens Prisma MRI scanner while viewing three separate naturalistic videos (158 seconds each): one video congruent with their training skill (BJJ) and two incongruent control videos (climbing and music). Whole-brain BOLD fMRI data were acquired using a multiband gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR = 750 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 mm³, nreps = 211). Here, we chose to compare the congruent video (BJJ) to one of the incongruent videos (climbing). After preprocessing and filtering, 85 subjects that had adequate data (preprocessed functional MRI data for both pre- and post-training sessions, including the two naturalistic videos of interest) were kept for this analysis. Others were excluded due to a lack of a second scan, technical scan errors (e.g., missing videos or both videos shown in one session), or failed quality control measures at various stages of preprocessing due to motion or other data artifacts. As more participants will complete their second scan, more data will be used for this analysis. 

PRE-PROCESSING OF THE FMRI DATA

Functional MRI data  were preprocessed using fMRIPrep 25.0.0,5 which performs a minimal preprocessing pipeline including head motion correction, slice timing correction, susceptibility distortion correction, normalization to standard MNI space, skull-stripping and brain segmentation, and co-registration of functional and anatomical images.  

After fMRIPrep, further processing steps were applied: spatial smoothing with a 5 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel, followed by denoising; six motion parameters (three translations, three rotations), the first aCompCor component from white-matter and CSF signals, second-order detrending polynomials,6 and lastly cosine bases for high-pass filtering (using a discrete cosine filter with cutoff: 100s or 0.001Hz).4 The resulting residual BOLD time series were then parcellated using the Schaefer 2018 & Tian 2020 combined atlas (400 cortical + 54 subcortical ROIs, 17-network parcellation)7,8 and resampled to the functional BOLD grid.

ROI-wise time series were extracted for subjects in the four datasets; pre- and post-learning scans for each of the congruent and incongruent conditions. Each ROI was z-scored across time, and the resulting data were stacked across subjects to yield 3D arrays of shape (TR × ROI × subjects)

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

To test whether learning (pre → post) induced a significant change in ISC at the level of individual ROIs, a leave-one-out ISC analysis was conducted within-group for each ROI {r} across subjects {i}, for each of the pre- and post-learning functional runs. The resulting ISC r-values across subjects were Fisher’s z-transformed in each ROI to stabilize variance and approximate normality. Then, a paired samples t-test (two-tailed) was conducted, comparing the post- vs. pre-learning z-value deltas across all subjects. The resulting p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons across the full set of ROIs using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure at α < 0.05. ROIs with FDR-corrected p < 0.05 were considered significant in ISC change (either increase or decrease) following training.

Results

The brain activity of participants watching the same video was found to be more synchronized in the congruent (bjj-bjj) condition than in the incongruent (bjj-climbing) condition. In the congruent condition, ISC increased between the pre- and post-sessions in 30 ROIs out of the 91 that were found significant under a paired two-sample, two-tailed t-test (FDR corrected for multiple comparisons). In contrast, in the incongruent condition, ISC increased significantly only in eight ROIs (out of 53 that were found significant). Looking into parcellated networks reveals that in the congruent condition, most of the significantly increased ROIs were found in the Dorsal Attention A and B networks (five out of 27 and 12 out of 25, respectively), while in the incongruent condition, no ROIs were significantly increased within these networks. In both conditions, the majority of ROIs with decreased ISC were found in visual-related areas, with 24 and 16 out of 47 ROIs significantly decreasing in the congruent and incongruent conditions respectively, while none of these ROIs significantly increased. It appears that in the congruent condition (Panel A), the within-group ISC tends to be more synchronized compared to the incongruent condition (Panel B).

Conclusion

Our results show that a shared learning experience can enhance inter-subject neural synchrony: after a 12-week training program, participants exhibited increased ISC, particularly in dorsal and ventral attention networks and other control-related systems, when watching a video congruent with their learned skill. This suggests that joint learning aligns participants’ neural processing of content relevant to their training, potentially via common attentional strategies or cognitive schemas, reflecting increased shared engagement.

Conversely, we observed a consistent decrease in ISC across visual cortex ROIs, regardless of whether the video was skill-related or not. This pattern may reflect habituation or neural adaptation to the repeated visual stimulus, as recently shown in a fMRI study on intersubject synchrony.9 As participants become familiar with the visual input, idiosyncratic processing in low-level areas may increase relative to the shared novelty in the first session, reducing across-subject correlation. These findings not only support our paradigm and analysis, but also strengthen the claim that the increases we observed in higher-order areas are not global artifacts but rather relate to mutual learned processing.

Overall, these results support the validity of using repeated naturalistic fMRI viewing in longitudinal designs and highlight that learning-related neural alignment may emerge at the level of cognitive control and attention alongside basic sensory processing. For future research, it will be valuable to test how such training-induced synchrony alters across different levels of familiarity with the relevant skill, and to examine how stable these synchrony changes are over time and across different kinds of stimuli and learning domains.
