Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke management relies on neuroimaging to guide treatment decisions1,2. Dynamic susceptibility contrast (DSC) perfusion-weighted imaging (PWI) is commonly used to assess penumbral tissue but adds complexity with contrast injection and increased scan time3,4. This study introduces a deep learning (DL) model to generate synthetic T-max perfusion maps from routine non-contrast MRI sequences, such as diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR). By eliminating the need for contrast agents and reducing acquisition time, this method could streamline acute stroke imaging, accelerate decision-making, and increase access to critical perfusion data. We hypothesized that DWI and FLAIR contain sufficient information to reliably infer perfusion patterns, offering a non-invasive, faster alternative to traditional PWI in acute stroke.

Methods

We analyzed MRI data from 355 acute ischemic stroke patients across two European centers. The dataset included DWI, FLAIR, and DSC-PWI sequences. We trained and validated six denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) and a generative adversarial network5 (GAN) to predict T-max maps using only DWI and FLAIR inputs. The models were optimized across 2D, 2.5D, and 3D architectures, incorporating binary infarct core masks to improve spatial accuracy. Training was conducted using 80% of the primary dataset, with internal validation on the remaining 20% and external validation on an out-of-sample independent dataset. Model performance was assessed using image similarity metrics (SSIM, PSNR, PCC) and segmentation overlap (Dice). Additionally, mismatch volumes (T-max > 6s) were compared between synthetic and ground truth maps. The model was further evaluated to assess the contribution of each input modality.

Results

The best-performing model, a 2.5D DDPM with modified loss function and infarct core mask (2.5D+P+C), achieved high accuracy in both internal (Dice = 0.82) and external validation (Dice = 0.60). Image similarity metrics showed SSIM (Structural similarity index measure) of 0.82, PSNR (Peak signal-to-noise ratio) of 20.96, and a MAE (mean absolute error) of 0.032 for internal validation. External validation demonstrated strong volumetric correlation for T-max > 6s regions, with a Dice coefficient of 0.60. The model generated synthetic T-max maps in under 110 seconds, confirming its potential for real-time clinical use. Further analysis revealed that excluding the infarct core mask resulted in a significant performance drop, highlighting its importance. The model also effectively captured perfusion-diffusion mismatch patterns, offering clinical relevance for decision-making in stroke treatment.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates the feasibility of generating synthetic T-max perfusion maps from non-contrast DWI and FLAIR sequences using deep learning. This approach could augment traditional DSC-PWI, reducing scan time, minimizing contrast agent use, and facilitating faster decision-making in acute stroke care. The strong generalizability of the model across diverse patient cohorts suggests that synthetic perfusion maps could be implemented in clinical workflows with minimal adjustments, potentially improving access to critical imaging information and enhancing patient outcomes. Further validation in multi-center studies6 and real-world settings will refine this approach.
