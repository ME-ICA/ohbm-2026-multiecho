Introduction

Effectively interrupting ongoing actions is essential for preventing maladaptive behaviour, and emotional states exert a profound influence on cognitive control and action regulation. Action inhibition supports flexible behaviour in dynamic environments and constitutes a core component of executive control. 

The emotional Stop-Signal Task (SST) provides a well-established experimental framework to estimate the time required to cancel a motor response under emotionally challenging conditions. Successful stopping relies on coordinated interactions between regions comprising the inhibition network1, whereas the affective component has yet to be investigated.

While these mechanisms are commonly examined during active task performance using task-based fMRI, they also exhibit structured patterns of intrinsic coupling at rest. Resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) has therefore emerged as a prominent tool for characterizing functional networks and relating individual differences in behaviour to intrinsic brain organization, under the assumption that baseline connectivity captures stable properties that constrain task-related dynamics. Against this backdrop, an open question is whether intrinsic functional connectivity between control and emotion-related networks predicts individual differences in emotionally modulated inhibitory control. This is theoretically consequential for understanding the relationship between trait-like brain organization and state-dependent executive processes. Moreover, inhibitory control in emotional contexts is especially relevant given the inherently social nature of human behaviour, which requires continuous regulation of actions and emotions in response to affective cues from others.

Examining this link provides a principled test of the extent to which resting-state connectivity can inform models of emotionally guided action control, while also critically evaluating the scope and limits of rsfMRI for predicting complex, state-sensitive behaviour.

Methods

Participants and procedure
Thirty healthy participants (14 females; mean age = 25.2 ± 4 years) underwent resting-state fMRI at IRCCS San Camillo Hospital (Venice, Italy) using a 3T Philips Ingenia scanner with a 32-channel head coil. Structural images were acquired with a 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (TR = 8.1 ms, TE = 3.7 ms, flip angle = 8°, 1 mm isotropic). Approximately 10 minutes of resting-state functional images were collected using a T2*-weighted multiband EPI sequence (TR = 1000 ms, TE = 23 ms, flip angle = 50°, MB = 4, 3 mm isotropic), along with diffusion images for distortion correction.

Prior to MRI acquisition, participants completed an emotional Stop-Signal Task (SST, Figure 1), a validated paradigm for assessing response inhibition indexed by the stop-signal reaction time (SSRT)2. The task was implemented in jsPsych (v6.1.0) and administered online on laptop or desktop computers. Following a brief practice session (32 trials), participants completed four blocks of 128 trials each (75% Go, 25% Stop). Go trials required speeded left/right key presses in response to arrow stimuli. On Stop trials, a neutral or fearful facial stimulus (Ekman set) appeared 70 ms after a variable stop-signal delay (SSD), instructing participants to withhold their response. The SSD was initialized at 150 ms and dynamically adjusted using a staircase procedure (±50 ms; range 50–650 ms) to achieve approximately 50% successful inhibition, consistent with current consensus recommendations3.

fMRI preprocessing and connectivity analysis
Data were analyzed using CONN4 (v22.v2407) and SPM (v25.25.01.02). Preprocessing included realignment with susceptibility distortion correction, outlier detection, segmentation-based normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing (6 mm FWHM). Denoising regressed out white matter and CSF signals, session effects and first derivatives, and linear trends, followed by band-pass filtering (0.008-0.09 Hz). The effective degrees of freedom after denoising averaged 96.1 across subjects.

ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity matrices were estimated at the first level using Fisher-transformed bivariate correlations from a weighted GLM between 17 HPC-ICA networks and Harvard-Oxford atlas ROIs belonging to the abovementioned inhibition network1. Scan weighting accounted for transient magnetization effects.
Second-level analyses used a random-effects GLM. ROI-to-ROI hypotheses were tested using multivariate parametric statistics with sample covariance estimation. Connectivity results were thresholded using a cluster-forming threshold of p < .001 and cluster-level FDR correction at p < .05.

To examine whether individual differences in inhibitory control were associated with resting-state functional connectivity, behavioural performance was quantified as the difference between fearful and neutral trials (ΔSSRT = SSRT_fearful − SSRT_neutral). ΔSSRT values were entered as a continuous second-level covariate in the group-level GLM, corresponding to a between-subject regression analysis in which connectivity estimates for each ROI pair were regressed onto the behavioural measure. This analysis tested whether interindividual variability in resting-state connectivity covaried linearly with behavioural performance, over and above the group mean connectivity effect. Multiple comparisons were controlled using FDR correction at the connection level (p < .05).

Results

Behavioural results

Behavioural performance on the SST was consistent with established benchmarks3. Participants responded accurately on Go trials (mean Go accuracy = 98.57 ± 1.61%), with low rates of choice errors and omissions, indicating adequate task engagement. Mean SSRTs were 228.78 ± 30.63 ms for emotional stop trials and 233.51 ± 25.65 ms for neutral stop trials. A paired-samples comparison revealed no significant difference in SSRT between emotional and neutral conditions (p = .29), indicating that, at the group level, emotional content did not reliably modulate inhibitory latency.

Brain-behaviour association

Group-level resting-state connectivity analyses revealed the expected pattern of intrinsic coupling among regions comprising the inhibition network (Image 2; cluster-level FDR correction at p < .05). However, no connections showed a statistically significant association with individual differences in emotionally modulated inhibitory control (ΔSSRT) after correction for multiple comparisons at the connection level (FDR, p < .05).

At an uncorrected level, two ROI-to-ROI connections exhibited positive associations with ΔSSRT (Anterior Cingulate Cortex - left Posterior Parietal Cortex: T(28) = 2.44, p uncorrected = .021; Anterior Cingulate Cortex - right Posterior Parietal Cortex: T(28) = 2.17, p uncorrected = .038). These effects did not survive FDR correction (both p FDR-corrected = .975) and were therefore not considered statistically significant.

No additional connections showed evidence of a linear relationship between resting-state functional connectivity and ΔSSRT, indicating that interindividual variability in baseline connectivity within the inhibition network did not reliably predict the degree to which emotional context modulated stopping performance.

Conclusion

In this study, we could not predict individual differences in emotionally modulated inhibitory control from intrinsic FC between control and emotion-related networks. 

Behavioural performance on the SST was consistent with established methodological benchmarks3, despite the absence of a mean difference between fearful and neutral conditions. This pattern suggests that emotional modulation of stopping was not expressed as a uniform shift in inhibitory latency, but rather as interindividual variability around a stable group mean.

A null association between rsFC and ΔSSRT (fearful - neutral) is not simply “uninformative”; it speaks to a theoretical mismatch that increasingly defines the limits of resting-state brain-behaviour mapping. Resting-state connectivity is an aggregate description of intrinsic, low-frequency coupling under unconstrained cognition, whereas ΔSSRT indexes a context-sensitive, phasic control operation that emerges from how inhibitory control reconfigures under affective salience. The key implication is that rsFC may be a necessary background condition for performance (i.e., it scaffolds possible network states) but is often an insufficient predictor of the specific, stimulus-locked control computations that determine stop performance under emotion.

Resting state FC is shaped by multiple latent processes, many of which vary within and between individuals and are only loosely related to “inhibitory control”. This means rsFC is not process-pure with respect to stopping. In contrast, ΔSSRT is a difference score meant to isolate the modulatory impact of emotion on stopping, which likely depends on dynamic gating and rapid network reconfiguration (e.g., salience/limbic to control network interactions) rather than on baseline coupling alone. Thus, the null result can be interpreted as a suggestion that emotionally modulated inhibition is not well-parameterised as a static connectome trait; at least not at the edge-wise resolution tested here. 

This aligns with emerging work suggesting that, for many cognitive phenotypes, task-evoked or task-constrained measures often carry more behaviourally relevant variance than rest, especially when the phenotype is inherently contingent on stimulus processing and control engagement (i.e., “doing” rather than “being”)5.

More broadly, these results contribute to ongoing debates regarding the scope of rsfMRI for predicting complex, context-sensitive behaviour. Rather than undermining the relevance of resting-state approaches, they underscore the importance of aligning neural measures with the temporal and computational properties of the behavioural construct of interest. Emotionally modulated inhibitory control may be better captured by task-evoked activation, dynamic connectivity, or rest-to-task reconfiguration metrics than by static resting-state coupling alone. As such, null findings play a critical role in refining theoretical expectations and methodological strategies for linking intrinsic brain organisation to behaviour.

Some limitations may hinder the interpretability of results. Statistically, considering the many rsFC edges tested against a noisy individual-difference regressor, power is limited and estimates are expected to be unstable at N=29. A constructive implication is that future work may benefit from (i) explicitly modelling measurement error (e.g., reliability-informed attenuation bounds), (ii) equivalence or Bayesian interval testing to quantify evidence for effects smaller than a theoretically meaningful threshold, and (iii) predictive, cross-validated multivariate approaches that reduce dimensionality and prioritise out-of-sample generalisation over edge-wise inference, while recognising that improved reliability does not guarantee construct validity.

In this framing, a null finding may reflect not the absence of a neurobiological relation, but a limited upper bound on observable correlations also given measurement noise in both rsFC and ΔSSRT.
