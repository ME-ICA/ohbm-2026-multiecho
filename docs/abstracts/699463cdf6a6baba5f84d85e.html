Introduction

Rasmussen’s encephalitis (RE) is a rare autoimmune disease presenting with focal epilepsy and progressive unilateral cerebral atrophy with concurrent neurologic dysfunction. Volumetric MRI analysis, particularly the use of hemispheric ratios, is an widely-used marker in both diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression, complementing clinical parameters.1 In addition, shape-based imaging markers are being used increasingly alongside volumetric measures to capture subtle morphometric changes in brain structures that may occur even when overall volume remains unchanged.2 The approach of complementing volume asymmetry with shape asymmetry of the mesiotemporal structures has already proven valuable in the diagnostic evaluation of autoimmune limbic encephalitis.3 We hypothesize that shape asymmetry will be more sensitive in detecting brain asymmetry indicative of an early unihemispheric disease process in Rasmussen’s encephalitis, and may serve as a complementary biomarker to volume asymmetry for monitoring disease progression.

Methods

We included 50 RE patients (age at MRI median 16 years, range 2-61; 33 female; 29 left-lesional) who were treated at our hospital and had at least two T1-weighted MRI scans available. MRI scans were performed on various 1.5/3T scanners as part of the diagnostic process. We further included 282 healthy control subjects (age at MRI median 28 years, range 1-74; 142 female) from an in-house research database with one T1w-MRI scan each. For RE patients, the first performed MRI scan took place 58.10 ± 90.11 months (range 0-514) after RE onset, for the last scan 120.35 ± 108.29 months (range 2-476).

For each T1-weighted scan, cortical surface reconstruction and segmentation of subcortical structures was performed using FreeSurfer (version 6.0). Manual edits were made as required. Based on the FreeSurfer output, the BrainPrint toolbox (https://github.com/Deep-MI/BrainPrint) was used to extract the volume and to compute a spectral shape descriptor based on the first 50 non-zero eigenvalues of the Laplace–Beltrami operator for 13 brain structures (including cortex, cerebellum, subcortical structures and lateral ventricles). Volume asymmetry was calculated as the left-right difference of a structure’s volume divided by the sum of the left and right volume. Shape asymmetry was calculated as the Euclidean distance between the left and right eigenvalue vectors.

For shape and volume asymmetry, group differences (first scan vs. last scan vs. controls) were tested using one-way ANOVAs and post-hoc Tukey-Kramer tests, and value differences of the 2 metrics were tested using paired t-tests for individual z-scores. Effect sizes for deviations of early/late scans from controls expressed as Glass’ delta. We consider differences with p-values smaller than 0.05 (p < 0.05) to be significant.

Results

Overall, both shape and volume asymmetry measures were significantly elevated in patients compared to controls across all structures except for the cerebellum (p < 0.05) in early and late scans. The most pronounced effects (Glass' delta > 7.5) were observed for the pial surface, cerebral white matter surface, and putamen for both metrics. When comparing the last versus the first scan, a significant increase of asymmetry (p < 0.05) in both metrics was found for the caudate nucleus, hippocampus, and pial surface. Shape asymmetry significantly increased in the cerebellar cortex, while volume asymmetry significantly increased in the lateral ventricle and thalamus. The effect sizes of the cerebellar white matter (p = 0.002 for the first scan; p < 0.001 for the last scan), putamen (< 0.001; 0.001), pallidum (0.002; 0.001), amygdala (0.016; 0.034) and white matter surface (< 0.001; 0.007) were significantly higher for shape asymmetry than their volume asymmetry counterparts, consistently across both the first and last scans. The nucleus accumbens also showed significantly higher effect size for shape asymmetry, however only for the first scan (p = 0.023)

Conclusion

Our results indicate potential for combining shape and volume asymmetry markers for diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression in Rasmussen’s encephalitis. Future work will focus on identifying the most sensitive markers for early diagnosis, as well as determining the most effective combination of markers for disease staging.
