Introduction

Functional lateralization is a fundamental organizing principle of the human brain, supporting specialized cognitive processes ranging from motor control to language and attention. While inter-hemispheric functional connectivity is often characterized by strong homotopic correlations, the relationship between left and right hemisphere (LH/RH) activation patterns is governed by complex, non-linear dependencies that extend beyond simple anatomical symmetry. Traditional approaches to modeling this relationship typically rely on linear regression or mirror-identity assumptions (i.e., assuming the RH is a direct flip of the LH). However, these methods fail to capture the subtle, non-local, and asymmetric functional topography inherent to high-order association cortices, limiting our ability to predict functional activation or understand the rules of brain organization.

Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence, particularly Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs), offer a powerful framework for modeling complex biological distributions. Unlike discriminative models that focus on boundary decision, generative models learn the underlying data manifold, allowing for high-fidelity reconstruction of structural patterns. Here, we propose a novel approach using a conditional Conditional Diffusion Probabilistic Model parametrized by a Vision Transformer (U-ViT) to predict RH task-fMRI activation maps solely from LH inputs. By leveraging a U-ViT backbone trained on a large-scale adolescent cohort (IMAGEN), we aim to test the hypothesis that a generative framework can disentangle subject-specific functional topography from group-level priors, capturing non-linear inter-hemispheric mapping rules that linear and mirror baselines miss. This study evaluates the model's structural fidelity, individual specificity, and robustness across the cortical hierarchy.

Methods

Participants and Data Acquisition

We utilized fMRI data from the IMAGEN project, a multi-site study of adolescents.1 Participants performed a modified Monetary Incentive Delay (MID) task involving reward anticipation and motor response. Data were acquired on 3T scanners using EPI sequences (TR=2200ms, TE=30ms) and preprocessed using fMRIPrep (v20.2.3), including motion correction, susceptibility distortion correction, and normalization to MNI152 standard space. First-level General Linear Model (GLM) analyses were conducted to generate 3D beta activation maps for specific task conditions (e.g., successful big-win trials with left-hand response). A total of 3,800 activation maps (derived from baseline and follow-up sessions) were curated and split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets.

UViT Model Architecture

To predict right-hemisphere (RH) functional topography from left-hemisphere (LH) inputs, we implemented a conditional Diffusion Probabilistic Model based on a Variance Preserving Stochastic Differential Equation (VPSDE) framework. The backbone network employed a U-shaped Vision Transformer (U-ViT), which integrates the long-range dependency modeling of Transformers with the spatial locality of U-Net skip connections.2 The model takes a 3D volume (32x64x64 voxels) as input, utilizing a patch size of 8^3. The architecture consisted of a depth of 10 transformer layers, an embedding dimension of 512, and 8 attention heads, with long skip connections between encoder and decoder blocks to preserve high-resolution spatial details.

Training Protocol

Inputs were Z-score normalized per hemisphere to standardize amplitude distributions while preserving spatial patterns. The model was conditioned on the contralateral hemisphere (LH) to guide the denoising process of the target hemisphere (RH). Training was optimized using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1x10^-4 and a cosine annealing warmup scheduler. The model was trained for 1000 epochs with a batch size of 64. To enable classifier-free guidance during inference, unconditional training was performed with a probability of p=0.05.

Inference and Evaluation

During inference, RH activation maps were generated from Gaussian noise conditioned on the unseen test subject's LH map using the DPM-Solver (50 steps) for efficient sampling. Performance was evaluated against: (1) Voxel-wise Linear Regression (trained on the same split), and (2) Mirror-Identity (Right = Left_flipped). Evaluation metrics included spatial Pearson correlation , Dice coefficients (overlap accuracy at top 1-20% thresholds), and fingerprinting identification rates.

Results

Global Prediction Accuracy and Baseline Comparison

We evaluated the model's performance on a large-scale independent test set (N=1,260). As illustrated in the global prediction accuracy analysis (Fig. 2e), the proposed Diffusion Model achieved the highest reconstruction fidelity with a mean spatial correlation of r=0.52. This performance significantly outperformed both the Voxel-wise Linear Regression baseline (r=0.36, p<0.001) and the Mirror-Identity baseline (r=0.44, p<0.001). The substantial improvement over the Mirror baseline (+18%) confirms that the generative model successfully captures non-linear inter-hemispheric dependencies that extend beyond simple anatomical symmetry.

Individual Specificity and Connectome Fingerprinting

To assess the preservation of subject-specific features, we performed a connectome fingerprinting analysis (Fig. 2a-d). The Diffusion Model demonstrated superior identifiability, achieving a near-perfect Identification Rate (ID Rate) of 99.52%, compared to 99.05% for the Mirror baseline and 94.29% for Linear Regression.

Crucially, the model improved the separation between subject-specific signals and group-level noise. As shown in the "Self vs. Others" fingerprinting plot (Fig. 2d), the Diffusion Model clusters distinctly above the baselines. Quantitative analysis of the Specificity Index (I_diff), defined as the difference between mean intra-subject (r_self) and inter-subject (r_others) correlations, revealed a progressive improvement: Linear (31.39) < Mirror (40.52) < Diffusion (44.89). While the generative process slightly increased group-level commonality (Mean r_others increased from 0.035 in Mirror to 0.072 in Diffusion), it disproportionately enhanced subject-specific reconstruction (Mean r_self increased from 0.441 to 0.521). This indicates that the diffusion model does not merely generate generic features but effectively disentangles individual functional topography from group priors.

Network-Level Fidelity

We further dissected performance across functional hierarchies using the Schaefer-400 parcellation (Fig. 2f). The Diffusion Model significantly outperformed both baselines across all seven functional networks. Interestingly, the performance profile across networks was consistent among the three models, indicating that the intrinsic predictability of functional topology is driven by underlying network properties. Within this shared trend, the Diffusion Model consistently maintained the highest fidelity, achieving its peak performance in the Default Mode Network (DMN). This demonstrates that the generative model provides a robust reconstruction advantage across the entire cortical hierarchy, effectively enhancing prediction accuracy in both sensorimotor and high-order association cortices.

Spatial Overlap and Robustness

Finally, we evaluated the spatial accuracy of activation foci using Dice coefficients across varying activation thresholds (top 1% to 20% of voxels). As shown in Fig. 2g-h, the Diffusion Model consistently yielded significantly higher Dice scores compared to both baselines at every threshold level. This superiority held true for both peak focal activations (Top 1%) and broader network topologies (Top 20%), demonstrating the model's robustness in localizing functional hubs regardless of the thresholding strategy employed.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that the functional topography of the human brain's right hemisphere can be predicted from the left hemisphere using generative artificial intelligence, significantly outperforming traditional linear and mirror-symmetry assumptions. The success of the Diffusion Model highlights three critical insights into brain organization and neuroimaging methodology.

First, the dissociation between the model's superior fidelity (spatial correlation and Dice scores) and the baseline's amplitude preservation underscores the utility of diffusion models in capturing functional topology. While simple mirror-flips preserve absolute amplitude, they fail to reconstruct the precise spatial layout of activation. In contrast, our diffusion model, despite inherent normalization constraints, successfully reconstructed the fine-grained "texture" of individual activation maps. This suggests that the rules governing inter-hemispheric transfer are fundamentally non-linear and cannot be captured by voxel-to-voxel linear mapping.

Second, the network-specific performance profile reveals that our model provides the greatest value in highly lateralized systems. The observation that the diffusion model achieved its peak performance and largest gain over baselines in the DMN indicates that it effectively learned the complex, asymmetric organization of high-order association cortices. This validates the model not merely as a denoising tool, but as a biophysically plausible model of functional lateralization.

Finally, the near-perfect identification rate (99.52%) in activation-based fingerprinting confirms that the generated maps preserve individual specificity. The model does not simply hallucinate a "group-average" brain; it synthesizes a subject-specific functional landscape that enhances the signal-to-noise ratio of individual features. This capability establishes conditional diffusion models as a powerful tool for investigating brain asymmetry. Clinically, this framework holds promise for "virtual brain" applications, such as predicting pre-morbid functional organization in patients with unilateral lesions (e.g., stroke), thereby offering a personalized baseline for rehabilitation and recovery assessment.
