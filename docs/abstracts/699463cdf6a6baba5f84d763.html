Introduction

The conjoint study of the structure and the function of the connectome is one of the essential goals to map the human brain. However, one main challenge remains: the connectome is difficult to study at the individual level. It is often studied at the group level by averaging multiple individual connectomes, providing useful information about the shared connections present in a population but not about the specificity of such connections in a particular individual. This issue is so closely linked to the choice of the parcellation - which determines the regions to be connected - that finding a suitable individualized parcellation has been deemed an “outstanding question” in order to link structure and function of brain networks1. In this study, we investigated the influence of various parcellation schemes on measures of structure-function coupling of 1000 connectomes of the Human Connectome Project dataset.

Methods

To do so, we computed multiscale subdivisions of the well-known Desikan atlas defined on an HCP group of 200 subjects by a structural connectivity-based clustering algorithm (Constellation2) using FSL and MRtrix tractographies with SIFT2 filtering. The group subdivisions were adapted to the individual tractographies by labelling each vertex of the individual cortical mesh according to the region in the group subdivisions with the closest connectivity profile3. Therefore, we obtained multiscale individual parcellations representative of the individual white-matter organization.

To select the adequate granularity level of these individual parcellations, we first built a multidimensional phenotype representative of the grey-matter microstructure of the cortex using 4 structural MRI metrics obtained with Freesufer and 4 diffusion MRI metrics. Structural MRI metrics consisted in cortical thickness, cortical thickness corrected by curvature with a Brainvisa algorithm based on an equivolumic principle, T1/T2 ratio and volume of grey-matter. Diffusion MRI metrics consisted in intracortical fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity, neurite density index and orientation dispersion index. To account for the shared variability between these phenotypes, we applied a principal component analysis at the individual level which reduced dimensionality of the multimodal phenotype from 8 to 6 for all subjects. This multimodal phenotype was then used to select the best number of parcels in each Desikan atlas region by finding the parcel configuration that maximizes pairwise contrast of parcels4. This pairwise contrast was computed by estimating the probability density function of the multimodal phenotype in each parcel followed by an estimation of the overlap of such density functions. The configuration of parcels that minimizes average overlap over the subjects of the database was then selected after bootstrapping, resulting in 2 sets of whole-brain individual parcellations adapted to individual tractographies - FSL and MRtrix - and with matching individual parcels across subjects. 

Interestingly, we obtained almost the same number of parcels based on FSL (399) and MRtrix (400) tractographies, which motivated us to compare our parcellations with state-of-the-art atlases of comparable granularity defined on other modalities (Glasser and Shaefer 400) and 100 randomly-generated spatially homogeneous parcellations (voronoi diagrams with 400 parcels). This comparison was done by computing structure-function coupling measures of the individual connectomes. Because the definition of the structural connectome is not straightforward and may differ according to the tractography algorithm, we studied 3 different structural connectivity measures previously used in the literature: number of fibers, fiber density and profile similarity. Number of fibers is the number of fibers connecting two regions divided by the total number of fibers of the individual tractography, fiber density is the proportion of fibers connecting two regions relative to the total number of fibers passing through each region, and profile similarity is the correlation of connectivity profiles of two regions. For all parcellations and at the individual level, we correlated the structural connectomes with functional connectomes obtained from resting state fMRI of the HCP dataset (preprocessed with ICA-FIX) and microstructural connectomes obtained by applying a recently published structural similarity score (MIND5) on the multidimensional phenotype previously used. Hence, for all parcellations schemes, we obtained intersubject distributions of correlations between structural connectomes and functional connectomes (SC-FC), correlations between structural connectomes and microstructural connectomes (SC-Struct) and correlations between functional connectomes and microstructural connectomes (FC-Struct).

Results

Figure 1 shows the results of the SC-FC correlations for 955 subjects of the HCP dataset when the structural connectomes are derived from MRtrix tractographies and with different structural connectivity definitions. Overall, SC-FC is highest when structural connectivity is defined as fiber density, attaining a maximum of 0.21 for the individual MRtrix-based Constellation connectomes. These connectomes present a mean increase of 8.2% compared to the ones defined with the group parcellation. Similarly, these results persist when defining connectomes with profile similarity: the mean SC-FC correlation of the individual parcellations-based connectomes is 0.19, representing a mean increase of 17.8% compared to the group parcellation-based ones. All other parcellations performed the same as the random parcellations based on voronoi diagrams, but it is worth noting that the widely used Schaefer 400 atlas performs a lot worse than the other ones when using profile similarity with 0.08 SC-FC correlation. Finally, connectomes based on the number of fibers generally performed worse than the others.

    Analogously, Figure 2 shows the results of the SC-Struct correlations. These results are similar to the results on SC-FC correlations with individual parcellation-based connectomes defined with fiber density performing the best at 0.23 mean correlation, which represents a mean increase of 9.0% compared to the group parcellation-based connectomes. When using profile similarity, the mean SC-Struct correlation diminishes to 0.20 but this represents 20.8% augmentation compared to the group parcellation-based connectomes. However, contrary to the SC-FC results, when connectomes are defined with the Schaefer 400 atlas and structural connectivity is defined with number of fibers, the SC-Struct correlation is fairly high with 0.22. This result hints that there may be different link structure-function with different parcellation schemes. 

    Finally, FC-Struct correlations are also maximal for the individual parcellations-based connectomes at 0.29, representing a 8.2% increase compared to the group parcellation-based connectomes.

For the sake of brevity, we have not presented the results obtained with FSL tractography but they generally perform similarly. However, it is worth indicating that profile similarity performs better than fiber density for FSL tractographies.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the influence of multiple parcellation schemes on different measures of structure-function coupling of the connectome at the individual level. We showed that parcellation choice does matter and that individualized parcellations appear to be a necessary condition for the obtention of individual connectomes, an important perspective for the brain mapping community.
