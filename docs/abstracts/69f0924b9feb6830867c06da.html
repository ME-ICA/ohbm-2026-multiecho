Introduction

Emotion regulation is the ability of an individual to adapt their emotional responses to environmental demands. Regulatory mechanisms have been known to be disrupted in neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression and anxiety. Conceptualizing emotions as dynamic processes rather than static states provides a more ecologically valid framework for understanding emotion regulation, as regulatory capacity is reflected in how emotions fluctuate and transition over time. The Extended Process Model of Emotion Regulation (EPMER)1 provides a unified framework that integrates emotion generation and regulation into a continuous, dynamic process. This model emphasizes on continuous identification, selection, and implementation of regulatory processes, however, its translation to neuroimaging paradigms remains limited.

Music as a naturalistic stimulus offers a promising approach to bridge this gap by eliciting rich, time-varying emotional experiences. Only a few neuroimaging studies capture first-person emotional dynamics alongside brain activity2. Here, we combined a continuous behavioral reporting paradigm with fMRI to quantify moment-to-moment emotional changes during music listening.

Using a stitched musical stimulus composed of validated Hindustani raga segments, we derived kinematic features from continuous valence-arousal ratings to monitor emotional dynamics. We tried to capture the dynamics features like transition, spread and inertia using these kinematic derivatives and tested their relation to individual differences in musicality and subclinical affective traits. We also checked whether emotion dynamics can be quantified into stable and transition states that engage distinct neural networks.

Methods

Participant Details

Fifty-nine healthy adults (18–35 years) participated in the study. Individuals with formal musical training ≥10 years or a history of neuropsychiatric illness were excluded. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with NBRC Institutional Human Ethics Committee approval. Three participants were excluded due to stimulus presentation issues, resulting in 56 participants for behavioral analyses. An additional eight participants were excluded from fMRI analyses due to excessive head motion or low signal quality, leaving 48 participants for neural analyses.

Stimulus and Experimental Paradigm

Participants listened to a 513 sec continuous musical stimulus designed to evoke dynamic emotional response. The stimulus consisted of ‘gat’ segments of eight different Hindustani classical ragas of known emotional valence3. Segments were stitched with 2 sec overlaps and linear fades to preserve musical structure and ensure seamless transitions. During fMRI scanning, participants were instructed to listen to the stimulus and report their perceived emotional state using an MR-compatible joystick on a two-dimensional valence–arousal circumplex scale (Figure 1A, 1B). Participants also completed standardized questionnaires assessing musical sophistication (Gold-MSI)4, depressive symptoms (BDI), and anxiety (GAD-7) prior to scanning.

Behavioral Preprocessing and Analysis

Continuous joystick position data (valence along X axis and arousal along Y axis) were recorded at ~60 Hz and downsampled for analysis. The 2-D time series were rescaled and denoised using a 5% dead-zone around the neutral position to take care of jitters. 

The distance formula was applied to 2-D behavioural time series and the velocity and acceleration were derived from it using the central difference scheme. Acceleration was binarized to define stable emotional states (no acceleration) and transition states (acceleration) (Figure 1C). From these data, three kinematic features were derived: (i) emotional shifts, quantified as the number of transition events; (ii) emotional spread, span of the participant’s response trajectory within the two-dimensional affective space; and (iii) emotional inertia, defined as the latency between stimulus change points and the onset of first acceleration/ transition point.

Neuroimaging Data Processing and Analysis

fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep 25.1.35, followed by spatial smoothing and regression of nuisance variables including motion parameters, white-matter and CSF signals, and scanner drift. Acceleration-based binary regressors (stable vs. transition states) were convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function and entered into subject-level general linear models. Group-level analyses identified brain regions associated with emotional transitions using one-tailed tests with false discovery rate correction (α = 0.05).

Statistical Analysis

Spearman rank correlations were used to assess associations between emotional kinematic measures and behavioral questionnaire scores. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

Results

To validate the stimulus design, we tested whether kinematic features derived from continuous emotional ratings related to trait emotional engagement with music. Emotional shifts showed a significant positive association with Gold-MSI4 emotional engagement scores (Spearman ρ = 0.27, p = 0.043) (Figure 2A), indicating that more emotionally engaged participants transitioned more frequently between affective states. Emotional spread showed a positive but non-significant association with engagement (ρ = 0.24, p = 0.075) (Figure 2A), suggesting a tendency for highly engaged individuals to traverse a broader emotional space.

The group level (averaged) behavioral responses closely followed the expected valence profiles3 of the musical segments (ρ = 0.56, p < 0.001), confirming reliable recognition of the stimulus’ emotional valence. Higher Beck’s Depression Inventory (BDI) scores were significantly associated with longer delays in detecting negative-to-positive valence transitions (ρ = 0.33, p = 0.015) (Figure 2B). Thus, individuals with higher depressive traits exhibited longer delays in updating their perceived emotional state, consistent with increased emotional inertia.After normalizing inertia values for segment duration, no significant differences were detected in median inertia across segments (Friedman χ² = 9.127, p = 0.166), indicating that inertia remained stable across stimulus types and was not driven by variations in segment length.

 A GLM contrast comparing transitory versus stable emotional states revealed distinct neural networks. Transitory states were associated with increased activation in frontal and sensorimotor regions, including the precentral gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, and ventral temporal cortex (Figure 2C). In contrast, stable emotional states engaged limbic and paralimbic regions, including the amygdala, hippocampus, orbitofrontal cortex, temporal cortex, and posterior parietal cortex. These findings demonstrate a dissociation between neural systems supporting affective state transitions and sustained emotional experience (Figure 2D).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that dynamic emotional experiences elicited by naturalistic Hindustani classical music can be reliably captured using continuous behavioral reporting. By moving beyond static or retrospective emotion measures, this approach enables fine-grained characterization of moment-to-moment affective dynamics and provides a more ecologically valid framework for studying emotion regulation.

Kinematic features derived from continuous valence-arousal trajectory allowed emotional experiences to be segmented into stable states and transitory states, offering a real-time behavioral measure of emotional maintenance and change. This distinction could be  mapped onto different neural systems; stable emotional states engaged core limbic and paralimbic regions, including the amygdala, hippocampus, and orbitofrontal cortex, whereas emotional transitions recruited broader networks involving executive, salience, default mode, and somatomotor systems. These findings suggest that transitioning between emotions is neurally demanding and requires flexible engagement of evaluative and control processes.

Behaviorally, individuals with higher emotional engagement with music exhibited more frequent emotional shifts and greater emotional spread, consistent with increased affective flexibility. In contrast, higher subclinical depressive traits were associated with delayed detection of negative-to-positive valence transitions, reflecting increased emotional inertia. Importantly, these effects were observed in a non-clinical sample, highlighting the sensitivity of continuous behavioral measures to subtle individual differences in emotional regulation.

Together, these results suggest that generation and regulation of emotion can be observed as continuous and interactive processes at behavioral and neural levels, reinforcing the validity of the Extended Process Model of Emotion Regulation1. By integrating naturalistic design, continuous behavioral tracking, kinematic derivaives and neuroimaging, this framework offers a promising avenue for identifying behavioral and neural markers of affective flexibility and inertia, with potential relevance for early detection and an understanding of affective vulnerability.
