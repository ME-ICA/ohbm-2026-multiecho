Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) afflicts 55 million individuals worldwide and is expected to double in the next 20 years. Tau neurofibrillary tangles are a pathological hallmark that co-localize with cortical atrophy and predict domain-specific cognitive impairments. While the Braak staging system characterizes population-level tau spread, in vivo tau-PET imaging reveals significant individual variability in progression patterns that deviate from typical Braak stages. Accurately predicting spatiotemporal tau progression in individuals for the entire disease course is critical for prognosis, targeted patient management, and precision therapeutic interventions. Statistical Event-Based Models (EBMs) transform cross-sectional data into cohort-level disease timelines by assigning subjects to stages based on sequential biomarker abnormalities.^1-3 While EBMs can achieve longitudinal biomarker trajectories, they cannot generate subject-specific predictions or model biophysical mechanisms underlying disease spread. Conversely, Network Diffusion Models (NDMs) provide mechanistic frameworks characterizing tau as a diffusive process along structural brain networks.^4-5 NDMs successfully predict spatiotemporal tau dynamics in animal models and capture anatomic connectivity-mediated pathology progression, but require extensive longitudinal data often unavailable in human subjects. Here we introduce Stage-based Network Diffusion (StaND), a novel framework combining event-based staging with network diffusion biophysics to predict complete tau trajectories in individuals using only single-timepoint tau-PET, MRI, and clinical-demographic data. StaND uniquely enables "going back to predict forward:" inferring each patient's disease-onset tau distribution to accurately forecast future progression across entire disease courses.

Methods

We implemented Stage-based Network Diffusion (StaND) on a multimodal dataset of 650 subjects from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), including cognitive assessments, structural MRI, and tau-PET imaging. Tau-PET scans were parcellated into 78 cortical regions using the Desikan–Killiany atlas. Structural connectivity was derived from probabilistic tractography applied to diffusion MRI data from 418 healthy participants in the Human Connectome Project, yielding a group-representative white matter connectivity matrix.

StaND consists of three modules. 

(1) Staging Module: An Event-Based Model (EBM) assigns each subject to a probabilistic disease stage along a shared pathological time axis based on the sequential emergence of biomarker abnormalities. Input biomarkers include ADAS11 cognitive scores, hippocampal volume, and regional tau-PET standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) in the entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala. Subject-specific posterior stage distributions were used to interpolate smooth, stage-wise cohort-level tau trajectories across all 78 brain regions. This staging axis subsequently serves as pseudo-time axis for biophysical model fitting.

(2) Biophysical Inference Module: Tau propagation was modeled using a Network Diffusion Model (NDM), which treats tau as a diffusive process constrained by white matter connectivity and governed by the equation dx/dt = βLx + αx. Here, x(t) is the 78-dimensional vector of regional tau concentrations, L is the graph Laplacian of the structural connectivity matrix, β is the inter-regional transmission rate governing tau spread along anatomical connections, and α is the agglomeration rate capturing local tau accumulation within regions. The NDM was first fit to the cohort-level tau trajectories derived from the staging module. Subsequently, the model was optimized for each subject to fit their empirically observed tau pattern at their assigned stage. This procedure infers (a) a subject-specific tau seeding pattern representing the most likely spatial distribution of tau at disease onset and (b) subject-specific kinetic parameters (α, β) governing progression speed. Model optimization minimized mean squared error (MSE) and maximized Pearson correlation between predicted and empirical tau distributions using gradient-based optimization.

(3) Prediction Module: Using each subject’s inferred tau seed and kinetic parameters, the NDM was simulated forward in time to generate subject-specific predictions of regional tau burden at baseline and at future unobserved timepoints. This “going back to predict forward” strategy enables forecasting complete tau trajectories from single-visit data. Predictive performance was evaluated using Pearson correlation between predicted and empirical tau distributions at baseline and longitudinal follow-up visits. Model selection was assessed using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), which balances fit quality against model complexity. To characterize heterogeneity in inferred tau origins, Gaussian Mixture Modeling (GMM) was applied to subject-specific seed vectors. An independent dataset from the UCSF Memory and Aging Center was used for external replication and generalizability testing.

Results

StaND-derived disease stage assignments aligned broadly with clinical diagnosis: most cognitively normal (CN) participants occupied early stages (0–4), mild cognitive impairment (MCI) spanned intermediate stages (0–12), and Alzheimer’s disease/dementia participants clustered in later stages (4–16). Interpolated cohort-level tau trajectories exhibited a stereotyped pattern of progression across disease stages, with early accumulation in the amygdala and entorhinal cortex followed by spread to inferior temporal regions. While the Network Diffusion Model achieved a strong fit to these cohort-level trajectories, correspondence with individual subjects’ baseline tau patterns at their assigned stages was only moderate (mean R = 0.53), indicating that group-average modeling alone fails to capture substantial inter-individual variability.

We next applied the biophysical inference module of StaND to generate individualized models of tau progression. Three model configurations were evaluated, differing in whether tau seeding patterns and kinetic parameters were inferred at the cohort or subject level. Inferring a single cohort-level tau seed while allowing subject-specific kinetic parameters yielded modest improvement (mean R = 0.57, mean MSE = 0.023), suggesting that individual differences in progression speed alone are insufficient to explain observed heterogeneity in tau spatial patterns. In contrast, inferring subject-specific tau seeding patterns with cohort-level kinetic parameters resulted in a substantial increase in baseline fit accuracy (mean R = 0.85, mean MSE = 0.009), highlighting the dominant role of heterogeneous tau origins. Allowing both tau seeds and kinetic parameters to vary by subject produced the highest overall performance (mean R = 0.87, mean MSE = 0.012), becoming our final methodology. Although joint subject-specific inference increased model complexity (k = 80) relative to models with only subject-specific kinetic parameters (k = 2) or only subject-specific seeds (k = 78), it yielded a markedly lower AIC (8,435) compared with kinetic-only (AIC = 33,166) and seed-only (AIC = 9,176) models, indicating superior explanatory power. 

Gaussian Mixture Modeling of subject-specific inferred tau seeds revealed two distinct etiologic archetypes: limbic-dominant and frontotemporal-dominant seeding patterns, each with distinct clinical and demographic profiles. The limbic-dominant group exhibited higher APOE ε4 positivity and worse baseline cognitive performance, whereas the frontotemporal-dominant group showed faster inferred tau spread rates and more aggressive pathology progression.

Longitudinal validation demonstrated that StaND can accurately predict future tau distributions at follow-up tau-PET visits (mean R = 0.81; Figure 2c). StaND substantially outperformed a benchmark model adapted from Vogel et al., which infers group-level binary tau seeds for AD subtypes (R = 0.81 vs. R = 0.31 and 0.39; Figure 2d).^3 Independent replication in the UCSF Memory and Aging Center dataset confirmed comparable predictive accuracy (mean R = 0.84), supporting the robustness and generalizability of the framework.

Conclusion

StaND enables individualized prediction of tau progression across the entire Alzheimer’s disease course by integrating statistical disease staging with mechanistic network diffusion modeling. Using only single–timepoint tau-PET, MRI, and clinical data, StaND infers each subject’s most likely initial tau distribution at disease onset together with subject-specific kinetic parameters governing local accumulation and network-mediated spread, and then simulates tau propagation forward over pathological time. This modeling strategy allows reconstruction of complete disease trajectories from cross-sectional observations, overcoming key limitations of existing approaches that require longitudinal data or rely on group-level assumptions. As a result, StaND captures both spatial and temporal heterogeneity in Alzheimer’s disease at the individual level.

Our findings demonstrate that inter-individual variability in tau progression arises primarily from heterogeneous seeding patterns rather than uniform divergence from a common origin. These results challenge traditional center-out models of tau spread and instead suggest that early etiologic heterogeneity progressively converges along the brain’s structural network as disease advances. Distinguishing subjects by etiologic seed archetype and kinetic progression rates provides a mechanistic basis for individualized prognosis, patient stratification, and precision intervention. More broadly, StaND offers a generalizable framework for forecasting network-mediated neurodegenerative disease trajectories from single-timepoint data, with potential applications to other disorders characterized by connectivity-constrained pathology spread.
