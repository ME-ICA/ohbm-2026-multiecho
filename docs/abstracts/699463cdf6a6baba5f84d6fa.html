Introduction

Stroke induces widespread structural alterations across cortical and subcortical components of the motor system. Beyond focal lesions, secondary degeneration affects multiple descending pathways, including the corticospinal tract, cortico-reticular projections, and brainstem motor nuclei, leading to distributed motor-network reorganization (Werring, Brain, 2000). Diffusion MRI metrics such as fractional anisotropy, radial diffusivity, and tract geometry are closely linked to motor impairment, indicating that residual motor capacity depends on the integrity of large-scale structural pathways (Lindenberg, Ann Neurol, 2010; Stinear, Nat Rev Neurol, 2017).
Accumulating evidence suggests that motor outcome after stroke is shaped by pre-existing structural organization, as pre-intervention MRI features often relate to subsequent functional performance (Lindenberg, Neurology, 2010). However, most studies have examined isolated regions or single diffusion metrics, even though motor impairment reflects distributed alterations across multiple descending systems rather than injury to a single tract (Zheng, Front Hum Neurosci, 2015). Because tractography yields hundreds of highly collinear microstructural and geometric features, univariate approaches cannot capture the integrated organization of the motor system.

Here, we conceptualize descending motor pathways as a high-dimensional structural space and apply principal component analysis to tractography-derived metrics obtained before intervention. By extracting latent structural components spanning corticospinal, cortico-reticular, and cortico-rubral pathways, we examined how distinct dimensions of motor pathway organization relate to upper-limb motor impairment after stroke. This multivariate framework aims to characterize the structural organization of the post-stroke motor system at the network level and to identify latent structural signatures that constrain residual motor function.

Methods

Twelve patients with chronic stroke participated in this study and underwent a two-week inpatient rehabilitation program. Upper-limb motor function was assessed using the Fugl–Meyer Assessment for the Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) both before and after the intervention. Diffusion-tensor MRI was also acquired at both time points. Only the pre-intervention MRI data were used for structural prediction analyses, while pre- and post-intervention FMA-UE scores were used to quantify motor improvement. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shonan Keiiku Hospital.

Diffusion MRI was acquired on a 1.5 T scanner (GE Optima MR450w, GE Healthcare) using a 2D single-shot spin-echo echo-planar imaging sequence. Acquisition parameters derived from the DICOM header were: repetition time 8.5 s, echo time 88.8 ms, flip angle 90°, slice thickness 3.5 mm with no inter-slice gap, and full phase field of view with 100% phase sampling. Diffusion weighting was applied along 23 non-collinear gradient directions with a b-value of 1000 s/mm², together with additional non-diffusion-weighted (b0) images, providing whole-brain coverage suitable for tractography.

For each subject, diffusion images were preprocessed using standard steps including correction for eddy currents and head motion, and co-registration to the individual T1-weighted structural image. Whole-brain fiber orientation information was reconstructed, and deterministic streamline tractography was performed using anatomically constrained ROIs. Cortical seed regions were defined in the precentral gyrus corresponding to the primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4; BA4) , and premotor/ supplementary motor areas (BA6). Target ROIs were placed in four descending motor pathway structures in the brainstem: the cerebral peduncle, red nucleus, pontine reticular formation, and medullary reticular formation. For each combination of seeds (BA4 or BA6) and target (cerebral peduncle, red nucleus, pontine reticular formation, medullary reticular formation), one million streamlines were generated, separately for the paretic and non-paretic hemispheres, and streamlines that successfully connected the seed and target ROIs were retained.

From the resulting seed–target connections, tract-specific quantitative indices were extracted using the tractography analysis software (DSI studio). For each of the bilateral BA4– and BA6-originating tracts to the four brainstem targets, we computed a set of diffusion- and geometry-based measures, including number of tracts, mean fiber length, spatial span, curl, elongation, total and segmental tract volume, surface-related indices, end-region area and radius, and diffusion-derived microstructural metrics such as fractional anisotropy, radial diffusivity, quantitative anisotropy, isotropic diffusion, and restricted diffusion imaging indices. Concatenating these measures across all seed–target pairs yielded a matrix of 12 subjects × 312 MRI-derived features, which was used as the input for the subsequent principal component analysis and regression modeling.

Results

Principal component analysis (PCA) reduced the 312 tract-derived structural variables into 11 interpretable components, with the first five components accounting for 88.84% of the total variance. Among these, PC2 showed the strongest association with FMA-UE scores (r = 0.56, p = 0.058), indicating a trend-level relationship between this latent structural dimension and motor impairment.

Model selection using Akaike’s Information Criterion identified a combination of latent structural components—PC2, PC4, PC5, and an interaction term between PC1 and PC5—as jointly explaining variance in motor impairment. In the final model, PC2 (Estimate = 0.226, p = 0.006) and PC5 (Estimate = 0.380, p = 0.011) were significant contributors, while the PC1 × PC5 interaction term (Estimate = −0.147, p = 0.029) further improved model fit. PC4 showed a trend-level contribution (p = 0.059). The model demonstrated substantial explanatory power (R² = 0.79, adjusted R² = 0.62).

Loading analysis revealed that FMA-related components were characterized by coherent variation across multiple descending pathways rather than isolated tract abnormalities. Contributing features included tract length and volume, end-region morphology within brainstem nuclei, and diffusion-derived microstructural indices such as fractional anisotropy and radial diffusivity. These findings indicate that motor impairment after stroke is associated with latent dimensions reflecting distributed structural organization across corticospinal and non-corticospinal descending systems.

Conclusion

This study identified latent dimensions of structural organization within descending motor pathways that relate to upper-limb motor impairment after stroke. By characterizing a high-dimensional structural space defined by 312 tractography-derived features from BA4- and BA6-originating projections to major brainstem motor nuclei, principal component analysis revealed 11 latent components capturing integrated properties of the post-stroke motor system. Among these, PC2 and PC5 accounted for substantial variance in FMA-UE scores, indicating that motor impairment reflects distributed alterations across multiple descending systems rather than damage to a single tract.

PC2 primarily represented the structural organization of BA6-derived pathways, including cortico-reticular and cortico-rubral projections. Loadings associated with tract count, tract volume, and end-region morphology suggest that the integrity of premotor-origin descending pathways constitutes a key dimension of residual motor capacity. Given the role of BA6 in movement preparation, proximal control, and bilateral coordination, preserved BA6 projections may support compensatory motor control when corticospinal integrity is compromised, consistent with emerging views on non-corticospinal contributions to post-stroke motor function.

PC5 captured a complementary structural dimension dominated by the morphology of terminal zones within brainstem motor nuclei. Features related to end-region size and geometry in the pontine and medullary reticular formations and red nucleus indicate that the downstream organization of descending projections influences residual motor ability. Such terminal field morphology may reflect convergence–divergence patterns and the potential for adaptive reorganization within brainstem motor circuits.

Together, PC2 and PC5 delineate complementary axes of motor system organization, spanning cortical-origin descending integrity and brainstem terminal architecture. Their combined contributions highlight that both upstream and downstream structural elements jointly constrain post-stroke motor impairment. Although the limited sample size warrants cautious interpretation, this multivariate approach provides a framework for mapping the latent structural organization of human motor pathways and for characterizing how distributed structural dimensions shape motor function after stroke.
