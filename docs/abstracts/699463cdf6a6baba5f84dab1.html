Introduction

Historically, research shows that in young individuals, unskilled effortful performance relies on a network of brain regions used to cope with novel task demands. With training, performance becomes efficient, and the processing network becomes specific and optimized1. Brain activity is inherently variable, and these complex dynamics may also be altered with training. Recent research suggests that our brain is a complex adaptive system, where networks constantly form, dissolve, and shift interactions with other networks. This complexity supports transitions between network configurations and enables the exploration of multiple networks to generate new behaviours2,3. If novel skill acquisition enriches processing, complexity should increase with training. Alternatively, if complexity decreases post-training, it may reflect processes being optimized for efficiency. 

Here we used single- and dual-task versions of a visual perceptual learning task, the texture discrimination task (TDT), to examine the interplay between building efficiency versus flexibility3 during training. In the TDT, participants identify the orientation (horizontal or vertical) of a target array of diagonal lines within a background of horizontal lines. The single-task trains only orientation identification, and behavioural improvement is often restricted to trained visual features with brain changes in the primary visual cortex4. We have additionally demonstrated changes in ERP amplitudes, with N1 decreases and P3 increases3. The dual-task adds a central letter identification task resulting in changes in attentional allocation. These may be reflected in P3 amplitudes in attentional regions5. We aim to replicate prior behaviour and ERP findings and extend them by examining complexity, an unstudied factor in this context. Given localized visual versus distributed attentional changes in single versus dual-task training, we expect greater and more widespread complexity changes for dual-task training.

Methods

Study participants included 44 neurotypical young adults (18-35). Inclusion criteria included normal or corrected-to-normal vision and sufficient proficiency in English to understand study requirements. Exclusion criteria included current or recent neurological, psychiatric, or cognitive conditions and/or the use of psychotropic medication within the last 6 months. 

Participants performed a texture discrimination task (TDT) over 4 study sessions. 26 participants completed a single task version, and 18 completed a dual task version. On each trial, participants saw a sequence of three displays—fixation cross, target, and mask. The fixation cross was presented until trial initiation. The target display was an array of horizontal bars. A horizontal or vertical pattern of three diagonal bars appeared in one of the four quadrants, with its exact location randomized. At the central position an L or T appeared. The mask consisted of randomly oriented V-shaped features. Participants indicated whether the central target is an L or a T for the dual task, and if the peripherally located pattern formed a vertical or horizontal array. The peripheral target angle (PTA) of the diagonal bars was continually adjusted to maintain performance at 80% accuracy to ensure comparable perceptual thresholds across participants. Task performance was quantified as modal PTA at pre- and post-training, as this was the task variable being manipulated in both single and dual task versions. We did not assess letter identification performance as this was trained only in the dual task. 

EEG data were collected across 600 TDT trials at session 1 (pre-training) and session 4 (post-training). Across the training sessions 2 and 3, participants completed 3000 TDT trials. Participants received auditory feedback for correct responses, but only during the training sessions.

Continuous EEG data were acquired with 64 active electrodes. Preprocessing included bandpass filtering (0.5-55 Hz), decomposition using independent component analysis to remove artifacts, and trial segmentation –200 ms and 1000 ms  around stimulus onset. EEG data from each participant at pre- and post-training were used to calculate ERP amplitudes. Brain signal complexity was calculated using multiscale entropy (MSE). First, a coarse-graining procedure was applied wherein data were resampled and averaged across increasing numbers of datapoints within non-overlapping windows to create discrete timescales, ranging from fine (scale 1; 2-ms windows) to coarse (scale 12; 24-ms windows). Second, sample entropy was calculated at each timescale to estimate the unpredictability of the underlying signal at every electrode for each timescale.  

Behavioural analyses were carried out in MATLAB 2019a (MathWorks, Inc.). To test changes in performance following task training, we used a 2 (Group: single, dual) x 2 (Time: pre, post) mixed ANOVA to compare modal PTA during EEG sessions. We performed task partial least squares (task PLS) analyses to examine the relationship between our brain measures (MSE and ERP) and the experimental design. Task PLS was performed to quantify the similarities and differences in MSE and ERP component amplitudes between groups (single, dual) from pre- to post-training. Overall significance was assessed using permutation testing, and LVs were considered significant if the observed singular value exceeded the permutated singular value in > 95% of the permutations (p < .05). The stability of the identified pattern across participants was assessed using bootstrap estimation. The analyses were repeated with 500 different bootstrap samples and a minimum bootstrap ratio threshold of 2.0, corresponding to a 95% confidence interval (p < .05) was applied.

Results

Participants had significantly lower modal PTA from pre-training [M=13.80°±2.81] to post-training [M=11.13°±2.65] EEG sessions regardless of group [F1,42=41.75, p=<0.0001, ηp2 =0.499], indicating improved performance after training.

The MSE task PLS analysis identified one significant LV. LV1 [p=0.008] showed that MSE changed from pre- to post-training selectively with dual task training. It was higher at mid-timescales in select regions (temporal and centro-parietal) during pre-training. Post-training, MSE was higher in widespread regions at fine timescales, and in parietal electrodes at coarse time scales. The overall pattern is illustrated in Figure 1(a,b). 

The ERP task PLS analysis identified one significant LV [p<0.0001] which showed that P3 ERP amplitudes were greater post-training as compared to pre-training in both groups. The most prominent effects were seen in P3 (200ms-400ms window) amplitudes across parietal regions as illustrated in Figure 1(c,d). There was some spread occipitally and centrally. N1 (100-150ms window) amplitudes decreased from pre- to post training in central and parietal electrodes, but these effects were much more circumscribed.

Conclusion

Our study both replicated and extended previous work on perceptual training. As expected, behavioural performance improved from pre- to post-training similarly for both single- and dual-task groups. The ERP analysis also showed similarities in ERP changes for both groups, with changes in the N1 and P3 components. These results are consistent with previous work examining single task training2, and additionally show that dual-task training produces similar effects. 

Complexity changes were unique to the dual training group with fine and coarse timescale MSE increasing, and mid-timescale MSE decreasing from pre- to post-training. Previous work suggests that finer timescales reflect flexibility in local, within-region processing whereas coarse timescales reflect flexibility in long-range processing where information is shared over distributed regions2,3. Therefore, our results suggest that greater flexibility, both localized and long-range, underlie skill acquisition when the demands on top-down attentional control are higher. 

Taken together, our results suggest that both single and dual task training alters efficiency in task performance and underlying evoked responses. However, building neural flexibility required dual task training, suggesting that a shift in the balance between fine, middle and coarse scale temporal dynamics appears to support improvements in task performance when increased attentional demands are involved.
