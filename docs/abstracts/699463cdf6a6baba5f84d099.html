Introduction

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is a non-pharmacological intervention that also improves mood.1 Despite group level improvements in depressive and insomnia symptoms, there is also substantial inter-individual variation in individual treatment response. Abnormal fMRI-derived resting state functional connectivity (rsFC) has been linked to depression and insomnia pathophysiology and can predict responses to pharmacological treatments, independent from CBT-I.2 For example, altered connectivity patterns across the default mode network and salience network, defined by the Yeo/Schaefer parcellations, are well-reported and replicated findings in predicting depression treatment outcomes.3 However, it is unknown whether rsFC – and if so, which networks – similarly predict depressive and insomnia symptoms improvements when only insomnia symptoms are targeted, as in CBT-I. Here we investigated whether fMRI-derived resting state functional connectivity (rsFC) can predict treatment response to CBT-I in an unmedicated adult sample with co-occurring depressive and insomnia symptoms. Specifically, we probed seven canonical functional networks and their independent contributions toward predicting treatment response status, and assessed whether depression and insomnia improvements are similarly or differentially predicted by rsFC networks.

Methods

This analysis focused on a subsample of a larger clinical trial of CBT-I in individuals with depressive (Beck Depression Inventory; BDI >= 14) and insomnia (Insomnia Severity Index; ISI >= 10) symptoms.4 Thirty-seven adults (23 females, aged 25-59) completed a 6-week treatment course and had high quality MRI data acquired before CBT-I treatment were analyzed. After treatment, subjects with >= 50% reduction of BDI scores (with sleep items removed) were considered responders in the depression domain, while subjects with >= 50% reduction of ISI scores were considered responders in the insomnia domain. T1-weighted structural image was acquired using a 3T GE Discovery MR750 machine with HCP-equivalent SPGR sequence ( TR/TE/TI=6.3ms/2.8ms/450ms, FA=12°, FOV=24cm, matrix 320x320, slice 0.8x0.8 mm2). Two 5-min, eyes-open resting state fMRI scans were acquired using the HCP protocol (TR/TE = 710ms/30ms, FA=54°, matrix 82x82x45, voxel 2.71x2.71x3 mm3). fMRIPrep v22.1.1 was used for preprocessing followed by denoising using XCP_D v0.9.1 with default parameters. The 4S156 atlas from XCP_D was used to extract residualized time series of 156 ROIs. rsFC was then calculated using Pearson correlation across all ROIs’ time series, followed by Fisher r-to-z transform. Gradient boosting classification (scikit-learn v1.7.0) was then used to predict response status using each of the seven Yeo networks: default-mode (Default), ventral attention (SalVentAttn), dorsal attention (DorsAttn), frontoparietal control (Cont), limbic, visual (Vis), and somatosensory-motor (SomMot). For each network, features were defined as all rsFC matrix elements pi,j, where i indexes ROIs only within the network, and j indexes all ROIs. Stratified 5-fold cross validation (CV) was used to assess classifier performance. Feature dimensionality was reduced in each fold by selecting the top 128 features after applying an F-test (SelectKBest, scikit-learn) based on the training set. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and area under the curve (AUC) were computed for the testing set in each fold and averaged. CV was also repeated 1000 times with shuffled class labels to compute ROC-AUC at chance level, and shuffled AUCs higher than non-shuffled AUCs were counted and divided by 1000 to obtain a p-value per network model. Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was then used to correct for seven comparisons. Demographic differences between responders and non-responders were assessed using a two-sample t-test.

Results

For depressive symptoms, 17 out of 37 subjects responded to CBT-I. Responders and non-responders showed no statistical differences in age (t=-0.868, p=0.391) or sex (t=-0.376, p=0.709). The Default network model (AUC=0.825±0.001, p=0.014, corrected p=0.049) and SalVentAttn network model (AUC=0.834±0.015, p=0.014, corrected p=0.049) achieved significant classification performances. All other network models did not achieve significantly higher AUCs than AUCs with shuffled-labels (p>0.991; Figure 1A). For insomnia symptoms, 22 out of 37 subjects responded to CBT-I. Responders and non-responders showed no statistical differences in age (t=1.528, p=0.136) or sex (t=0.218, p=0.829). All models achieved non-significant classification performances (corrected p>0.763; Figure 1B).

Conclusion

To our knowledge, these are the first data to indicate rsFC can predict treatment response of CBT-I in people experiencing both depressive and insomnia symptoms. We further identified Default and SalVentAttn as two predictors for depression symptom response, matching previous reports of these two networks as key hallmarks in pathophysiology and predicting treatment response in depression.2,3 Interestingly, the same approach could not predict treatment response in the insomnia symptoms domain. While this could be due to limitations of our modeling approach, it also highlights the possibility that susceptibility to CBT-I treatment response in depression and insomnia domains might be differentially underpinned by functional networks. Our ongoing work will introduce a control arm and a larger sample to further support the out-of-sample validity of our approach. If replicated, these insights may ultimately inform personalized therapeutic strategies for individuals with comorbid insomnia and depression using CBT-I.
