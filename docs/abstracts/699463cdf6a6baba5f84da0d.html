Introduction

The brain does not age uniformly. Heterogeneous aging of the brain manifests in a variety of ways. Within the cortex, prefrontal and temporal are especially vulnerable to atrophy with aging, while occipital regions are relatively resilient1. Understanding the basis of this heterogeneity is pivotal for the treatment of age- and disease-related deficits. For an example of the latter, the parts of the brain that are most vulnerable to age-related atrophy are also among those that first show amyloid deposition and degradation in Alzheimer’s disease. Unfortunately, regional heterogeneity in aging is a complex phenomenon with no simple explanation.

Brain age (BA) models are powerful tools for investigating normative aging. A (potentially deep learning) model is trained to estimate the age of a healthy person’s brain, based on their MRI or other features. The trained model develops a representation of the normative brain throughout the lifespan. When applied to new subjects, the difference between their predicted and true ages reflects how much older or younger their brains appear relative to the norm. This difference is referred to as the brain age gap (BAG). While standard BA models produce a single, global age estimation, specialized “local” brain age models can produce spatially resolved age estimations for every part of the brain2. The average of these predictions over a healthy cohort, referred to here as M, is a map that captures normative heterogeneity in aging. 

Imaging transcriptomics can help to investigate what such a map represents. Comparing neuroimaging phenotypes with spatially resolved gene transcription can reveal associated biological mechanisms. Nonlinear dimensionality reduction allows large groups of genes to be summarized and compared with M without loss of power. 

Here, we combine a deep learning model for local brain age with imaging transcriptomics to investigate the basis of heterogenous cortical aging. 

Methods

The local brain age model (validated elsewhere, under review) is an autoencoder trained to take T1-weighted MRI volumes as input and to output voxel-wise BA predictions. BAG is calculated as (BA – true age). Loss was computed as the magnitude of the age gap at each brain voxel, which was then averaged across brain voxels to yield mean absolute error. The local brain age model was trained on a diverse dataset of 14,555 participants. Input MRI volumes were preprocessed using FreeSurfer’s recon-all function, then downsampled to dimensions of 1283 with voxel dimensions of 2 mm3. The local brain age model achieved an MAE of 5.94 years in the validation dataset. 

The trained local brain age model was applied to an auxiliary dataset of 6,000 participants from the UK BioBank. Participants ranged in age from 41 to 70, with mean age of 55.4. Participant were selected for each 5-year age bracket from 40-70 to maintain even age and sex distributions, such that the total sample consisted of 3,000 males. After computation of local brain age volumes for the 6,000 participants, values were projected onto a mesh representation of the cortical surface. This was done to respect the folded structure of the cortex. For this projection, a weighted average was taken of the voxels lying between each pair of points on the white and pial cortical surfaces, with voxels closer to each boundary being weighted lower. Resulting surface projections were then registered to a shared atlas surface with 10,242 vertices using FreeSurfer’s mri_surf2surf function. This atlas registration enabled averaging across all subjects to produce M, depicted in Figure 1A. 

Transcriptomic data was retrieved from the Allen Human Brain Atlas3, an extensive resource containing bulk RNA sequencing data for 300-500 samples across the left hemispheres of six human donors. Non-cortical samples were removed from further processing. The bulk RNA microarray assay contains data for 58,592 mRNA probes. The probes were matched to genes using ReAnnotator software, filtered to remove noisy probes, then averaged over targeted genes. This yielded transcription data for 15,358 genes. 

To translate transcriptomic data onto the cortical surface, a midthickness surface was generated for each AHBA subject between their white and pial surfaces. Each mRNA sample was matched to the closest vertex on the midthickness surface, after which nearest-neighbor interpolation was used to populate all vertices with mRNA sample indices. Native surfaces were then registered to a shared atlas surface with 10,242 vertices using FreeSurfer’s mri_surf2surf function. Sample indices were then populated with gene transcription values. This produced an array of size 15,358 x 10,242 x 6 gene transcription values. Genes were filtered using differential stability (DS), which quantifies how well a gene correlates between the six donor brains. Any gene below the 50th percentile DS value was removed, leaving 7,482 genes. The remaining genes were averaged across the six subjects. 

Nonlinear dimensionality reduction was applied to the resulting gene transcription matrix using Laplacian eigenmaps. The first three nontrivial eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian were computed, yielding a 7,482 x 3 matrix of eigenvectors (components) that capture the local structure of the transcription data. The first and most explanatory of the three components is depicted in Figure 1B. 

Each transcriptomic component was correlated with M, with correlation significance assessed using a spatial permutation test. After identifying significant correlations, to find which genes are most strongly represented by a given eigenvector, all genes were correlated with the significant component. Unsigned correlation coefficients were converted to z-scores, and any gene with z>1.65 was considered significantly correlated with the component. Significant genes were used in a gene ontology analysis to identify implicated biological processes. 

Results

The local brain age model achieved high accuracy in the 6,000-participant set, with global MAE = 4.48 years and a standard deviation of 3.26 years. M, the mean over the local brain age maps, exhibits a clear anterior-posterior gradient (Figure 1A), such that posterior parts of the brain have lower BAGs than anterior. Much of the cortex exhibits negative BAGs, with only frontal regions exhibiting positive BAGs. Across the lateral surface, gyral crests exhibit lower BAGs than sulci. The maximum value of M is 0.35, and the minimum is -0.5 years. Note that positive values indicate that a given brain vertex has aged more than expected.

Only the first transcriptomic component (Figure 1B) exhibits a significant correlation with M after FDR correction (r = 0.62, p = 0.006). The second and third transcriptomic components are very weakly correlated with M (r = -0.008 and r = 0.12, respectively). Once the significantly correlated component was identified, each gene was evaluated to determine how much it contributed to the transcriptomic component. 412 genes correlate significantly with the first transcriptomic component. 

Using the 412 genes as input in a gene ontology analysis for biological processes returns multiple consistently enriched categories, summarized in Figure 2. Many of the genes are involved in processes related to signal transduction, especially in the context of regulation of biological processes. Another common regulatory motif is cell development. Finally, many of the enriched categories are related to mitochondrial function, oxidative phosphorylation, and ATP synthesis. 

Interestingly, of the 412 genes, 316 of them are labeled as “unmapped IDs” in the gene ontology annotation sets, meaning that the datasets do not contain annotations for these gene IDs. From the filtered set of 7,482 genes, only 527 are “unmapped” in the gene ontology datasets, yet 316 of those 527 are found in the 412 significant genes. By chance, only 30 of the 412 genes would have been expected to be unmapped – a tenfold difference, indicating that unmapped IDs are overrepresented in the significant gene set.

Further investigation reveals that, of these 316 unmapped gene IDs, 197 correspond to long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), 36 correspond to pseudogenes, and 69 correspond to antisense genes, with overlap between these categories. Of the 36 pseudogenes, 21 are processed and 15 are unprocessed. This is unexpected, given that unprocessed pseudogenes are known to heavily outnumber processed pseudogenes, although the fact that only transcribed pseudogenes could be recognized in this study may cause a bias. The biotype of the 93 remaining genes is currently unknown.

Conclusion

Despite being derived from a dataset representing a relatively wide range of ages, M exhibits a strong spatial pattern, indicating consistency across the subjects. When applied to a group of healthy control subjects, standard BA models would ideally estimate age with zero error. However, local BAs being nonzero is interpreted here as signifying spatial heterogeneity in aging. The original training dataset notably also exhibits a similar spatial pattern of mean local BAG, indicating that this pattern does not merely reflect model error or distributional differences inherent to the dataset used here.

As a map capturing heterogeneous aging in the cortex, M aligns with several established depictions. The anterior-posterior gradient of aging has been well documented. For example, the prefrontal cortex is known to be vulnerable to volume loss, while occipital and cingulate portions of the cortex are resilient4. While the temporal lobe does not exhibit positive BAGs in M, note that one small portion on the medial surface of the temporal lobe does exhibit higher BAG than the surrounding vertices. This portion corresponds to part of the medial temporal lobe, near the entorhinal cortex and parahippocampal gyrus. It is well documented that the medial temporal lobe is especially vulnerable to age-related atrophy1. 

The strong, significant correlation between the first transcriptomic component and M could indicate transcriptomic control of heterogeneous aging, and the gene ontology results reflect multiple proposed mechanisms for regional heterogeneity in aging. Enriched categories relating to mitochondrial function and oxidative phosphorylation evoke theories that oxidative stress drives brain atrophy. It has been postulated that frontal and temporal parts of cortex are more vulnerable to oxidative stress due to their higher metabolic demands. Enrichment of signal transduction categories may relate to degradation of neurotransmitter systems. Dopaminergic and cholinergic signaling may decline in ways that preferentially affect certain parts of the brain4. Decreases in serotonergic signaling have been associated with dysfunctional synaptic plasticity and neurogenesis. Synaptic plasticity is itself investigated for being related to heterogeneous aging – it has been shown that the regions exhibiting the most vulnerability to aging tend to be the most plastic parts of the brain1,4. This could be reflected in the enriched GO terms that relate to cell development. 

The large set of unmapped IDs that are associated with the significant transcriptomic component may also be related to development. lncRNAs and pseudogenes remain understudied, but their importance is becoming increasingly clear. Though they are a diverse class of molecules, several lncRNAs have been ascribed regulatory roles important to development. This aligns with the enriched GO categories related to regulation. Furthermore, both pseudogenes and lncRNAs can modulate chromatin structure and thereby influence epigenetics. The unmapped IDs may also contribute to aging heterogeneity by serving protective roles. SNHG8, one of the unmapped IDs found here, has been shown to reduce stress granulate formation and protect against tau protein aggregation, which could relate to protecting plastic parts of the brain5. These associations may help to uncover more of the diverse functions of noncoding RNAs.

These findings are another step towards unraveling the diverse mechanisms underlying regional heterogeneity to aging. Our results support certain theorized mechanisms and suggest interplay between multiple processes. Importantly, we find an unexpected link between noncoding RNAs and heterogeneous aging, bridging two nebulous but critical areas of research.
