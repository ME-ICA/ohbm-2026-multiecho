Introduction

Functional connectivity (FC) analyses of resting-state fMRI data have revealed key principles of network reorganization and their relationship to motor recovery after stroke1. More recently, dynamic FC approaches have started to characterize dynamic recurring changes in motor network connectivity over time2. After a stroke, changes in network integration (coordinated communication across distributed modules) and segregation (module specialization or fragmentation) have been reported2,3. To further our understanding of how changes in motor network integration and segregation may contribute to the reorganization of motor control, we used the recently developed edge timeseries (ETS) approach to capture moment-to-moment network co-fluctuations4 and quantify dynamic expressions of network integration and segregation. Our goal was to (i) characterize how ETS measures differ between chronic stroke patients and healthy controls and (ii) assess their associations with distinct aspects of basal or complex motor control. We hypothesized measures of dynamic large-scale integration and modular segregation to be altered after stroke. Capturing distinct features of network reorganization, we assumed differential associations with basal and complex motor control. 

Methods

We analyzed resting-state fMRI data from 66 chronic stroke patients (>6 months post-stroke) and 60 age-matched controls. Complex motor function was assessed via the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT) while more basal aspects of motor control were quantified via the Motricity Index (MI). Patients were stratified into fully recovered versus non-fully recovered subgroups based on their motor recovery. Preprocessing included spatial smoothing, nuisance regression, motion censoring, and bandpass filtering. We computed ETS as instantaneous co-fluctuations (element-wise multiplication of z-scored BOLD signals) for all region pairs4. ETS-based metrics included: (1) Root Sum Square (RSS): temporal standard deviation of co-fluctuation amplitude; (2) RSS Synchrony: cross-correlation between left and right hemisphere RSS time series; (3) RSS-Variability: standard deviation of the RSS-timeseries. We identified high-amplitude (top 10% RSS frames) co-fluctuation events and computed event-specific modularity (Q, Louvain algorithm). Analyses were performed on the whole brain and on a motor network defined via Neurosynth meta-analysis. Statistical comparisons were conducted between controls and stroke patients and between recovery subgroups. Brain-behavior relationships were examined via Pearson correlations within the patient cohort.

Results

We observed several significant differences in dynamic network measures between patients and controls. On the one hand, stroke patients featured lower levels of interhemispheric co-fluctuation synchrony (RSS synchrony) at the whole-brain and motor network levels. Moreover, higher RSS synchrony within the motor network and interhemispheric co-fluctuation strength (mean RSS) were associated with superior basal motor control in non-fully recovered patients. Additionally, fully recovered patients showed reduced RSS variability. On the other hand, modularity during high-amplitude co-fluctuation events revealed recovery- and function-dependent dissociations. Non-fully recovered patients showed lower motor network modularity across the whole brain and the contralesional hemisphere, consistent with reduced network segregation during synchronized activity. Of note, modularity in the motor network was associated with motor control at several levels: On the one hand, motor-network modularity in the lesioned hemisphere was associated with both basal and complex motor function. Conversely, motor network modularity in the contralesional hemisphere was exclusively related to complex motor control, in line with hemisphere-specific maladaptive segregation patterns.

Conclusion

We show that interhemispheric co-fluctuation dynamics differ between chronic stroke patients and controls and that changes in ETS distinctly relate to impairment in basal and complex motor control. 

On the one hand, preserved large-scale integration between motor areas reflected by RSS synchrony supports basal motor control in non-fully recovered patients. In contrast, lower RSS variability and more moderate dynamics may reflect stabilized patterns of motor network integration in fully recovered patients. On the other hand, higher modularity within the ipsilesional motor network of non-fully recovered patients was associated with worse basal and complex motor control, consistent with a maladaptive increase in segregation of motor network organization when coordinated activity may be needed for efficient integration. Finally, higher motor network modularity in the contralesional hemisphere was associated with worse complex motor control, in line with the notion that excessive contralesional segregation may represent task-specific maladaptation reorganization of complex motor control. 

Taken together, our findings extend our current understanding of how dynamic changes in motor network segregation may support or hinder reorganization of distinct aspects of motor control.
