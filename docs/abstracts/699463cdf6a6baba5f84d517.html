Introduction

Background: Epilepsy is recognised as one of the most predominant neurological disorders, affecting approximately 1% of the global population1. Catamenial epilepsy (CE) refers to seizure exacerbation during specific phases of the menstrual cycle, affecting up to 40% of women with pre-existing epilepsy and is known to be treatment-resistant2. The most prevalent subtype is perimenstrual catamenial epilepsy (PCE), in which seizure frequency can increase by up to two-fold around menstruation3. Progesterone, estrogen, and allopregnanolone (ALLO) are neuroactive steroids, and growing evidence suggests that these reproductive steroids play a crucial role in regulating the balance between neuroexcitatory and neuroinhibitory processes in specific brain regions by influencing synaptic plasticity3. Broadly, oestradiol promotes glutamatergic (NMDA) excitation, whereas progesterone enhances GABAergic inhibition through its neuroactive metabolite allopregnanolone4. The current leading hypothesis based on rodent studies states that neurosteroid withdrawal around the time of menstruation is likely a major factor in the elevated excitability and increased seizure susceptibility in PCE4. Visually induced long-term potentiation (LTP) can serve as a measure of neuronal plasticity and the balance between cortical excitation and inhibition5. This is the first-ever hormonal interventional study to test the neurosteroid withdrawal hypothesis in humans using visual LTP and electroencephalography (EEG). 

Methods

This study employed a within-subject, randomised, repeated-measures, counterbalanced intervention design. Seventy-five healthy female participants were recruited, with 25 participants assigned to each of the three pharmacological intervention cohorts (n=25): oral micronised progesterone (P4), estradiol (E2), and levonorgestrel (LNG). Participants attended two sessions in the mid-follicular phase (days 5–8): a baseline session and a dosing session. A second dosing session was conducted in the perimenstrual phase (1–2 days after menstruation began). Session order was randomised across participants to control for order effects. Visual LTP was induced using high-frequency visual stimulation (Photic tetanus)3. At each session, visually induced LTP was recorded via 64-channel EEG and assessed as changes in the amplitude of visual evoked potential (VEP) after tetanus in the early and late post-tetanus conditions. Plasma concentrations of progesterone, oestradiol, and ALLO have been collected but have yet to be analysed.

Results

Results:  The data from the entire E2 cohort were included in the analysis. ALLO and LNG analyses are underway. There was a significant effect of E2 on LTP, specifically in the P2 component of the VEP during the late post-tetanus time block (F(2,288) = 11.74, p = 0.008, FWE-corrected). P2 enhancement was significantly greater in the follicular baseline session compared to the follicular intervention session (t(288) = 4.11, p = 0.014 FWE-corrected). Similarly, the perimenstrual session exhibited significantly greater P2 potentiation compared to the follicular intervention session (t(288) = 4.68,  p = 0.001, FWE-corrected).

Conclusion

Discussion: This clinical trial examined brain sensitivity to administered hormonal neurosteroids in the mid-follicular and perimenstrual phases of the menstrual cycle in healthy females. In the E2 cohort, VEP modulation was suppressed during the follicular intervention session compared to both follicular baseline and perimenstrual intervention sessions, despite both phases being characterised by similarly low serum oestradiol and progesterone concentrations4. This distinction suggests that receptor dynamics mediating sensitivity to oestradiol may be crucial in shaping visual LTP. Given prior evidence that visual LTP is typically suppressed during the perimenstrual phase, it is possible that long estradiol exposure during the luteal phase leads to upregulation of NMDA receptors4. Under this model, exogenous E2 administered during the perimenstrual phase may act on a greater number of NMDA receptors to enhance visual LTP until it is no longer different from baseline mid-follicular levels. 

By contrast, the follicular phase exhibits relatively stable hormone levels, which might correspond to stable receptor dynamics. However, the mechanism underlying LTP suppression by E2 during this phase remains unclear. To resolve this, a future study will recruit a follow-up E2 cohort with two perimenstrual sessions, a baseline, and a dosing session, to more rigorously test these preliminary observations and the proposed receptor-based hypothesis.
