Introduction

Multivariate predictive models are crucial for enhancing the understanding of complex biological systems and developing innovative, replicable tools for translational medical research. However, the complexity of machine learning (ML) methods and extensive data pre-processing and feature engineering pipelines can lead to overfitting and poor generalizability. An unbiased, reliable evaluation of predictive models necessitates external validation (EV), which involves testing the finalized model on independent data. Despite its critical importance, external validation is often neglected in practice due to the high costs and time required for acquiring additional, independent data. Furthermore, determining the optimal fixed split of a total sample size into discovery (training) and external validation sets is extremely challenging to determine a priori.
To maximize credibility, we propose that model discovery and external validation should be explicitly separated by a public disclosure (e.g., pre-registration) of feature processing steps and final model weights, defining the concept of "registered models”. Furthermore, we introduce a novel approach called "AdaptiveSplit" to optimize the trade-off between efforts spent on model discovery and external validation in studies with prospective data acquisition. AdaptiveSplit1 is implemented as a Python package and utilizes a flexible, dynamically calculated stopping rule, which allows to determine the ideal discovery/external validation split of the data during prospective data acquisition. The rule and its associated model hyperparameter are then pre-registered before data acquisition ends.

Methods

The stopping rule implements a dynamic data allocation strategy designed to replace traditional fixed sample splits in prospective predictive modeling studies (see Figure 1). This adaptive strategy is iteratively evaluated during the data acquisition and model discovery phase. This rule determines the optimal point to cease training and allocate the remaining sample size budget to the external validation set. The current decision is governed by two integrated criteria: a Performance-rule, detecting well-powered situations, where adding more training data comes with negligible benefits, and a Power-rule, which ensures that the resulting external validation sample will have sufficient statistical power to conclusively reject the null hypothesis of zero predictive performance. The statistical power is estimated using a robust, two-stage resampling method combining bootstrapping and permutation testing. The procedure involves repeatedly resampling the validation data (bootstrapping) and calculating the observed correlation. For each resample, the true outcomes are randomly shuffled (permutation testing) to generate a null distribution of correlations. The bootstrap-specific p-value is then derived by comparing the observed correlation to this null distribution. The final statistical power is the proportion of these bootstrap p-values that are less than the significance level, quantifying the sample's ability to detect meaningful model performance.
 The stopping rule can utilize one of these criteria, both of them or can be user-customized in the AdaptiveSplit package (prior to data acquisition). In our work, we compare the AdaptiveSplit methodology against conventional, fixed data splitting strategies (80/20%, 50/50%, 90/10%, 75/25% and 70/30%) to determine whether these rule-of-thumbs based strategies are generally outperformed by the AdaptiveSplit method and highlight the pros and cons of every strategy. We evaluate the stopping rule, based only on the Power-rule with a minimum power accepted of 0.8, on four publicly available datasets. The Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE)2, The Human Connectome Project (HCP)3, the Information eXtraction from Image (IXI)4, and the Breast Cancer Wisconsin (BCW)5 datasets,  for a grand total of over 3,000 participants.

Results

Our empirical analysis confirmed the efficacy of the proposed adaptive splitting approach, demonstrating its ability to successfully identify the optimal stopping point for acquiring training data. This strategy consistently maintained a favorable compromise, maximizing both the predictive performance and the external validation power across all tested sample size budgets. In all four samples, the applied models achieved statistically significant predictive performance at much lower sample sizes than the total dataset size, indicating that all datasets were sufficiently powered for the analysis. Consequently, we focused our reported results on realistic scenarios, omitting testing sample sizes that were either too low (where no strategy yielded significant performance) or too high (where performance plateaued across all strategies), thereby allowing for meaningful comparison between the splitting techniques. 
Upon training with the full sample size and cross-validation, the models delivered strong results: functional brain connectivity from the HCP dataset explained 13% of the variance in cognitive abilities; structural MRI data in the IXI dataset explained 48% of the variance in age; classification accuracy for autism diagnosis in the ABIDE dataset reached 65.5%; and classification accuracy for breast cancer diagnosis in the BCW dataset was 92%. When comparing splitting strategies, the adaptive approach provided external validation performances comparable to the commonly used Pareto (80/20%) or similar splits (75/25% and 70/30%) in most cases. However – especially in case of low total sample sizes budgets, static splits with low external validation size (90/10% and Pareto splits) often gave inconclusive results during external validation (p>0.05), despite good model performance (red and green lines on Figure 2). The adaptive splitting strategy consistently delivered sufficient statistical power for external validation in most circumstances by dynamically adjusting the training/validation ratio. For low total sample sizes, it retained a larger proportion for external validation (up to 79%) to ensure sufficient power (at the cost of slightly lower overall predictive power), while for large sample sizes, it allowed the model to benefit from larger training samples, retaining as little as 8% for external validation. The half-split (50/50%) approach assigned the least data to training, typically resulting in a smaller effect size (See Figure 2, for the comparison regarding the 75/25% and the 70/30% splits see 1). 
These findings suggest that in research where the expected predictive performance is low, researchers should either utilize the proposed adaptive procedure or prioritize a relatively large pre-fixed external validation sample. Ultimately, when focusing on successful, conclusive external validations, the proposed adaptive strategy provided performance comparable to the alternative fixed splitting strategies, but crucially, it offers solid guarantees for the success of the external validation phase independent of the sample size budget.

Conclusion

The combination of the proposed "registered models" design and the AdaptiveSplit methodology provides a robust, transparent, and dynamic framework for predictive modeling studies. This novel design and the AdaptiveSplit software package directly contribute to addressing critical issues such as replicability, effect size inflation, and generalizability that plague complex machine learning research. The framework offers a practical and scientifically rigorous solution for optimizing the allocation of resources in prospective predictive studies, thereby enhancing the scientific validity of ML models in translational research.
