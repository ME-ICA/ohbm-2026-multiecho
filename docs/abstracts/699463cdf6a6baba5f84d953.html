Introduction

White matter microstructure plays a critical role in central neural regulation through myelinated axonal pathways. Large-scale diffusion MRI studies have identified numerous genetic variants influencing white matter integrity, many of which colocalize with neurological disorders such as glioma and stroke.1 Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is characterized by ventricular diastolic dysfunction and is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder involving autonomic nervous system dysregulation and neuroinflammatory processes.2 Disruption of white matter microstructure, reflected by reduced fractional anisotropy, may impair central autonomic control, promoting sympathetic overactivation and downstream activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, thereby exacerbating myocardial fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction. Conversely, preserved microstructural integrity in specific white matter regions may confer protection against HFpEF by maintaining autonomic homeostasis. Moreover, genetic determinants of white matter microstructure may interact with HFpEF susceptibility genes, jointly shaping disease risk. In summary, investigating the causal and genetic links between white matter microstructure and HFpEF offers a novel brain–heart crosstalk perspective for disease risk prediction, early intervention, and therapeutic target identification.

Methods

This study adopted a multi-omics framework to integrate Mendelian randomization (MR) and transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS). MR analysis utilized genome-wide significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with white matter metrics from the UK Biobank and HFpEF from MVP and HERMES database, employing the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method and generalized summary-data MR (GSMR) for analysis. Transcriptomic integration employed four approaches: Multi-marker Analysis of Genomic Annotation (MAGMA) for gene-set enrichment analysis; Joint-Tissue Imputation PrediXcan (JTI-PrediXcan) for tissue-specific expression prediction; Sparse Multi-Tissue Imputation Xcan (SMulTiXcan) for cross-tissue meta-analysis; and Fine-mapping of Causal Gene Sets (FOCUS) for Bayesian fine-mapping. Comorbidity-related genes were validated using Functional Summary-based Imputation (FUSION) and prioritized based on the Polygenic Priority Score (PoPS). Single-cell spatial transcriptomics (sc-ST) was performed on E16.5 embryonic mice, and tissue-specific expression profiles were mapped via genetically informed spatial mapping (gsMap).

Results

MR showed that the mean fractional anisotropy (FA) of the corpus callosum body (OR_IVW = 0.791, 95% CI = 0.706–0.887, P_FDR = 0.0116), the first FA component of the corpus callosum body (OR_IVW = 0.794, 95% CI = 0.705–0.894, P_FDR = 0.0122), and the mean FA of the fornix (OR_IVW = 1.204, 95% CI = 1.077–1.346, P_FDR = 0.0483) exerted causal effects on HFpEF risk. TWAS identified comorbid genes, which were further validated using the FUSION framework and spatial localization analysis. After correcting for false discovery rates, we confirmed the presence of significant genome-wide comorbid genes associated with the brain-heart axis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that abnormalities in white matter microstructure contribute to HFpEF through neuroinflammatory pathways and autonomic nervous system regulatory hubs, linking central neural dysregulation to systemic cardiovascular and metabolic dysfunction. Targeting brain–heart crosstalk pathways may therefore help reduce HF progression and support precision risk stratification and therapeutic strategies for HFpEF.
