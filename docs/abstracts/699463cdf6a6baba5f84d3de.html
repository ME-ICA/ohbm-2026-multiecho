Introduction

Humans are social creatures and while the bulk of past neuroimaging work has taken place in solitary settings, much of what we call living takes place in the company of others. Given the breadth of social experience, a significant part of understanding how the brain behaves socially is developing useful abstractions: tools and methods that strike the right balance between fine-grained neural responses to stimuli, and broader concepts in social settings.

Methods

In this study, we identified patterns of network engagement and brain complexity from 23 pairs of participants (dyads) in a series of resting state tasks: solo rest in separate rooms, rest in the same room with and without visual contact with partners, and rest in the same room with 1m or 3m of distance between partners. Using brain data collected via EEG as well as physiological measures (eye tracking, facial recordings, and body movements), we compared the different states using multi-scale entropy, hidden Markov modelling (HMM, Vidaurre et al., 2016) and partial least squares (PLS; McIntosh et al., 1996). Results

We found different patterns of complexity and network engagement based on proximity and visual status. When participants could see each other, complexity was highest at fine temporal scales, indicating local brain processing. These measures were further linked to eye tracking and facial recording data.

In network space, visual conditions implicated bilateral temporal, left temporal-parietal, and right frontal networks while solo rest and non visual tasks implicated inferior temporal and pericentral networks. Eye measures were positively correlated with brain state occupancy as well as with between-network transitions at close proximity. Facial measures were correlated with between-network transitions at a distance. 

Conclusion

We conclude that the salience of physiological information changes with proximity to others, and discuss future directions of multi-modal social neuroscience work, particularly with clinical populations.
