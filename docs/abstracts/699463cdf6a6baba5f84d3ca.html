Introduction

Low-rank outer-product matrix decompositions allow the discovery of patterns and structure in data and are widely used in brain imaging. Examples include PCA and ICA for analysing resting state FMRI data [1], or Non-negative matrix factorisation to model dMRI-based connectivity data [2]. Joint low-rank decompositions are a set of methods for finding common patterns across different data matrices and examples in brain imaging include linked-ICA [3], or Partial Least Squares [4]. But these joint analyses require the data matrices to share at least one of their dimensions, i.e. that they can be concatenated (e.g. spatially or temporally for FMRI data).

Here we propose a new analysis method, PathFinder, that enables co-analysis of disparate datasets that do not necessarily share dimensions (e.g. cortical voxels or resting state time courses of different species - Fig1A). This can enable joint estimation of common patterns across different domains (modalities, species, scales, etc.). The key idea is that as long as pairs or subgroups of matrices do share some dimension, and that there are one or more paths that link across the data matrices, a global joint decomposition is possible. This then provides us with a common space for co-analysing different species and modalities, and can be used to make predictions of missing data (a.k.a. matrix completion, à la Netflix). 

Several common brain imaging analysis methods can be cast within the PathFinder model. Here in addition to simulations and an application in retinotopy task FMRI (Fig 2A,B), we show that the dual regression approach for resting-state FMRI analysis of group and individual data also fits the PathFinder framework (Fig1B and 2C).

Methods

We denote each dataset as a matrix Xd,m where d ∈ {1,2,...D} indexes the domain (e.g. species) and m ∈ {1,2,...M} indexes the modality. Each matrix Xd,m has dimensions rd x cm, so that matrices within domain d share rd rows, and matrices within modality m share cm columns. Not all domain-modality combinations necessarily exist. We define M={ (d, m) | Xd,m exists} as the set of available matrices, with Md and Mm denoting available modalities for domain d and available domains for modality m, respectively.

Results

Simulations.

We simulated matrices Xd,m spanning two domains and three modalities where d=1,2 and m=1,2,3. X(0,2) and X(1,1) were marked as missing data. The pathfinder algorithm reconstructed the unobserved data perfectly from the estimated domain factor A1, A2 and S1, S2, S3. (Fig. 2A)

Retinotopy Task Stimuli Recovery.

We applied Pathfinder to HCP 7T retinotopy data, treating spatial locations (cortical surface or visual field) as domains and task time series as modalities (Fig. 2B). Different retinotopy tasks (e.g., rotating wedge, expanding ring) constitute separate modalities. Using this arrangement, Pathfinder was able to recover the temporal pattern of a held-out stimulus.

Dual Regression Analysis

Pathfinder can perform dual regression by arranging the data matrices as shown in Fig. 1B to recover both group-level spatial maps and individual-level spatial and temporal components (Fig. 2C). The resulting spatial maps show strong correspondence with those obtained from standard dual regression.

Conclusion

We propose PathFinder, a matrix decomposition framework for finding shared components across disparate datasets that need not share dimensions. This approach enables a range of applications, including joint analysis of multimodal data across species, hierarchical modelling at group and individual levels, and recovery of spatiotemporal series.
