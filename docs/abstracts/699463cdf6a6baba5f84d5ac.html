Introduction

There has been an increase in fMRI datasets that sample a large number of naturalistic stimuli1, which, among many other uses, have led to more advances in encoding models for brain function or served as training data for novel machine learning approaches. In most cases, however, these studies often only examine only a single task state. Of course, other work has explored dozens or even over 100 tasks2, however in each task the stimulus, and therefor the bottom-up input, was radically different, making it impossible to explore how unique top-down contributions produced the measured brain responses. Efforts that combine several tasks with identical simple stimuli (or equivalently, several stimuli with identical tasks) find that input or stimulus variability does dominate signal variance in early and higher level visual areas3. In prefrontal regions this effect is reversed, but within subject variance is much larger.

What is missing is an intensive exploration of the interaction between naturalistic, stimulus driven, bottom-up signals and the top-down feedback from a relatively large number of varying high-level task demands4. Here I have densely sampled a single subject, over 10 scanning sessions at 7T and explored a task design that presents identical sets of naturalistic stimuli (20 videos, 5s in duration) with 21 questions asked of every video (fixation maintained unless otherwise instructed). This has produced a large dataset that in one view contains matched input (due to fixation) while only the state, as manipulated by the “task question” or prompt, has changed. It equivalently can be examined from the perspective of task demands remaining fixed while the stimulus varies.

Methods

Thus far, a single subject completed 10 sessions at the MRI. The primary task is here termed the matched/varied + prompt/stimulus (MVPS) task. Prior to video presentation (singularly or in sets of 5), the question text, or “prompt”, was shown around the fixation point. Videos were repeated 3 times/per task, 5s in duration and taken from the Ego4D collection5 (Figure 1A). Twenty videos were selected on the basis of maximizing distance of CLIP embeddings.

The subject was instructed to perform the task mentally, elaborating the answer with internal speech – as the “task” or prompt instructions varied, example: “Describe the general activity being performed?” (See Figure 1B for full list). No button presses were recorded, except that the beginning of each run which allowed the subject to indicate readiness. All possible pairs of tasks and stimuli were shown – that is, every task (21 total) was evaluated on every video stimulus (20 total), with each pairing repeated 3 times.

Whole brain BOLD data was acquired (1.6mm iso, 1s TR, MB5, R2) over 45 separate runs, with reverse phase images to correct for distortion and MP2RAGE anatomical images. Other collected data, not considered here, include 30 runs of Multi Domain Task Battery (MDTB), with 24 tasks each repeated 10 times, 5 runs of the Efficient Multifunction fMRI Localizer (EMFL), 10 resting state scans and a breath hold scan.

Anatomical data was processed through FreeSurfer to generate cortical surfaces. Functional data was processed using AFNI and ANTs, and included slice timing, motion correction, distortion correction, non-linear alignment across runs and sessions to correct for any remaining subtle distortions along with alignment to anatomy. Data were scaled to have a mean of 100 prior to the GLM. No denoising or smoothing was performed.

For MVPS, a conventional GLM (polynomials up to 6th order, motion estimates and canonical HRF) was used to fit each task+stimulus pairing individually (i.e. fitting to 3 repeats spread over the study), generating 420 betas-of-interest. Given the design matrix size (17140 timepoints x 936 columns, with motion regressors and polynomials included), in house code was used to perform GPU-accelerated GLSQ, validated on a subset of the data against AFNI 3dREMLfit.

Task Responsive Map: To explore how each task contributed to brain activity, I applied a reasonable but arbitrary threshold to each task contrast (the average response across all stimuli per each task) of p<0.001, 5 voxel clusters to produce 21 masks of task-responsive voxels (positive responses only). I then summed these masks to determine how many tasks contributed to activity in each brain voxel.

Searchlight Decoding: I projected the GLM betas to the cortical surface (averaging over cortical depth) and estimated cross-validated decoding accuracy (8 folds), using nilearn, for either stimulus prediction or task prediction using a surface searchlight (6mm). Adjacency was determined on the mid gray surface using geodesic distance. Labels were shuffled 1000 times and the entire searchlight was repeated to obtain an estimate of the null distribution per vertex.

Representational Similarity Analyses (RSA): As a first approach, the task instructions, as seen by the participant, were embedded using the pretrained Qwen-4B large language model yielding vector representations of the text. From these vectors, I created a candidate representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM, 21x21) using pairwise cosine distance. After projecting the betas to the fsaverage surface, I used rsatoolbox to perform RSA, calculating the correlation, using each Glasser Atlas ROIs for each hemisphere, between an RDM derived from the betas and the Instruction RDM.

Results

Data quality is high, and even in the absence of any denoising, strong activation is found throughout the brain in response to the task with significant voxels confined to cortical and subcortical grey matter.

Task responsive voxels: Figure 2A shows the ~91k voxels that were above threshold. Unsurprisingly, all tasks produced significant activity in the visual cortex, along with distributed islands in parietal and frontal areas (~18k voxels). More interestingly, even more voxels, distributed across the cortex, were marked as active during only 1 task (~21k). These were distributed across the 21 unique tasks – no one task was responsible for more than 20% of the single task responsive voxels. Positive responses are also well distributed across subcortical regions (i.e. LGN, pulvinar) and the cerebellum. Evidence for fixation compliance is visible in the data itself, as there is a clear boundary in the visual cortices which transitions from 21 (central vision) to only 2 tasks (peripheral areas), corresponding to the 2 tasks which manipulated fixation.

Searchlight decoding. Significantly above chance (0.05) stimulus decoding was achieved in 84% of vertices (above 0.20 in 27%, Figure 2B), with greatest decoding in early and higher-level visual areas. In frontal regions, decoding was near chance. Task decoding was significantly above chance (0.048) in 100% of searchlights (above 0.24 in 66%), but accuracy was lowest in early visual areas.

RSA: Correlations between the Instruction RDM were high across the cortex, with 105 parcels in the left hemisphere and 85 parcels in the right hemisphere having r>0.32 (p<0.001, corrected for 360 parcels). This ROI based approach overlaps with many areas with high accuracy in both the searchlight decoding cases.

Conclusion

Tasks contributed to unique variance despite identical input. This is clear in that a larger proportion of voxels were significantly responsive to only a single task compared to those that were active due to all tasks (which included extensive visual areas). Of course, there is substantial overlap even outside of visual areas and this can be investigated to determine, in this dataset, what tasks contributed to the greatest brain response variability to guide future work. The potential of the task variability is clear from the large number of voxels found significant in 18 of the tasks that combined fixation with a question (~80,000), relative to the pure fixation case (~30,000).

The accuracy maps from the searchlight show an inversion in the distribution of highest accuracies between task and stimulus decoding. Unsurprisingly, visual stimuli decoding is strongest in visual areas, including the ventral temporal cortex. Task decoding performed remarkably well, with above chance decoding in every searchlight. The highest decoding accuracy is found in areas that showed the greatest variety in the task responsiveness analysis above.

The RSA approach extends these findings and shows that the task demands are encoded as structured responses despite identical stimuli, and this is present throughout the brain. The neural representations of task state are organized in a way that mirrors the semantic similarity structure of the task instructions, despite their short length. These above chance regions are outside of narrowly defined executive hubs and was strongest in regions that also showed high task decoding accuracy, suggesting that task-discriminative activity patterns are organized according to the semantic relationships between task prompts. The obvious next steps for this approach include comparing against several alternative models, and understanding how these models contribute to the explainable variance3. Also, the data will be explored after removing the 2 non-fixation conditions to confirm that these effects hold for effectively identical bottom-up input. These RSA and decoding results also need to be placed in the context of individual resting state networks and the findings from the MDTB, potentially as an approach to understand sources of subject-specific variance.

There are several limitations of the present work. Despite the large data collection effort, this pilot work only showed 21 unique tasks and 20 unique stimuli to a single participant. Even with efforts to increase variability in tasks (using CLIP embedding distance to select the most distant videos from the Ego4D dataset) 20 videos represent a very small portion of a perceptual space! The multiplicative nature of designing a study around matched tasks and stimuli presents and remains a large challenge. Pilot datasets like this one (which will be shared) can serve to test hypothesis about optimal stimulus selectivity. While the current findings are expected to generalize, further work is needed to determine what patterns are stable between individuals and determine the magnitude of effects from individual variability. In addition, no button presses were recorded. Future versions of this approach may benefit from requiring button responses and performing a more comprehensive sampling of “task-demand space”.
