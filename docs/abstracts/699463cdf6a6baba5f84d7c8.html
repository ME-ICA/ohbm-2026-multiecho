Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) is an established treatment for Parkinson’s disease (PD).1 Although highly effective for motor symptoms, STN-DBS is thought to be associated with a risk of adverse cognitive effects, particularly in individuals with pre-existing cognitive vulnerability.2

Given the central role of the hippocampus in memory processes, structural changes in this region are a point of interest when evaluating the cognitive effects of STN-DBS. To date, longitudinal MRI studies examining hippocampal volume after STN-DBS have suggested postoperative atrophy.3,4 Notably, one study reported annualized hippocampal volume loss of approximately 14–15% using manual segmentation in a small unilateral STN-DBS cohort scanned an average of 15 months after implantation.3 A similar study also reported hippocampal atrophy following STN-DBS, though at lower rates.4 Both studies were limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up intervals, and the absence of comparison groups of PD patients without DBS, making it unclear whether these changes reflect PD progression, postoperative factors, or a specific effect of stimulation.

To address these gaps, we conducted a retrospective comparison of longitudinal hippocampal volume change in a large cohort of STN-DBS patients relative to two PD control groups without DBS, using harmonized segmentation methods and mixed-effects modeling.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective longitudinal analysis of hippocampal volume change in three cohorts of individuals with Parkinson’s disease (PD). The STN-DBS cohort consisted of 76 patients who underwent implantation at Toronto Western Hospital between 2003 and 2018. Baseline T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired 1–2 days after surgery, and follow-up scans were available across a wide range of intervals (mean 916 days, range 104–4958). The comparison groups included 124 PD participants from the Ontario Neurodegenerative Disease Research Initiative (ONDRI) and 244 PD participants from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI), each with longitudinal T1-weighted MRI collected under standardized acquisition protocols.

All MRI data underwent non-uniformity correction (N3) and skull stripping using a consistent preprocessing pipeline. Hippocampal volumes were estimated using SynthSeg+, a deep-learning segmentation tool chosen for its robustness to heterogeneous imaging parameters and distortions produced by DBS leads. Segmentation accuracy was validated in 52 randomly selected scans from the DBS and PPMI cohorts using manual hippocampal tracing based on the Pruessner protocol. Intra-rater, inter-rater, and SynthSeg+ ICCs exceeded 0.80 for all conditions, confirming high reliability. Volumes deviating more than ±3% from baseline were visually inspected to confirm segmentation quality.

Mixed-effects linear models were used to estimate daily hippocampal volume change for each cohort, with fixed effects including baseline age, intracranial volume (ICV), and number of days since baseline. These predictors were chosen based on preliminary models identifying age and ICV as significant contributors to hippocampal volume across groups. Separate models were fit for left and right hippocampi. Annualized atrophy rates were computed from the fixed-effect estimates using standard linear conversion.

To assess variability in hippocampal change, Levene’s tests compared volume-change variance between DBS patients and each control cohort. Variance was assessed both in the full longitudinal dataset and in the subset of patients scanned within the first two years (≤730 days) after baseline, reflecting the period in which variability appeared most pronounced.

To determine whether hippocampal volume change was associated with memory outcomes, verbal memory measures were harmonized across cohorts. DBS patients completed the California Verbal Learning Test–II (CVLT-II), ONDRI participants the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT), and PPMI participants the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test–Revised (HVLT-R). To ensure temporal alignment with MRI, only assessments within one year of the corresponding scan were included. Change scores were computed as follow-up minus baseline for both hippocampal volume and memory performance. Spearman correlations evaluated associations between left hippocampal volume change and memory change within each cohort. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests.

Results

Mixed-effects models revealed significant longitudinal hippocampal atrophy in all three cohorts. Annualized atrophy rates were approximately 1% bilaterally across STN-DBS, ONDRI, and PPMI patients. Rates did not differ meaningfully between DBS and non-DBS groups. In the STN-DBS cohort, fixed-effect estimates for time were small and negative, consistent with gradual age- and disease-related loss. This pattern matched the control cohorts, which demonstrated similar daily atrophy estimates and annual volume conversions. Across all groups, baseline age and intracranial volume were significant predictors of hippocampal size, whereas imaging sequence, stimulation laterality, PD onset side, cognitive status, disease duration, UPDRS III scores, sex, and education were not significant contributors after correction.

Variance analyses revealed greater inter-individual variability in hippocampal volume change in the STN-DBS cohort, particularly within the first two years after implantation. Levene’s tests confirmed significantly higher variance in the DBS group compared with both ONDRI and PPMI within the early postoperative window (p < 0.001). When including longer-term follow-up, variance differences were reduced, particularly in the right hippocampus. Despite this increased variability, mean atrophy rates in DBS patients did not exceed those of the control cohorts, and no clinical or demographic predictors explained the extreme upper or lower quartiles of annualized volume change.

Visual inspection of individual volume trajectories confirmed this pattern: a subset of DBS patients demonstrated apparent early postoperative increases or decreases that were larger than typical PD variability, yet these fluctuations were not systematic and did not persist across time. Six DBS patients with extreme annualized changes had follow-up scans acquired as part of a reduced-slice fMRI protocol, suggesting that scan quality and slice count may have contributed to segmentation variability, although follow-up manual checks did not reveal overt segmentation errors.

Associations between left hippocampal volume change and verbal memory outcomes were limited. In the DBS cohort, a moderate positive correlation was found between volume change and total CVLT-II score change (p = 0.02). No significant associations emerged for delayed recall or for the right hippocampus. In ONDRI, a weak positive correlation was observed with RAVLT immediate recall (p = 0.019). No significant correlations were found in PPMI, and delayed recall measures were unaffected across all groups. T-tests comparing baseline and follow-up memory scores revealed significant delayed-recall decline only for CVLT-II in DBS patients and RAVLT in ONDRI participants. Overall, memory performance remained largely stable, and relationships with hippocampal volume change were inconsistent and small in magnitude.



Conclusion

This study provides the largest longitudinal assessment of hippocampal volume in STN-DBS patients and the first to include PD comparison groups without DBS. Across all cohorts, annualized hippocampal atrophy rates were approximately 1%, consistent with expected PD-related and age-related decline. Critically, STN-DBS patients did not exhibit accelerated hippocampal atrophy relative to PD controls. Although early postoperative variance in hippocampal change was greater in DBS patients, this variability did not reflect a systematic volume-loss process and diminished with longer follow-up.

Associations between hippocampal volume change and verbal memory outcomes were limited and inconsistent. Memory performance remained largely preserved across cohorts, with only small changes observed in delayed recall in DBS and ONDRI groups. These findings suggest that hippocampal structural change after STN-DBS is modest, similar to that of PD without DBS, and not strongly linked to cognitive decline.

Taken together, the data challenge prior small-sample imaging studies reporting substantial hippocampal atrophy after STN-DBS and support the structural and cognitive safety of STN stimulation. They also highlight the importance of using large, well-controlled longitudinal datasets and robust segmentation methods when evaluating DBS-related brain changes. This work helps clarify the structural consequences of STN-DBS and informs clinical decision-making for patients with cognitive vulnerability.
