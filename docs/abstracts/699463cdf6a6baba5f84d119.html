Introduction

Concussion, or mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), in youth and athletes remain a diagnostic and scientific challenge, particularly due to variability in injury biomechanics and recovery trajectories. While clinical assessment tools guide management, the underlying neural mechanisms and time course of recovery are not well understood. Advanced neuroimaging, such as resting-state fMRI, has revealed functional brain alterations following mTBI; however, the uncontrolled nature of real-world concussions make it difficult to link specific biomechanical events to imaging biomarkers. To overcome this limitation, the pig has emerged as a translational model that replicates key clinical, behavioral, and physiological aspects of human concussion. The model allows for standardized head injury and longitudinal MRI assessment. In our previous study1, eight concussed collegiate football players and eleven controls underwent longitudinal rs-fMRI and T1 MRI scans at 24 hours, 1 week, and 1 month post-injury. The concussed group exhibited reduced connectivity in the default mode network (DMN), with a delayed and prolonged disruption peaking at 7 days and only partial recovery by 1 month as shown in Figure1. Connectivity in the default mode and salience networks was significantly reduced, and brain entropy remained elevated, peaking at 1-week post-injury, indicating persistent functional disorganization despite symptom resolution. Our recent study established a closed head mTBI model in adolescent Yucatan pigs that reproduces MRI features consistent with human concussion2.

Methods

Twelve adolescent Yucatan minipigs (16 weeks old) were randomly assigned to either an mTBI (n=6) or sham (n=6) group under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Michigan State University. The pigs underwent a controlled closed-head injury using an Electromagnetically Driven Impactor (EMDI). Structural MRI scans (T1, DTI) were acquired using a Siemens 1.5T scanner at 1 and 3 months post-injury to assess longitudinal changes in brain volume and white matter integrity.

Conclusion

In our studies, both structural and functional data suggest a delayed recovery trajectory following concussion. In the pig model, FA in the corpus callosum increased significantly from 1 to 3 months of post-injury. Given the corpus callosum's critical role in interhemispheric communication and its structural support of the DMN, this progressive FA increase may reflect neuroplastic processes such as axonal growth. In athletes and individuals with sports related concussions, rising FA values may reflect underlying processes such as white matter tract reorganization, or glial scar formation2. Supporting this, advanced imaging techniques like neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging suggest that injury can lead to increased neurite density over time3.

Our future studies will integrate resting-state fMRI of the controlled pig concussion model to link structural and functional brain changes. This will allow us to track how white matter alterations such as the longitudinal FA increases observed in the corpus callosum relate to evolving network recovery. We expect to identify concussion related deficits in networks homologous to those observed in humans, such as the default mode network. Previous studies in pigs with traumatic brain injury have already demonstrated acute reductions in connectivity within visual, sensorimotor, and executive networks post-injury4. By aligning rs-fMRI connectivity patterns in pigs with our earlier findings of delayed DMN recovery in athletes, we aim to establish a causal structure function trajectory that mirrors human concussion, supporting the model’s utility for biomarker discovery and recovery monitoring.
