Introduction

Heschl’s gyrus (HG) contains the primary auditory cortex medially and parts of the secondary auditory areas laterally. Its morphology varies strongly in humans and is often visible as an omega- or heart-shaped structure, due to partial or complete duplications linked to an intermediate transverse sulcus1. These duplications are frequent in healthy adults (present in one or the other hemisphere in 60% of cases) and have been associated with differences in cortical thickness and planum temporale morphology1.

Functionally, the HG transforms sensory frequency representations into higher-order auditory features such as speech cues, pitch, melody, rhythm, and timbre2. Hemispheric differences have been proposed, with the left HG preferentially processing temporal information and the right HG more spectral information1, forming the basis for lateralised language and music functions2. The developmental origins of HG morphological variability remain unclear with evidence for both genetic contributions3 and experience-dependent plasticity2.

Great apes offer a valuable yet under-explored comparative model for clarifying the relationship between HG morphology and auditory function. Unlike macaques, whose primary auditory cortex lacks a distinct gyral landmark, great apes have a more gyrified cortex and produce more complex vocalisations. Preliminary reports in four individuals indicate two strong, one weak and one absent HG elevation4, suggesting that great ape HG morphology represents an evolutionary intermediate between monkeys and humans. However, potential duplications have not yet been systematically investigated in any non-human great ape species. Determining whether HG duplication is unique to humans or shared across hominids is essential to reconstruct the evolution of auditory cortical organisation and the neural substrates of human speech and music.

Methods

We manually segmented the HGs and quantified their morphological structure in a developmental and cross-species sample of 39 post-mortem brains from wild and captive great apes from African field sites, sanctuaries and European zoos collected in the Evolution of Brain Connectivity (EBC) project. This allows us to address three key questions: (1) whether great ape HG morphology is more similar to that of monkeys or humans, (2) whether their anatomy provides insight into the unclear structure-function relationship in humans; and (3) whether HG gyrification develops with age.

Population:

29 chimpanzee brains (13 females, age range: 1-56 years) 4 gorillas (3 females; age range: 33-43), 3 orang-utans (3 females; age range: 19-50), 3 bonobos (3 females; age range: 5-31) were analysed in this study. The post-mortem interval (PMI) before fixation was 12 ± 8 h for brains from field sites and 9 ± 7 h for brains from sanctuaries and zoos. Tissue quality, integrity, and fixation were good and of a high standard, as assessed by MRI- and histology screenings.

MRI imaging and analysis:

High-resolution structural MRI images were acquired using a 3D FLASH MRI sequence (500 µm isotropic, TR = 50 ms, TE = 9 ms ) in a small-bore 9.4 Tesla Bruker Biospec 94/30 MRI scanner5. To generate different contrasts, data was acquired with multiple flip angles of ϑ = [5, 12.5, 25, 50, 80]°. Brain white matter was segmented using multi-contrast fuzzy c-means clustering, and the white matter surface of the superior temporal plane was reconstructed and visualised for further analysis. All brains were normalised to a common great-ape template (JUNA) using ANTs SyN, and averaged separately for each species (see Figure 1A).

Heschl’s gyrus segmentation:

The HG was segmented as the second most posterior elevation in the lateral sulcus, oriented diagonally. The anterior medial boundary of the HG is marked by the anterior transverse temporal sulcus where it joins the insular circular sulcus, and the posterior lateral limit is Heschl’s sulcus2.

Heschl’s duplication criteria:

The criteria from previous studies1,2 were adapted to non-human great apes to classify HG variability across subjects as either single or duplicated. A straight reference progression line was defined along the axis of Heschl’s gyrus. A second line perpendicular to this axis was placed at the midpoint. If the gyrus was clearly divided by an intermediate sulcus at that intersection point, the HG was classified as duplicated (see Figure 1 B). The occurrence of duplications was calculated by strict application of this definition.

Results

In chimpanzees, 18 of 29 hemispheres showed duplications on the left side and 19 of 29 on the right, resulting in 37 of 58 hemispheres (~2/3) exhibiting duplication. In contrast to humans, only partial duplications with a common medial stem, were observed.

Within chimpanzees, a significant age-related effect was identified for the right hemisphere only. Older individuals were more likely to show HG duplication. Two-sided logistic regression confirmed a significant effect of age on the probability of right-hemisphere duplication (p < 0.0134), following the expected sigmoidal pattern using log-transformed age.

Left side shows a duplication; right side shows a single HG marked by a circle, in a typical chimpanzee displaying both variants. The blue area shows the segmentation of the HG on the white matter surface of the superior temporal plane (turquoise).

Conclusion

Our results depict a view of a more convoluted area in great-apes than described before, with duplications like those found in humans, demonstrating a continuous evolution of the auditory cortex gyrification. This finding contrasts with the absent HG in the monkey (e.g.4). However, unlike humans1, our chimpanzee sample did not show a complete duplication. Therefore, the human HG may have undergone increased gyrification during evolution.

Based on the rich human literature, it is possible that also in chimpanzees a morphological variation of the Heschl’s gyrus indicates variation in functions linked to auditory processing. Duplication of the HG may lead to expansion of the primary auditory cortex. Depending on the specific subfields involved, this expansion could lead to enhanced performance in discriminating pitch variations and temporal and spectral dynamics, which could improve the processing of vocalisations or rhythmic behaviours such as buttress root drumming. Future studies need to examine the possible structure-function relationships of the HG anatomy in great apes.
