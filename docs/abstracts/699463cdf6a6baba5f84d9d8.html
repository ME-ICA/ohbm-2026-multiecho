Introduction

Cognitive flexibility, the ability to shift efficiently between rules or attentional sets, is a core component of executive functioning. It undergoes rapid development during early childhood and is strongly linked to academic achievement1. Most existing paradigms assessing neural processing of cognitive flexibility focus on switching between visual stimulus dimensions such as color and shape2. Developmental neuroimaging studies indicate that children recruit a distributed frontoparietal control network, including dorsolateral, ventrolateral prefrontal and parietal association regions3, but that the modulation and specialization of these regions for switch-related control demands still matures until adulthood4. However, despite the relevance of multimodal integration for real-world learning and communication, little is known about children’s ability to flexibly switch between sensory modalities5. To address this gap, we developed a novel audiovisual switching paradigm in which participants sort stimuli based on either visual or auditory information. Using familiar cross-linguistic cognates ensures that the participants can reliably identify the stimuli independent of their language background.

The present pilot study evaluated the feasibility, task engagement, and initial performance patterns in children and adults. Our goals were to determine whether children can complete the paradigm reliably, whether switch costs are observable at this age, and whether adults show the expected performance advantages. Furthermore, we assessed whether children recruit similar brain networks as adults during task switching, providing preliminary insights into developmental differences in neural processing.

Methods

We assessed cognitive flexibility in adults and children using an audiovisual rule-switching paradigm adapted for children. Each trial began with a visual cue indicating the relevant modality (eye = visual, ear = auditory), followed by simultaneous but incongruent auditory and visual stimuli (e.g. hearing “Pizza” while seeing a picture of “Lego”, or vice versa). Participants categorized the stimulus in the cued modality as either “food” or “toy” via button press. A brief response window and feedback on participants response followed each trial. The task included 49 trials: one unclassified initial trial, followed by 24 switch and 24 nonswitch trials. Trials were classified as nonswitch when the cued modality was the same as on the preceding trial, and as switch when the cued modality changed. Adults additionally completed a short single-modality block (visual-only or auditory-only) beforehand and received slightly different stimuli. However, only the mixed block conditions that were comparable across groups were analyzed.

For the analysis, data from 32 participants was included; 12 healthy adults (21.3-30.4 years; M = 24.48 ± 3.10, 11 female) and 20 healthy children (8.0-13.2 years; M = 11.34 ± 1.56, 9 girls).

Behavioral analyses focused on accuracy and reaction times to quantify switch costs. For each participant, performance on switch versus nonswitch trials was compared, and group-level differences between adults and children were evaluated.

MRI data were collected on a Philips Achieva 3T scanner using a 32-channel head coil. Functional images were acquired with a T2*-weighted gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR = 1.395 s, TE = 35 ms, flip angle = 80°, voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3 mm³, 44 slices, multiband factor = 2). We obtained 416 volumes for adults (single-modality block followed by a mixed block) and 275 volumes for children (mixed block only). High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were obtained using an MPRAGE sequence (TR = 6.96 ms, TE = 3.2 ms, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³). Stimuli were presented via MR-compatible goggles and headphones.

Default preprocessing was performed in SPM12. Pediatric tissue probability maps were applied to enhance segmentation accuracy in children. Framewise displacement was used to quantify head motion between successive volumes, and any volume exhibiting movement greater than 0.9 mm was removed. Participants for whom more than 20% of volumes exceeded this threshold were excluded from further analysis.

The first-level GLMs included two regressors for the nonswitch conditions (one for the visual modality and one for the auditory modality) and two switch regressors (auditory-to-visual and visual-to-auditory). Each of those regressors encompassed the entire stimulus period, comprising cue presentation, stimulus display, and the response window. An additional regressor for the feedback period was included but not further analyzed. At the second level, we conducted group comparisons between adults and children, as well as separate within-group analyses for each age group. Activation results are reported based on a cluster defining threshold of pCDT < .005 and a cluster extent correction of pFWEc < .05.

Results

Reaction times (RTs) were analyzed for correct trials only, and outliers were excluded (values exceeding ±3 x the interquartile range). Both children and adults showed significant switch costs: RTs were slower on switch than nonswitch trials for children (t(18) = -4.01, p < .001) and adults (t(11) = -4.03, p = .002). As expected, children responded overall more slowly than adults on both nonswitch (t(28.0) = -4.81, p < .001) and switch trials (t(26.2) = -4.75, p < .001). However, the magnitude of the switch cost (nonswitch minus switch RT) did not differ significantly between groups (t(28.3) = 1.14, p = .263). In children, mean accuracy was overall high, but lower on switch trials (nonswitch: M = 0.93 ± 0.09; switch: M = 0.88 ± 0.12), with a trend toward reduced accuracy during switching (t(19) = 2.06, p = .053). In contrast, adults performed near ceiling, with minimal variability across conditions (nonswitch: M = 0.99 ± 0.02; switch: M = 0.98 ± 0.03; p = .275). Adults were significantly more accurate than children on both nonswitch (t(20.9) = 3.18, p = .004) and switch trials (t(22.1) = 3.65, p = .001). There was no significant difference in the accuracy of the switch cost between groups (p = .146).

At the neural level, adults showed robust switch-related activation across the frontoparietal cognitive control network. Relative to nonswitch trials, switch trials were associated with increased BOLD responses in bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the inferior frontal junction, as well as in the anterior cingulate cortex and pre-supplementary motor area. In contrast, children showed no significant switch > nonswitch activation. Instead, nonswitch compared with switch trials revealed a trend towards greater activity in sensorimotor (precentral and postcentral gyri), attentional (supramarginal gyrus, parietal operculum), and hippocampal regions (pCDT < .005).

Between group analyses showed that adults exhibited greater switch-related activation than children within the frontoparietal cognitive control network. For both switch > baseline and nonswitch > baseline contrasts, children exhibited greater activation than adults in the bilateral superior frontal gyri. Adults showed greater activation than children across a broad frontoparietal and temporo-occipital network, including superior parietal, opercular, temporal, and occipital regions for both switch and nonswitch versus baseline contrasts.

Conclusion

The present pilot study provides initial insights into children’s and adults’ performance and neural responses during an audiovisual switching task. Both groups demonstrated clear switch costs, although children were substantially slower overall. Notably, the magnitude of children’s switch costs was comparable to that of adults, suggesting that they successfully engaged in modality-based switching despite requiring more time to process the stimuli. However, important developmental differences emerged in the neural mechanisms supporting cross-modal cognitive flexibility.

Adults demonstrated robust switch-related activation across the canonical frontoparietal cognitive control network, including dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, inferior frontal junction, anterior cingulate cortex, and pre-supplementary motor area. This pattern aligns with established models of task-switching4 and the coordinated engagement of these regions during switch trials reflects the mature capacity to flexibly reconfigure attentional priorities and response mappings when task demands change.

Children, however, did not exhibit activation specifically associated with switching, suggesting the absence of specialized neural mechanisms for managing cross-modal task-set transitions. This finding extends beyond prior developmental task-switching studies, which typically report immature but present switch-related activation in children and adolescents4. This absence of switch-specific activation in children may reflect the particularly high demands of cross-modal switching compared to traditional unimodal paradigms. Switching between auditory and visual dimensions requires not only task-set reconfiguration but also fundamental shifts in sensory attention and perceptual weighting. Our findings suggest that this combination of demands exceeds the capacity of children's developing frontoparietal networks, revealing a developmental bottleneck in multimodal cognitive flexibility that has important implications for real-world learning contexts where children must flexibly integrate information across sensory modalities. Instead, children exhibited greater activation during nonswitch trials in sensorimotor, attentional, and hippocampal regions, indicating that maintaining the same task rule may place higher cognitive demands especially on attentional and memory systems in young children, while neural networks supporting cognitive flexibility may still be immature and not yet reliably engaged during switching.

Between-group analyses showed stronger recruitment of frontoparietal and temporo-occipital regions in adults compared with children for both trial types, whereas children exhibited greater activation in bilateral superior frontal regions across both switch and nonswitch trials, a pattern characteristic of immature, effortful engagement of prefrontal control for task-set maintenance and overall cognitive demands4. This nonselective recruitment indicates that children engage domain-general cognitive control mechanisms to meet overall task demands rather than deploying specialized neural processes for specific control operations, underscoring the protracted development of functional specialization within prefrontal cortex. The adult advantage across both conditions points to more robust and stable rule representations held in working memory, as well as more efficient sensory attention allocation.

Despite the limited group sizes in the present analyses, which preclude a detailed examination of the maturation of behavioral and neural processes across childhood, the initial results nevertheless highlight developmental differences in both behavioral performance and neural recruitment during audiovisual task switching. Future work may explore whether auditory and visual switching differ in their behavioral profiles or neural patterns, and whether cross-modal task switching poses specific developmental challenges beyond those observed in traditional unimodal paradigms.
