Introduction

Self-evaluation and social comparison play pivotal roles in shaping self-concept, which influences mental and social well-being.1 During young adulthood, a phase marked by identity exploration and social interactions, the role of social comparison becomes particularly prominent in shaping self-evaluations.2 Using trait judgement tasks, prior neuroimaging studies have uncovered a set of brain regions associated with evaluating the self or others, revealing both shared and distinctive neural responses. Specifically, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) is more prominently engaged during self-evaluation, while the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) and temporal parietal junction (TPJ) play a greater role in evaluating others.3 However, it remains unclear whether social comparison engages the same brain regions as those involved in self-evaluation or evaluations of others. Therefore, the first aim of this study was to investigate whether core regions implicated in self-evaluation are also implicated in social comparison. Furthermore, previous research suggests that perspective-taking might result in more overlap between self- and other-related processing.4 However, the relationship between perspective-taking and neural activity during self-evaluation has not yet been examined. Thus, the second aim of this study was to explore the relationship between perspective-taking and neural activation in the dmPFC and bilateral TPJ during self-evaluation, as these areas are involved in both self- as well as other-referential processing.3 

Methods

We included 33 Dutch young-adults (age-range: 19-29, M = 24.26, SD = 2.46), of which 22 identified as female, 10 as male, and one as non-binary. Perspective-taking was evaluated by the perspective-taking subscale of the Dutch version of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI). Furthermore, we conducted an fMRI trait judgment task in which participants were asked to indicate whether a trait adjective described their own personality (self-evaluation condition), whether a trait adjective described their own personality or that of a close friend better (social comparison condition), or whether the letter A was present in the trait adjective (control condition). The task used positive- and negative trait adjectives in the social domain (e.g. “kind” or “jealous”) and the academic domain (e.g. “ambitious” or “lazy”). All MRI data were acquired on a 3.0 Tesla Philips Ingenia CX MRI scanner, with a 32-channel-phased array head coil (Spinoza Center for Neuroimaging; Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands). A total of 368 functional images were obtained during the trait-judgement task using a two-dimensional echo planar imaging-sensitivity encoding sequence. A structural image was acquired using a three-dimensional fast field echo sequence. MRI data were pre-processed and analyzed using SPM12 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). First, the functional images were realigned to the reference image, followed by co-registration of the structural image to the mean functional image acquired after realignment. Next, the images were segmented and transformed into Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. In the last preprocessing step, the normalized functional images were spatially smoothed. During first-level analyses, contrast images were created by comparing both the self-evaluation and social comparison condition to the control condition, and to each other. Next, we performed ROI-analyses in order to investigate the involvement of core regions implicated in self- and other-referential processing (the dmPFC, bilateral TPJ, and vMPFC) in social comparison. Specifically, we first conducted one sample t-tests to assess neural activation in the ROIs during self-evaluation (relative to control) and social comparison (relative to control). Furthermore, to examine differences activity between the self-evaluation and social comparison condition (both relative to the control condition), we conducted a paired sample t-test per ROI. A Bonferroni correction was applied to correct for the multiple comparisons. Finally, to examine the relationship between perspective-taking and neural activation during self-evaluation in the dmPFC, left TPJ, and right TPJ, we performed a linear regression analysis per ROI with perspective-taking as the predictor and neural activation during self-evaluation as the outcome.

Results

First, we examined the involvement of the dmPFC, bilateral TPJ, and vMPFC in self-evaluation and social comparison (see Figure 1). We observed increased activity within the dmPFC (t(32) = 9.99, p < .001), left TPJ (t(32) = 3.17, p = .003), and vmPFC (t(32) = 10.40, p < .001) during self-evaluation relative to the control condition. Also, during social comparison relative to the control condition, we observed increased activity in all four ROIs (dmPFC: t = 10.76, p < .001; left TPJ: t = 5.81, p < .001; right TPJ: t = 5.00, p < .001; vmPFC: t = 12.20, p < .001). Next we assessed differences in activity between the social comparison and self-evaluation condition (both relative to the control condition), and based on previous research we expected increased activity in the dmPFC and bilateral TPJ during social comparison, and increased activity in the vmPFC during self-evaluation.3 Our results provided partial support for our hypotheses, as we observed increased activity during social comparison (relative to control) as compared to self-evaluations (relative to control) in all four ROIs (dmPFC: t = 3.16, p = .003; left TPJ: t = 7.48, p < .001; right TPJ: t = 7.24, p < .001; vmPFC: t = 4.46, p < .001). Second, we performed a linear regression analysis per ROI to examine the relationship between perspective-taking and neural activation during self-evaluation in the dmPFC, left TPJ, and right TPJ. The linear regression analyses revealed that the total perspective-taking score predicted left TPJ activation (b  = 0.35, b = 0.09, SE = 0.04, t = 2.08, p = .046). There was no significant relation between the total perspective-taking score and activation in the dmPFC (b  = 0.12, b = 0.04, SE = 0.06, t = 0.67, p = .508) or right TPJ (b  = 0.29, b = 0.06, SE = 0.04, t = 1.66, p = .107) during self-evaluation relative to the control condition. Conclusion

We examined neural activity during self-evaluation and social comparison, and, in line with our hypothesis, found significantly higher activation in the dmPFC and bilateral TPJ during social comparison as compared to self-evaluation. These results are in line with previous research demonstrating that the dmPFC and bilateral TPJ are more involved in evaluating others.3 Contrary to our predictions, we found higher neural activity in the vmPFC during social comparison as compared to during self-evaluation, despite this area’s typical association with self-referential processing. Furthermore, our results indicated that perspective-taking predicted higher neural activity in the left TPJ during self-evaluation, but not in the right TPJ and dmPFC. Given the role of the left TPJ in social-cognitive processing and other-referential processing, this may suggest that with higher perspective-taking, more social-cognitive or other-referential processes are elicited during self-evaluation. Taken together, our findings suggest that perspective-taking and the neurocognitive mechanisms of self-evaluation and social comparison are connected in young-adults, but further research with a larger sample size is needed to improve our understanding of these processes.
