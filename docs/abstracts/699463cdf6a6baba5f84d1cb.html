Introduction

Female sex hormones play a critical role in shaping brain structure across multiple transition periods, emphasizing the influence of hormonal changes on the brain over time. The menstrual cycle, governed by fluctuations in estradiol (E2) and progesterone (P4), presents a unique model to investigate the brain’s sensitivity to hormonal variation in healthy females. Given the widespread distribution of E2 and P4 receptors across the brain, the hormonal shifts directly affect brain structure and function. Emerging evidence supports the idea that naturally cycling women exhibit dynamic neuroplasticity in response to hormonal shifts1,2. Structural MRI studies indicate cycle-related changes in gray matter volume (GMV), particularly in the hippocampus and amygdala, both highly sensitive to E2 and P4. However, most research has compared the follicular and luteal phases leaving the midcycle phase, marked by peak E2 and rising P4, and often followed by ovulation underexplored.

Methods

Using structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), we assessed GMV and cortical thickness (CT) in 32 healthy women during ovulation (peak E2) and menstruation (low E2 and P4). Region-of-interest (ROI) and exploratory whole-brain analyses were performed, complemented by multiple regression models to assess associations between E2, P4, and brain morphology. Spatial colocalization analyses were further applied to link observed macrostructural changes with receptor density maps.

Results

Predefined ROI analyses targeting the amygdala and hippocampus yielded no significant differences between OV and ME after FDR correction. At an uncorrected threshold, greater volume during OV emerged in the right hippocampus (p = .014) and right amygdala (p = .026). Exploratory whole-brain analyses revealed increased GMV during ovulation in regions including the amygdala, hippocampus, temporal cortex and fusiform gyrus. E2 correlated positively with parietal regions at ovulation, while P4 exhibited phase-dependent associations: with cerebellar and fusiform volumes during ovulation, and with frontal volumes and widespread CT during menstruation. Spatial colocalization with hormone receptor distributions indicated stronger structural changes in regions with higher P4 receptor density. 

Conclusion

Together, these findings demonstrate that endogenous hormonal fluctuations contribute to dynamic, phase-dependent variation in brain morphology across the menstrual cycle. We observed increased total GMV during ovulation, consistent with volumetric expansion during high-hormone phases. Although exploratory regional effects did not survive multiple-comparison correction, their anatomical distribution aligns with prior work showing that hormone-sensitive regions, such as limbic, temporal, visual, and cerebellar areas, exhibit cycle-related structural variability1,2. Crucially, progesterone emerged as the more robust modulator of both GMV and cortical thickness, with phase-specific associations suggesting that P4 contributes to regionally selective neuroplastic adaptation. This pattern supports the view that progesterone plays a central role in shaping short-term structural brain dynamics in healthy women.
