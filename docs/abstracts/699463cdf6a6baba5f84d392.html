Introduction

Structural and functional connectivity provide complementary metrics of brain organization and communication. While structural connectivity maps the physical white matter pathways that connect regions of the brain, functional connectivity measures functional co-activations based on the correlation of blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal time-courses between regions. Throughout the course of the lifespan, and especially during development, structural and functional networks undergo remarkable changes in which experience seems to play a crucial role. In this regard, congenital blindness is a powerful model for investigating the impact of experience. In fact, the lack of visual input from birth has been found to result in large-scale changes both within and beyond visual brain regions. However, it remains unclear whether such reorganization reflects irreversible adaptations to visual deprivation, or flexible experience-dependent mechanisms that could enable recovery. Individuals born with bilateral cataracts and for whom sight was restored later in life present an opportunity to address this question and thus explore whether or not sensitive periods for the development of structural and functional brain connectivity exist. We expected structural and functional connectivity metrics to reflect some persisting alterations in congenital cataract reversal individuals (CC). However, given the observed recovery of vision, we hypothesized that sight restoration would enable partial recovery of typical structural and/or functional brain organization.

Methods

We investigated 20 CC individuals, 8 permanently congenitally blind (CB) individuals, and 18 age-and-sex matched typically sighted controls (SC), all recruited at the L V Prasad Eye Institute (LVPEI) in Hyderabad, India. Magnetic resonance imaging was performed using a 1.5T GE Signa HDxt scanner at the Lucid Medical Diagnostics radiological clinic in Banjara Hills, Hyderabad, India. Image acquisition included a whole-brain diffusion weighted image, resting-state fMRI acquired for both an eyes open (EO) and eyes closed (EC) condition, and a T1-weighted anatomical image. 

Diffusion weighted images were processed using a standard MRTrix (https://www.mrtrix.org/) pipeline, and 10 million streamlines were generated via probabilistic, anatomically constrained tractography. Overlaying Freesurfer-based segmentations of the Destrieux atlas enabled the calculation of a weighted structural connectome where the streamline weights between two regions of interest (ROIs) signify their structural connectivity.

Resting-state functional analyses were performed using a standard functional connectivity toolbox (CONN 1) pipeline. ROIs were defined by Freesurfer-based segmentations of the Destrieux atlas, and directed functional connectivity between regions was calculated via regression.

ROIs were grouped into sub-networks according to neuroanatomical location and function, and the structural and functional connectivity between sub-networks was calculated as the median of all connection weights between ROIs of each sub-network. For example, occipital-sensorimotor connectivity would be the median of all connection weights where one ROI is from the occipital sub-network and one is from the sensorimotor sub-network. This procedure was carried out for a number of a priori defined connections. All sub-networks, and the ROIs they contain, can be found in table 1.

Lastly, connectivity values were compared across groups using independent two-sample t-tests, such that CB and CC individuals were compared with each other, and with their respective matched sighted control groups.

Results

Overall, CB individuals showed a number of significant reductions in structural connectivity for the thalamus and the occipital sub-network compared to normally sighted controls. While some of these reductions were also seen in CC individuals relative to SC subjects, a direct comparison of CC and CB groups revealed that CB individuals exhibited lower connectivity of the thalamus with various sensory regions, including the occipital sub-network. See table 2 for full details.

For CB individuals relative to SC subjects, functional connectivity of the thalamus with various sub-networks was largely increased, while the occipital sub-network showed both increases and decreases in functional connectivity with other sub-networks. Additionally, auditory and visual regions of the temporal lobe exhibited increased connectivity with each other. 

CC individuals displayed both increases and decreases in functional connectivity compared directly with CB individuals. Notably, occipital sub-network connections were increased, and some thalamic connections were decreased in CC compared to CB individuals. However, relative to SC participants, CC individuals still exhibit increased connectivity for some connections. See table 2 for full details.

Conclusion

Overall, CB individuals demonstrated considerably altered structural and functional connectivity. Structurally, deficits may be linked to reduced white matter integrity of major visual tracts as observed in the same subjects,2 while changes in functional connectivity could indicate decoupling of visual and non-visual sensory regions, as well as cross-modal plasticity. Structural and functional connectivity of the thalamus with other regions seems to be additionally altered. After sight restoration, partial recovery of structural and functional connectivity was observed. Specifically, structural connectivity results may be linked to a relative degree of recovery of white matter microstructure in major visual tracts.2 For functional connectivity, visual regions seem to re-integrate with non-visual sensory areas, and thalamic connectivity seemed to partially recover. Ultimately, our results supply evidence for the crucial role of early visual input for both structural and functional connectivity, as well as persisting experience-dependent mechanisms later in life that could enable partial recovery.
