Introduction

Anxiety disorders are thought to arise from disruptions in fronto-temporal circuitry involved in emotion processing1, including amygdala and prefrontal cortex2. Links between these factors have been well-established with traditional non-invasive neuroimaging assessments, however, coarse temporal resolutions (1-2s) and indirect measures limits the understanding of temporal network dynamics underlying emotion dysfunction. Stereo-electroencephalography (sEEG) is a critical tool for localizing the ictal onset zone in patients with treatment-resistant epilepsy and it also provides an unparalleled window into neural circuitry underlying emotions, cognition, and behaviors. Additionally, treatment-resistant epilepsies have a high prevalence of mood and anxiety disorders (i.e., 50%) that are thought to arise from overlapping fronto-temporal network disruption3. Further, although ictal onset in these epilepsies most often occur in medial temporal areas, we hypothesized that propagation to prefrontal regions through this network would lead to diminished top-down control of emotion responses and processing. Thus, the current study utilized sEEG to investigate relatively more precise temporal network dynamics during emotion regulation of threat responses. Based on our model and prior work3,4, we predicted that anxiety severity would be related to regulation of responses to threat occurring earlier within prefrontal (dorsolateral [dlPFC], dorsomedial [dmPFC]) regions and later in amygdala regions.  

Methods

14 patients undergoing sEEG monitoring at the University of Alabama at Birmingham Epilepsy Monitoring Unit were recruited to voluntarily complete an auditory Pavlovian fear conditioning task. Inclusionary criteria included ability to understand and perform the task and surgical monitoring plans that included implanted standard-of-care electrodes within frontal and temporal regions of interest (bilateral ROIs: dlPFC, dmPFC, amygdala). During the Pavlovian fear conditioning task4, one warning cue (700Hz tone, 8s) was paired with a predictable threat (100 db white noise, 0.5s) while a second unpredictable threat was presented identically to predictable threats, but without the warning cue. Participants were instructed to continuously rate, on a scale of 0-100, whether they thought a static sound would occur on a moment-to-moment basis. 25 presentations of each threat condition were arranged in pseudorandomized orders with unique trial sequences for each participant. Auditory-visual stimuli were presented with a standard PC monitor and noise-canceling ear buds. Participants additionally completed the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) to index severity of anxiety symptoms. All sEEG data processing was conducted using standard pipelines included within the Reproducible Analysis and Visualization of intracranial EEG (RAVE) library. To assess each ROI’s response during a 1s window following presentation of threats, log-transformed decibel power within high gamma (40-150 Hz) was calculated as percent signal change from baseline (~0.5 s before threat onset [7.0-7.48s]). Emotion regulation of neural responses to threat were indexed by a diminished threat response to predictable threat compared to unpredictable threat. A linear mixed-effects (LME) model predicting decibel power change following threat presentation was fitted with fixed effects of trial type (Predictable, Unpredictable), time window (early:<550 ms, late >500 ms), and ROI (dlPFC, dmPFC, amygdala), with random intercepts for subject, region, and electrode. Three additional LMEs for each ROI assessed the interaction between BAI scores and time window. 

Results

The results of the initial LME analysis revealed a significant 3-way interaction between Trial type × ROI × Time window: [χ²(4)=14.65, p < 0.005]. Follow up contrasts revealed that the diminished response to predictable compared to unpredictable threats occurred in the early window within dmPFC (-0.09 + 0.03, z = -3.39, p < 0.001) and dlPFC (-0.10 + 0.02, z = -5.50, p < 0.001) and the later window within amygdala (-0.11 + .03, z = -3.54, p < 0.001) and dlPFC (-0.04 + 0.02, z = -2.37, p = 0.02). The three 3 additional LMEs for each ROI that assessed interactions between BAI scores × time window revealed that higher anxiety severity was associated with larger predictable threat responses in the dmPFC and dlPFC only during early Time windows (dmPFC: BAI x Time window, χ²(1)=9.24, p < 0.005; dlPFC: BAI x Time window, χ²(1)=36.99, p < 0.001). Alternatively, increased anxiety severity was not associated with increased predictable threat responses in the amygdala overall (χ²(1)=1.96, p = 0.16) or as a function of individual Time windows (χ²(1)=2.21, p = 0.14).

Conclusion

Refining models of network dysfunction that underly comorbidity between anxiety and epilepsies can provide a generalized and complete framework to understand the neurobiology underlying mental health. In the current study, we found that earlier processing in the dlPFC and dmPFC was related to diminished responses to predictable threats, and that this activity varied with individual differences in the level of anxiety. By comparison, activity during predictable threat response in the amygdala was unrelated to anxiety. These findings provide novel evidence that early top-down anticipatory activity in the PFC modulates amygdala responses4 linked to anxiety, rather than earlier bottom-up emotion processes. Specifically, the current sEEG assessment of temporal dynamics within the fronto-temporal network during regulation of threat responses revealed that anxiety symptoms may critically arise from frontal lobe disruptions of top-down emotion regulation processes2,3, which are subsequently followed by altered amygdala activity. A criticism of prior work asserts that indirect and correlative neuroimaging may fail to identify precise causal brain-behavior relationships, which prevents translation of findings to clinical applications5. Our findings demonstrated successful replication of neuroimaging results and revealed new timing information critically needed for testing future planned mechanistic hypothesis tests of emotion processing and mental health states. These studies can further harness these new understandings and validated methods to identify potential deep brain targets for future randomized controlled treatment trials.
