Introduction

Procrastination, the irrational delay of intended actions despite foreseeable negative consequences, is a remarkably pervasive problem in modern societies. 75% of over 50,000 college students report chronic procrastination, while employees lose 1.5 - 3.0 hours daily in procrastination-related activities. Despite its clear personal and societal costs, procrastination is moderately heritable (h2 = 0.18 - 0.43) and polygenic, prompting a long-lasting question: how the neurogenetic foundations underpin this maladaptive tendency for persisting inhuman populations, given no perceived societal and bioadaptive benefits. 

A prominent hypothesis suggests that procrastination is not an independent adaptation but a byproduct of impulsivity, particularly its non-planning component 1. As an ancestral trait suited for rapid, short-horizon decisions, non-planning impulsivity (NPI) may, in modern contexts requiring sustained planning, bias individuals toward immediate gratification and thereby foster procrastination. Consistent with this view, numerous studies have documented robust phenotypic associations between impulsivity and procrastination. However, because most prior work has examined impulsivity as a single, undifferentiated construct, the specific role of NPI—characterized by poor foresight, avoidance of future-oriented effort, and preference for immediate rewards—remains insufficiently understood.

Relating to this conceptual gap, empirical support for this association remains mixed, likely reflecting methodological heterogeneity. Phenotypic correlations between procrastination and impulsivity vary widely (rs = 0.23 - 0.70), and twin-based estimates of genetic overlap show similarly broad ranges (rg = 0.30 - 1.00). Besides, though neuroimaging studies implicate the DLPFC as a potential shared neurobiological substrate of both traits 2, it still remains open for identifying whether and how procrastination and NPI are empirically shared in convergent neural substrates. 

Methods

To characterize the shared neurogenetic architecture linking NPI and procrastination, we implemented a closed-loop, multimodal analytic framework that systematically integrates behavioral, genetic, and neuroimaging evidence. 

Specifically, beginning with an 8-year Longitudinal Twin Cohort comprising 154 participants (77 pairs: monozygotic twins, MZ = 40 pairs; dizygotic twins, DZ = 37 pairs) and two independent cross-sectional samples (Cross-sectional Adolescent Cohort: N = 327; Cross-sectional Large-scale Adult Cohort: N= 1,543), we assessed impulsivity using the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS-11) in both the Longitudinal Twin Adolescent Cohort and the Cross-sectional Adolescent Cohort. We also administered the Chinese version of the BIS-11 in the independent adult cohort to evaluate measurement consistency and enhance generalizability across cultural and linguistic contexts. Procrastination was measured using the 20-item General Procrastination Scale, a well-validated self-report instrument designed to assess chronic tendencies to delay intended actions despite potential negative consequences.

With these assessments in place, we first examined whether adolescent NPI prospectively predicts later-life procrastination (Figure 1a). However, despite this prospective design, an important conceptual concern warrants caution: NPI may partially reflect the same psychological constructs underlying procrastination itself. Therefore, to address this issue, we conducted a Gaussian graphical model-based topological analysis to identify item-level psychometric similarities between the two traits. These shared item characteristics were subsequently included as covariates of no interest in all downstream analyses to more precisely isolate their unique phenotypic association (Figure 1b). 

Building on this refined phenotypic foundation, we next quantified shared and unique heritability using multivariate AE twin modeling (Figure 1c). Moreover, given substantial heterogeneity in prior reports of the genetic correlation between impulsivity and procrastination (rg = 0.29 - 1.00), we curated a single-paper meta-analytic sample synthesizing 3,656 twin pairs across existing empirical studies to estimate a pooled effect size (k = 4，Figure 1c). Extending beyond behavioral genetics, we then performed genome-wide association analyses (GWAS) at a suggestive threshold (p < 5×10-5) in a sample of 936 participants of Asian ancestry to identify overlapping SNP signals between NPI and procrastination (Figure 1d). 

To localize convergent neural substrates, we further conducted two independent neuroimaging meta-analyses: a term-based decoding via NeuroSynth and a structural synthesis via seed-based d mapping (SDM), drawing upon 508 and 198 independent primary studies, respectively. Both approaches systematically identified neural loci associated with the two traits, and these loci were subsequently mapped from volumetric space onto surface-based parcels to enable fine-grained neuroanatomical alignment (Figure 1e). 

Advancing this neural characterization, we then integrated the ENIGMA lifespan sample of 37,407 participants (aged 3-90 years) with T1-weighted neuroimaging data to quantify region-wise neurodevelopmental deviations in the Twin Adolescent Cohort 3. These deviations were used to test whether candidate regions statistically mediate the genetic covariance between NPI and procrastination (Figure 1f). 

Finally, to interrogate potential directional relationships, we conducted two-sample Mendelian randomization using independent imaging-genetics data from 7,058 Chinese Han participants in the CHIMGEN 4 dataset to evaluate bidirectional effects between genetically predicted NPI/procrastination and DLPFC morphology (Figure 1g). 

Taken together, these integrated analyses emphasize empirical convergence rather than theoretical presupposition, allowing the data to delineate where NPI and procrastination share neurogenetic pathways and where their pathways diverge.

Results

Using the 8-year longitudinal twin cohort, we found that NPI during late adolescence and early adulthood uniquely predicts procrastination in later adulthood (β = 0.38, p = 0.016, R2 = 0.44), exceeding all other impulsivity subcomponents. This effect replicated across two independent samples (Figure 2a). After removing three bridge items to minimize construct overlap (Figure 2b), the association remained highly consistent (βoriginal = 0.38, βcalibrated = 0.34), and this calibrated effect was likewise replicated (Figure 2c). Together, these findings indicate that NPI prospectively and robustly predicts procrastination, supporting the hypothesis that procrastination may emerge as its behavioral byproduct at the phenotypic level.

Having established this phenotypic link, we next investigated their genetic underpinnings using ACE structural equation modeling. The AE model showed the best fit, revealing moderate heritability for NPI (h2 = 37.6%, 95% CI: [0.08, 0.60]) and higher heritability for procrastination (h2 = 63.01%, 95% CI: [0.41, 0.77]) (Figure 2d). Crucially, both traits shared genetic influences (rg = 0.42, 95% CI: [0.10, 0.70]; Figure 2e). A single-paper meta-analytic model synthesizing four prior studies yielded a pooled genetic correlation of 0.66 (p < .001; Figure 2f, 2g). These converging results indicate a moderate genetic overlap between NPI and procrastination.

To explore genomic loci underlying this overlap, we conducted GWAS and identified 149 suggestive loci for procrastination and 202 for NPI (p < 5 × 10-5). Six loci were found to be overlapped between the two traits, including rs9325070, rs9791022, rs111890148, rs72660259, rs17107747 and rs6887442 (Figure 2h). 

We next examined whether convergent neural substrates might account for these shared influences. NeuroSynth meta-analysis revealed impulsivity-associated activations in left DLPFC, right caudate, and left vmPFC, while SDM meta-analysis of structural studies identified procrastination-related clusters in left DLPFC, right PHC, and left mPFC. Spatial overlap between these maps yielded two shared loci—the left DLPFC (MNI coordinate: -40, 42, 20) and mPFC (MNI coordinate: 0, 46, -10), which mapped onto the rostral middle frontal cortex and medial orbitofrontal cortex (Figure 2i). These regions thus emerged as candidate neural markers jointly implicated in NPI and procrastination.

Leveraging these neural loci, we applied normative modeling to quantify deviations in cortical thickness and surface area within the Twin Adolescent Cohort. Cholesky AE model comparison identified the AE structure as the best-fitting model. Following the pathway morphology → NPI → procrastination, the common genetic factor (Acom) explained substantial variation in CT deviations in left DLPFC (a1 = 0.70, 95% CI: [0.48, 0.90]). After accounting for this contribution, the genetic factor shared between NPI and procrastination still explained variance in NPI (a2 = [0.60, 95% CI: [0.21, 0.85]), but not in procrastination, which retained a strong unique genetic component (a3 = 0.71, 95% CI: [0.40, 0.90]) (Figure 2j). Overall, DLPFC deviations partially accounted for common genetic influences, whereas procrastination also exhibited a substantial trait-specific genetic component.

Finally, to test directional neurogenetic pathways, we performed bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization using CHIMGEN imaging-genetics data. Three valid IVs were identified for NPI and four for procrastination. IVW analyses showed a significant causal effect of genetically predicted NPI on DLPFC morphology (βNPI = -0.05, 95% CI: [-0.10, -0.002], p = .04) and a trend-level for procrastination (βProcrastination = -0.01, 95% CI: [-0.012, -6.4×10-5], p = .05). Robustness and reverse-causation checks supported these findings. Thus, although NPI and procrastination do not appear to fully share identical neurogenetic substrates, both traits nonetheless converge on the left DLPFC.

Conclusion

In this study, we addressed the long-standing theoretical question of whether human procrastination may partly arise as an evolutionary byproduct of impulsivity. Using a longitudinal twin design, we examined the neurogenetic underpinnings linking NPI to procrastination. Our findings showed that NPI assessed in late adolescence and early adulthood prospectively predicted procrastination trait in later adulthood, indicating a robust phenotypic association that was replicated in independent samples. Furthermore, using AE model, both traits exhibited moderate-to-high heritability, and significantly shared genetic influences in accounting their phenotypic association, partly supporting this notion that procrastination may theoretically share as a byproduct. To delineate genetic mechanism underlying this association, we have performed GWAS, and demonstrated statistically significant SNPs overlapped between the both traits, which were involved into specific protein coding on neurodevelopment. Therefore, through systematic meta-analytic synthesis, DLPFC and mPFC were identified as shared impulsivity-procrastination loci. Using normative modeling to quantify neuroanatomical developmental deviations, we found that deviations in DLPFC morphology partially accounted for the shared genetic variance between NPI and procrastination, whereas procrastination also exhibited substantial trait-specific genetic contribution. Consistent with this mapping, MR analyses provided suggestive evidence for a directional association from NPI to left DLPFC morphology deviations, whereas the procrastination to DLPFC analysis was not statistically significant. Taken together, this study systematically clarified neurogenetic underpinning of NPI-procrastination mapping, thus supporting this theoretical notion that procrastination may partly reflect a byproduct from impulsivity. 

In conclusion, this study provides empirical support for ongoing theoretical debates regarding the origins of procrastination—demonstrating that it may indeed partly emerge as an evolutionary byproduct of NPI, with neurodevelopmental deviations in the DLPFC accounting for their shared neurogenetic architecture. Moreover, procrastination exhibits substantial unique genetic variants and demonstrates causal influences on shaping DLPFC morphology, suggesting the distinct behavior-brain epigenetic mechanism that contribute to its neurogenetic origins.
