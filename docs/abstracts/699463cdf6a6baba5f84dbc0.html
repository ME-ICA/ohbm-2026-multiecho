Introduction

Understanding how neurobiological processes underpin the large-scale organization and developmental evolution of human brain remains a central challenge in neuroscience1,2. Normative growth charts derived from brain phenotypes have enabled the mapping of developmental evolution across the human lifespan3. Emerging evidence suggests that functional network connectivity (FNC) patterns are shaped by microscale neurobiological underpinnings, such as regional cellular composition, neurotransmitter receptor system, and mitochondrial metabolism4. However, the neurobiological signatures of functional brain organization across lifespan remain largely unknown.

Methods

We assembled a large-scale, multi-site cohort for FNC analysis using the Neuromark_fMRI_1.05 template, comprising a total of 3,834 neurotypical participants drawn from HCP-D, HCP-YA, HCP-A, FBIRN COBRE, LA5C, Chinese Han MDD, ABIDE (I and II), and ADHD200 datasets. Using these data, we constructed edge-level normative models for FNC edges (Fig. 1A). Normative modeling was performed with the warped Bayesian linear regression, as implemented in PCNtoolkit. For each FNC edge, connectivity strength was modeled as a function of age, sex, head motion, and site. Using the fitted normative models, we generated lifespan trajectories for each edge by sampling 75 discrete integer ages spanning 6 to 80 years. To quantify developmental and aging-related dynamics, we computed annualized FNC change using a one-year sliding window (e.g., FNC at age t minus FNC at age t–1), providing estimates of edge-wise reorganization across the lifespan.

To uncover the underlying neurobiological substrates, we integrated multiscale neurobiological spatial maps spanning cell types, neurotransmitter systems, and mitochondrial phenotypes. For each spatial map, we constructed pairwise regional microprofile networks to capture the spatial coherence of specific biological markers. Specifically, we computed the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD), a symmetric and bounded measure from information theory that quantifies the dissimilarity between two probability distributions. Pairwise similarity between brain regions was then defined as 1−JSD, resulting in a symmetric similarity matrix for each marker. To constrain the analysis to spatially relevant regions, we applied a threshold of |z| > 3 to the ICN spatial maps from the Neuromark_fMRI_1.0 template, thereby excluding weakly activated voxels and focusing on regions with strong functional engagement. Only voxels exceeding this threshold within each ICN were included in the similarity computation. This procedure yielded 49 region-by-region neurobiological microprofile networks: 24 for cell types, 19 for neurotransmitter receptors and transporters, and 6 for mitochondrial phenotypes (Fig. 1B).

To investigate the neurobiological substrates underlying normative FNC organization and its developmental change across the lifespan, we quantified the spatial correspondence between age-specific normative FNC and FNC change patterns, and a set of neurobiological microprofile networks (Fig. 1C). For each age point, we constructed multiple linear regression models with selected microprofile networks (cellular/neurotransmitter/mitochondrion) as predictors and the FNC (or FNC-change) matrix as the dependent variable. Euclidean distance and Dice similarity were included as covariates and regressed out prior to multivariate modeling. Model performance was quantified using the adjusted r² which reflects the proportion of variance in normative FNC explained by the combined neurobiological predictors. To evaluate the contribution of individual predictors, we conducted dominance analysis. Statistical significance was assessed using permutation-based null models. For each analysis, we generated 1,000 surrogate FNC maps by randomly permuting edge positions, yielding null distributions for the adjusted r² and predictor-specific dominance. The p-values for adjusted r² were corrected for 75 age-point comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. The p-values for dominance metrics were corrected using modality-specific comparison counts (75 × 24 for cell type, 75 × 19 for neurotransmitter, and 75 × 6 for mitochondrion). Neurobiological markers that survived FDR correction and exhibited relative importance exceeding the average expected contribution (1/24 for cell-type, 1/19 for neurotransmitter, and 1/6 for mitochondrion) were identified as dominant individual predictors.

Results

Across the lifespan, multivariate analyses revealed sustained colocalization between FNCs and neurobiological microprofile networks across life stages for all three categories: cell types (adjusted r2 = 0.21 ± 0.01), neurotransmitters (adjusted r2 = 0.14 ± 0.02), and mitochondria (adjusted r2 = 0.12 ± 0.01). All associations were statistically significant (FDR q < 0.05). Dominance analysis further revealed marker-specific contributions throughout the lifespan. For cell types, layer 6b neurons (L6b), astrocytes (Astro), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC), and oligodendrocytes (Oligo) consistently exhibited high relative importance across life stages, with Astro emerging as the top contributor at nearly all ages. For neurotransmitter systems, 5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 1A (5-HT1a), dopamine D2 receptor (D2), and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDA) showed persistent contributions across the lifespan, with NMDA being the most dominant across nearly all stages. For mitochondrial markers, complex 1 (C1) and mitochondrial tissue density (MitoD) robustly explained FNC variance throughout the lifespan.

For FNC change patterns, multivariate models confirmed modality-specific explanatory power (Fig. 2A): cell types (adjusted r2 = 0.10 ± 0.03), neurotransmitters (adjusted r2 = 0.08 ± 0.03), and mitochondria (adjusted r2 = 0.03 ± 0.02). All models showed statistically significant variance explained across the lifespan (q < 0.05). The peak explanatory power of cell-type features was observed during adolescence, whereas neurotransmitter systems peaked in mid–young adulthood, and mitochondrial contributions reached their maximum in early adulthood (Fig. 2A). Dominance analysis revealed stage-specific contributions of individual neurobiological markers (Fig. 2B). For cell-type features, OPC showed selective contributions during late childhood, adolescence, and middle adulthood; parvalbumin interneurons (PVALB) contributed predominantly in middle and late adulthood; and synuclein gamma (SNCG) showed importances in young and middle adulthood. For neurotransmitter systems, D1 exhibited selective contributions in late childhood, while NMDA showed its contributions in late adulthood. Among mitochondrial phenotypes, CI displayed selective contributions during adolescence, young adulthood, and late adulthood. Interestingly, several markers exhibited pronounced inverted U-shaped trajectories, with maximal contributions around major developmental transitions. For example, layer 6 intratelencephalic neurons (L6IT) increased from young adulthood to mid–middle adulthood and then declined toward early late adulthood; Oligo and 5-HT1a increased from childhood to a peak in adolescence and decreased thereafter in adulthood; cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1) increased beginning in middle adulthood before declining in late adulthood; and mitochondrial respiratory capacity (MRC) increased from young adulthood and declined beginning in middle adulthood.

Conclusion

Leveraging a large aggregated, multi-site FNC cohort spanning ages 6–80 years, we established normative trajectories of FNC across the human lifespan. By integrating multiscale neurobiological reference maps, we systematically linked macroscale functional network organization and its developmental reconfiguration to underlying cellular, neurotransmitter, and mitochondrial microarchitectures.

Our findings demonstrate that normative FNC patterns and their age-related changes align with distinct neurobiological gradients whose relative contributions vary across developmental stages. Cell-type features, particularly astrocytes, oligodendrocyte lineage cells, and layer-specific neuronal populations, showed strong and sustained correspondence with normative FNC organization, with temporally selective influences on developmental reconfiguration. Neurotransmitter systems, especially NMDA, 5-HT1A, and D2 receptors, exhibited both persistent and stage-specific associations, highlighting their roles in shaping functional integration and plasticity across maturation and aging. Mitochondrial phenotypes, including CI and MitoD, further revealed a metabolic substrate underlying functional network stability and change, with peak contributions around major developmental transitions.

Together, these results provide converging evidence that human functional brain organization and its lifespan evolution emerge from coordinated interactions across cellular composition, molecular signaling, and bioenergetic capacity. By embedding normative FNC trajectories within a biologically interpretable multiscale framework, this work advances a mechanistic understanding of how microscale neurobiology constrains and shapes macroscale functional network development and aging, offering a foundation for future investigations into atypical neurodevelopment and neuropsychiatric vulnerability.
