Introduction

Associative learning is recognized as a fundamental mechanism through which organisms acquire knowledge about patterns and regularities in their environment. It provides a powerful framework for understanding the developmental trajectories of cognitive functions in the brain. The brain is conceptualized as a complex nonlinear dynamical system, whose activity is shaped by recurrent interactions across multiple spatial and temporal scales and exhibits hallmark properties of nonlinear dynamics.1

One such property is the largest Lyapunov exponent (LLE), a widely used measure of local instability. The LLE quantifies the average exponential rate at which nearby trajectories in a reconstructed state space diverge over time. Positive LLE values indicate locally divergent, complex dynamics, whereas negative values reflect contracting, more stable dynamics. Despite its theoretical relevance, the LLE has rarely been applied to the investigation of trial-based cognitive processes such as associative learning. There are some studies of reinforcement learning associated with dynamic flexibility of brain networks.2,3

Another limitation of the existing literature is the relative scarcity of developmental studies that combine explicit learning tasks with nonlinear dynamical analyses. Consequently, it remains unclear how neural complexity associated with learning evolves from late childhood through young adulthood. At the same time, educational and clinical practice increasingly demand quantitative tools capable of characterizing individual differences in learning efficiency, identifying atypical developmental trajectories, and evaluating the effectiveness of interventions.

The aim of the present study is  examining the nonlinear dynamics of EEG signals during the feedback-based reinforcement learning task performance of a in school-aged children, adolescents, and young adults.

Methods

For this experiment, 120 participants were recruited from the local population of Almaty. To be eligible for participation in this research, all participants were required to have no history of neurological or mental health disorders. For participants with reduced visual acuity, corrective lenses (glasses or contact lenses) were permitted. Participants of legal age provided their own consent, while for those underage, a signed consent form was obtained from a parent or legal guardian prior to participation in the experiment. The study protocol was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Higher School of Public Health, Al-Farabi Kazakh National University, in accordance with established ethical standards. During the preprocessing stage, data from 8 participants were excluded due to incomplete recordings and critical signal artifacts. The main dataset consisted of 112 participants, aged between 7 and 20 years, with 63 females (mean age = 13.6 years, SD = 4.0)

Participants were classified into four age groups: 1) 7-10 years (n = 34), 2) 11-13 years (n = 25), 3) 14-16 years (n = 25), 4)17-20 years (n = 38). 

The experimental task was designed to probe feedback-based associative learning using a simple two-choice visuomotor paradigm. The task was adapted from the reinforcement learning paradigm originally developed by Eliassen et al. (2012).4 The task was designed in the E-Prime software environment (Psychology Software Tools, Inc.), which allowed precise control over stimulus timing and response registration, as well as synchronization with EEG recording.  Participants were shown easily nameable color images (e.g., objects such as a book or an apple) and were instructed that each image was consistently associated with one of two response buttons according to four specific rules. The correct mapping was to be determined through trial-and-error, with feedback provided after each response. All sets of images were presented in pseudorandom order."

EEG signals were recorded using ANT Neuro eego™mylab (Germany, 64 channels) during reinforcement learning task performance with sampling rate of 4096 Hz. All the stages of the preprocessing pipeline were executed in MATLAB software using EEGLAB and ERPLAB packages. Data preprocessing included resampling to 1024 Hz, band-pass filtering between 0.3 and 40 Hz, re-reference to whole brain average, ICA decomposition, artifacts removal. Epochs were extracted around the events [-200 800 ms]. Rosenstein’s algorithm (TISEAN package) was used to compute LLE.5 Statistics were done in Python (version 3.12.5).

Results

Behavioral data analysis revealed a significant effect of stage ('Early' vs. 'Late') (F = 18.641, p < 0.001), as well as an age effect (F = 10.7428, p < 0.001). Reaction times were slower during the early stage of learning, and were faster for older children.

Repeated Measure ANOVA of LLE parameters of EEG signal revealed significant stage effects (“Early” vs “Late”) for the visual network (F=5.7536, p=0.0181), the left frontal network (F=5.3668, p=0.0224), the left parietal (F=11.2685, p=0.0011), with lower magnitude in the “Early” stage of learning. 

Significant differences were observed between learning trials and feedback trials (F = 7.4715, p = 0.0073), with lower LLE values found during feedback trials. This suggests that, during feedback trials, the brain's neural dynamics are more stable and controlled, potentially reflecting the cognitive processes involved in integrating corrective feedback and adjusting behavior. In contrast, learning trials may involve more complex, exploratory neural activity as participants attempt to discover patterns or rules.

Age effects were significant for whole brain LLE (F = 9.9382, p < 0.001), with LLE increasing as age increased. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons. The significant changes in LLE across networks could highlight how different brain systems mature at different rates. All brain networks exhibited a significant increase in LLE with age, though the extent of this increase varied across networks.

Conclusion

In summary, the Largest Lyapunov Exponent measured from epoched EEG data may serve as an index of learning stage and brain development. The lower LLE during the early stage of learning suggests that the brain's activity is more stable, less sensitive to small perturbations, and potentially less complex or adaptable at this stage. During the early stage of learning, the brain’s electrical activity may be more stable, reflecting a period where cognitive control mechanisms are still being established. The lower LLE could indicate that the brain is operating in a more controlled, organized state, possibly relying on basic cognitive control processes to regulate attention, task performance, and response inhibition. This stability may be crucial for ensuring that participants can focus and consistently engage with the learning task before more complex patterns emerge. As learning progresses, the brain begins to adapt and develop more flexible cognitive control strategies, which can lead to increased complexity and higher LLE values.

A higher LLE in older individuals may indicate that their brain networks are more interconnected and capable of responding to a wider range of stimuli. It could also suggest an increase in the brain's ability to adapt to new information, learn new tasks, and exhibit more flexible cognitive processing. Significant changes in LLE across networks could also indicate how neural activity changes over the lifespan.
