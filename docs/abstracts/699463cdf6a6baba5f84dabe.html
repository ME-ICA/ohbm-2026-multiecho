Introduction

In psychosis, widespread cortical thickness and gray matter abnormalities have been associated with illness progression and functional outcomes, and studies of individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis (CHR) suggest that structural brain changes may precede symptom onset1. Consequently, regional neuroanatomical measures are being explored as candidate biomarkers predictive of medium-term clinical outcomes in the Accelerating Medicines Partnership® Schizophrenia (AMP® SCZ) program. AMP SCZ is a large public-private partnership that aims to develop biomarker algorithms to predict individual clinical trajectory and outcomes, improve understanding of psychosis-related disease pathways, and identify novel treatment targets. Within this context, stable quantification of brain structure from MRI is essential for assessing group differences, characterizing longitudinal change, and supporting downstream analyses. 

AMP SCZ is releasing structural MRI derivatives processed with FreeSurfer2 through the National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive (NDA) AMP SCZ Data Repository. These include outputs from the widely used ‘recon-all’ stream (v7.4.1), as well as segmentations generated using FreeSurfer’s newer deep learning–based ‘SynthSeg’ approach3. While both pipelines provide estimates of cortical and subcortical structures, they rely on distinct modeling approaches and may yield systematically different regional measures, potentially impacting downstream regional analysis and predictive modeling.

In this study, we compare regional brain measures generated by FreeSurfer v7.4.1 recon-all and SynthSeg using baseline and longitudinal MRI data from the AMP SCZ cohort, including CHR individuals and community controls (CC). We characterize cross-method agreement, systematic differences, and longitudinal sensitivity, and assess whether the segmentation method affects downstream predictive modeling by evaluating logistic regression performance for CHR versus CC classification.

Methods

MRI data were obtained from 2,282 participants (1725 CHR, 557 CC), with 1603 participants (1388 CHR, 215 CC) contributing longitudinal follow-up data, acquired approximately three months apart (98.7 days ± 52.7, mean ± std), across 38 imaging sites. Mean age at baseline was 20.9 ± 4.1 years in CHR and 21.3 ± 3.6 years in CC (t = -2.0, p = 0.5), with sex distributions of 630M/1095F and 216M/341F respectively (χ2 = 0.8, p = 0.4). Imaging was conducted using a multi-vendor protocol developed for AMP SCZ and implemented on Siemens, GE, and Philips scanners, with centralized quality control and standardized data handling procedures4.  

Each MRI included T1-weighted (T1w) and T2-weighted (T2w) scans at 0.8 mm isotropic resolution with 2x phase acceleration2. Scans were subsequently processed using FreeSurfer v7.4.1, with cortical and subcortical volumetric measures extracted using the standard recon-all pipeline including pial surface refinement using the T2w scan. In parallel, the same T1w images were processed with FreeSurfer’s SynthSeg tool (v7.4.1 version)3. Analyses were restricted to ROIs with consistent anatomical labels across both pipelines. For bilateral structures, hemisphere volumes were averaged to derive hemisphere-combined ROIs (yielding 18 total ROIs). Cross-sectional agreement between recon-all and SynthSeg regional measures was assessed separately at baseline and follow-up using Spearman rank correlations. Longitudinal change was quantified as within-subject percentage change between baseline and follow-up for each ROI, restricted to participants with data at both timepoints.

To evaluate systematic differences between segmentation methods, paired permutation tests (20,000 repetitions) were applied to within-subject differences (recon-all minus SynthSeg) for each ROI at baseline, follow-up, and for longitudinal percentage change, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied within each family of tests. To assess practical interchangeability beyond statistical significance, equivalence testing was performed, which tests (for a given ROI) whether the 90% confidence interval of the mean paired difference lies entirely within a data-derived equivalence margin, using two one-sided tests (‘TOST’). This approach complements the permutation testing by assessing whether the observed differences are small enough to be considered negligible, rather than statistically significant. 

Longitudinal stability was quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) computed separately for recon-all and SynthSeg. For each ROI, ICCs were calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement for single measurements (ICC(3,1)), restricted to participants with both baseline and follow-up scans and without intracranial volume (ICV) correction. Differences in ICCs between methods were evaluated using permutation testing.

Finally, to assess downstream predictive utility, elastic-net logistic regression models (using default hyperparameters of C = 1, L1 ratio = 0.5) were trained to classify CHR versus CC participants using either recon-all or SynthSeg-derived ROI features. Models were evaluated separately using volumes from baseline (normalized by the ICV provided by each method’s own ICV estimate) and longitudinal percentage-change, with age and sex included as covariates. Predictive performance was assessed using repeated stratified 4-fold cross-validation with 100 repeats and quantified by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

All statistical analysis was conducted in Python 3.13.5 using ‘sklearn’ and ‘scipy’, with figures generated in ‘matplotlib’.

Results

Across the 18 ROIs, recon-all and SynthSeg demonstrated high cross-sectional agreement at baseline (Figure 1A; Spearman ρ range: 0.74–0.99; n = 2,282). Agreement was strongest for larger ventricular and global measures (e.g., lateral ventricles: ρ = 0.99; cerebral gray matter: ρ = 0.97; cerebral white matter: ρ = 0.97) and lowest for smaller subcortical structures (e.g., inferior lateral ventricles: ρ = 0.74; cerebellar white matter: ρ = 0.76). The correlation of the estimated total intracranial volume from recon-all (“eTIV”) and from SynthSeg was ρ = .81. Correlation patterns were consistent at follow-up (not shown).

Scatterplots of baseline ROI volumes for the two methods are shown in Figure 1A. Despite high rank-correlations, paired permutation tests on the within-subject differences showed significant mean differences between recon-all and SynthSeg estimates for all ROIs (q < 0.05), with the directions and magnitude of paired Cohen’s d varying by structure. Differences were largest for cerebral gray matter, accumbens area, and pallidum. Similar patterns were observed at follow-up (not shown). Similarly, equivalence testing indicated that, for most ROIs, recon-all and SynthSeg measurements were not equivalent, despite high correlations. Six ROIs met equivalence criteria in both CHR and CC at baseline: ICV, cerebellar cortex, cerebellar white matter, lateral ventricles, thalamus, and the hippocampus (which correspondingly all have scatterplots close to the line-of-identity in Figure 1A). There was considerably more variability in volume percentage change between baseline and followup in recon-all than SynthSeg (Figure 1B). Variability between baseline and followup was largely consistent between CHR and CC (not shown). ICC of the stability between baseline and followup volumes was high in all ROIs for both methods (.76-.99 in recon-all, .96-.99 in SynthSeg) but SynthSeg had significantly higher ICC between baseline and followup in all ROIs (FDR corrected q < 0.05) except lateral ventricle (q = .45), with a mean ICC increase (across ROIs) of 0.04  ± 0.05 (mean ± std). 

In predictive modeling, elastic-net logistic regression models comparing CHR and CC participants performed near chance when using either segmentation method. At baseline, mean AUCs were 0.51 ± 0.02 (mean ± std across 100 repeats) for recon-all and 0.51 ± 0.02 for SynthSeg, with no significant difference between methods. Models trained on longitudinal percentage-change values showed below-chance performance (recon-all: 0.48 ± 0.03; SynthSeg: 0.46 ± 0.04). Thus, the segmentation method did not meaningfully affect predictive performance.

Conclusion

In this large, multi-site cohort, FreeSurfer recon-all and SynthSeg produced highly correlated regional brain measures at both baseline and follow-up, indicating strong rank-order agreement across a broad set of ROIs. However, differences in absolute volume estimates were observed across nearly all regions, as demonstrated by paired permutation testing. For instance, ICV estimates were systematically higher in SynthSeg, and neighboring basal ganglia structures showed evidence of differing border definitions between Freesurfer methods (e.g., higher accumbens and lower pallidum). Equivalence testing showed that recon-all and SynthSeg measurements were generally not interchangeable, with equivalence supported for only a small subset of regions, including lateral ventricles, thalamus, and hippocampus. 

Importantly, longitudinal percent-change estimates and ICCs exhibited clear differences in the stability of volumes estimated from the two methods. Although both methods showed high longitudinal ICCs, SynthSeg consistently showed higher ICC in all ROIs, significantly improving upon recon-all except in the lateral ventricles. These findings show that SynthSeg provides more stable longitudinal estimates, which is particularly relevant for studies focussing on change over time. Notably, ICV, which should not vary between scanning visits, was more consistent between baseline and followup sessions using SynthSeg. Therefore SynthSeg ICV should be used instead of the one estimated by recon-all (“eTIV”) . 

Despite the differences between SynthSeg and recon-all, there was little effect on predictive modeling. Elastic-net classifiers performed poorly in distinguishing CHR from CC using either baseline or short-term longitudinal percent change (~ three month mean interval), and the segmentation method had no impact on classification performance. However, this poor CC vs. CHR classification performance does not preclude the possibility that certain morphometric features, such as cortical thickness, which we did not examine here, may be predictive of downstream outcomes. For example, such measures may better capture clinical trajectories, including likelihood of conversion to psychosis (not yet available). 

Together, these findings demonstrate that while recon-all and SynthSeg showed high correlations between regional brain volumes, they produce systematic differences in terms of absolute values and longitudinal change. Notably, SynthSeg exhibited higher longitudinal stability and tighter percentage-change distributions across ROIs, suggesting greater measurement stability over short intervals. Accordingly, SynthSeg-derived measures may be preferable for longitudinal analyses when available, though notably we used the cross-sectional Freesurfer rather than the longitudinal recon-all stream. Careful consideration of the segmentation method is recommended when comparing studies, aggregating data across pipelines and software versions, or when utilizing AMP SCZ structural derivatives.
