Introduction

Understanding how functional and structural brain networks jointly support cognitive abilities across the adult lifespan remains a fundamental challenge in neuroscience. Although connectome-based predictive models have demonstrated promise in linking brain connectivity to cognition¹, many approaches rely on single imaging modalities or linear assumptions, limiting their ability to capture nonlinear interactions and to provide interpretable insights into brain organization. To address these limitations, this study proposes a multimodal, explainable connectome-based framework that integrates functional connectivity, structural connectivity, and anatomical morphology to predict individual differences in cognition across healthy adulthood.2,3

Methods

We utilized multimodal neuroimaging data from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult and Aging cohorts, including resting-state fMRI, diffusion MRI, and structural MRI, together with four cognitive measures: working memory, episodic memory, executive function, and fluid cognition. Brain networks were constructed using a fine-grained multimodal cortical parcellation, with functional connectivity estimated from inter-regional temporal correlations and structural connectivity derived from diffusion-based tractography. Cortical thickness, surface area, and subcortical volumes were extracted as complementary morphological features.

A hybrid prediction framework was developed to integrate network-level connectivity information with region-specific anatomical characteristics. Brain connectivity was modeled using an attention-based graph neural network4, which learns weighted interactions among brain regions and enables data-driven identification of salient nodes and connections. In parallel, morphological features were processed using a deep multilayer perceptron to capture nonlinear structure–cognition relationships. Representations from the two modules were integrated through multimodal fusion mechanisms, allowing both modality-specific and cross-modal interactions to contribute to cognitive prediction.

Model training employed stratified cross-validation to preserve age distributions across splits. To enhance representation learning and improve robustness, a graph contrastive learning strategy5 was incorporated during training. By contrasting augmented views of individual brain graphs, the model was encouraged to learn invariant and discriminative network representations prior to or alongside supervised cognitive prediction. Prediction performance was optimized using a robust regression loss, and model interpretability was assessed through attention weight analysis and SHAP-based feature attribution.

Results

The proposed framework demonstrated stable predictive performance across all four cognitive domains, with accuracy comparable to or exceeding recent multimodal connectome-based approaches. Beyond prediction accuracy, multi-level interpretability analyses revealed systematic age- and sex-dependent patterns across regional, structural, and network levels.

At the regional level, attention-based analyses showed that younger adults primarily relied on posterior sensory and visuospatial regions, whereas middle-aged adults increasingly engaged prefrontal and medial temporal areas. In older adulthood, predictive importance became more distributed and included limbic and olfactory-related regions, suggesting age-related reorganization and compensatory recruitment. Across the lifespan, females exhibited broader and more bilaterally distributed regional importance, while males showed more focal and regionally concentrated patterns, particularly in posterior cortices at older ages.

At the structural level, morphological feature attribution revealed an age-dependent shift in model reliance. Cortical features in frontoparietal and temporal regions were most predictive in younger and middle adulthood, whereas subcortical structures such as the thalamus and striatum became increasingly important in later life. This transition indicates a gradual shift from cortically driven to subcortically supported contributions to cognitive prediction with aging.

At the network level, analyses of attention-weighted inter-regional connectivity revealed non-linear lifespan trajectories. Network interactions showed a U-shaped pattern, with dense and distributed connectivity in young adulthood, a relative reduction and focalization in midlife, and a re-expansion of large-scale network engagement in older age. This re-emergence was characterized by increased involvement of default mode, frontoparietal, and cross-hemispheric connections. Females consistently demonstrated more distributed network-level engagement, whereas males exhibited more selective and localized connectivity patterns.

Conclusion

This study introduces an interpretable multimodal connectome-based framework that integrates functional, structural, and morphological brain information to predict cognitive function across the adult lifespan. By combining attention-based graph modeling with contrastive representation learning and multi-level interpretability, the proposed approach captures biologically plausible age- and sex-dependent patterns at regional, structural, and network scales. These findings highlight the dynamic reorganization of brain systems supporting cognition and provide a scalable normative modeling framework for future studies of healthy aging and clinical populations.
