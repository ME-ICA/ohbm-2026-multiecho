Introduction

There is an urgent need for improved systems of diagnosis within psychiatry. Current research on psychiatric illnesses makes heavy use of self-reported symptom measures oriented around specific diagnostic categories. However, these categories exhibit limited biological validity and lack a solid foundation within neuroscience. The development of biologically-based tests and measures has been proposed as a promising route to enhance clinical utility through stratifying diagnostic categories, predicting therapeutic outcomes, and identifying novel treatment targets. Over the last two decades, human neuroimaging has revealed candidate biomarkers associated with a range of psychiatric symptoms and conditions. These biomarkers have not yet been actualized for clinical use, in large part due to the resources required to operate imaging technology at scale. There is a pressing need for scalable, low-cost measures of psychiatric biomarkers that could be readily incorporated into existing diagnostic frameworks and deployed for use in the clinic. Here, we use Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) to develop self-report psychometric scales that target discrete brain systems, forming the foundation for psychiatric assessments that are firmly grounded in neuroscience.

Methods

A transdiagnostic cohort of participants1 (n=302) completed a comprehensive psychometric battery (317 test items) and eight fMRI runs. Psychometric instruments include the Adult Temperament Questionnaire (ATQ), Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI), Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function - Adult (BRIEF-A), Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), and the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule - Extended (PANAS-X). 

The eight fMRI runs consisted of six task runs bookended by two resting runs. The Shen268 atlas was used to parcellate the brain into 268 contiguous, non-overlapping regions. Functional connectivity matrices were generated for each participant and run by correlating the BOLD time series for each pair of regions, resulting in a 268 x 268 x 8 matrix for each participant. These matrices were further partitioned by network (medial frontal, frontoparietal, default mode, salience, motor cortex, visual A, visual B, visual association, subcortical, and cerebellum) to represent within-network connectivity. 

Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR) models (α=0.01, γ=0.4) were trained on connectivity data using 10 cross-fold validation (CV)  and predicted item scores for left-out groups of subjects that were retained across folds. Predicted item scores were correlated with observed item scores across folds using Spearman rank correlation yielding correlation coefficients (⍴) for each item. This process was repeated for 100 iterations per item, randomly permuting the subject order for each. For models trained on functional networks, all within-network edges were retained across the eight runs and used as input features. Predictive power coefficients were compared to matched null models in which subject labels were randomly permuted in order to calculate significance. 

Network composite scores were calculated by aggregating item scores across groups determined by the predictive power of different networks. The top ranked items were summed using the sign of their respective PCA loading within network composites in order to avoid conflation of reverse-correlated items. These composite scores were then predicted using the methods described previously. 

Sequential Feature Selection (SFS) (10 splits, 15 max forward selection) was used with Ridge regression models (α=0.1) to predict mean absolute connectivity (across all tasks) of within-network edges from scaled item scores. This was repeated for 100 iterations per network, randomly permuting subject order for each. Model weights were extracted and averaged for items that were selected in 7 out of 10 splits or greater. This process was conducted in a 10-fold CV manner and prediction strength was calculated by correlating predicted connectivity with observed connectivity across folds using Spearman rank correlation yielding correlation coefficients (⍴) for each network. Predictive power coefficients were compared to matched null models in which subject labels were randomly permuted in order to calculate significance. 

The degree of statistical significance for model predictions was estimated by randomly permuting null vs. experimental observations (1000 iterations) and calculating the proportion of permuted predictive power differences that were equal to or greater than the observed difference.

Results

Overall, whole-brain functional connectivity across all tasks significantly predicted 76% of psychometric items (FDR < 0.01) with a moderate maximum predictive power (ρ = 0.29). For every item, connectivity within one or more canonical networks showed significant predictive power (FDR < 0.01) and generally improved over predictions using whole-brain connectivity (mean Δρ = 0.05, max. Δρ = 0.21, min. Δρ = -0.13). Furthermore, we observed a moderate positive correlation (R2 = 0.27) between whole-brain predictive power and best-network predictive power across items. Importantly, predictive power showed no correlation with item response variation in either whole-brain or best-network conditions (R2 = 0.019 and R2 = 0.016 respectively). 

Using network connectivity to predict symptom-based categories revealed item composites that showed increased predictive power from the aggregation of their constituent item scores (ex. BSI Depression, BSI Interpersonal Sensitivity, and BRIEF-A Inhibition) and those that decreased when items are combined (ex. ATQ Sociability, BRIEF-A Self-monitoring, and ATQ Inhibition). Notably, the power of predicting symptom category aggregates with functional networks showed a positive correlation with the mean predictive power of their constituent items (R2 = 0.38) but not the correlation between constituent item scores (negative correlation between predictive power and mean item variance inflation factor, R2 = 0.19). 

The medial frontal, cerebellar, and motor networks showed strong predictive power across all psychometric items (AUC = 22.5, 19.5, 17.1 respectively) while the visual A, visual B, and frontoparietal networks showed the weakest (AUC = 8.7, 9.8, 12.0 respectively). Importantly, each network exhibited a unique ranking of items predicted when compared to other networks (all cross-network item rank correlations < 0.19). Furthermore, predicting the functional network-based composite scores of the top 10 items for each network yielded predictive power improvements over the top ranking individual item in six networks (medial frontal, motor cortex, visual B, salience, subcortical, and cerebellum) and the mean predictive power of the top 10 items in all networks. Additionally, the power of networks to predict their respective network composite was only weakly correlated with the intra-correlation of the constituent item responses (negative correlation with mean variance inflation factor, R2 = 0.14). 

Networks were able to significantly predict the unique composites of other networks to varying degrees. The medial frontal, cerebellar, and motor networks showed the strongest power to predict their respective  network composite scores (top 10 items, ρ = 0.36, 0.36, 0.30 respectively) as well as predicting other network composites (mean predictive power across other networks, ρ = 0.19, 0.14, 0.16 respectively). Furthermore, the visual A, salience, and default node networks showed the highest selectivity for predicting their respective network composite (self predictive power to mean other-network predictive power ratio, ρs/ρo = 8.5, 4.4, 3.8 respectively). Importantly, these patterns were not meaningfully different with other composite sizes tested (top 3, 5, 25, and 50 items). 

Sequential Feature Selection (SFS) was then used to determine the optimal item combinations that can be linearly combined to estimate bulk connectivity across a more granular set of 17 functional networks. 14 networks were significantly predicted using the extracted item weights. Item sets generated to predict DefaultC, VisPeri, and ContB connectivity showed the strongest predictive power with sets of 14, 12, and 17 items comprising their network scales respectively. Furthermore, networks scale scores showed a high overall correspondence with bulk connectivity across psychiatric categories (Panic Disorder, GAD, MDE, PTSD, Bipolar Disorder, Manic Episodes, SUD, and AUD) and groups defined by a general factor of psychopathology (p-factor). 

Conclusion

First, we demonstrate that individual psychometric items, aiming to measure granular symptoms and behaviors, can be predicted by whole-brain connectivity. Furthermore, leveraging the neural specificity of functional networks improves our predictive capacity, indicating that different brain systems may contain distinct information tied to transdiagnostic behavioral constructs. Next, we show that symptom-derived categories display differing degrees of neural heterogeneity with items aggregated based on behavioral constructs potentially drawing upon distinct brain systems. This opens up the possibility for the reorganization of psychometric items into brain-based scales as opposed to symptom-based scales. Next, we show that aggregate scores made up of top ranked items predicted by distinct functional networks can be strongly predicted by their respective networks, indicating that brain systems may link unique groups of items that contain network-specific information and cut across symptom categories. We then algorithmically define scales optimized to linearly predict bulk connectivity within functional networks and show that these network-targeted psychometric scales capture the connectivity profiles of different psychiatric groups. Together, these results indicate that psychometric scales can be developed to target specific functional networks and estimate their connectivity – providing a platform for the cheap, rapid, and scalable measurement of neural biomarkers. This work opens up a new direction for leveraging insights from neuroimaging to advance systems of diagnosis and accelerate the treatment of psychiatric illness.
