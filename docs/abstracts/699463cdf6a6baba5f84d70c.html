Introduction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) can induce significant anxiety due to exiguity of the bore, the loud noises produced by the scanner and the need to remain completely still throughout the procedure. This is especially true for children, and often leads to excessive head motion, which compromises image quality. For many years, the standard solution to this problem has been sedation. Although this method drastically improves image quality, it may have adverse consequences for neurodevelopment of children1, and is not always feasible, particularly when the aim is to assess cognition using functional MRI. In recent years, a variety of pre-scan training strategies have been evaluated, ranging from simple written or illustrated manuals to more realistic preparation tools such as mock scanners, as well as mobile applications that gamify the procedure explanations2,3.  

One particularly promising approach is the use of Virtual Reality (VR). We recently developed ‘MRI Adventure’, an immersive VR serious game that recreates the scanner environment with child-friendly characters and eye/head-tracking mini-games to teach safety rules, familiarize children with the procedure, and train them to keep their head still. Retrospective analyses of pediatric MRI sessions suggest that ‘MRI Adventure’ is associated with reduced head motion compared with standard preparation alone, especially in younger children, thus supporting VR serious games as a feasible and potentially more effective preparation tool compared to traditional approaches. However, evidence from randomized controlled trials is still lacking, and the impact of VR-based training on subsequent in-scanner motion, scan success rate, and anxiety have yet to be determined.

Methods

Participants and design: This retrospective study included 262 children who underwent a clinical MRI scan and completed one of three preparation protocols: (1) standard oral explanations only (group 1, N = 55, age range: 6-10), (2) oral explanations plus a mock-scanner session (group 2, N = 70, age range: 8-18), and (3) oral explanations plus mock scanner plus VR training (group 3, N = 137, age range: 6-10). No participants were excluded from the analysis.

Preparation protocols: In group 1, children received standardized oral explanations about the MRI examination, including the purpose of the scan, the types of sounds produced by the scanner, the need to remove all metal objects, and the importance of remaining still throughout the entire session. Children were encouraged to ask questions, and the examiner provided clarification as needed. Group 2 received the same oral explanations as group 1, complemented by a mock-scanner session. Children could lie in a mock MRI scanner, listen to recorded scanner sounds, and familiarize themselves with the physical environment and noise level before the actual scan. Group 3 combined oral explanations and the mock-scanner experience with an additional VR-based training. In this condition, children completed ‘MRI Adventure’ (Figure 1), an immersive VR serious game specifically designed to teach children MRI safety rules and prepare them for the scan. The VR session lasted approximately 10 minutes and was administered once in the waiting period before entering the scanner.

MRI acquisition: After completion of the assigned preparation protocol, children underwent an MRI session on one of two 3T Siemens Prisma Fit scanners equipped with a 64-channel head coil (University Hospital of Lausanne or Brain and Behaviour Laboratory of Geneva). Resting-state fMRI data were acquired using a single-shot gradient-echo echo-planar imaging sequence. For groups 1 and 2, 720 volumes were collected (TR = 500 ms, voxel size = 2.5 mm isotropic); for group 3, 510 volumes were collected (TR = 1’000 ms, voxel size = 2.5 mm isotropic).

Preprocessing and motion metrics: Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using the fMRIPrep pipeline. Framewise displacement (FD) was computed for each volume. For each participant we extracted mean FD and maximum FD across the resting-state run as primary measures of head motion.

Statistical analysis: All statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS. To assess group differences in head motion while accounting for developmental effects, we performed analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) with preparation group (1, 2, 3) as a fixed factor and age as a covariate, separately for mean FD and maximum FD. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using Bonferroni correction. To further explore age-related effects, we conducted exploratory analyses based on a median split on age (median = 7.77 years-old), creating a younger group (N = 130) and an older group (N = 132). Within each age subgroup, one-way ANOVAs were performed to compare preparation groups on mean FD and maximum FD, followed by post-hoc tests. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η²ₚ).

Results

We found that mean FD was significantly different between the three groups (F(2,258) = 12.779, p < 0.001, η²ₚ = 0.129), indicating a large effect of preparation protocol on average head motion after controlling for age. Post-hoc comparisons showed that children in the VR group (group 3) exhibited significantly lower mean FD than those who received only oral explanations (group 1; p < 0.001) and those who received oral explanations plus mock scanner (group 2; p < 0.001). Thus, VR-based preparation was associated with the lowest average head motion among the three protocols. For maximum FD, we also found a significant group effect (F(2,258) = 6.002, p < 0.001, η²ₚ = 0.065). Post-hoc analyses indicated that the VR group showed significantly lower maximum FD compared to both group 1 (p < 0.001) and group 2 (p = 0.005). These findings suggest that VR-based training not only reduces average motion but also mitigates peak movement during the scan.

To further examine the benefits of VR-based preparation across age range, we used the median split method to divide the sample into a younger subgroup and an older subgroup. In the younger subgroup, mean FD showed a robust effect of preparation group (F(2,127) = 13.594, p < 0.001, η²ₚ = 0.176). Post-hoc tests revealed that the VR group had significantly lower mean FD than the group that received only oral explanations (group 1: p < 0.001), whereas the difference between the VR group and the mock-scanner group (group 2) did not reach significance. For maximum FD, the younger subgroup also showed a significant group effect (F(2,127) = 5.854, p = 0.004, η²ₚ = 0.084). In line with the mean FD results, post-hoc tests revealed that the VR group had a lower maximum FD than the group receiving explanations only (p = 0.003), whereas there was no significant difference when compared to the mock-scanner group (p = 0.762).  In the older group, mean FD remained significant, although with a smaller effect size (F(2,129) = 3.337, p = 0.039, η²ₚ = 0.049). Post-hoc comparisons indicated that the VR group still outperformed group 1 (p = 0.036), but not group 2 (p= 0.698). For maximum FD, there was no significant effect of preparation group among older children (F(2,129) = 1.483, p = 0.231, η²ₚ = 0.022). This result suggests that preparation type has a more limited impact on peak movement in older children.

Conclusion

Our retrospective study demonstrates that adding the VR serious-game preparation ‘MRI Adventure’ to standard pre-scan explanations and a mock-scanner session is associated with a robust reduction in both average and peak head motion during pediatric MRI. Across the full sample, children who received VR-based training exhibited significantly lower mean and maximum framewise displacement than those who received only oral explanations or oral explanations plus a mock-scanner session. Furthermore, analyses stratified by age indicated that these benefits are particularly pronounced in younger children, who typically present the biggest challenge for motion-free imaging. By contrast, differences between preparation methods were less pronounced in older children, especially for peak movement, suggesting that VR-based preparation may be most impactful in early and middle childhood. These age-stratified findings should, however, be interpreted with caution given the unbalanced sample sizes across preparation groups. These findings have important clinical and practical implications. Reducing head motion can directly improve image quality, decrease the need for repeated sequences, and potentially reduce reliance on sedation, which carry costs, logistical constraints, and possible neurodevelopmental risks1 in children. VR serious games such as ‘MRI Adventure’ offer a scalable, engaging, and child-friendly approach that can be integrated into the waiting period before the scan, with minimal additional burden for clinical staff once the system is in place. The present work has several limitations. First, the design was retrospective thus non-randomized, with unbalanced group sizes and differences in acquisition protocols (including TR and number of volumes) across groups. Second, although motion was characterized using widely accepted metrics (mean and maximum FD), we did not include subjective outcomes such as pre-scan anxiety, perceived comfort, or acceptability of the different preparation procedures.

As a next step, we plan to address these limitations and rigorously assess the causal impact of VR-based MRI preparation in a prospective randomized controlled trial. In this study, children will be randomly assigned to one of two preparation arms: (1) standard care consisting of structured oral explanations, or (2) standard care plus ‘MRI Adventure’ VR training. Group sizes will be balanced, and both preparation and scanning procedures will be standardized across conditions. The primary endpoints will be objective motion indices, while secondary outcomes will include measures of child anxiety, perceived comfort, and acceptability, as well as the subjective experience of time in the scanner. This trial will provide higher-level evidence on whether VR serious-game training can reliably improve pediatric MRI data quality and patient experience, and will inform future recommendations for implementing VR-based preparation in clinical practice.
