Introduction

Transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) is a non-invasive brain stimulation technique that applies low intensity alternating currents (1-2 mA) between at least two scalp electrodes. The current then spreads to underlying brain areas and can modulate endogenous neuronal oscillations at a specific frequency driven with tACS frequency. tACS is increasingly used in cognitive neuroscience to probe and enhance cognitive functions in healthy subjects. Furthermore, it has been shown that tACS effects on cognition are often stronger after stimulation (so-called “aftereffects”) than during the stimulation itself.1

One key cognitive function is inhibition, i.e. the ability to suppress an ongoing mental process. Inhibition is notably associated with beta band oscillatory (13-30 Hz) activity in the right inferior frontal gyrus (rIFG) and in the pre-supplementary motor area.2 A standard paradigm used to assess inhibition is the stop-signal task (SST), in  which participants are asked to provide rapid responses  to visual stimuli. However, following certain stimuli, a stop signal requires participants to stop the ongoing response.3

Here, we report the results from the first phase of the Stimpark clinical trial, which is aimed at improving inhibition in Parkinson’s disease patients using tACS. Participants completed a visual SST before and after receiving personalized rIFG beta tACS, while  high-resolution EEG (HR-EEG) was recorded simultaneously. The study is currently ongoing with Parkinson’s disease patients, but the cognitive performance results of the healthy controls (HCs) group are presented here.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-seven (N=37) HCs were included in this study. All participants had no major cognitive impairment, psychiatric or neurologic conditions, and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. 

Study design

Participants were enrolled in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossoverdesign and completed a neuropsychological assessment to evaluate overall cognitive functioning with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA).4 HCs performed an inhibition task during which HR-EEG was simultaneously recorded. In the second visit, participants received in a pseudo-randomized order, either real tACS or sham stimulation. The stimulation condition was reversed during the third visit. The study design is described in Figure 1A.  

Inhibition task

Inhibition was measured using a SST, according to the standards described in the literature.3 HCs had to press a left arrow or right arrow key as fast and as accurately as possible in response to a visual stimulus: the direction of an arrow displayed at the center of a screen (Go trials). On 25% of trials, a stop signal appeared just after the stimulus, requiring HCs to withhold their response (Stop trials). The delay between presentation of the arrow stimulus and the stop signal was dynamically adjusted to achieve approximately 50% accuracy (decreased following an accurate response and increased after an error), which is necessary to comply with the assumptions for a robust calculation of the stop signal reaction time (SSRT). The SST is described in Figure 1B.

The SSRT represents the time needed to stop the ongoing action. Other measures such as the probability to respond (i.e. to press an arrow button) during a stop trial (p(resp/sign)) and the delay between a stimulus and a stop sign (stop-signal delay : SSD) were computed. If p(resp/sign) was below 0.25 or above 0.75, the SSRT was not calculated because of low reliability.3

Determination of participant-specific beta frequency    

    

The participant-specific beta frequency during the SST was determined to personalize tACS. HR-EEG data were analyzed by focusing exclusively on correct “Stop” trials to specifically investigate the inhibition process involved in the SST. The beta peak frequency was calculated after source reconstruction with the weighted minimum norm estimates and spectral parameterization with the specparam toolbox on Brainstorm respectively. We targeted the right pars opercularis region of the rIFG (Desikan-Killiany atlas).5 If no beta peak was detected in the pars opercularis, we selected the pars orbitalis or pars triangularis. If no beta peak was detected in any of these regions, participants received an arbitrary 30 Hz tACS (7/37 HCs).

tACS protocol

A 10-minute tACS session was applied over the rIFG at 1mA during the SST with the stimulation frequency set to each participant’s individual beta frequency. A 10-second fade-in/fade-out period was included to minimize tACS perception at onset and offset. Stimulation was applied using two electrodes located at F8 and Cz standard EEG positions. Sham stimulation used the same protocol, except that no stimulation was delivered during the SST.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed with the Jamovi toolbox (version 2.5) on R (version 4.4.1.). The significance threshold was set at 0.05. Mean, standard deviation (sd), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for models’ parameters estimates were calculated. We used linear mixed models to compare pre and post-tACS, p(resp/sign), in both sham and real tACS conditions. We preferred this method to ANOVA in order to account for missing data, for fixed and for random effects. The models included age and MoCA scores as fixed effects and participants as a random effect.

Results

Demographical data

Thirty-seven HCs (18 males, 19 females) aged between 46-73 years old (mean = 63, sd = 7.6) with an education level between 11-20 years (mean = 15.3, sd = 2.6) were included in this study. Participants had a MoCA score between 26 and 30 (mean = 28.1, sd = 1.15). All MoCA scores were therefore considered as normal (Superior or equal to 26/30).

Sham tACS

Thirty (30) pre-tACS SSRTs and twenty-nine (29) post-tACS SSRTs were calculated in the sham tACS condition. Mean pre-tACS and post-tACS SSRT were 249 ms and 231 ms; with  sd = 54.7 ms and 43.7 ms, respectively. Minimal and maximal pre-tACS SSRTs were 122 ms and 405 ms, respectively; and the minima and maximal post-tACS SSRTs were 141 ms and 298 ms, respectively. 

Thirty (30) pre-tACS p(resp/sign) and twenty-nine 29 post-tACS p(resp/sign) were computed. The mean pre-tACS p(resp/sign) was 0.61 (sd = 0.09) and the post-tACS p(resp/sign) was 0.63 (sd = 0.07). The minimal and maximal pre-tACS were 0.37 and 0.73 respectively, and their post-tACS counterparts were 0.5 and 0.73 respectively (Figure 2A). 

Finally, HC had a mean pre-tACS SSD = 333 ms (sd = 75.4 ms, minimum = 188 ms, maximum = 475ms) and mean post-tACS SSD = 322 ms (sd = 66.6, minimum = 215, maximum = 472).

The linear mixed models did not reveal  any significant effects of tACS, age or MoCA on SSRT, nor on p(resp/sign). Besides, all estimates in the p(resp/sign) model were close to zero, indicating an extremely weak effect.

The detailed model parameter estimates for the sham tACS SSRT and the p(resp/sign) are in Figure 2B. 

Real tACS

Thirty-one (31) pre-tACS SSRTs and thirty (30) post-tACS SSRTs were computed in the real tACS condition. The mean pre-tACS SSRT and post-tACS SSRT were 228 ms and 233 ms; sd = 52.3 ms and 52.1 ms, respectively. The minimal and maximal pre-tACS SSRT values were 141 ms and 379 ms, respectively; and the minima and maximal post-tACS SSRT values were 103 ms and 309 ms, respectively. 

Thirty-one (31) pre-tACS p(resp/sign) and thirty (30) post-tACS p(resp/sign) were computed. The mean pre-tACS p(resp/sign) = 0.64 (sd = 0.08) and the post-tACS p(resp/sign) = 0.66 (sd = 0.07). The minimal and maximal pre-tACS values were 0.37 and 0.73, respectively; and their post-tACS counterparts were 0.53 and 0.73, respectively (Figure 2A). 

Finally, HC had a mean pre-tACS SSD = 328 ms (sd = 71 ms, minimum = 207 ms, maximum = 516 ms) and mean post-tACS SSD = 310 ms (sd = 73.9, minimum = 211, maximum = 489).

The linear mixed models did not show  any significant effects of tACS, age or MoCA on SSRT, nor on p(resp/sign). Besides, all estimates in the p(resp/sign) model were close to zero, indicating an extremely weak effect.

The detailed model parameter estimates for the real tACS SSRT and the p(resp/sign) are in Figure 2B.

Conclusion

Our results found that rIFG beta tACS does not improve inhibition in HCs. SSRTs were globally stable during the task, whether participants  received  real or sham stimulation. Similarly, p(resp/sign) remained globally unchanged. In other words, neither the time needed to effectively suppress an unwanted response, nor the probability to erroneously respond to a stop sign were impacted by tACS in our experiment. 

A plausible explanation for why our patients’ inhibition did not improve may be that they already had optimal inhibition performance, and so could not be enhanced any further. The study is currently ongoing with Parkinson’s disease patients. Since these patients commonly experience executive impairment, among which inhibitory control disorder, we expect tACS to have a measurable effect on their SSRT performance.
