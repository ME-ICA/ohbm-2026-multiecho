Introduction

Musically trained individuals often show stronger performance across a range of auditory and cognitive abilities, including language, attention, working memory, and executive control. Neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies also suggest differences in how auditory and executive-control networks are engaged in musicians compared with non-musicians. Yet relatively few studies have followed children over time to determine how these differences unfold from early childhood into adolescence. Emerging evidence further suggests that the “musician advantage” may be strongest when training begins earlier, potentially during sensitive developmental periods, highlighting timing as a key factor in shaping outcomes. Because developmental trajectories change substantially from childhood to adolescence, responses to musical training may also vary across these stages.

Methods

To address these gaps, we present findings from a longitudinal investigation that harmonized data from two independent samples examining the effects of after-school music programs on cognitive and brain development in children from low-income communities in Los Angeles (total N = 164, ages 6–14).

Sample 1 came from a 7-year longitudinal study (N = 72; 29 female; mean age at baseline = 6.99, SD = 1.44). Children were recruited from community-based, free music and sports programs and from public schools in Los Angeles. Participants in the Music group (N = 22; mean age = 6.53, SD = 0.44) and Sport group (N = 23; mean age = 6.98, SD = 1.15) had enrolled in their respective programs prior to recruitment but had not yet begun training. They completed all baseline assessments before starting program activities. A third group of children not enrolled in any enrichment program served as the Control group (N = 27; mean age at baseline = 7.37, SD = 2.00).

Sample 2 included students from two public charter schools in similarly underserved neighborhoods of Los Angeles. One school offered an after-school music program (N = 42; mean age = 10.82, SD = 0.52), while the other offered free after-school enrichment programming that did not include music (N = 50; mean age = 9.27, SD = 0.66). Children in both samples were assessed annually at USC’s Brain and Creativity Institute using a comprehensive battery of cognitive and auditory measures, along with neuroimaging that included structural MRI and diffusion-weighted imaging

Results

Across samples, children in the music programs showed faster development of working memory and inhibitory control relative to non-musically trained peers. However, this advantage diminished by ages 9–10, when non-musically trained children reached comparable performance levels and duration of music training no longer predicted age-related growth slopes. In contrast, we did not observe a comparable age-dependent effect for cognitive flexibility.

Neuroimaging analyses further indicated reduced cortical thinning in the music group within a right-lateralized set of auditory and motor regions, including superior temporal and inferior frontal areas, a pattern that was not observed in the sports or control groups. This suggests that music training may be associated with altered maturation of brain regions supporting auditory–motor integration during childhood, consistent with the observed timing-related differences in cognitive development.

Conclusion

Together, these findings suggest that music training is associated with accelerated development of specific executive functions and with altered trajectories of brain maturation during childhood. Importantly, the cognitive benefits of music training appear to be emerging most strongly in early childhood and diminishing as non-musically trained peers reach similar levels of performance by late childhood. This pattern highlights the importance of developmental timing and sensitive periods in shaping the effects of musical intervention. At the neural level, differences in cortical maturation within auditory and motor regions suggest that music training may influence how these systems develop during early stages of learning. Taken together, our results underscore the value of early, sustained access to music education, particularly in underserved communities, while also emphasizing the need for longitudinal designs that span multiple developmental stages to fully capture when, how, and for whom music training exerts its greatest impact.
