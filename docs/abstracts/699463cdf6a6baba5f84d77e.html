Introduction

The informative nature of brain network representations, established across various neuroscientific disciplines, provides a crucial lens for understanding neurological function 1,2. However, a complete mechanistic understanding of how focal lesions impact the brain's complex relational structure remains elusive, hindering mechanistic inference and treatment innovation. Our work seeks to advance this area by developing an expressive network model of the lesioned brain. We combine foundation models 3 with the disconnectome, a precise structural network representation quantifying fibre loss, demonstrating the high expressive potential of this approach. Furthermore, we introduce a deep Graph Auto-encoder that represents disconnectomes as a short latent space vector, a high-fidelity compression of the patient’s lesion disruption network. Crucially, these topology-informed representations, both the disconnectome as well as its latent representation, exhibit superior performance in predicting individual patient clinical deficits across NIHSS sub-scores compared with standard clinical covariates, solidifying further innovation towards network-based prognostication and personalized intervention in the lesioned brain.

Methods

We used a large dataset of 4732 ischaemic stroke patient lesion masks 4created via automated lesion segmentation described elsewhere 5. To create graphs of each patient’s lesion-induced white matter disruption, processing of the data involved generating a disconnectome network representation using the arise toolbox 6. This approach quantifies lesion disruption based on a normative white matter tractogram, with network nodes defined by the Schaefer cortical parcellation 7with 400 cortical regions-of-interest (ROIs), and weighted edges representing the magnitude of fibre tract loss between regions. The resulting disconnectomes were binarized to improve comparability and are described via a 400 by 400-dimensional adjacency matrix. The data was partitioned into a training set (N=3732) and a validation set (N=1000). Available clinical covariates were age, gender, lesion volume, lesion laterality, and 15 individual NIHSS items.

The core of the analysis rests on a specialized Graph Auto-encoder (GAE) model. The encoder module is built upon SAGEConv graph convolutional layers 8 which learn node embeddings by aggregating information from local neighbourhoods using message-passing algorithms. This is sequentially paired with TopKPooling layers 9 for hierarchical graph down-sampling. Node embeddings increase along encoding layers from the initial single value to 32, 64, and 128 in the final encoding layer. The architecture utilizes a ReLu activation function and a 0.1 dropout rate to prevent overfitting. The final graph embedding is transformed by a single fully connected layer into a concise 64-dimensional latent vector z, which serves as the compressed lesion fingerprint. The decoder module symmetrically reconstructs the inverse structure of the encoder (see Figure 1 A). Training was performed using a 90/10 train/test split, minimizing the Binary Cross-Entropy loss between the input adjacency matrix (Ainput) and the reconstructed matrix (Arecon). We additionally employ a simpler graph-to-tabular approach, as introduced in 10, wherein the matrix representation of the lesion disconnectome, specifically the adjacency matrix, is provided as input to the tabular foundation model utilised for subsequent classifications. To achieve this, the adjacency matrix is reduced to a 400-dimensional vector that contains the node degree for each node in the graph, thus encoding both the structural integration and interconnectedness of the regions of interest (ROIs). To quantify the variation in predictive utility across three distinct modelling approaches (pure clinical variables, the compressed latent vector z, and the raw disconnectome adjacency matrices), we employed tab-PFN2.5 11 an established foundation model designed for tabular data classification. This model was used to predict the categorical NIHSS sub-scores for the N=625 patients in the validation set with complete data. To mitigate significant class imbalances inherent to individual NIHSS sub-scores, SMOTE imputation 12 was applied, strictly limited to a maximum 20% imputation to avoid artificial performance increases while at the same time ensuring a maximum class-imbalance ratio of 2:1. Classification was subsequently performed with a 90/10 train/test split, and results were averaged across 10 independent runs to ensure robustness. Where applicable statistical testing of performance increases was performed using related one-sided t-tests.

Results

The Graph Auto-encoder (GAE) model demonstrated successful reconstruction of the stroke patient disconnectomes across all patients in the validation set, evidenced by a strong reduction in the validation loss during training (see Figure 1 B). The fidelity between the ground truth and reconstructed graphs was captured across key network measurements, highlighting the high accuracy of the GAE-based reconstructions. Specifically, the mean (± standard deviation) for the average node degree was 23.6774 (± 17.486) for the ground truth and 20.6053 (± 18.8651) for the reconstruction. The ground-truth and reconstruction network density were 0.0593 (± 0.0438) and 0.0516 (± 0.0473) respectively. Furthermore, the clustering coefficient was 0.4266 (± 0.1543) for the ground truth and 0.3868 (± 0.1877) for the reconstructed graphs, confirming the model's ability to preserve essential topological properties across embedding into latent space and performing reconstructions (see Figure 1 C). Classification of clinical deficits was limited to nine specific NIHSS sub-scores (1 Conscious-Questions, 2 Facial Palsy, 3 Motor Arm Left, 4 Motor Arm Right, 5 Motor Leg Left, 6 Motor Leg Right, 7 Sensory, 8 Language, and 9 Dysarthria) due to the application of the 20% upper limit on SMOTE imputation. This strict constraint was essential to mitigate the risk of artificially overfitting the classifier to the synthetic samples generated for the minority classes, thereby ensuring the predictive power was learned from genuine patient features rather than imputed data. Classification accuracy showed a strong predictive capability of the tabular foundation model, tab-PFN2.5, across the three different input feature sets utilised. The first model, using clinical covariates (age, gender, lesion volume, and lesion laterality) alone, showed the lowest overall performance across NIHSS scores (1: 0.6157 +/- 0.0436, 2: 0.5940 +/- 0.0422, 3: 0.7320 +/- 0.0462, 4: 0.6081 +/- 0.0323, 5: 0.6531 +/- 0.0497, 6: 0.6292 +/- 0.0358, 7: 0.6109 +/- 0.0329, 8: 0.6773 +/- 0.0587, 9: 0.4997 +/- 0.0179) , particularly in the critical motor (scores 3-6) and language (score 8) categories, which represent predominant deficits in ischaemic stroke populations. The latent representations combined with covariates significantly (p-value=0.005) improved upon these baseline accuracies across all categories (1: 0.6829 +/- 0.0453, 2: 0.6186 +/- 0.0745, 3: 0.7019 +/- 0.0447, 4: 0.6615 +/- 0.0643, 5: 0.7401 +/- 0.0510, 6: 0.7568 +/- 0.0646, 7: 0.6761 +/- 0.0346, 8: 0.7364 +/- 0.0528, 9: 0.5092 +/- 0.0311). The raw disconnectomes combined with covariates showed even further enhanced predictive performance, yielding the highest balanced accuracy scores in the range of (1: 0.6423 +/- 0.0636, 2: 0.6125 +/- 0.0601, 3: 0.7506 +/- 0.0372, 4: 0.7625 +/- 0.0600, 5: 0.7920 +/- 0.0467, 6: 0.7968 +/- 0.0558, 7: 0.7467 +/- 0.0619, 8: 0.7920 +/- 0.0413, 9: 0.5124 +/- 0.0435) (Figure 1 D). This demonstrates the increasing predictive value gained by the synergy of foundation models and disconnectome representations of the lesion disruption.

Conclusion

We have introduced a Graph Auto-encoder (GAE) architecture capable of extracting patient-specific network information, encoding it into an informative latent representation, and accurately reconstructing the original relational structure of the input disconnectome network.  This reconstruction capability represents a necessary, foundational functionality for the establishment of generative graph models of pathological brains. Such generative models are required for downstream foundational modelling across diverse lesion pathologies, enabling the creation of synthetic, yet biologically plausible, lesion-disruption scenarios, and thereby truly capturing the complex, distributed response pattern of the brain to focal injury. Critically, we provide robust evidence that the topological network representations captured in patient disconnectomes and by extension our model's internal latent representation of that topology, provide sufficient differential information to significantly improve classification of clinical severity with a more fine-tuned prediction model for task-specific NIHSS items, compared to the current clinical standard of aggregated NIHSS scores. These aggregated clinical scores oftentimes fall short of providing an accurate, granular depiction of individual clinical deficits 13. Our results demonstrate that the latent lesion representation is a closer approximate of neurological outcome than traditional covariates, stimulating innovation towards highly expressive topology-informed clinical inference. Future work will build on these foundations to advance to generative models of lesion disconnectomes, driving innovation toward a truly comprehensive, predictive model of lesion impact on the inherently relational human brain.
