Introduction

Identifying epileptogenic regions from SEEG in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) remains a critical yet challenging step in surgical planning. While computational models such as The Virtual Brain (TVB) offer valuable insights into seizure dynamics, their spatial granularity is often insufficient for delineating patient-specific surgical targets.[1][2] 

In this work, we present a surface-based modeling framework that enhances spatial resolution while maintaining computational efficiency through geometric mesh reduction techniques. The forward model emphasizes large-scale patterns of seizure spread rather than detailed local parameterization, allowing propagation to be simulated within a compact temporal window.[3] A complementary reconstruction strategy is then used to relate simulated activity to observed SEEG features, enabling the estimation of cortical regions plausibly involved in seizure generation.

Overall, this framework provides a high-resolution, data-informed approach for examining seizure propagation and supports more refined assessment of candidate surgical margins.

Methods

Patient-specific cortical surfaces were reconstructed from T1-weighted MRI using established neuroimaging pipelines. White-matter and pial boundaries were extracted, and an intermediate cortical sheet was derived for subsequent modeling. To enable efficient simulations, the high-density surface mesh was reduced to a lower-resolution representation through a geometry-preserving procedure that maintains the overall shape and mesh quality while substantially decreasing computational demands.

The original cortical surface consisted of 327,684 vertices and 655,360 triangular faces. For computational feasibility, this surface was downsampled to a mesh containing 5,124 vertices and 10,240 faces while preserving overall geometry and mesh uniformity. To ensure that seizure propagation dynamics remained comparable to those observed on the full-resolution surface, local coupling parameters were rescaled in accordance with the change in mesh density, allowing the downsampled model to reproduce propagation speeds consistent with the high-resolution cortical geometry.

SEEG electrode positions were obtained by registering post-implant CT to the corresponding T1-weighted MRI, followed by preprocessing to extract individual contact coordinates. Across subjects, 8–12 contacts were typically identified, resulting in a total of 112 labeled SEEG contact positions stored in the MRI-native coordinate space.

Seizure propagation was simulated using a neural field formulation of the Epileptor 6D model implemented on the downsampled mesh. Local spatial coupling was introduced through a Laplacian convolutional kernel defined on the cortical surface, enabling smooth, topology-aware propagation of activity across neighboring vertices. This construction allowed large-scale ictal dynamics to evolve on the cortical sheet while remaining independent of long-range tractography-derived connectivity. Simulated signals were then projected into SEEG space using a forward model derived from the surface geometry and electrode configuration.

SEEG features used for comparison with simulated activity were derived by extracting early ictal recruitment patterns. High-frequency activity envelopes were obtained for each channel, and onset timings were estimated using threshold-based criteria. These timings provided an ordered sequence of channel activations, serving as the primary empirical feature for model–data comparison.

Cortical regions contributing to the observed SEEG pattern were estimated through a reconstruction procedure evaluating how well each surface location supports the measured onset sequence. The resulting values were transformed into spatial probability maps, highlighting regions most consistent with the empirical data. These maps were used to identify candidate areas potentially relevant for surgical consideration.

Results

To generate synthetic data for validation, a simplified ground-truth configuration was constructed on the 5,124-vertex mesh. Ten vertices were selected as the epileptogenic zone and assigned a higher excitability level, while all remaining vertices were configured as propagation zone. Forward simulation of seizure spread yielded vertex-wise time series, which were subsequently projected into SEEG space using a distance-based gain matrix designed to mimic amplitude attenuation with increasing depth and distance. These synthetic SEEG signals served as input for evaluating the inverse reconstruction performance.

Inverse reconstruction was performed by computing a vertex-wise loss value reflecting the discrepancy between simulated and observed SEEG activity. When the candidate set was restricted to vertices spatially adjacent to the SEEG contacts, the optimization converged to a compact cluster that closely matched the ground-truth epileptogenic region (10 vertices). This restricted-domain approach improved localization precision by reducing the number of competing solutions and yielded the smallest spatial deviation between reconstructed and true onset nodes.

When the search space was expanded to include all 5,124 cortical vertices, the method produced broader spatial distributions-consistent with the increased solution space-yet still identified a focused area that overlapped substantially with the ground-truth region. Although reconstruction margins widened under full-domain conditions, the peak of the inferred map consistently fell within or immediately adjacent to the synthetic EZ cluster, demonstrating robustness despite the absence of spatial priors.

A major benefit of the framework lies in the computational efficiency gained through GPU acceleration. The original CPU-only implementation required over 10 hours to complete the forward workflow. After GPU compilation, the total processing time was reduced to approximately 3 minutes, enabling rapid experimentation and making large-scale parameter sweeps or repeated reconstructions feasible.

Conclusion

The framework demonstrated its ability to recover predefined onset regions in controlled tests, supporting its internal validity. Future work will extend this evaluation to clinical SEEG datasets to explore its utility in identifying patient-specific targets and its potential relationship with postoperative outcomes. We also plan to incorporate subject-derived structural information to better capture individual patterns of seizure spread and to further refine the reconstruction process.

While GPU acceleration enables efficient forward simulations, the inverse estimation remains computationally demanding due to the model’s spatial resolution. Ongoing efforts aim to streamline this step through methodological refinements that maintain reconstruction reliability while reducing processing time. These developments are expected to enhance the framework’s practicality for broader clinical application.
