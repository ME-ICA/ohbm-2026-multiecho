Introduction

Cognitive fatigue (CF) is one of the most frequent and disabling symptoms in multiple sclerosis (MS), with a marked impact on quality of life, and is typically defined as a subjective feeling of mental exhaustion that emerges or intensifies during sustained cognitive effort, often accompanied by objective performance decline and/or increased variability on demanding tasks. CF is only partly explained by white‑matter lesion load, and can be prominent even in patients with relatively preserved physical disability, underscoring its partly independent clinical course. Despite its clinical relevance, the pathophysiology of CF remains only partially understood. Current models converge on a multifactorial origin that includes neuroimmune and neuroendocrine dysregulation, together with structural and functional changes in distributed cortico‑subcortical networks. Network alterations often co‑occur with gray matter (GM) damage, which can be detected from the early stages of MS across different phenotypes, progresses over time, and shows stronger associations with cognitive outcomes and fatigue than classical measures such as lesion load1. Structural MRI studies support an association between GM loss and CF in MS: higher fatigue levels have been linked to reduced volume in deep GM nuclei (most consistently the thalamus and basal ganglia) and in frontal cortical regions that subserve attention, executive control and effort‑based decision making. Temporal and limbic structures (e.g., hippocampus and amygdala) also emerge as relevant nodes, in line with the emotional and interoceptive components of CF2. However, evidence on how GM changes relate to different levels of experienced CF (for example, low versus high fatigue) remains limited and heterogeneous. Regions implicated vary across studies, so the relationship between regional GM damage and subjective CF is still not clearly defined3.

Methods

Here, we investigate the potential association between GM atrophy and CF levels (AULSS2 Ethical approval protocol #1332). 30 relapsing-remitting MS patients have been enrolled and divided into two groups with different CF level (low vs high; LF vs HF) according to neurologists’ clinical evaluation. 15 healthy participants (HC) have been also included, with age and education levels similar to MS groups (HC: mean 45.3 y, sd 4.1; LF: mean 41.9 y, sd 3.5; mean HF: mean 45.3 y, sd 3.8). All participants underwent a comprehensive cognitive and mood assessment: CF was assessed with Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS), Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), Piper Fatigue Scale (PFS) and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Cognitive functioning was further characterized by the Cognitive Reserve Index questionnaire (CRIq) and the Brief Repeatable Battery of Neuropsychological Tests in Multiple Sclerosis (BRB-NT). Mood and anxiety were evaluated with Beck Depression Inventory–II (BDI-II) and State-Trait Anxiety Inventory 1 e 2 (STAI-Y) and sleep quality was assessed with the Pittsburg Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). ANOVA and post-hoc t-tests were conducted on normally distributed variables, while Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn’s test were adopted for non-normally distributed data. T1-weighted high resolution images were acquired for all participants on a 3.0 T system (Siemens VIDA - Erlangen, Germany) at the Neuroradiology Department of Treviso Hospital (Treviso, Italy) with the following parameters: 176 axial slices, voxel size (isotropic): 0.9 mm, repetition time (TR) = 2200 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.53, flip angle (FA) = 8◦, field of view (FOV) = 260*260 mm. Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) was conducted on MRI data, preprocessed and analyzed with the Computational Analysis Toolbox (CAT125), denoised and corrected for intensity nonuniformity (bias-field). Then tissue segmentation has been performed relying on probabilistic priors, and resulting GM images have been spatially normalized to the MNI standard template using a nonlinear registration approach. Jacobian modulation has been applied, to preserve local volumes. Finally, GM images have been smoothed with a 6*6*6 FWHM Gaussian kernel to improve signal-to-noise ratio. The resulting images were entered into a One-way ANOVA, comparing GM regional volumes between the three groups with the ROI analysis tool implemented in CAT12, according to the Neuromorphometrics atlas (p <.05, Bonferroni-Holm correction for multiple comparisons). Total Intracranial Volume (TIV) was entered as a covariate to account for possible differences due to head volume. 

Results

Neuropsychological assessment: For variables with a normal distribution, data were analysed using ANOVA followed by one-tailed post-hoc t-tests, whereas non-normally distributed measures (as determined by the Shapiro–Wilk test) were examined using Kruskal–Wallis tests with one-tailed Dunn post-hoc contrasts. In the HC>LF comparison, the two groups showed broadly similar profiles on almost all measures, including direct fatigue indices (MFIS, FSS); however, LF patients displayed evidence of psychological distress, with significantly lower coping resources (CRIq, z = -2.330, p = 0.01) and elevated state and trait anxiety (STAI-Y1, z = -1.916, p = 0.028; STAI-Y2, z = -2.123, p = 0.017) relative to HC. In contrast, the HC>HF comparison indicated marked impairment in the HF group across all domains assessed, including mood (BDI, z = 3.307, p = 0.001; STAI-Y1, z = 1.769, p = 0.038), coping (CRI, z = -3.158, p < 0.001), fatigue scales (FSS, z = 3.992; MFIS, z = 4.571; PFS, z = 3.879; all p < 0.001), cognitive performance (PASAT, z = 2.719, p < 0.001), and sleep quality (PSQI, z = 2.258, p = 0.011). Consistently, HF patients also scored significantly worse than LF patients on nearly all measures—with the exception of coping—showing higher depressive symptoms (BDI, z = -4.147), greater state anxiety (STAI-Y1, z = -3.685), altered trait anxiety (Y-2, z = 3.369), more severe fatigue (FSS, z = -4.499; MFIS, z = -4.822; PFS, z = -4.791), poorer performance on the PASAT (z = 2.300; all p < 0.001), and reduced sleep quality (PSQI, z = -2.258, p = 0.011). No further significant between-group differences emerged.

VBM analysis: Consistently with the literature, larger GM volume in the right thalamus were detected for HC compared with the whole MS group (p=0.001; F-value: 8.721). This effect became even more evident when comparing HC with the two MS subgroups separately: the comparison HC>LF revealed bilateral reduced thalamic volume (left: p=0.035; F-value: 3.04; right: p=0.005; F-value 3.373). The comparison HC>HF confirmed the thalamic bilateral reduction (left: p=0.029; F-value: 3.739; right: p=0.007; F-value 3.714). In addition, HF showed a GM loss involving also the left transverse temporal gyrus (p=0.046; F-value: 2.729), putamen (p=0.0439; F-value: 2.078) and amygdala (p=0.027; F-value: 1.979) when compared to HC. Finally, LF appeared to be characterized by a GM increase in the right orbital gyrus in comparison to HF (p=0.044; F-value: 3.081). 

Conclusion

Our results highlight a clear dissociation between LF and HF in MS, both clinically and anatomically, suggesting that clinically relevant fatigue is not a mere subjective complaint, but is embedded in a broader pattern of emotional, cognitive and structural brain alterations.

Neuropsychological conclusions

Overall, LF patients are largely comparable to HC on core cognitive and fatigue measures, but already show a more vulnerable emotional profile, with less effective coping strategies and higher state/trait anxiety. HF patients, in contrast, present a pervasive impairment spanning mood, coping, subjective fatigue, cognitive efficiency and sleep quality, and differ not only from HC but also markedly from LF. This pattern supports the view of CF as occurring with affective dysregulation and cognitive/sleep disturbances, rather than as a simple increase along a single fatigue continuum progressing with disease.

VBM conclusions

Structurally, both MS subgroups show reduced thalamic gray matter compared with HC, in line with the central role of the thalamus in MS-related network disruption and fatigue. In HF, this subcortical involvement extends to additional limbic and temporal regions (putamen, amygdala, transverse temporal gyrus), suggesting a more widespread degeneration encompassing motor, emotional and sensory–cognitive networks. The relative GM increase in the right orbital gyrus in LF compared with HF may reflect preserved or compensatory prefrontal resources in patients who, despite having MS and some distress, do not develop the full-blown HF phenotype (yet).

Taken together, neuropsychological and VBM findings converge in supporting a model in which subcortical GM damage seems to provide a structural substrate for vulnerability to CF, while prefrontal reorganization could shape the transition from low to high CF states. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these hypotheses and clarify their prognostic implications.
