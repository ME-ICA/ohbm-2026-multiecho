Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) can transform healthcare by enabling earlier diagnosis, personalized treatment, and scalable decision support. Achieving this requires training models on large, diverse, multi-institutional datasets that reflect real-world variability, making data sharing essential. However, sharing sensitive health data introduces significant privacy challenges. When two datasets share records of the same individual without explicit identifiers, implicit features can reveal connections, enabling linkage of health records. This process poses privacy risks and allows attacks such as re-identification, attribute inference, and cross-dataset matching. 

It is well-known that individual facial features can be reconstructed based on T1-weighted Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI). Moreover, even after skull stripping, brain parenchyma can still serve as a link to match other MRIs of the same participant.1

This link could pose a risk, as matching a participant’s MRI across databases may allow an adversary to identify other scans from the same participant. In addition, associate them with clinical or demographic records and reconstruct unintended longitudinal health profiles, possibly compromising anonymity and exposing sensitive medical information.

To access this matching property of MRI, current research has used supervised, training-based, or deep learning based workflows2,3. Such methods may not qualify as "reasonably likely" as mandated by regulatory frameworks such as European GDPR, the US HIPAA, and the Australian Privacy Principles for the assessment of privacy risks, considering all the means (effort, time, technology, and expertise).

We here demonstrate that even with simple and standard MRI processing pipeline, combining preprocessing-based harmonization of T1-weighted MRI with similarity assessment using standard similarity metrics, can effectively link an individual's MRIs across longitudinal, cross-site, and multi-protocol cases.

Methods

We used standard pre-processing and image similarity assessment to evaluate whether a brain MRI could serve as a link between other MRIs from the same participant. The tools used in our work were open-source and widely available. We conducted our analysis in two phases: pre-evaluation and evaluation.

The pre-evaluation phase used two simulated datasets:

  1. SLDM: based on the LDM 100K dataset4, containing synthetic MR images.
  2. SHCP: based on the HCP Young Adult dataset5, comprising T1-weighted MRI from healthy individuals.

From each dataset, we randomly selected 100 images and applied four common perturbations using MONAI: affine transformation, intensity shift, contrast adjustment, and bias-field-based intensity distortion. This produced 500 images in total—100 originals and 400 transformed variants.

In the evaluation phase, we analyzed five public research datasets: two longitudinal, two traveling-participant, and one multi-protocol.

The longitudinal datasets included the Hormonal Health Study (HHS, OpenNeuro: ds005360) and a running intervention study (OpenNeuro: ds004937), both providing defaced T1w (MPRAGE) MRI scans from a 3T Siemens scanner. HHS comprised 66 scans from 30 healthy women (18–29 years) across three sessions, while the running study included 84 scans from four sessions of male participants (20–31 years).

Traveling-participant datasets included the Traveling Human Phantom (THP, OpenNeuro: ds000206) and SDSU Traveling Participants (SDSU-TS, OpenNeuro: ds005664). THP offers T1w scans of five adults scanned repeatedly across eight sites using Siemens, GE, and Philips scanners. SDSU-TS provides defaced MRI scans (3D MPRAGE or FSPGR) from nine adults (22–55 years) across two sites with varying voxel resolutions.

For the multi-protocol study, we used Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI, http://adni.loni.usc.edu). ADNI-1 and ADNI-2 employed different MRI protocols, so we treated them as separate datasets. We included 228 participants with scans in both phases, selecting one scan from ADNI-1 and the first from ADNI-2, yielding 456 scans from individuals with varying cognitive statuses (controls, MCI, dementia).

All images were harmonized using affine registration and bias correction with histogram matching for voxel-wise comparability. Figure 1 illustrates the pipeline, a sample image, its transformed versions, and the post-harmonization results. For ADNI, we applied CAT12 Version 9.0 (https://neuro-jena.github.io/cat/)  as a standard toolbox-based harmonization procedure.

Post-harmonization, we computed pairwise similarity using 11 metrics, evaluated for accuracy and efficiency on pre-evaluation sets. Scores were visualized as histograms, and pairs classified as intra- or inter-participant using thresholds estimated by Kernel Density Estimation (KDE).

Based on performance and computational time, we selected four metrics for the final evaluation.

Note: Our study does not attempt to match individuals across databases or break data silos. All analyses were performed within a single, authorized data environment using pre-existing, study-provided links between MRI scans solely to evaluate methodological claims. No external records were linked, and no attempts were made to re-identify participants, ensuring full compliance with data access and user agreements.

Results

Our study demonstrates that T1-weighted brain MRI could serve as a link across databases with near-perfect accuracy. This was achieved by harmonizing the MRI data using standard pre-processing, followed by a similarity assessment. 

Pre-Evaluation Phase: Demonstrating Feasibility of Accurate MRI matching in Simulated Real and Synthetic MRI Datasets:

In SLDM, all metrics, except Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), matched perturbed images to originals with an AUC of 1.0000, high sensitivity (0.9780--0.9990), and perfect specificity, based on the predicted thresholds. The top six similarity metrics, in descending order of performance, were Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), Multi-Scale SSIM (MS-SSIM), Four-Component Gradient-Regularized SSIM (4-G-R-SSIM), Normalized Mutual Information (NMI), Gradient Similarity, and Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC), all showing excellent separability after harmonization. For SHCP, SSIM and 4-G-R-SSIM again delivered the highest and similar performance, followed closely by MS-SSIM, NMI, Gradient Similarity, and PCC. FID remained the weakest metric across both datasets due to voxel-based analysis.

Most metrics computed similarity in under 0.5 seconds per image pair on a CPU, e.g., SSIM took 0.450 seconds, and MS-SSIM took 0.452 seconds. In contrast, 4-G-R-SSIM required 1.651 seconds due to rotation and multi-scale processing, while FID was the slowest at 2.590 seconds. Most metrics were custom implementations, other than MS-SSIM, SSIM, and Cosine Similarity, which were PyTorch-based.

Considering both accuracy and computational efficiency, SSIM, PCC, NMI, and Gradient Similarity outperformed other metrics, making them the most suitable choices for the evaluation phase.

Perfect accuracy in linking pseudonymous MRIs within longitudinal samples:

Evaluation on two pseudonymous longitudinal datasets, the Hormonal Health Study and a running intervention, achieved perfect scores in classifying MRI pairs from the same source, showing a clear divergence between the inter-participant and intra-participant histograms (metric values) across the four selected metrics.

Cross-site MRI scans linkable despite scanner variability:

On the THP dataset, after harmonization, we achieved an AUC of 0.963, sensitivity of 0.900, and specificity of 1.000 on the SSIM metric in linking the same participant's brain MRI across different sites. Gradient Similarity produced similar scores, while PCC and NMI, despite expected distributions, failed due to outliers and limited sample images, resulting in ineffective thresholding. 

However, similar results emerged when thresholds from pre-evaluation scenarios were applied: threshold = 0.45 for NMI and threshold = 0.97 for PCC.

On the SDSU-TS dataset, we achieved near-perfect performance across all metrics. The AUC was 1.000 for every metric except PCC, which recorded 0.996. Gradient Similarity showed the highest sensitivity (0.989), followed by NMI (0.979), SSIM (0.926), and PCC (0.915). Importantly, all metrics achieved 100%  specificity.

MRI matching remains highly probable regardless of cognitive status, imaging protocol, or the time interval between MRI acquisitions:

NMI achieved perfect performance with AUC = 1.0, specificity = 1.0, and sensitivity = 1.0. SSIM also reached AUC = 1.0 and specificity = 1.0, with a slightly lower sensitivity of 0.99. In contrast, PCC obtained AUC = 0.9998, specificity = 1.0, and sensitivity = 0.9427, while Gradient Similarity showed slightly weaker performance with AUC = 0.9885, sensitivity = 0.8150, and specificity = 1.0.

Conclusion

Previous studies have shown that a brain MRI can be linked to other MRIs of the same participants, thereby serving as a link for potentially combining demographic and health-related information between two databases. However, previous research has relied either on computationally intensive or training-based methods. We confirm the result and, more importantly, demonstrate that linking brain MRIs is feasible, even with standard and easy-to-compute methodologies. 

To demonstrate this, we used standard preprocessing methodologies to harmonize 3D T1-weighted scans and then computed their similarity using widely-used measures. We first pre-evaluated our strategy on two simulated datasets, and then confirmed the findings with experiments on five research databases, each containing several MRIs of the same participants.   

We achieved nearly perfect accuracy, with the AUC score reaching 1.0 in all cases except one, where it decreased to 0.96.

These findings support the implementation of stronger privacy safeguards and responsible data governance. We suggest that consent forms should include a question about prior participation in data sharing initiatives involving MRI scans. This additional context can help participants proactively inform data controllers if they have previously contributed MRI data. These results aim to contribute meaningfully to the development of thoughtful, forward-looking policies in data sharing.
