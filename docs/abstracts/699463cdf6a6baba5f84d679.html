Introduction

The hippocampus receives a cholinergic innervation via the is the septohippocampal (SH) pathway, a subcortical input shown to play a key role in synaptic plasticity, memory formation and network oscillations1. As the hippocampus is not a uniform structure and contains several functionally differentiated subfields, the latter may differently depend on cholinergic modulation. Yet, in the human brain, little is known about the exact extent of the SH cholinergic pathway and regional cholinergic pattern within specific hippocampal subfields1. Furthermore, an age-related decline of the cholinergic neurotransmission in some hippocampal subfields has been reported in rodents but to date, such a detailed analysis has not been performed in humans. Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging with [18F]Fluoroethoxybenzovesamicol ([18F]FEOBV), a radioligand binding selectively to the vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT), is a suitable tool for imaging presynaptic cholinergic innervation in human brain2. The main objective of the present study was to investigate whether [18F]FEOBV PET imaging is reliable to measure cholinergic signal in several hippocampal subfields in normal individuals. Secondly, we compared the VAChT density across subfields and, in each of them, searched for a potential relationship between cholinergic innervation and the age of the participants. 

Methods

We analyzed [18F]FEOBV PET and MR images of a group of healthy subjects consisting of 13 healthy participants aged between 25 and 75 years old without cognitive or neurological disorders. For image processing and analysis, we first segmented each participant’s MR image using the Freesurfer® software to define the hippocampal subfields and — for further analysis — some extra-hippocampal regions of interest (ROIs) including the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and the white matter of the posterior cingulate cortex (WM-PCC). To capture both a broader and more detailed anatomy, we applied two different hippocampal atlases: the HBT atlas, which divides the hippocampus into head, body and tail, and the FS60 atlas, which provides a more fined-grain segmentation including the subiculum, CA1, CA3, CA4 and dentate gyrus (DG). Using the PMOD®software, each participant's PET image was coregistered to the 3DT1 MR image for accurate anatomical alignment, thanks to the structural CT image provided by the PET scanner. Subsequently, we applied a partial volume effect (PVE) correction to adjust each PET image for signal spillover between neighboring regions. Then, after applying the Freesurfer atlases on each PVE corrected PET image, we obtained radioligand concentrations in each ROI and calculated a radioligand binding ratio (BR) by taking into account  the non-specific binding of [18F]FEOBV in a reference region, i.e. the WM-PCC3. In the first part of our study, to assess the consistency of our measures, we compared 6 ratios of averaged BR values (CA1/CA3, CA1/DG, CA1/PFC, CA3/DG, CA3/PFC, DG/PFC) with those obtained in a post-mortem [18F]FEOBV autoradiography study4. For this, a linear regression analysis was performed between the two datasets. In the second part of our study, the BR values were compared across hippocampal subfields using both the FS60 and HBT atlases, and plotted with age to identify potential patterns of cholinergic changes during healthy aging.

Results

A significant positive correlation was observed between our [18F]FEOBV data obtained in vivo and those from the post-mortem study (R=0,94, P = 0,017). Using the FS60 segmentation, BR measured in the subiculum, CA1 and CA3 were significantly lower than in the DG (P ≤ 0.05) and BR measured in the CA1 and CA3 were significantly lower than in the CA4 (P ≤ 0.05, see Figure, A). Statistical comparison of BRs measured across hippocampal subfields using the HBT segmentation did not reveal significant differences among the tail, body, and head, suggesting more uniform BR values along the hippocampal axis with this segmentation (see Figure, B). Regardless of the segmentation used, the BR values measured in each hippocampal subfield did not significantly correlate with age (see Figure, C).  

Conclusion

In this study, the significant correlation between our in vivo measures of VAChT density and those obtained post-mortem support the reproducibility and external validity of [18F]FEOBV PET imaging to measure cholinergic innervation of hippocampal subfields. We found a pattern of differential cholinergic innervation within hippocampus, resulting in significantly lower VAChT expression in the subiculum, CA1 and CA3 than in CA4 and DG. As output hippocampal structures of subiculum and CA1 are involved in memory retrieval, consolidation and output processing5, the lower BRs found in these subregions could reflect processes where ACh plays a less critical role. On the contrary, input structures such as DG being involved in memory encoding and new information processing5, higher BR in this subfield could reflect processes more heavily dependent on cholinergic modulation. The uniform distribution of BRs across anatomical segments further supports the idea that functional specialization rather than gross structure governs hippocampal cholinergic modulation. We did not find significant correlations of cholinergic binding with age however the high variability observed may hint a more complex, possibly nonlinear pattern of cholinergic changes across lifespan that deserves further exploration. All together, these results support [18F]FEOBV PET imaging as a valuable and usable tool for finely unravelling the role of hippocampal cholinergic signaling in future research on memory and aging.
