Introduction

Compared with massed learning, spaced learning posits that the lags between initial encoding and relearning foster robust memory formation, particularly when the lags include sleep 1, 2. Theoretically, the benefits of spaced learning are generally attributed to more effective encoding and greater retrieval effort during relearning 3. However, the specific contributions of external stimulus-driven encoding and internal memory-based retrieval to the formation of robust memory in spaced learning remain unclear. Based on the counterbalancing relationship between external and internal information processing in the human brain 4, the balance between encoding and retrieval may serve as a key mechanism underlying the spacing effect. Specifically, in massed relearning, learning may be dominated by externally driven, stimulus-based processes, whereas spaced learning likely benefits from both externally driven and internally memory-based processes during relearning. Correspondingly, during offline consolidation, spaced learning may facilitate effective and durable memory storage by balancing encoding-like replay of external detailed representations and retrieval-like replay of internal abstract representations 5. Nevertheless, an integrated theoretical framework and corresponding neural evidence are still lacking to unify the balance of encoding and retrieval during both online relearning and offline consolidation period. Here, we propose a theoretical hypothesis in which spaced learning promotes encoding-retrieval balance in relearning and post-learning rest, which may predict memory durability (Fig. 1a).

Methods

To test our hypothesis, we evaluated the encoding-like versus retrieval-like neural representations during relearning and post-learning rest, using the encoding-evoked and retrieval-evoked neural activity as templates. Quantitatively, the neural lateralization of encoding-retrieval processes was calculated (Fig. 1b). We hypothesized that whereas massed learning would produce lateralization toward encoding process, spaced learning would yield more balanced neural lateralization (Fig. 1c), which also has a quadratic effect on memory durability (Fig. 1d). 

Experimental design: In this study, sixty-four participants were randomly assigned to either the spaced (1-day encoding-relearning interval) or massed (8-minute encoding-relearning interval) learning group. Resting-state and task-based fMRI data were collected during encoding, relearning, and retrieval, followed by immediate and one-month delayed behavioral memory tests. All participants learned 60 object-scene pairs (4 scenes, 15 objects per scene) twice during the encoding phase and twice during the relearning phase (Fig. 1e). 

Memory performance assessment: To evaluate memory durability, at least two tests were required. In the present design, memory durability was defined as the percentage of memories that can be successfully retrieved in both immediate and delayed tests relative to those successfully retrieved in the immediate test. Additionally, accuracy in the immediate test was quantified as the total number of correct trials divided by the total number of immediate test trials.

Functional localizer: To identify the masks of interest, group-level analyses on brain activation were conducted using one-sample t-tests for different task phases (encoding, relearning, and retrieval) in each learning group. The whole mask involved the brain regions that were commonly activated across the encoding, relearning, and retrieval phases in both learning groups. Considering the functional dissociation between low-order sensory and high-order association cortices, the whole mask was divided into a visual cortex mask and an association cortex mask. Subsequent neural analyses were therefore based on these three masks, respectively.

Activity pattern lateralization analysis: To estimate the lateralization of the activity pattern associated with encoding-retrieval processes during relearning, the representational similarity analysis approach was applied. The encoding-like and retrieval-like neural pattern similarities during relearning were estimated across all correct trials in the immediate test. The activity pattern lateralization was then calculated by using the encoding-like and retrieval-like neural pattern similarities. 

Replay lateralization analysis: To explore the lateralization of neural configurations during post-learning rest, we quantified the emergence of encoding-like and retrieval-like replay for each participant within each mask. We created the multivoxel neural activity templates for estimating encoding-like and retrieval-like replay based on the encoding-evoked and retrieval-evoked activity according to correct trials at the immediate test. Correlation analyses were performed between the task-evoked templates and the frame-wise resting activity patterns. Neural replay was identified by resting-state activity patterns exhibiting high similarity (above the 90th percentile) to the templates. We estimated the changes in the number of replay from the baseline to the post-learning rest. The replay lateralization was calculated using the changes of encoding-like and retrieval-like replay. 

Statistical analysis

One-sample t-tests were conducted to determine whether the values significantly differed from zero, and independent t-tests were employed to compare between-group differences. Quadratic regression analyses were conducted to test for non-linear relationships.

Results

Spaced learning leads to better memory durability

We observed that memory durability after spaced learning was significantly higher than that after massed learning (t(61) = 2.17, p = 0.034, Cohen’s d = 0.55). However, the between-group difference was not significant for the accuracy in the immediate test (t(62) = 1.89, p = 0.063, Cohen’s d = 0.47) (Fig. 2a). 

Group-level brain activations

Brain regions that were commonly activated across the encoding, relearning, and retrieval phases in both learning groups involved bilateral visual, parahippocampal, precuneus, parietal, and frontal regions (Fig. 2b).

More balanced activity pattern lateralization of encoding-retrieval processes during relearning in spaced learning predicts memory durability

Massed learning groups showed significant lateralization to encoding during relearning in the whole mask (t(28) = −7.26, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −1.35), visual cortex (t(28) = −7.39, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −1.37) and association cortex (t(29) = −5.10, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.93). The lateralization to encoding during relearning in spaced learning group was significant in the whole mask (t(28) = −3.24, p = 0.003, Cohen’s d = −0.60), but not in the visual cortex (t(28) = −2.33, p = 0.055, Cohen’s d = −0.43) and association cortex (t(29) = −1.50, p = 0.29, Cohen’s d = −0.27). For the between-group differences, significantly more lateralization to encoding was observed during relearning for massed than spaced learning in the whole mask (t(56) = 3.26, p = 0.002, Cohen’s d = 0.86), visual cortex (t(56) = 3.78, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.99) and association cortex (t(58) = 2.12, p = 0.038, Cohen’s d = 0.55). Regression analysis revealed a significant quadratic effect of activity pattern lateralization on memory durability in the association cortex for the spaced learning group (adjusted R2 = 0.30, p = 0.001, t(28) = −3.66) but not for the massed learning group (adjusted R2 = −0.01, p = 0.407, t(27) = 0.84) (Fig. 2c). However, the quadratic effects were not significant in the whole mask and visual cortex for either group (adjusted R2 < 0.08, ps > 0.078). These findings suggest that spaced learning enables a better balance in encoding-retrieval processes during relearning in the association cortex, which also predicts memory durability in spaced learning. 

Spaced learning induces the lateralization to retrieval-like replay during post-learning rest

Spaced learning groups showed significant lateralization to retrieval-like replay during rest in the whole mask (t(29) = 7.42, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.35), visual cortex(t(28) = 6.35, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.18) and association cortex (t(28) = 6.41, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.19). The lateralization to retrieval-like replay during rest in massed learning group was significant in the whole mask (t(26) = 2.77, p = 0.021, Cohen’s d = 0.53) and association cortex (t(26) = 3.33, p = 0.005, Cohen’s d = 0.64), but not in the visual cortex (t(28) = −2.33, p = 0.055, Cohen’s d = −0.43). For the between-group differences, we observed significantly more lateralization to retrieval-like replay in spaced learning compared with massed learning (t(55) = 2.95, p = 0.005, Cohen’s d = 0.78), visual cortex (t(55) = 2.40, p = 0.020, Cohen’s d = 0.64) and association cortex (t(54) = 3.62, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.97). Regression analysis revealed a significant quadratic effect of the replay lateralization on memory durability in the association cortex, only in the spaced learning group (adjusted R2 = 0.19, p = 0.013, t(26) = -2.67) (Fig. 2d). The quadratic effects were not observed in the whole mask and visual cortex for both learning groups. These findings suggest that spaced learning induces more lateralization to retrieval-like replay during post-learning rest. Furthermore, more balanced replay lateralization in the association cortex predicts better memory durability in spaced learning. 

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate the importance of balanced encoding-retrieval processes during relearning and consolidation in promoting memory durability in spaced learning. This study provides a theoretical and mechanistic framework for understanding how durable memory are formed through spaced learning.
