Introduction

Classical conditioning of the eye blink (i.e., eye blink conditioning or EBC) has been used widely as a paradigm to study the fundamental mechanisms of associative learning (Welsh et al 2023). Nearly a century of experimental psychology research in humans and non-human mammals has revealed similar behavioral attributes in the EBC learning process which has led to the assumption that similar neural mechanisms are responsible across phylogeny. The latter has been investigated with invasive electrophysiology in many non-human species, revealing important involvements of the medial prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, brainstem reticular formation, and cerebellar cortex. PET and fMRI studies during EBC affirmed those activations in humans, consistent with conserved brain circuitry underlying this form of associative learning. In addition, the utility of trace EBC has been affirmed for clinical use as it has been used to distinguish differences in learning among people with Alzheimer’s disease and profound autism, thereby indicating that there are underlying changes in the neural circuit properties associated with neurological disorders affecting memory and intellect.

Here, we used the high temporal and spatial resolution of on-scalp OPM-MEG and its greater recording efficacy for small heads to assess the sequence of brain circuit activation associated with associative learning in children. As a check on recording quality and test-retest reliability, we compared pediatric OPM-MEG results to OPM-MEG and cryo-MEG results from a well-conditioned adult. The findings are the first comparisons of the brain circuit activations underlying associative learning across post-natal development by MEG. This work is a component of our larger programmatic goal to elucidate changes in the mechanisms of associative learning with intellectual disability.

Methods

Two sessions of trace EBC were conducted using a 1-kHz tone conditioned stimulus (CS) presented binaurally (200 ms, 80-dB re: 20-μPa SPL) and a corneal air puff unconditioned stimulus (US) to the right eye (100 ms, 5 psi source pressure). An EBC trial consisted of the CS followed by the US at a 700 ms CS-US interval (20 ± 5 s intertrial interval). A conditioned response (CR) was defined as an eye blink that occurred during the CS-US interval later than 80 ms after CS onset in order to exclude trials with an auditory-evoked startle response. Fig. 1A-C show properties of the EBC paradigm.

A 90 sensor (180 channels in two axes) HEDscan OPM-MEG (FieldLine Medical, Louisville, CO USA)(Alem, 2023) smart helmet was attached to a bed within a 3-layer (1 aluminum, 2 mu metal) MSR. Fig 1D shows the HEDscan smart helmet with sensors and the position of an infrared (IR) blink detector and air puff delivery tube. Binaural intra-aural earphone tubes delivered the CS.  

The participants were 3 typically-developing children (13 ± 2 years old) and one adult. 135 trace EBC trials were collected during OPM-MEG. Epochs post CS-onset were analyzed to localize cerebral cortical, thalamic, and cerebellar activations. An event-related beamformer (Jobst, 2018) was used to extract time-dependent activity from tone onset and eyeblink onset. The child participants did not have MRI’s. We used a child template MRI warped to the shape of their heads, which was collected using a Polhemus digitizer. The resulting network of nodes was examined in relation to CS and CR onsets. 

Results

The percentage CRs during OPM-MEG for the three children was 42 ± 7% and 89% for the adult. As measured by the IR blink detector, mean CR onset latency was 497 ± 32 ms for the children and 371 ms for the adult. The higher percentage CRs and earlier onset latency in the adult was consistent with asymptotic performance due to substantial overtraining. The adult was also unique in having been tested with trace EBC during cryo-MEG 7 years earlier, allowing test-retest comparison of brain activation patterns using both methods and with de novo associative learning in children.

Localized wide-band brain activity (3-50 Hz) in the adult demonstrated a stable sequence of activated brain regions across MEG methodologies and time. In the adult, before the electromagnetic fields due to activation of the orbicularis oculi muscle that generates the CR (~340 ms post CS), both MEG methods recorded bilateral activation of the primary auditory cortex at ~70 ms post CS onset, bilateral activation of the medial prefrontal gyrus at ~140 ms, followed by synchronous activation of the anterior thalamus, precuneus, and anterior lobe cerebellum (lobules I-V) at ~260 ms post CS onset.

In the children, a similar sequence was observed but at a slower rate: bilateral auditory cortex activation at ~100 ms post CS, bilateral medial prefrontal cortex activation at ~175 ms, and synchronous activation of the anterior thalamus, precuneus and anterior cerebellar cortex (lobules V-VII) at ~320 ms post CS, again all prior to the earliest activation of the orbicularis oculi muscle at ~545 ms.

Conclusion

The results demonstrated a stereotypic sequence of brain activation during trace EBC across ages and across prior experience with the learning paradigm that was recorded similarly by OPM- and cryo-MEG. From the perspective of brain physiology, the major difference between the children and adult was the latency to activation across all brain areas involved in this form of associative learning. The temporal differences between the children and the adult were on the order of tens of milliseconds and may relate to differences in brain development that affect conduction, differences in prior experience with the learning paradigm, or both. Technologically, the experiments demonstrate the utility of OPM-MEG for learning experiments in children.
