Introduction

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) and magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (MRSI) are powerful techniques that enable non-invasive detection of endogenous molecules in the human brain. These molecules, known as brain metabolites and neurotransmitters, are closely linked to essential physiological processes underlying normal brain function, including cellular metabolism and synaptic neurotransmission. Therefore, MRS(I) provides a unique window into neurochemical aspects of human brain function, complementing blood oxygen-level dependent (BOLD) and arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI, which primarily probe vascular activity.

However, practical utility of MRS(I) in brain functional imaging remains hindered by several technical challenges. To measure the transient molecular changes evoked by task paradigms, repeated single-voxel MRS acquisitions, known as functional MRS (fMRS), are often used. Although fMRS has revealed stimulus-related metabolic changes in visual, motor, and pain paradigms1, its applicability is constrained by its inherently limited spatial coverage. MRSI offers spatially resolved molecular information, but it often requires more than 20 min for 3D imaging even at low spatial resolution, which makes it impractical for brain functional studies.

Recently, our group developed a subspace-based ultrafast high-resolution MRSI technique and demonstrated its clinical feasibility in multiple diseases2. In this work, this technique was further developed and tailored for functional brain imaging. Using a variable-density spatial-temporal sparse sampling strategy, we achieved whole-brain MRSI with a temporal resolution of 1 min/frame. A subspace-based data processing method was developed to remove tissue lipid and water signals from sparsely sampled data and to reconstruct dynamic MRSI signals. This method has been validated in healthy subjects during visual stimulation, demonstrating the feasibility and potential of high-resolution functional MRSI (fMRSI).

Methods

The proposed data acquisition scheme, as illustrated in Figure 1A, consists of two complementary measurement modules: reference MRSI and dynamic MRSI. The reference MRSI scan acquired a fully sampled high-resolution MRSI dataset in 11.5 min, which provided high-quality sensitivity map, B0 inhomogeneity map, and water/lipid spectroscopic signals required for reconstruction of dynamic MRSI data. The dynamic MRSI scan acquired a series of sparsely sampled MRSI datasets to efficiently capture temporal variations in metabolite and neurotransmitter signals.

The reference MRSI data acquisition maintained the key features of subspace-based MRSI (known as SPICE, spectroscopic imaging by exploiting spatiospectral correlation)3. Specifically, non-water-suppressed FID MRSI was acquired with Ernst-angle excitation (27˚), short TR (160 ms) and ultrashort TE (1.6 ms), as shown in Figure 1B. The dynamic MRSI used the same excitation and timing parameters, but with variable-density sparse sampling of the k-space to achieve high temporal resolution, as shown in Figure 1C. The central k-space was fully sampled to allow adaptation of reference MRSI data to dynamic MRSI data (to account for field inhomogeneity variations and other experimental non-idealities). The peripheral k-space was undersampled by a factor of 8, with complementary sampling patterns across temporal frames. This design enabled effective reconstruction of dynamic MRSI signals through joint modeling of spatial, spectral, and temporal correlations using low-rank models and sensitivity-encoding constraints. Motion navigators were embedded in the data acquisition scheme (Figure 1B) to improve robustness to subject motion during the scan. This strategy achieved whole-brain dynamic MRSI at 2×3×3 mm3 spatial resolution and 1 min temporal resolution. Other imaging parameters were: FOV = 240×240×120 mm3, readout bandwidth = 1.67 kHz, and echospace = 1.76 ms.

In data processing, we first estimated and corrected B0 inhomogeneity, field drift and motion effects in both reference and dynamic datasets. Then the lipid and water signals were estimated from reference MRSI data using a union-of-subspaces model4, which were subsequently used as the reference for lipid and water removal from sparse dynamic MRSI data through a generalized series model5. Finally, metabolite and neurotransmitter signals were reconstructed for each dynamic frame, leveraging pre-learned subspaces3.

Experimental data were acquired from three healthy subjects from a 3T scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany), with informed consents. All participants underwent the same visual stimulation paradigm (Figure 1A), in which a radial red-black checkerboard flickering at 8 Hz was presented via a projector and mirror mounted on the coil. During resting periods, a dark background with a central gray fixation cross was displayed. The task/rest blocks were repeated 16 to 32 times for each subject. BOLD fMRI was acquired with the same flickering checkerboard stimuli with a 20 s ON/ 20 s OFF block design (FOV = 240×240×120 mm3, TR/TE = 2000/25 ms, resolution = 3×3×3 mm3, 120 volumes, scan time = 4:08 min). The protocol also included a T1-weighted image (FOV = 192×240×240 mm3, TR/TE/TI = 2200/2.29/1100 ms, resolution = 1×1×1 mm3, scan time = 5:07 min).

BOLD fMRI data were analyzed using FEAT (FSL). The first two volumes were discarded, and functional images were registered to T1-weighted image. A generalized linear model (GLM) was using a boxcar regressor corresponding to the visual task. Task-related activation contrasts were computed, and a threshold of t > 3 was used to identify activation regions for each subject. Molecular differences between task and rest frames within the activation region were assessed using Student’s t-tests, with values of P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

As can be seen in Figure 2A, BOLD fMRI t-map revealed task-related activation localized to the occipital cortex, providing individualized activation regions for fMRSI analysis. High-resolution whole-brain metabolic maps were successfully reconstructed using the proposed dynamic MRSI framework. Representative N-acetylaspartate (NAA) maps exhibited high spatial resolution and good tissue contrast (Figure 2B), demonstrating stable reconstruction from sparsely sampled data. Similarly, glutamate/glutamine complex (Glx) maps showed clear spatial structure and good signal quality throughout the brain (Figure 2C).

Within BOLD-activated regions, task-related metabolic changes were observed. Comparisons between rest and task frames revealed a significant increase in Glx concentration during visual stimulation (+2.85%, P = 0.0452; Figure 2D), suggesting elevated excitatory neurotransmission. Lactate (Lac) levels showed a more pronounced task-related increase (+12.07%, P = 0.0006), suggesting enhanced glycolytic metabolism associated with neuronal activation. These effects had some minor variations across individual subjects, as illustrated by subject-level comparisons (Figure 2E). Other molecules did not show statistically significant task-related changes, including NAA (P = 0.4831), creatine (P = 0.0774), choline (P = 0.5335) and myo-inositol (P = 0.2990). These observations are consistent with previous signal-voxel fMRS studies1.

Together, these results demonstrated that the proposed high-resolution fMRSI approach enabled whole-brain, time-resolved detection of task-evoked metabolic and neurotransmitter changes in the human brain.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of high-resolution fMRSI with whole-brain coverage, enabling dynamic mapping of metabolite and neurotransmitter changes with a spatial resolution of 2×3×3 mm³ and a temporal resolution of 1 min.

Compared with current fMRS approaches, the proposed fMRSI technique provides substantially extended spatial coverage and improved spatial resolution, offering several key advantages. First, instead of relying on a preselected voxel, the proposed method acquires molecular signals from the entire brain, allowing subject-specific analyses within task-activated regions. This strategy helps reduce variations in voxel localization across subjects, which is a well-known limitation of fMRS. Second, the increased spatial resolution and activation-guided analysis substantially mitigate partial volume effects, which have been a technical challenge for fMRS. By minimizing signal contributions from non-activated regions, this approach improves the spatial specificity and interpretability of task-evoked molecular changes. Third, conventional fMRS is highly sensitive to subject motion, as motion can shift the sampled voxel across different tissue locations. By incorporating embedded motion navigators and image-domain reference-based corrections, the proposed fMRSI approach can effectively compensate for motion-related artifacts, which is an important advantage for studies involving lengthy functional paradigms and/or patient populations. 

Despite these strengths, several aspects need future investigation. First, as a feasibility study, this work included only a limited number of participants. Validation in larger cohorts is needed to establish robustness, sensitivity, and reproducibility of the proposed technique. Second, future studies may leverage the whole-brain capability of this framework to examine correlations and interactions among task-evoked molecular responses across different brain regions, which may provide insights into large-scale metabolic coupling and network coordination underlying brain function. Third, extension to additional task paradigms, clinical populations, and multimodal integration with structural and functional MRI is a promising direction.

In conclusion, we have developed a high-resolution whole-brain fMRSI technique capable of capturing dynamic metabolic and neurotransmitter changes with minute-level temporal resolution. We expect this technique to open up new opportunities for investigating brain-scale metabolic networks in various functional, cognitive, and behavioral studies.
