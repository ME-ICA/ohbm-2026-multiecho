Introduction

The brain’s most remarkable abilities, including imagination, problem solving, memory, learning and consolidation, and planning emerge when neural activity is decoupled from external input. However, the intrinsic mechanisms by which spontaneous brain activity supports these functions remain debated.1 A recently proposed framework suggests that spontaneous activity implements statistical inference, such that endogenous dynamics reflect the brain’s generative model of the environment and the body, expressed as spatiotemporal priors that guide behavior.2 A candidate mechanism supporting this process is neural replay: a brain wide sequential reactivation of events embedded in fast oscillatory activity. Although in humans, replay has been linked to planning, decision-making and memory recall,3 its role in online statistical learning remains unclear. 

Here, we tested whether neural replay supports ongoing sequential statistical learning in humans. Our first hypothesis predicts that stronger replay should be associated with superior learning performance, consistent with a role in statistical inference. To test this, we characterised behavioural learning profiles, comparing individuals with high versus low learning performance.

Our second hypothesis is focused on competing accounts that propose replay as either guiding ongoing behaviour or supporting offline construction of abstract environmental representations independent of immediate goals.4 To address this, we included resting-state periods both during and after learning. We predicted that replay should emerge during task performance if it supports online learning, or during post-task rest if it reflects offline consolidation.

Overall, this study aims to clarify whether spontaneous brain activity supports statistical inference in humans, how neural replay relates to learning performance, and whether it reflects online behaviour, or offline consolidation.

Methods

We investigated how neural replay supports online learning and post-learning consolidation using high-density electrophysiology and a sequential learning task (N=40, mean-aged 24.55 years with standard deviation of 3.18 (male=20, female=20). Participants viewed images from five categories (face, scene, body, tool, scrambled). For each image (excluding the scrambled ones, which required no response), they were instructed to press a specific key corresponding to its category. The task was divided into eight blocks with 80 trials each, each varying in the probability that images would appear in a specific sequential order (e.g., face → scene → body, etc.) versus being presented in a random order. The sequence probabilities across blocks were: 100%, 90%, 80%, 70%, 30%, 20%, 10%, and 0%. These blocks were counterbalanced across participants to ensure that each experienced the conditions in a different order. Resting-state data were recorded with eye open before the task (5 minutes), after each task block (1 minute each), and post-task (5 minutes; Fig.1A). For behavioral data, a generalized linear model predicted reaction times based on sequence probability, with slopes indicating individual learning. To measure learning we characterised each participant with their learning slope. We grouped participants into high and low learners using a median split of learning slopes.

To investigate neural replay, we implemented temporally delayed linear modelling. 5 First, lasso-regularized logistic regression models were trained for each image category, with trials from other categories grouped into an "other" label. Input features were activations from 256 channels sampled at 100 Hz. Models were trained and tested on time points from -0.5s to 0.7s relative to image onset using 5-fold cross-validation and L1 regularization to prevent overfitting. Beta estimates and classifier prediction performance (~200ms post-image) were used to decode task representations during rest (Fig. 1B-C). Temporally delayed linear modelling identified sequential reactivation of task-related representations. A decoded state-space matrix, with rows as reactivated states and columns as resting time points, was used to compute pairwise transitions. Multilinear regression produced empirical transition matrices for lags (Δt) from 10ms to 500ms. A second-level general linear model predicted these matrices using forward and reverse transition models. Replay significance was assessed via non-parametric permutation tests. Null distributions were formed by shuffling state identities and recalculating analyses. Replay strength was deemed significant (peak-level FWE < 0.05) if it exceeded 95% of permutation peaks. Significant replay segments were epoched around onset, decomposed into time-frequency representations (20–150 Hz), and analyzed using cluster-based permutation tests for power modulations (Fig. 1D). The ending result of the analysis is a timeseries of betas that show how strong is the replay at each tested lag plotted over the permutation threshold. 

Results

We first investigated sequence learning. Although participants were not informed about the existence of a sequence, we expected that incidental learning would allow them to anticipate upcoming stimuli, thus respond faster with learning. Accuracy remained high across all sequence levels (F7,273 =0.37, ps=0.91), while participants got faster as a function of sequence probability (F7,273 =22.383, ps<0.001). Aiming at summarizing individual learning gain, we fitted a linear model with block treated as a monotonic continuous variable and we summarized performance with a slope for each participant describing a linear effect of sequence progression. We performed a median split based on individual slope values, therefore obtaining two groups. We labeled these groups high learners (N=20,age =24.30 sd=2.03, 9 female and 11 male) and low learners (N=20, age=24.60, sd=3.94, 11 female and 9 male). High-gain learners were characterized by greater performance improvements, showing faster reaction times during sequence presentation. In contrast, low-gain learners showed minimal evidence of performance gains, with little to no improvement in reaction times across the sequence (Fig. 2A).

We next analysed replay within each group. No evidence of replay was observed during post-task rest. In contrast, significant replay emerged during interspersed resting states within the task, with distinct temporal profiles across learning groups. High learners exhibited forward replay compressed to a 40 ms lag during the first interspersed rest period, which gradually diminished as the task progressed. Conversely, low learners showed backward replay with a slower lag (50 ms), emerging later during the third interspersed rest period (Fig. 2B).

Finally, replay effects were primarily visual in nature, localised to occipital EEG sensors. Forward replay in high learners was associated with high-frequency activity (>90 Hz), spanning the ripple band and extending into high gamma (>30 Hz). In contrast, backward replay in low learners was characterised by slower frequency activity (20–60 Hz), while remaining similarly localised to occipital sensors (Fig. 2C).

Conclusion

We investigated how intrinsic mechanisms of spontaneous brain activity contribute to statistical learning, with a particular focus on neural replay and its relationship to learning performance, online behaviour, and offline consolidation. Using high-density electrophysiological recordings acquired during a sequential category-learning task, we tested two primary hypotheses. First, we asked whether stronger replay would be associated with superior learning performance. Second, we examined whether replay would preferentially occur during task execution, consistent with a role in online behavioural control, or during post-task rest, indicative of offline consolidation.

Our results provided no evidence for replay during post-learning rest. Instead, replay emerged during the task itself, specifically within brief resting-state pauses. Although the contribution of replay to immediate behaviour has been debated, this finding suggests that replay may primarily support online behavioural performance and the pursuit of ongoing goals, rather than the offline construction of a generalised cognitive map.4 

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, individual differences in learning were not explained by the overall amount of replay, but rather by its temporal characteristics, including directionality and timing. High-performing learners exhibited an early-emerging forward replay with a compressed temporal lag of approximately 40 ms, whereas low-performing learners showed a later-onset backward replay with a lag of approximately 50 ms during task performance. Such rapid temporal compression is consistent with previous rodent and human studies demonstrating that replayed representations are temporally condensed.3 Moreover, replay direction is known to vary as a function of task demands; for example, during recall, replay direction can depend on the preceding cue. Forward and backward replay have also been dissociated in planning and decision-making contexts, with forward replay more strongly linked to prospective planning and backward replay associated with memory retrieval and recency effects3. Extending this literature, our findings indicate that replay direction is additionally modulated by learning success, with forward replay predominating in high-performing learners and backward replay characterising poorer performance.

Finally, the two replay signatures differed in their spectral profiles. Forward replay was embedded within high-gamma and ripple-like activity, consistent with findings from rodent models and prior human studies. In contrast, backward replay was associated with slower dynamics, modulating lower-frequency bands. 

Taken together, these findings suggest that, in humans, neural replay primarily supports online behavioural optimisation rather than offline consolidation of learned contingencies. Specifically, forward replay appears to facilitate improvements in sequential task performance by shaping ongoing behaviour during learning, rather than by reinforcing representations after task completion.
