Introduction

Neural mass models (NMMs) are mathematical descriptions of neural dynamics extensively used to test theories of neural mechanisms and brain function, predict pathological implications and inform treatment, and in other scenarios where a direct means of obtaining insights might be infeasible1. Fundamentally, NMMs predict the future state of a system keeping with known or theorised mechanisms, upon specification of some initial conditions. The predictive capabilities of NMMs, make it well suited for application in the domain of neurostimulation, which strives to drive the brain to clinically favourable states via exogenous perturbations. Although widely regarded a formidable solution to several conditions that are otherwise treatment-resistant using conventional means, development or refinement of stimulation paradigms can often be slow due to the potential risks involved in testing drastically new paradigms on humans. In this setting, NMMs offer an interesting opportunity as a virtual testing ground where stimulation paradigms can be simulated and the response across the brain can be ascertained in detail, and with a degree of certainty. A researcher would also have limitless capability to systematically analyse the effects of varying stimulation parameters, simulate effects of drugs, preexisting conditions etc.

In this study, we take a step towards exploring the potential of NMMs in neurostimulation, by studying the stimulus propagation characteristics of popular established NMMs. We test three classes of NMMs with increasing complexity and biophysical realism, and study how regions embedded in complex whole-brain tractography-derived networks respond to a stimulus, as model parameters are systematically varied. Importantly, we compare stimulus-response patterns against an empirical reference provided by the Functional brain Tractography (F-TRACT) project2, and discuss features essential to accurately model real-world stimulus propagation using NMMs.

Methods

Neural Mass Models
We simulate a system of networked in silico neural masses embedded in a 74-node tractography-derived undirected binary network. The neural masses were placed at each node and configured to exhibit either of the following dynamics (in increasing complexity and biophysical realism): 1) Ornstein-Ühlenbeck (OU)3; 2) Stuart-Landau (SL)4; and 3) Jansen-Rit (JR)5 (Fig.1A). 

In a simulation, all masses were configured with identical parameters. Each simulation run proceeds as follows:

  1. A mass is randomly initialised (random values assigned to the relevant state variables) and the system is allowed to stabilise over 30 seconds.
  2. At the end of 30s, a region is chosen as a “source”, and its activity (the state variable) is set to a fixed high amplitude and maintained at that value for 5ms. This simulates an exogenous perturbation that offsets the source’s dynamics. After 5ms, the source’s activity is allowed to evolve as per the chosen dynamics.
  3. The system is allowed to evolve undisturbed for a duration of 1s.
  4. The activity time courses (which capture the systemic response to the source’s perturbation) are processed for cortico-cortical evoked potentials (CCEP) – see below.
  5. Steps 2-4 are repeated for a different source. This is repeated until all nodes in the network have been assigned as a source once.

 For each set of parameters for a chosen dynamical model, we run 30 simulation trials. All models are numerically integrated using the Modified Euler method, with a timestep of 10e-4 seconds.

CCEP processing
We follow a processing pipeline derived from the original F-TRACT dataset2, as summarised below (Fig.1B):

  1. The activities of the neural masses are divided about the stimulus onset time (set to 0ms) into a pre-stimulus baseline (-200ms to -10ms) and post-stimulus period (0ms-1000ms).
  2. The post-stimulus activities are z-scored with respect to the baseline’s mean and standard deviation.
  3. If the post-stimulus activity of a region (other than the source) crosses a z-threshold of 5, it is considered a response to the stimulus.
  4. The number of responses of a region to the perturbation of a source is divided by the number of simulation runs, to yield the response probability. Since we stimulate all regions (individually) in each simulation run, we obtain a 74 x 74 probability matrix after CCEP processing. 

Empirical reference
The F-TRACT dataset is a compilation of response characteristics of brain regions to single-pulse direct electrical stimulation, with measurements of source-target pairs spanning almost the entire cortical surface, and available in multiple parcellations. This makes it an excellent empirical reference to benchmark the response of NMMs that undergo stimulation. For this study, we investigate how well NMM response probabilities correlate to those in F-TRACT (Fig.1C-left). To ensure that model probabilities are compared to reliable empirical estimates, we only consider F-TRACT source-target pairs whose probability estimates represent an average of over 200 stimulation trials. The empirical alignment is quantified as the Spearman correlation between the F-TRACT and NMM probabilities.

For model configurations that maximised correlation to F-TRACT, we estimated the distribution of the response probabilities as a function of how separated the responding region was from the source (defined as the number of hops/connections along the shortest path between them) (Fig.1C-right). This distribution was compared to the empirical response probability distribution from F-TRACT to check whether signal propagation in the NMM was not only restricted to regions directly connected to the source (1-hop) but also elicited responses in indirectly connected regions (2- / n-hop). In other words, we wanted to check whether the extent of a stimulus’s influence in the NMM, in terms of the likelihood of responders at various separations, matched that of empirical stimuli.

Results

Ornstein-Ühlenbeck
OU-NMMs were configured by systematically varying the restoring force, θ, and the global coupling strength, K (all interactions are scaled by a factor of K).
A reduction in the restoring force, which effectively reduces the attenuation of the signal, resulted in an increase in correlation to F-TRACT response probabilities for low values of the global coupling. For stronger global coupling, we observe an intermediate optimal value of the restoring force at which correlation to F-TRACT is maximal. In contrast to low-coupling behaviour, for coupling strengths beyond ≈0.14, maximal correlation is observed for the high levels of the restoring force, and the correlation reduces as restoring force is reduced. The highest correlation to F-TRACT for the entire tested parameter space was 0.286, recorded for a restoring force of 41.67 and global coupling of 0.22 (Fig.2A-left).

The distribution of the response probability across separation from the source (Fig.2A-right) revealed that in the best performing OU-NMM, regions that were directly connected to a source had a higher probability to respond relative to F-TRACT. In contrast, regions that were 2- or 3-hops away from a source were much less likely to respond, with probabilities that were lower than observed in F-TRACT.

Stuart-Landau
SL-NMMs were configured by systematically varying the bifurcation parameter, λ, and the global coupling strength between nodes.

Across coupling strengths, the correlation to empirical probabilities decreased as λ was increased from -15 to around 4, after which we see an increase as λ is further increased to 20. The maximum correlation across both parameters was 0.312, for λ=-6 and K=1 (Fig.2B-left). 

Interestingly, comparing the simulated and empirical probability-separation distributions (Fig.2B-right), we see that while directly connected regions (1-hop) are extremely likely to respond (considerably higher than F-TRACT), there is a notably greater likelihood for 2-hop and 3-hop regions to also respond, compared to F-TRACT. This is in contrast to OU-NMM, where the 2- and 3-hop regions had a lower probability to respond compared to F-TRACT.

Jansen-Rit
The JR-NMMs were configured by varying the average background noise firing rate, p, and the global coupling strength, K.

We observed a clear increase in correlation as p was increased, for a given K (Fig.2C-left). Interestingly, the correlation seemed to plateau once the average background firing rate crossed 47Hz, irrespective of K. Within this plateau regime (p>47), however, the correlation strength was higher for NMMs with stronger global coupling. The highest correlation we recorded was 0.2878, for a JR-NMM with p=67 and K=40. 

The probability-separation distribution for the maximally correlated JR-NMM (Fig.2C-right) revealed that 1-hop regions were more likely to respond than F-TRACT, with probabilities close to 1. Much like the OU-NMMs, the 2- and 3-hop separated regions were considerably less likely to respond, relative to F-TRACT.

Conclusion

  • Multi-hop or polysynaptic propagation is an important feature of whole-brain communication. This is evidenced by non-zero empirical (F-TRACT) probabilities for regions separated from a source by 2- and 3-hops to respond to a stimulus.  
  • All tested NMMs have parameter configurations that allow multi-hop stimulus propagation. However, the probability-separation distributions for the best-performing models reveal that the NMM response probabilities fail to match empirical distributions. Specifically, directly connected regions are strikingly more likely to respond in all tested models, whereas directly unconnected regions (2- and 3-hop) are less likely respond in the OU- and JR-NMMs. The SL-NMM however, shows a greater recruitment of both connected and unconnected regions when compared to F-TRACT. The consistently inflated probability across separations suggests that making refinements to increase accuracy in terms of neural communication and stimulus propagation might be easier in SL-NMMs, since, in principle, all probabilities need to be scaled down (as opposed to selectively scaling down connected and scaling up unconnected probabilities in OU- and JR-NMMs).
  • An increase in biophysical realism or complexity, does not necessarily imply accuracy in terms of stimulus propagation characteristics. Although the JR-NMM was the most advanced of the tested models, capable of exhibiting complex dynamics, the more phenomenological SL-NMM had a higher correlation to empirical data.
  • The highest correlation to empirical response probabilities, across the tested models, was for the SL-NMMs, although the magnitude of the correlation was low, at 0.3. This indicates that despite being able to simulate empirically relevant multi-hop propagation, the original formulation of the tested NMMs fail to adequately capture features of empirical propagation of focal perturbations, explaining less than 10 percent of the variance in empirical response probabilities.
  • Overall, our results suggest that while the tested NMMs might be suitable for further investigations into potential in silico neurostimulation testing platforms, approaches to improve their stimulus propagation characteristics is essential. Empirical benchmarks like the F-TRACT dataset are valuable resources that will aid in this endeavour.
