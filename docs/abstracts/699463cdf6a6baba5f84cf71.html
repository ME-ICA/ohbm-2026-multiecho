Introduction

The hippocampus is a unique cortical structure and central hub for memory and learning, characterized by distinct macro- and microstructural properties. While previous imaging-genetics studies have identified genetic variants influencing global hippocampal volume, the broader genetic architecture of human hippocampal organization across different imaging modalities remains insufficiently characterized. Leveraging genome-wide association analyses of multimodal hippocampal imaging phenotypes, we charted the genetic architecture shaping hippocampal organization across different scales, and their shared-versus-distinct biological pathways.

Methods

Participant. We analyzed high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and diffusion-weighted imaging) and genome-wide genotyping data of neurotypical adolescents from the Adolescent Brain Cognition Development (ABCD) study (baseline sample; n = 4,349; 2,042 females; mean ± standard deviation age = 9.95 ± 0.61 years).1,2

Data processing. Multimodal imaging phenotypes, including volume, T1-weighted intensity, T2-weighted intensity, fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity, longitudinal diffusivity, transversal diffusivity, were provided by ABCD, and site harmonized using ComBat.3 Genotyping was quality-controlled following previously established recommendations which includes filtering based on (i) missingness of SNPs and individuals, (ii) sex discrepancy, (iii) minor allele frequency, (iv) Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, (v) heterozygosity, (vi) relatedness, and (vii) population stratification.4

Statistical analysis. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) were conducted to test linear associations between genetic variants and global hippocampal imaging phenotypes, while controlling for age, sex, and genetic ancestry (top 10 genetic principal components).4 We further estimated single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) based heritability and pairwise genetic correlations across all imaging phenotypes.5 Further correlations were computed between GWAS of hippocampal imaging phenotypes and different neuropsychiatric and neurological conditions (Alzheimer’s disease, epilepsy, hippocampal sclerosis, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and schizophrenia).

Results

We identified 66 independent genome-wide (P < 1 ´10-5) loci across all phenotypes (Figure 1A; volume: 6; T1-weighted intensity: 17; T2-weighted intensity: 8; fractional anisotropy: 10; mean diffusivity: 4; longitudinal diffusivity: 7; transverse diffusivity: 14). Patterns of genetic and phenotypic correlations across imaging phenotypes showed strong correspondence (Mantel’s test: r = 0.93, p < 0.01; Figure 1B). Notably, these correlations revealed two coherent genetic-phenotypic clusters, a microstructural cluster (diffusion MRI-derived metrics) and a meso- and macrostructural cluster (T1-weighted and T2-weighted intensity and volume), suggesting that partially distinct sets of genetic variants preferentially influence micro- versus meso-/macrostructural aspects of hippocampal organization.

Further disease contextualization analysis revealed distinct imaging-genetic correlation profiles across neurological and psychiatric conditions (Figure 1B), pointing to disease-specific mechanisms linked to hippocampal alterations.

Conclusion

Capitalizing on recent imaging-genetic initiatives, we provide new insights into the genetic organization of the human hippocampus across multiple spatial scales, identifying genetic variants as well as shared and distinct pathways linked to heterogeneous macro- and microstructural properties. This highlights the multifaceted biological architecture of the hippocampus and its relevance in brain disorders. Ongoing analyses will extend this framework to vertex-wise mapping to resolve fine-grained genetic influences of hippocampal structure.
