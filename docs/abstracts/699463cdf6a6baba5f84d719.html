Introduction

Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) is a heterogeneous psychiatric disorder characterized by substantial interindividual variability in neurobiological, physiological, and behavioral aspects. Despite extensive neuroimaging and behavioral research, reliable biomarkers reflecting individual differences that generalize across individuals remain elusive, limiting early identification and personalized intervention of latent AUD patients. One major challenge is that most prior classification studies have relied on a single modality (e.g. resting-state fMRI) thereby considering only a limited representation of the AUD.

Recent precision medicine approaches emphasize capturing multidimensional phenotypes at the individual level. Accordingly, this study utilizes multimodal baseline data integrating resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), resting-state electroencephalography (EEG), resting-state heart rate variability (HRV) and survey/demographic self-reports for classifying AUD high risk groups in the pre-clinical stage of evident clinical symptoms. 

By systematically comparing single-modality and multimodal models across multiple ML algorithms, our study provides a rigorous evaluation of how multimodal neurophysiological information can be optimally integrated for AUD classification. Furthermore, we applied topological data analysis (TDA) using the Mapper algorithm to both resting-state fMRI and EEG data as a complementary, representation-level approach to probe whether whole-brain topological organization captures trait-like individual differences associated with AUD risk. Collectively, our findings underscore not only the necessity of multimodal integration in AUD phenotyping but also highlight the representational limits of global topological features derived from resting-state data, informing future applications of TDA in precision psychiatry.

Methods

A total of 70 high-risk alcohol users without explicit clinical diagnosis were recruited. All participants completed up to three study visits including demographic assessment, self-report questionnaires, resting-state fMRI, resting-state EEG, and HRV measurements. After quality control, data from 59 participants with complete and analyzable datasets were included in the final analyses. Alcohol use risk was defined based on the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) score obtained at the first visit, with participants classified into an AUD high-risk group (AUDIT ≥ 20) or an AUD mid-to-low risk group (AUDIT < 20), which served as the binary prediction target for ML classification.

Resting-state EEG data were recorded using a 64-channel EEG cap, with 19 electrodes from the international 10–20 system analyzed in the eyes-closed condition. Sensor-level EEG features comprised absolute power and coherence computed separately across canonical frequency bands (delta: 1–4 Hz, theta: 4–8 Hz, alpha: 8–12 Hz, beta: 12–25 Hz, and gamma: 30–45 Hz). In parallel, standardized weighted low-resolution electromagnetic tomography (swLORETA) implemented in NeuroNavigator was used to estimate source-level functional connectivity across the same frequency bands, yielding 128 reconstructed sources from which source-to-source coherence-based connectivity features were derived.

Resting-state fMRI data were acquired from 63 participants with a repetition time (TR) of 2 s, yielding 200 volumes over a total scan duration of 400s per session. Preprocessing was performed using the CONN toolbox and included motion correction, spatial normalization, and nuisance regression following standard CONN toolbox pipelines. Following preprocessing, resting-state functional connectivity was estimated by computing Pearson correlation coefficients between regional time series defined by the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas comprising 116 cortical and subcortical regions. For each participant, the resulting 116 × 116 connectivity matrix was vectorized by extracting the lower triangular elements, yielding 6,670 unique connectivity features for subsequent analyses.

To extend beyond conventional connectivity measures, TDA using the Mapper algorithm was applied separately to resting-state fMRI and resting-state EEG data, following previously established frameworks (e.g., Saggar et al., 2018). This procedure generated participant-specific shape graphs representing the organization of individual brain state dynamics during resting-state sessions. From each Mapper graph, 20 graph-topological metrics were extracted per modality (e.g., betweenness centrality), reflecting topological properties of the underlying brain dynamics.

Following feature generation, a two-stage feature selection procedure was performed independently for resting-state EEG and resting-state fMRI features. First, features were prefiltered based on their absolute Pearson correlation with continuous AUDIT scores, retaining the top 300 features per modality. In the second stage, a Random Forest–based stability selection procedure was applied to the reduced feature set using 100 repeated subsampling iterations and class-balanced ensemble learning, yielding a final subset of 30 stable features per modality. All feature selection procedures were nested within the cross-validation (CV) loop and applied exclusively to training data.

Binary classification models distinguishing AUD high-risk from mid-to-low risk participants were trained using four supervised ML algorithms: ElasticNet-regularized logistic regression (LR), linear support vector machine with L2 regularization (Linear-SVM), radial basis function support vector machine (RBF-SVM), and gradient boosting machine (GBM). Model performance was evaluated using 5-fold CV repeated 100 times, with classification accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) computed.

Results

In single-modality analyses, ML models trained exclusively on HRV features (n = 17) demonstrated modest but above-chance performance, with test accuracy ranging from 0.61 ± 0.12 (GBM) to 0.69 ± 0.16 (RBF-SVM) and corresponding test AUC values between 0.61 ± 0.15 and 0.66 ± 0.16. Models trained solely on survey-based features (n = 12) achieved higher performance, yielding test accuracies of 0.71 ± 0.12 (GBM), 0.77 ± 0.11 (Linear-SVM), 0.75 ± 0.12 (LR), and 0.77 ± 0.11 (RBF-SVM), with test AUC values of 0.79 ± 0.12, 0.86 ± 0.10, 0.84 ± 0.11, and 0.85 ± 0.11, respectively, indicating that self-reported behavioral measures capture clinically relevant variance associated with alcohol use risk. When alcohol-related survey items were excluded (n = 8), classification performance consistently declined across classifiers, with test accuracies dropping to 0.59~0.67 and AUC values to 0.62~0.72, demonstrating that alcohol-specific self-report measures substantially contribute to predictive performance.

Using neurophysiological features, models trained on selected EEG connectivity features (n = 30) showed further improvements in generalization, with test accuracies of 0.74 ± 0.12 (GBM), 0.81 ± 0.11 (Linear-SVM), 0.83 ± 0.11 (LR), and 0.77 ± 0.11 (RBF-SVM), and corresponding test AUC values up to 0.91 ± 0.09. Models trained on selected fMRI connectivity features (n = 30) exhibited more variable test performance, with accuracies ranging from 0.76 ± 0.13 (GBM) to 0.86 ± 0.09 (Linear-SVM) and AUC values between 0.78 ± 0.14 and 0.95 ± 0.06, suggesting increased model complexity and potential overfitting in unimodal fMRI-based models.

Combining non–alcohol-related survey features with HRV measures (n = 25) resulted in moderate performance improvements relative to HRV-only models, with test accuracies ranging from 0.64 ± 0.12 to 0.68 ± 0.12 and AUC values from 0.69 ± 0.15 to 0.76 ± 0.14, though performance remained lower than that of neuroimaging-based models. In contrast, bimodal neuroimaging models integrating EEG and fMRI connectivity features (n = 60) yielded consistently superior performance for linear classifiers, achieving test accuracies of 0.87 ± 0.09 for both Linear-SVM and LR and test AUC values of 0.96 ± 0.05 and 0.95 ± 0.06, respectively, highlighting the complementary nature of electrophysiological and hemodynamic information.

When Topological Data Analysis (TDA) using the Mapper algorithm was applied to resting-state fMRI and EEG data, classification models trained on Mapper-derived topological metric features (n = 20 per modality) exhibited near chance-level accuracy for both modalities, indicating limited utility of global graph shape descriptors alone for capturing trait-level baseline differences in AUD risk. 

Multimodal models integrating EEG, fMRI, survey, and HRV features (n = 85) achieved high training performance but reduced generalization, whereas applying stability selection to retain the top 60 features resulted in more robust performance, yielding test accuracies of 0.81 ± 0.10 (Linear-SVM) and 0.83 ± 0.11 (LR), along with test AUC values of 0.88 ± 0.11 and 0.87 ± 0.11, respectively. Importantly, the retained features were overwhelmingly neural in origin and converged on well-established networks implicated in addiction vulnerability, including medial prefrontal and orbitofrontal cortices, default mode network regions, fronto-striatal circuits, and cerebello–cortical pathways, alongside EEG connectivity alterations predominantly expressed in gamma frequency bands.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that multimodal integration of resting-state neuroimaging data provides a robust and generalizable framework for high-risk alcohol users without clinical AUD diagnosis. Instead of optimizing a single predictive model, this study systematically examined how behavioral, autonomic, and neurophysiological measures contribute to robust and generalizable phenotyping of AUD risk under controlled feature dimensionality and model complexity. This design directly addresses a major shortcoming of prior AUD ML research that has focused predominantly on single modalities and post-diagnostic samples with limited translational applicability.

Behavioral self-report and autonomic measures demonstrated modest and context-dependent predictive performance. Survey-based models performed well primarily when alcohol-specific items were included, while performance declined substantially when such items were excluded, indicating that self-report measures largely reflect explicit symptom endorsement rather than latent vulnerability. HRV features similarly showed limited generalization. These findings underscore the insufficiency of non-neural modalities alone for detecting preclinical AUD risk.

In contrast, neuroimaging-based features, particularly functional connectivity measures, yielded substantially higher and more stable performance. Both EEG and fMRI connectivity features outperformed behavioral and autonomic models, and their integration produced the most consistent generalization, especially for linear classifiers. This bimodal advantage suggests that electrophysiological and hemodynamic signals encode complementary aspects of AUD vulnerability across distinct neurophysiological timescales. Notably, the application of stability selection to the multimodal features resulted in the preferential retention of neural connectivity features, which were consistent with established neurobiological models of addiction vulnerability, including medial prefrontal and orbitofrontal cortices, default mode network regions, and fronto-striatal circuits identified in fMRI. The selected EEG source-level connectivity features predominantly involve fronto–insular–striatal–cerebellar circuits that are centrally implicated in reward valuation, interoceptive processing, and cognitive control, all of which are core neurobiological substrates of addiction vulnerability. Notably,the predominance of EEG gamma-band connectivity further suggests frequency-specific alterations in cortical excitation–inhibition balance and limbic–prefrontal coupling in individuals at elevated risk. Because these features were identified from resting-state data in individuals without clinical diagnosis, they may indicate trait-like neurophysiological predispositions that confer elevated risk for alcohol use disorder. 

Taken together, these findings support a network-level model of AUD vulnerability characterized by impaired regulation of reward and habit systems, disrupted integration of salience signals, and altered cortical–subcortical coordination. Importantly, the superior performance of linear classifiers indicates that these neurobiological differences may be expressed along relatively low-dimensional and interpretable axes. In contrast, Mapper-based topological features contributed little, suggesting that short resting-state acquisitions may be insufficient for robust trait-level characterization.

Overall, this study highlights the value of multimodal neuroimaging integration combined with rigorous feature selection for developing interpretable and generalizable biomarkers of alcohol use risk, offering a scalable framework for precision psychiatry and early intervention before overt AUD pathology.
