Introduction

Multilingualism has become increasingly prevalent worldwide due to globalization and digital connectivity. Despite this growth, research on how multilingual speakers process and manage multiple languages in the brain remains limited. Language use is a fundamental cognitive function that relies on distributed neural networks, with language processing traditionally considered a predominantly left-lateralized function ^1. While considerable progress has been made in identifying language networks in monolinguals, defining these mechanisms—particularly in multilingual speakers—continues to pose a challenge.

Neuroimaging techniques have provided valuable insights into language processing, yet studies employing functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to investigate bilingual and multilingual language processing remain scarce ^2. Existing research has largely focused on monolinguals or bilinguals, leaving multilingual speakers underrepresented.

This involvement of bilateral and peripheral brain regions becomes especially relevant for multilingual speakers, who must continuously regulate interference between languages through cognitive control and inhibition mechanisms. Understanding how these mechanisms operate during language processing is essential for developing a more comprehensive model of multilingual cognition. 

Given the non-invasive nature of fNIRS and its ability to capture cortical hemodynamic responses during overt speech tasks, it offers a practical approach for investigating multilingual language processing. The present study addresses the existing research gap by employing fNIRS and a picture-naming paradigm to examine prefrontal cortical activation patterns in neurotypical multilingual adults. By comparing neural responses across native, first, and second languages, this study aims to elucidate the role of hemispheric lateralization and cognitive load in multilingual language processing.

Methods

This study investigated language processing in multilingual speakers using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) during a picture-naming task. The experimental design aimed to examine cortical activation patterns associated with three languages—native language (NL), first language (Urdu), and second language (English)—while enforcing language-specific inhibition.

A total of 26 right-handed, neurotypical adult participants (12 males, 14 females; mean age ≈ 20 years) were recruited from a public university in Pakistan. Participants represented all four major provinces of the country and were native speakers of Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashto, or Balochi. All participants shared the same language acquisition sequence: native language (L1) acquired from birth, Urdu as first language acquired around age three, and English as a second language acquired formally from early schooling. Participants were screened to exclude any disorders. Right-handedness was confirmed using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory. Ethical approval was obtained, and informed consent was taken.

Language background and proficiency were assessed using the Language Experience and Proficiency Questionnaire (LEAP-Q), ensuring consistent multilingual profiles across participants ^3. Participants were instructed to avoid caffeine intake prior to the experiment and completed a health screening protocol to ensure neurotypical status.

Brain activity was recorded using the NIRSport2 fNIRS system (NIRX Medical Technologies®) with a sampling rate of 10.2 Hz. The optode montage consisted of 8 sources and 7 detectors arranged over the prefrontal cortex according to the international 10–20 system, with a 3 cm source–detector distance. Data acquisition was managed using Aurora Software®, while PsychoPy® was used to design and present the experimental paradigm and synchronize event markers.

A block-design picture-naming task was employed to isolate language-specific processing. Participants named pictures aloud in one language per block, following a visual prompt indicating which language to use. Each activity block lasted 30 seconds and consisted of six images, each displayed for 3 seconds with a 2-second interstimulus interval. Activity blocks were followed by 20-second rest periods, with a 60-second rest at the beginning and end of the experiment.

Each participant completed two full runs of the task, covering all three languages with different image sets. The total experiment duration per participant was approximately six minutes. Participants were instructed to say “pass” if unable to identify an object. Overt speech was required, and all sessions were audio-recorded for offline accuracy verification.

Stimuli were selected from the Boston Naming Test and the Snodgrass–Vanderwart picture database ^4. A total of 72 object images were used, with a balanced mix of high- and low-frequency words. Word frequency and appropriateness were verified through consultations with native speakers of each language. Synonyms were documented to ensure accurate scoring and to assess inhibitory control.

Raw fNIRS data were processed using nirsLAB software. Poor-quality channels and artefacts were removed, followed by band-pass filtering (0.01–0.2 Hz) to eliminate physiological noise such as cardiac, respiratory, and Mayer wave signals. The modified Beer–Lambert law was applied to convert optical intensity data into changes in oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR).

Channel activation was assessed using a t-value–based statistical method derived from a general linear model. A canonical hemodynamic response function (cHRF), modeled using two gamma functions, was convolved with a boxcar task function. Linear regression was used to estimate activation coefficients, and statistically significant channels were identified based on positive t-values exceeding a critical threshold. Analysis focused on HbO changes within the prefrontal cortex as the primary region of interest.

Results

Across all languages, picture naming elicited robust activation in the left prefrontal cortex, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), superior frontal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, and inferior frontal gyrus—regions consistently associated with language production and lexical retrieval. Importantly, substantial activation was also observed in the right hemisphere, particularly in the right DLPFC and right medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC), indicating bilateral engagement during multilingual language processing.

The extent of cortical activation varied systematically by language. Native languages showed the least overall activation, with an average of 7 out of 20 prefrontal channels activated. Urdu (first language) produced greater activation, with approximately 12 channels showing significant oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) increases. English (second language) elicited the strongest response, activating an average of 14 channels, with more pronounced right-hemispheric involvement than observed for the other languages. These findings indicate increasing cognitive load from native language to first language and highest for the second language.

Spatial probability mapping and t-value analysis confirmed that English consistently produced higher activation across participants, particularly in bilateral DLPFC and MPFC regions. This pattern reflects the greater executive control, inhibition, and attentional resources required when processing a less dominant language. Waveform analyses further demonstrated sustained increases in HbO concentration during task blocks, confirming task-related cortical engagement.

The findings support all three initial predictions of the study. First, English produced the highest neural activation, consistent with prior evidence that second-language processing is more cognitively demanding. Second, language processing extended beyond traditional left-lateralized regions, with significant right-hemisphere involvement. Third, distinct neural activation patterns emerged for each language, underscoring the dynamic nature of multilingual language control.

The prominent activation of the right DLPFC aligns with existing literature linking this region to language control, inhibition, and selective attention. During picture naming, participants were required to suppress non-target languages and retrieve the appropriate lexical item, activating executive control mechanisms. The observed right MPFC activation further suggests involvement in monitoring and regulating task demands, particularly under higher cognitive load.

The greater activation observed for English may also reflect affective and sociolinguistic factors. As English is not the participants’ dominant language and is often associated with formal or evaluative contexts, increased neural activation may partly reflect anxiety, reduced automaticity, or heightened self-monitoring. These neural findings corroborate behavioral research showing increased effort and reduced fluency when speakers use a non-dominant language.

Importantly, the study demonstrates that multilingual language processing relies on a distributed and bilateral neural network rather than a strictly left-hemisphere system. The results support models of bilingual and multilingual language control that emphasize inhibition and executive regulation, particularly when multiple linguistic systems compete for activation.

By focusing on healthy multilingual adults, this study establishes baseline activation patterns that can inform future clinical research, including investigations of aphasia, brain injury, or neurodegenerative conditions. The use of fNIRS provides an ecologically valid and non-invasive method for studying overt speech, offering advantages over more restrictive imaging techniques.

Conclusion

The present study employed functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to investigate language processing in multilingual adults during a picture-naming task specifically designed for multiple native languages of Pakistan. Picture naming, which requires lexical retrieval and overt speech production, reliably engages core language networks in the brain. Consistent with prior research, the findings confirmed robust activation of left-hemispheric language regions. However, beyond this expected pattern, the study provides compelling evidence for substantial involvement of the right prefrontal cortex in multilingual language processing.

Notably, significant activation was observed in several right-hemispheric prefrontal regions, including Brodmann areas 8, 9, 10, and 46. These findings challenge the traditional view that language processing is predominantly confined to the left hemisphere and instead support a more distributed, bilateral model of language control. The results suggest that the right prefrontal cortex plays a critical role in managing cognitive demands associated with word retrieval, inhibition, and control during multilingual language use.

A key outcome of the study was the differential neural activation observed across languages. Among the three language conditions—native language(s), first language (Urdu), and second language (English)—English elicited the strongest and most widespread cortical activation. This heightened neural response indicates a greater cognitive load when participants used their second language, likely reflecting reduced automaticity, increased executive control demands, and the need to suppress competing languages. These neural findings align with existing behavioral and sociolinguistic research suggesting that second-language use is often associated with increased effort and anxiety, particularly in formal or evaluative contexts.

By focusing on neurotypical multilingual adults, this study establishes baseline patterns of cortical activation that are valuable for future clinical and applied research. Such normative data can serve as a reference for examining language impairments resulting from neurological damage, stroke, or neurodegenerative disorders. Furthermore, the use of fNIRS offers a practical and ecologically valid approach to studying language production, as it allows for overt speech and naturalistic task engagement.

Despite its contributions, the study has certain limitations. The sample size was relatively small, partly due to limited availability of participants with specific native language backgrounds, particularly Balochi speakers. Additionally, Pakistan’s extensive linguistic diversity—encompassing more than 60 languages—was not fully represented. Future research would benefit from larger and more diverse samples, broader age ranges, and the inclusion of additional cognitive or memory-based tasks. Comparing bilingual and multilingual populations may also yield deeper insights into how increasing linguistic complexity shapes neural organization.

In conclusion, this study advances understanding of multilingual language processing by demonstrating bilateral prefrontal involvement, language-dependent cognitive load, and the utility of fNIRS in multilingual neuroscience research. These findings contribute to a more nuanced and inclusive model of language processing in the human brain.
