Introduction

Self-transcendence (ST) refers to experiences in which the perceived boundaries of the self become more permeable, generating a sense of connection to something greater than oneself. Although ST is associated with psychological well-being and prosocial emotion, its neural basis remains poorly understood. Neuroscientific work has begun to probe the biological basis of ST. Functional neuroimaging studies consistently implicate the default mode network (DMN), particularly the medial prefrontal cortex and posterior cingulate cortex, in self-related processing and self-transcendent experiences. Additional evidence points to parietal regions involved in bodily boundaries and spatial self-representation, and to neuromodulatory systems such as brainstem serotonergic nuclei. However, these studies are correlational and cannot establish whether implicated regions play a causal role in ST.

Lesion network mapping (LNM) enables identification of these circuits by linking focal lesions to whole-brain connectivity using normative resting-state data. LNM has revealed causal networks for diverse traits, including spirituality. Here, we applied LNM to a dataset of 88 neurosurgical patients who completed the TCI before and after tumor resection. Our goal was to derive a circuit in which connectivity to lesion sites predicts changes in ST and to evaluate its construct validity through comparison with networks implicated in self-referential processing and compassion. This work provides a circuit-level account of the neural architecture supporting self-transcendence.

Methods

We analyzed a previously published dataset of 88 neurosurgical patients with focal brain lesions (Urgesi et al., 2010) who completed the Temperament and Character Inventory (TCI) before and after tumor resection. Our behavioral measure was pre- to post-operative change in the self-transcendence (ST) domain. Post-operative structural MRI scans were manually traced to generate binary lesion masks. Masks were normalized to MNI152 space using SPM12 with cost-function masking, and all masks were visually inspected for accuracy in native and standard space.

Lesion network mapping followed validated procedures (^1,2). Each lesion mask served as a seed region in a normative resting-state connectome derived from 1,000 healthy adults. For each patient, voxelwise Pearson correlations were computed between the lesion seed and all brain voxels; maps were Fisher z-transformed to generate individual lesion networks. To identify connectivity patterns predictive of ST change, we performed voxelwise regression relating each patient’s lesion-network connectivity to their pre–post ST score difference. Statistical significance was assessed using permutation testing in PALM (5,000 permutations), yielding whole-brain t-maps indicating where stronger positive or negative connectivity to lesion sites predicted corresponding increases or decreases in ST. Unthresholded maps were visualized in MRIcroGL (v1.2).

To situate the ST network within large-scale cortical organization, we next quantified its overlap with the 17 canonical Yeo networks (^3) using the Network Correspondence Toolbox (^4). For each Yeo network, we computed (1) the Dice coefficient indexing raw spatial overlap and (2) a standardized overlap score expressing how extreme the observed overlap was relative to a spatially constrained null. Statistical significance was obtained using a surface-based spin test: the ST map was projected to a spherical surface, randomly rotated 10,000 times, and the distribution of rotated overlaps formed the null. Empirical p-values reflected the proportion of spins whose overlap exceeded the observed value.

Results

Voxelwise lesion network mapping revealed a distributed whole-brain pattern in which connectivity to lesion sites significantly predicted pre- to post-operative changes in self-transcendence. The resulting ST circuit prominently incorporated the inferior posterior parietal regions previously identified by Urgesi et al. (2010), but extended beyond this locus into a broader frontoparietal and default-mode architecture.

Canonical network characterization showed that the ST map exhibited its strongest positive correspondence with frontoparietal control networks, led by ControlB (overlap score = 1.104, p = 0.002) and ControlC (0.888, p = 0.002), meaning that damage to ControlB and ControlC connections is significantly associated with increased ST. A positive, though weaker, correspondence was also observed in the same functional direction for DefaultA (0.600, p = 0.048 uncorrected).

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that self-transcendence is not localized to a single cortical region but emerges from a distributed circuit convergent with functional downregulation of selective and specific frontoparietal control and default-mode systems. By extending lesion-based evidence beyond the inferior posterior parietal cortex to a broader functional architecture, this work provides a causal, network-level account of self-transcendence. The involvement of control and default-mode networks suggests that self-transcendent experiences may arise through interactions between systems that regulate internal attention, perspective shifting, and self-related processing. More broadly, this study illustrates how lesion network mapping can clarify the neural mechanisms underlying complex psychological constructs, offering a framework for future translational work linking contemplative practices, prosocial emotions, and circuit-based interventions.
