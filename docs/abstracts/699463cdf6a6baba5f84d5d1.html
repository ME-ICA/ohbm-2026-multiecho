Introduction

High-density, directional stereo-electroencephalography (sEEG) electrodes are increasingly used to sample neural activity at finer spatial scales than conventional clinical depth electrodes[1]. By increasing contact density and introducing directional sensitivity, these devices offer new opportunities for improved characterization of local field potentials and for more precise localization of intracranial sources. At the same time, the interpretation of signals recorded with directional electrodes becomes more complex, as measured amplitudes and spatial patterns are strongly influenced by electrode geometry, insulation, and tissue conduction properties.

Accurate forward modeling is therefore essential for understanding how directional sEEG electrodes sample neural activity and for translating their measurements into reliable physiological and clinical insights. The finite element method (FEM) modeling provides a principled framework for capturing complex electrode geometries and heterogeneous volume conduction[2,3]; however, such modeling has traditionally relied on commercial solvers, which limit accessibility and reproducibility. Although open-source FEM tools have matured substantially in recent years, their accuracy in modeling next-generation directional intracranial electrodes requires careful validation [2].

In this work, we address this need by validating an open-source FEM pipeline for modeling lead fields of high-resolution directional sEEG electrodes and by characterizing how electrode geometry shapes directional sensitivity and source localization performance.

Methods

We developed a finite element method framework for computing lead fields of high-resolution directional depth electrodes using the open-source Brainstorm–DUNEuro pipeline and validated it against a commercial FEM solver (ANSYS)[4–6]. Lead fields were computed using Helmholtz reciprocity with two complementary approaches: electrode-based stimulation in ANSYS and source-based recording in Brainstorm–DUNEuro. These approaches are theoretically equivalent and provide an effective basis for cross-validation[7].

Validation was first performed using idealized single-layer and multi-layer spherical head models with known analytical solutions[8,9]. Lead fields computed with both solvers were compared to analytical ground truth using standard accuracy metrics, including relative difference measures, magnitude error, and correlation coefficients.

Following validation in spherical models, we simulated a high-density, directional sEEG electrode embedded in a bio-relevant volume conductor. The electrode geometry included a cylindrical insulating shaft with densely distributed surface contacts. FEM meshes were refined locally to accurately represent the electrode structure and surrounding tissue. To isolate the effect of electrode geometry on directional sensitivity, simulations were repeated with the shaft modeled as either an insulator or a conductor.

Finally, source localization performance was evaluated using simulated dipolar sources placed around pairs of directional sEEG electrodes. Localization accuracy and dipole orientation recovery were compared between high-density directional sEEG arrays and conventional sEEG configurations derived from the same geometry.

Results

In spherical head models, both FEM solvers closely matched analytical solutions, demonstrating high numerical accuracy. Across all tested configurations, relative difference measures were below 5 percent, and correlation coefficients exceeded 95 percent, indicating strong agreement between the open-source and commercial pipelines.

In simulations of the directional sEEG electrode, lead-field patterns computed with Brainstorm–DUNEuro and ANSYS were highly consistent, despite differences in solver implementation. A detailed analysis revealed pronounced directional sensitivity, driven by the insulating shaft of the electrode. Contacts facing a source exhibited higher sensitivity, while sensitivity was reduced on the opposite side of the electrode, producing a clear substrate shielding effect. When the shaft conductivity was set equal to that of the surrounding medium, this directional bias disappeared, confirming that the observed effects arise from electrode geometry rather than numerical artifacts.

Source localization simulations showed that high-density directional sEEG arrays substantially improved localization accuracy and dipole orientation recovery compared with conventional sEEG configurations. Localization errors were typically below one millimeter for the high-density arrays, whereas sparser configurations showed larger errors and increased variability.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that open-source FEM tools can accurately model complex, high-resolution directional intracranial electrodes and achieve results comparable to those obtained with commercial solvers. By validating the Brainstorm–DUNEuro pipeline against analytical solutions and a commercial FEM framework, we provide confidence in its use for realistic modeling of directional sEEG recordings.

Our results highlight the critical role of electrode geometry, particularly insulating substrates, in shaping directional sensitivity and lead-field structure. Incorporating these effects into forward models is crucial for accurately interpreting intracranial recordings and for reliably localizing sources.

The proposed pipeline, available within the Brainstorm software, supports reproducible, accessible, and patient-specific modeling of directional sEEG electrodes, providing a practical foundation for improved analysis of intracranial data. These advances are directly relevant for applications such as epilepsy mapping, interpretation of local field potentials, and the development of next-generation sensing and stimulation strategies.
