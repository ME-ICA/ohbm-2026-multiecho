Introduction

Anxiety (AD) and obsessive-compulsive disorders (OCD) are common and disabling conditions with immense personal and socioeconomic impact. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has uncovered altered functional connectivity between and within functional brain networks and altered network topology in both AD and OCD1,2. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and pharmacological therapy are the recommended first-line treatments for both AD and OCD, but around 50% do not recover after evidence-based treatment1,2. Understanding why some do not recover is important to advance and personalize future treatment approaches, with evidence suggesting that functional network connectivity may predict outcome and normalize after treatment. However, existing studies are small, do not include a control group, and mostly focus on single disorders3. Here, we present the results of one of the world’s largest treatment studies investigating functional network connectivity in both individuals with OCD or AD (focusing on panic and social anxiety disorders) compared to healthy controls using rs-fMRI, investigating if functional connectivity predicted treatment response/remission and changed after exposure therapy. We measured both functional connectivity between and within functional brain networks as well as graph theoretical measures describing the brains connectome, including global effectiveness and modularity. We also investigated if functional network connectivity was associated with medication use, comorbidity, age of onset, or illness duration.

Methods

Structured diagnostic interviews by trained raters were used to diagnose individuals with panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, OCD, comorbid disorders, and ruled out current or past mental disorders in healthy controls. Symptom severity was measured using the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale, Panic Disorder Severity Scale, and Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale, respectively. Response and remission after treatment was defined using empirical, disorder-specific criteria. Before treatment, 146 individuals (mean age= 26.0, SD=8.75, 60% female) with AD or OCD (64 AD, 82 OCD) underwent 10 min eyes-closed rs-fMRI at one of two sites using an identical imaging protocol (750 volumes, TR=800ms, TE=30ms, flip angle=52⁰, voxel size=2.4x2.4x2.4mm, 60 slices, multiband acceleration factor=6, anterior-posterior field maps). Individuals with OCD or AD received concentrated CBT over four consecutive days, and 133 were rescanned after two weeks, and 121 three months after treatment. Fifty healthy controls were also scanned at the same intervals. One individual was excluded for mean framewise displacement over 0.55mm, two due to incomplete scan sequence/imaging artifact, and one due to enlarged ventricles impeding image normalization. MRI data was processed and denoised using fMRIPrep and XCP-D, including nuisance regression of BOLD signal related to ICA-AROMA noise components and mean cerebrospinal fluid and white matter. Pearson’s r was used to correlate BOLD signal timeseries in 400 cortical and 56 subcortical regions and functional connectivity was estimated between and within the visual, somatomotor, dorsal and ventral attention, frontoparietal, limbic, default mode, subcortical and cerebellar networks. Global efficiency and modularity were estimated using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox. Site effects were removed using ComBat harmonization for connectivity matrices while retaining variance related to diagnosis, sex, age, and years of education. Linear or linear mixed models adjusting for sex, age, years of education, and motion during scanning (mean framewise displacement) were used to compare groups and estimate change over time. The False Discovery Rate (FDR) was used to correct p-values for multiple comparisons.

Results

Two weeks after treatment, the remission and response rates were 70% and 90% for OCD and 47% and 80% for AD, respectively. Three months after treatment, the rates were 71% and 15% for OCD and 63% and 86% for AD.

At baseline, individuals with AD showed less connectivity compared to healthy controls between the default and somatomotor (d=-0.589, pFDR=0.048), limbic and ventral attention (d=-0.580, pFDR=0.048), limbic and somatomotor networks (d=-0.572, pFDR=0.048), as well as more network modularity (d=-0.497, pFDR=0.020).

Analyses including all three time points uncovered significantly less connectivity in AD compared to healthy controls across time between the default and somatomotor (d=-0.593, pFDR=0.017), limbic and somatomotor (d=-0.586, pFDR=0.017), limbic and ventral attention (d=-0.573, pFDR=0.018), default and dorsal attention (d=-0.486, pFDR=0.043), limbic and dorsal attention (d=-0.497, pFDR=0.043), default and ventral attention (d=-0.486, pFDR=0.043), dorsal attention and somatomotor networks (d=-0.483, pFDR=0.047), and within the somatomotor network (d=-0.600, pFDR=0.017). In addition, AD showed less global efficiency (d=-0.368, pFDR=0.039) and more modularity (d=0.513, pFDR=0.010) compared to healthy controls across time.

No significant findings were found when investigating main effect of time, group by time interaction, predicting treatment response/outcome, or when investigating individuals with OCD. The findings were not influenced by medication use, comorbidity, age of onset, or illness duration.

Conclusion

Concentrated CBT for OCD and AD is associated with rapid and sustained improvements in symptom severity, in line with previous clinical studies. We find that functional network connectivity does not significantly change or predict response/remission after treatment. In contrast, individuals AD, compared to healthy controls, show significantly less connectivity in and between functional brain networks, less global efficiency and more modularity at all time points. This suggests that resting-state functional network connectivity in AD is hypoconnectected, less effective, and more segregated compared to healthy controls. The present study is substantially larger than previous studies, investigated two disorders, and included healthy controls with longitudinal fMRI1,2,3, however, it did not include a control treatment condition and was only powered to detect moderate to large effect sizes. In conclusion, altered resting-state functional network connectivity appears to be a trait-related characteristic in AD.
