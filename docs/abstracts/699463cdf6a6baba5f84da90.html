Introduction

Listening to music has been found to be positively associated with mental health outcomes.1 It has been hypothesized that music may affect mental wellbeing through emotion regulation. However, little research has examined the neural mechanisms linking music and mental health. The present study examined connections between listening to music, limbic system structure, and mental health outcomes utilizing structural equation modeling in a large sample of youth ages 9 and 10. Our first hypothesis was that limbic system volume is a latent factor indexing volume in regions of the amygdala, hippocampus and thalamus. Secondly, we hypothesized that longer term engagement (number of years) of listening to music would be associated with increased limbic volume and lower levels of internalizing and externalizing problems. 

Methods

The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study Baseline (data release 6.0) data was used for analysis (n = 11,859; 47.8% female, ages 8-11). This study utilized cumulative lifetime number of years that youth had listened to music for pleasure, ranging from 0 to 10, as reported at their baseline visit. The Structural MRI (sMRI) data contains T1-weighted 3D structural images. The present study utilized subcortical volume measures (in mm³) from the following bilateral subcortical structures: amygdala, hippocampus, and thalamus. For each structure, left and right hemisphere volumes were extracted. All volume variables were standardized (M = 0, SD = 1) to place them on a common scale and facilitate comparisons across regions. 

Data analysis was conducted using R Version 4.4.2.2 Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables to assess for normality. The percentage of missing data was 10.2% for the listening to music variable, 0% for CBCL variables, and 0.9% for sMRI variables. To address missing data as well as concerns of normality, maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR estimation) was used.

Based on the known subcortical structures in the limbic system, a one-factor model was specified in which left-hemisphere amygdala volume (ag.l), right-hemisphere amygdala volume (ag.r), left-hemisphere hippocampal volume (hc.l), right-hemisphere hippocampal volume (hc.r), left-hemisphere thalamic volume (th.l) and right-hemisphere thalamic volume (th.r) loaded onto the latent variable of Limbic Volume. The indicators were cortical volume measures (in mm³) of subcortical structures for each participant. Left and right hemisphere volumes were allowed to be correlated within each subcortical structure. The model was overidentified with 6 df. The measurement model is shown in Figure 1. 

The confirmatory factor analysis for Limbic Volume was used as the measurement model for the following structural equation model. This model included a path from listening to music to internalizing and externalizing problems through the limbic volume latent factor, as well as direct paths from listening to music to internalizing and externalizing problems. Both the measurement model and the final model were evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). We used cutoff values of above .90 for acceptable fit and above .95 for good fit for the CFI and TLI. We used values less than .08 as acceptable fit and less than .05 as good fit for the RMSEA and SRMR.

Results

The measurement model for the limbic volume latent factor showed adequate to good model fit (CFI = .995; TLI =.987; RMSEA = .065, 90% CI = [.058 - .072]; SRMR = .008). The chi-square p value was significant, but given that this is sensitive to sample size, and that the rest of the fit statistics were either good or adequate, the model shows overall good fit. The factor loadings revealed that each subcortical region loaded strongly onto the latent factor of limbic volume, suggesting that these are all good indicators of the latent limbic structure (range of R2s =.48 - .72). The standardized estimates for these residual covariances between left and right hemispheres showed that the bilateral counterparts were all highly correlated with each other, as expected biologically (amygdala: θag.r,ag.l = .526; hippocampus: θhc.r,hc.l = .503; thalamus: θth.r,th.l = .743). 

The structural model showed good model fit (CFI = .995; TLI =.991; RMSEA = .036, 90% CI = [.033 - .040]; SRMR = .007; shown in Figure 2). The regressions revealed that listening to music was a significant predictor of limbic volume (β = -.047, p < .001). Limbic volume negatively predicted both internalizing (β = -.022, p < .031) and externalizing problems (β = -.034, p < .001). Listening to music also positively predicted internalizing (β = .043, p < .001) and externalizing (β = .019, p < .049) problems directly.  All the effects were small, and overall, the model explained 0.2% of variance in both internalizing and externalizing problems (R2 =.002). 

Conclusion

The results of the CFA provide evidence for a latent construct of limbic volume that is well represented by bilateral amygdala, hippocampus and thalamus volumes. The latent factor explained a meaningful proportion of variance in each region, suggesting that these structures reliably contribute to the limbic system construct. Notably, the hippocampal volumes showed the strongest factor loadings, indicating that they were a particularly good indicator of the latent construct. Longer term engagement in listening to music was associated with increased limbic volume and lower levels of internalizing and externalizing problems, however these effects were small. Thus, while there is statistical support for these relationships, they are not clinically meaningful. 

            The current study had several strengths, including a large sample size with diverse demographics, a strong measurement model, and the use of a structural model to test relationships between listening to music, limbic volume and mental health outcomes. Future research may draw from the results indicating that the latent factor representing limbic volume was measured reliably through the subcortical brain regions. 

            One limitation of note is the limited interpretability of the effects in the structural model, making it difficult to draw conclusions about underlying mechanisms between these constructs. Furthermore, all measures were cross-sectional, meaning causality was not assessed. Future research may utilize longitudinal measures of these constructs in the ABCD and can look at multiple variables across multiple timepoints. 

            Future research might also examine music engagement in different ways than just number of years the youth had listened to music in their free time. For example, music engagement could include a combination of listening to music, singing, playing an instrument, and taking dance classes. Latent class analysis may help to identify subgroups of youth who engage in musical activities in different ways. These subgroups could uncover associations between musical engagement and brain or behavioral outcomes. It may also be relevant to include functional connectivity measures of brain networks to expand upon the structural measures.

            Overall, these findings demonstrate the importance of distinguishing between statistically significant effects and clinically meaningful effects in large datasets. Despite the latent factor of limbic volume being measured reliably and the hypothesized paths being statistically significant, the effects were too small to support practical conclusions regarding relationships between music listening, limbic volume, and mental health symptoms.
