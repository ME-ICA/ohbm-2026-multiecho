Introduction

Heartbeat-evoked responses (HER), measured with EEG or MEG, represent neural activity time- and phase-locked to heartbeats and serve as a key marker of cardiac interoception in brain-body interaction research1. HERs have been linked to diverse cognitive and emotional functions, including perception, attention, motor activity, and affective processing. However, the field faces substantial variability in reported findings, largely due to the diverse methodological choices. Furthermore, unlike traditional evoked responses, HERs require special care due to the presence of cardiac artifacts3, potential overlap with task-evoked activity4, or superimposed responses from successive heartbeats. These complications make numerous data-processing decisions necessary, yet no consensus exists on which approach is optimal. Understanding and summarizing current practices is essential for developing evidence-based recommendations and improving the robustness of HER research. To improve reproducibility and develop a standardized procedure, we systematically reviewed methodological practices across published HER studies, including examination of data acquisition, preprocessing, HER calculation, and statistical approaches taken. 

Methods

We conducted a systematic literature search using PubMed available by 05.08.2024 to identify studies investigating HERs with electroencephalography (EEG) or magnetoencephalography (MEG). Search terms included combinations of "EEG" "electroencephalography" "MEG" "magnetoencephalography" "heartbeat evoked potential" and "heartbeat evoked response". After removing duplicates, 749 unique studies were identified. We included studies that (1) examined HERs, (2) applied noninvasive EEG or MEG recordings, (3) were conducted in human participants, (4) reported original data, (5) were in the English language, and (6) had full text available. Exclusion criteria were (1) intracranial recordings due to the different data analysis approaches, (2) non-original research (reviews, commentary papers, and toolboxes), and (3) studies not analyzing evoked responses (e.g., time-frequency analysis only), (4) lack of a peer review.  Following screening, 132 studies comprised the final pool for review (Fig. 1A). We systematically extracted methodological information across multiple processing stages: data acquisition (recording parameters, ECG placement), preprocessing (filtering, re-referencing, artifact correction, segmentation, baseline correction), HER estimation (averaging approaches, time windows), statistical analysis (methods, control analyses). A standardized codebook, containing the description of all extracted information, ensured consistency in labeling methodological categories. We quantified methodological heterogeneity using normalized Shannon entropy, with higher values indicating greater variability across choices. To assess the statistical power of each individual study, we estimated minimal detectable effect sizes based on sample sizes, group, condition numbers and statistical test types, converting all estimates to Hedges' g for comparison with previously reported meta-level effect sizes⁵. Spatial distributions of reported HER effects were aggregated across different EEG layouts by interpolating channel-level counts into layout independent topographies. 

Results

Our final sample included 132 HER studies published between 1986 and 2024 (Fig. 1B). Analysis of the methodological choices revealed substantial methodological heterogeneity across nearly all HER processing steps. Normalized Shannon entropy values indicated high variability in filtering parameters, reference electrode selection, cardiac field artifact (CFA) removal approaches, and statistical testing methods. Critically, some methodological information was frequently unreported: up to 80% of studies omitted essential details for certain processing steps, including CFA removal criteria, number of rejected independent components, and epoch counts per participant. Furthermore, only one study systematically compared several methodological choices per processing step to assess the effects of researcher-dependent decisions on HER results. 

Regarding data acquisition, 93% of studies used EEG while 7% used MEG (Fig. 1C). Most studies (70%) analyzed HERs during task performance rather than resting state  (Fig. 1D). Sample sizes ranged from 1 to 552 participants (Mdn = 32), with the number of heartbeat-locked epochs ranging from 22 to 5517 (Mdn = 300) per participant and condition, though 55% of studies did not report this information.

For preprocessing, high-pass filter cutoffs varied considerably, with common choices including 0.1 Hz, 0.5 Hz, and 1 Hz. The choice of an adequate high-pass filter cutoff might affect HER results, since its slow components might be attenuated by cutoffs above 1 Hz. 

The common average reference was the predominant offline reference choice (45% of the  pipelines), which facilitates across study comparability. ICA was frequently used for artifact removal (73% of the pipelines), but approaches for identifying CFA-related components varied considerably, with no dominant choice emerging, raising questions about the consistency and comparability of CFA removal across studies. Regarding HER computation, 93% of studies used R-peak as the epoch onset (Fig. 2A), with effects observed typically spanning 200-600 ms post R-peak (Fig. 2D), which is generally in agreement with intracranial studies demonstrating cardiac locked activity mostly 200 ms after the R-peak. Across cluster- and region-of-interest based average analyses, effects are most consistently  found over frontocentral electrodes (Fig. 2C/D/E). While baseline correction is applied in 56% of studies (91% of those reporting, Fig. 2B), it is generally not advised due to the potential temporal overlap of successive heartbeat epochs

Statistical power analysis revealed that only up to 33% of studies reached sufficient power (80% at α = 0.05) to reliably detect meta-level HER effect sizes reported previously5. Furthermore, control analyses were applied inconsistently: while 65% of studies included the ECG and other heartbeat-related controls in their statistical analysis. While the majority of studies investigated HERs in conjunction with a task, only 19% performed surrogate heartbeat analyses to verify that effects were genuinely heartbeat-locked and not driven by task related confounds. Preregistration was rare (4%), yet 93% of studies reported statistically significant findings, suggesting potential publication bias.

Conclusion

This systematic review comprehensively characterizes the diverse set of methods used in non-invasive HER research using M/EEG. Our analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity across many processing steps. While some predominant methodological patterns emerge, such as a higher percentage of EEG compared to MEG studies and the frequent use of the R-peak as a cardiac reference marker, most other steps show considerable variability. The finding that fewer than one-third of studies were adequately powered to detect established effect sizes raises concerns about the reliability of current findings and highlights an urgent need for larger, well-powered investigations.
To address these challenges, we developed a comprehensive quality-control checklist and tentative analysis pipeline to guide future research. Some key recommendations include using high-pass filters ≤0.5 Hz to preserve slow HER components, recording multiple ECG leads to capture the three-dimensional cardiac field for robust control analyses, reporting the number of epochs per participant and condition (which directly affects signal-to-noise ratio), documenting CFA removal criteria and number of rejected components, performing surrogate heartbeat analyses to confirm effects are genuinely heartbeat-locked, verifying that results are robust to cardiac artifact removal, and open data and code sharing to enable replication.
To support the research community, we provide an interactive web application for exploring our extracted dataset, allowing researchers to apply custom inclusion criteria and generate updated visualizations (https://paulsteinfath.shinyapps.io/hep-systematic-review/). The full dataset, codebook, and analysis scripts are openly available. We hope these resources will foster greater methodological consistency and accelerate progress toward understanding how the brain processes cardiac signals.
