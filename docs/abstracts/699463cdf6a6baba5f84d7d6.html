Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a clinically and biologically heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive motor and non-motor symptoms, suggesting the existence of distinct progression subtypes. Among multiple interacting pathogenic mechanisms, abnormal iron accumulation plays a critical role by facilitating α-synuclein aggregation and dopaminergic neuronal loss. Given the marked heterogeneity of PD, there is increasing emphasis on pathogenesis-driven biomarkers to enable biologically informed patient stratification and disease monitoring. Within this framework, iron-related imaging markers represent mechanistically relevant candidates for subtyping and tracking disease progression.

Quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) is one of the most sensitive MRI techniques for assessing brain iron distribution in vivo. QSM studies have consistently demonstrated increased nigral iron in PD and established it as a surrogate marker of disease severity and progression. However, PD pathology extends well beyond the midbrain, involving widespread cortical and subcortical regions. Nevertheless, most prior studies have focused on isolated regions or group-level comparisons, offering limited insight into how iron-related abnormalities evolve along distinct disease trajectories. The Braak staging framework proposes a characteristic topographical progression of pathology, consistent with a prion-like spreading process in which early-affected regions show progressively increasing involvement over time.1

To model such cascading and heterogeneous disease processes, the Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) model provides a data-driven framework that simultaneously infers disease subtypes and progression stages.2 In this study, our aims were to identify distinct PD subtypes characterized by unique trajectories of iron accumulation measured by QSM, assess their longitudinal stability, and examine their clinical correlates. 

Methods

A total of 574 participants (368 PD, 206 controls) were included. Comprehensive clinical assessments covered motor, non-motor, gait, olfaction, and cognition. MRI data acquisition was performed on a 3.0 Tesla MRI. Quantitative susceptibility maps were reconstructed using Laplacian unwrapping, V-SHARP filtering, and STAR-QSM inversion (STISuite). Cortical parcellation was performed using the Desikan-Killiany atlas, and subcortical nuclei (caudate, putamen, pallidum, red nucleus, and substantia nigra) were segmented with a deep-learning model.3 To minimize venous contamination, subject-specific vessel probability maps were generated from QSM images using a multiscale vessel filter.4 Cortical regions were grouped into five lobes plus the insula (frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, and cingulate/insula). In total, 11 regional measures were averaged bilaterally and standardized to HC-based z-scores adjusted for age and sex.

To identify subtypes with distinct biomarker progression patterns, we employed the Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn), which is a probabilistic, unsupervised machine-learning algorithm that combines clustering and disease progression modeling (Fig1). The z-score thresholds were set at 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0, representing subtle, moderate, and marked susceptibility increases. The optimal number of subtypes was determined by assessing models with up to five subtypes using 10-fold cross-validation, based on the cross-validation information criterion and out-of-sample log-likelihood. Uncertainty in the inferred event sequence for each subtype was estimated using 1 × 10⁵ Markov Chain Monte Carlo iterations per run, which approximate the posterior distribution over possible biomarker progressions.

Continuous clinical variables were compared across SuStaIn-defined subtypes using ANCOVA, with post hoc pairwise comparisons. Three sets of comparisons were performed: (1) one-versus-one comparisons among subtypes S1, S2, and S3; (2) one-versus-all comparisons to identify subtype-specific characteristics relative to the average of the other two subtypes; and (3) comparisons between each subtype (S1–S3) and the S0 group. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s χ² test. The associations between SuStaIn stage and clinical measures were further examined using multiple linear regression models, both across the three subtypes combined and within each subtype separately.

To evaluate the longitudinal stability of SuStaIn-derived subtypes, we examined whether individual participants retained the same subtype classification across follow-up MRI scans. Longitudinal data were available for subsets of patients with PD who completed MRI assessments at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 year follow-ups. For each participant, the SuStaIn model trained on the baseline cross-sectional data was applied to follow-up MRI data to infer the most probable subtype and stage as described above. The proportion of such participants relative to the total number of those with longitudinal scans was calculated for each follow-up year to assess temporal subtype stability. To examine whether baseline subtype confidence differed between participants who remained stable and those who changed or advanced, baseline posterior probabilities were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. To assess SuStaIn stage progression among participants with stable subtypes, the change in model stage (ΔStage = follow-up stage − baseline stage) was computed for each individual. One-sample t-tests were used to determine whether the mean ΔStage differed significantly from zero within the stable group.

Results

Three distinct PD subtypes were identified with unique spatiotemporal trajectories of iron accumulation(Fig2). The S1 (insula-cortical-first) shows early insula and cortical regions iron increases, spreading to subcortical nuclei. The S2 (striatal-first) shows early caudate and putamen, accompanied by moderate changes in the red nucleus. The S3 (midbrain-first) showed prominent early changes in the red nucleus and substantia nigra, while the caudate and putamen remained relatively preserved at onset. As the disease stage advanced, both S2 and S3 showed gradual propagation to widespread cortical regions. S2 and S3 share a bottom-up pattern beginning in subcortical nuclei and extending cortically, whereas S1 follows a top-down course originating in insula cortex. A subset (n = 64) showed no ≥ 0.5 SD deviations and was categorized as sub-threshold (S0).

Patients classified into S1 were significantly older at both baseline and disease onset compared with those in other subtypes (P < 0.05), whereas S2 and S3 tended to be younger. Compared with the S0 group, both S2 and S3 had a significantly longer disease duration (S2, p < 0.001; S3, p < 0.001), while S1 exhibited a mild but non-significant increase (p = 0.466). All three subtypes exhibited significantly higher UPDRS Part II scores, reflecting greater impairment in motor experiences of daily living, compared with the S0 group (S1, p < 0.001; S2, p < 0.001; S3, p < 0.01). Scores on the Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (FOGQ) were higher in S2 compared with the S0 group (p < 0.05). For fine motor dexterity measured by the Purdue Pegboard Test, lower scores were observed in S2 compared with the other groups across the left-hand and both-hands subtests (p < 0.05). In contrast, S1 generally performed better than other groups on the left-hand (p < 0.01), both-hands (p < 0.01) and assembly (p < 0.05). On the olfactory identification test, both S2 and S3 showed significantly lower scores than the S0 group (S2, P < 0.05; S3, P < 0.05), while S1 exhibited a trend toward lower performance (P = 0.059). Regarding autonomic symptoms assessed with Scales for Outcomes in Parkinson’s Disease Autonomic symptoms (SCOPA-AUT), higher total scores were observed in S2 and S3 compared with the S0 group (S2, p < 0.01; S3, p = 0.059). S1 exhibited lower SCOPA-AUT scores than S2 and S3 (p < 0.001).

Across all subtypes combined, higher SuStaIn stage was significantly associated with longer disease duration (β = 0.50, p < 0.001), greater impairment in activities of daily living (UPDRS II; β = 0.45, p < 0.001), and more severe motor symptoms (UPDRS III; β = 0.10, p = 0.095). No significant association was observed between SuStaIn stage and MMSE scores (β = -0.058, p = 0.299). When examined within each subtype, higher SuStaIn stage was positively associated with disease duration in S1 (β = 0.23, p < 0.05), S2 (β = 0.77, p < 0.001), and S3 (β = 0.54, p < 0.001). Significant correlations were also observed between SuStaIn stage and UPDRS II scores in S2 (β = 0.76, p < 0.001) and S3 (β = 0.66, p < 0.001), and between SuStaIn stage and UPDRS III scores in S2 (β = 0.37, p = 0.01).

At the 1- and 2-year follow-up scans, 70.2% (n = 33/47) and 61.4% (n = 27/44) of patients remained in the same subtype. Similarly, at later follow-up visits, 73.1% (n = 19/26), 50.0% (n = 9/18), and 60.0% (n = 9/15) of participants remained subtype-stable at the 3-, 4-, and 5-year visits. Among those classified as S0 at baseline, 44.4% (n = 4/9) progressed to one of the three subtypes at the 1-year follow-up. At the 2-year visit, all baseline S0 participants (n = 6, 100%) had transitioned into defined subtypes, with further progressions observed at later visits (50.0%, n = 2/4 at 3 years; 75.0%, n = 3/4 at 4 years). Participants with stable classifications had higher baseline subtype confidence (p < 0.001), and the SuStaIn stage increased significantly over time (p < 0.01), indicating measurable progression even without subtype transition.

Conclusion

Using the SuStaIn algorithm, we identified three distinct PD subtypes characterized by unique spatial and temporal trajectories of magnetic susceptibility changes, reflecting heterogeneous patterns of iron-related pathology across cortical and subcortical regions. Based on the earliest regions showing deviation from normative susceptibility values, these subtypes were labelled as S1: insula–cortical-first, S2: striatal-first, and S3: midbrain-first. In addition, a subset of patients was assigned to SuStaIn stage 0 (S0), indicating sub-threshold abnormality with no region exceeding a z-score of 0.5. The insula–cortical-first subtype (S1) was the most prevalent and showed early susceptibility increases in the insula and adjacent cortical regions, followed by later involvement of subcortical nuclei, consistent with a top-down progression pattern. Clinically, patients in S1 tended to have older disease onset, relatively mild motor impairment, preserved manual dexterity, and fewer autonomic symptoms. In contrast, the striatal-first (S2) and midbrain-first (S3) subtypes exhibited early abnormalities in subcortical structures, particularly the striatum, red nucleus, and substantia nigra, followed by progressive cortical involvement, representing a bottom-up trajectory. These two subtypes were associated with longer disease duration, greater motor disability, impaired manual coordination, and more prominent autonomic and olfactory dysfunction. Together, these findings suggest that PD heterogeneity may partly arise from differences in the initial regional vulnerability to iron-related neurodegeneration and distinct pathways of pathological propagation.

Across all subtypes, higher SuStaIn stages were associated with longer disease duration and greater motor and daily-living impairments, supporting the interpretation of SuStaIn stage as a marker of disease severity. Longitudinal analyses demonstrated moderate stability of subtype membership, with approximately two-thirds of patients retaining the same subtype over 1–3 years. Importantly, patients with stable subtype assignments had significantly higher baseline subtype confidence, suggesting that probabilistic assignment provides an internal measure of reliability. More than half of individuals initially classified as S0 progressed into one of the defined subtypes within three years, consistent with S0 representing an early disease state. Within subtype-stable individuals, SuStaIn stage increased significantly over time, indicating that the model captures continuous disease progression even when subtype identity remains unchanged.

Previous SuStaIn studies in PD have primarily relied on T1-weighted structural MRI measures of atrophy,5 which may be insensitive to early subcortical pathology. In contrast, quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) provides superior sensitivity and contrast for detecting iron accumulation, especially in the substantia nigra, a core region of PD pathology. Given the established role of iron-mediated oxidative stress in promoting α-synuclein aggregation and dopaminergic neuronal loss, QSM offers a mechanistically relevant biomarker for modeling PD progression. The observed SuStaIn subtypes may reflect distinct initiation sites and propagation routes of α-synuclein pathology. Within the brain-first versus body-first framework of PD, the insula–cortical-first subtype aligns with a brain-first phenotype, whereas the striatal- and midbrain-first subtypes resemble a body-first pattern associated with greater autonomic involvement. 

In conclusion, QSM-based SuStaIn modeling delineated three PD subtypes with distinct spatiotemporal trajectories of iron accumulation and differential clinical profiles. Subtype membership was largely stable over time, while SuStaIn stage captured progressive disease burden, highlighting the potential of this framework for biologically informed stratification and longitudinal monitoring in PD.
