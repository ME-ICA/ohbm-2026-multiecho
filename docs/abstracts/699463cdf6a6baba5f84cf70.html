Introduction

Access to educational and material resources shapes the functioning of cognitive and emotional systems across the lifespan. Socioeconomic status (SES) has been linked to stronger executive function, language skills and emotion regulation1. However, far less is known about how SES influences social-affective responses, which support everyday interactions and relationship quality. Social-affective responses also affect how individuals cope with chronic illness by shaping interpersonal distress and access to social support2, and the brain’s ability to manage affective reactions during pain or emotional suffering in oneself or others.

Responding to others suffering involves appraisal, self/other distinction and regulation of affective arousal. These capacities influence communication, support seeking and relationship dynamics. Vicarious pain fMRI paradigms offer a controlled way to measure these processes at behavioral and brain levels3. Observing or imagining another person in pain consistently engages the midcingulate cortex, the precuneus/posterior cingulate cortex and temporoparietal regions that support attention to others, mentalizing, and interpretation of social meaning3,4.

This study examines how education and income relate to vicarious pain unpleasantness (i.e., distress experienced when observing someone else in pain), trait empathy, and brain activation during a validated vicarious pain task. This approach allows us to test whether educational and material resources relate to social-affective processing in similar ways. We included healthy women and women with chronic pain or depression to test whether SES influences are similar across the health-illness continuum. Based on evidence showing that education is more strongly linked to cognitive flexibility and emotion regulation than income5, we expected education to show more robust associations with behavioral measures and with activation in brain regions involved in social cognition during vicarious pain.

Methods

This study included 144 adult women aged 18-65 (61 women diagnosed with fibromyalgia,  32 women diagnosed with major depressive disorder, and 51 healthy women). All participants completed a validated vicarious pain fMRI task. Before the task, participants were asked to select a significant adult in their life (e.g., a partner, close friend) to focus on during the experiment. During the task, they viewed photographs depicting hands or feet undergoing painful situations (e.g., a foot stepping on a sharp object). Participants were instructed to imagine the chosen person experiencing the injury shown in each image. For each image, they rated how unpleasant it was to imagine their significant other experiencing the depicted injury on a numeric rating scale ranging from 0 = not at all  to 10 = the most imaginable (Figure 1). This provided a continuous measure of vicarious pain unpleasantness (VPU). Participants also completed the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) to assess multidimensional trait empathy, and self-reported their highest completed educational degree and their yearly household income. The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Commission of the University of Barcelona. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

We first performed partial correlations in the whole sample to examine associations between SES-related variables (education and income) and empathy-related variables (VPU and IRI-derived measures), controlling for group using a single dummy-coded covariate (healthy controls = 0, patients = 1). Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Functional MRI data were acquired during the task on a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma Fit 3T scanner using a 64-channel head coil. T2*-weighted functional images were collected with a multiband-accelerated echo-planar imaging sequence optimized for BOLD contrast (TR: 800 ms, TE: 37 ms, flip angle: 45°, matrix: 64×64, voxel size: 3.3×3.3×3.5 mm, 48 axial slices, multiband acceleration factor: 4). Data preprocessing was performed using the CONN toolbox (v23) running on MATLAB R2025a in a BIDS-compliant workflow. The first 12 volumes of each functional run were discarded to allow stabilization of magnetization. Preprocessing included realignment and unwarping, centering, ART-based outlier detection (framewise displacement > .9 mm or global signal change > 5 SD), normalization to MNI space using CONN’s functional-only segmentation procedure, and spatial smoothing with a 6-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. All preprocessing outputs were visually inspected prior to statistical modeling.

Regarding brain imaging, first-level analyses were conducted in SPM12 running on MATLAB R2025a. For each participant, we modeled BOLD responses to two task events: pain images and rating periods. Regressors were convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function, and a high-pass filter of 180 seconds was applied to remove low-frequency drifts. Motion-related confounders were modelled as subject-specific nuisance regressors containing FD values and motion outliers derived from ART (volumes with framewise displacement > .9 mm and/or global signal changes > 5 SD), with contiguous motion outliers clustered into single regressors to avoid over-parameterization. Contrast images were computed for task activation (vicarious pain > baseline) and carried forward to group-level analyses. At the second level, socioeconomic variables (education and income) were entered as regressors in separate multiple regression models, controlling for group. Statistical significance was set as voxel-wise p < .001 and cluster-level family-wise error correction at p < .05. Mean beta values were extracted from each significant cluster. We then assessed the association between brain activation in these SES-linked regions and empathy-related variables, including VPU and IRI-derived trait measures in the whole sample controlling per group and also within each group.

Results

In the sample of 144 adult women, higher educational attainment was associated with reduced vicarious pain unpleasantness (r = -.17, p = .04) and lower empathic personal distress (r = -.28, p = .001). In contrast, yearly household income was not significantly associated with vicarious pain unpleasantness nor with any of the IRI subscales. Importantly, these correlations did not differ between the clinical and non-clinical group (all difference p’s > .45).

In the whole sample, education also predicted increased brain activation during vicarious pain in the midcingulate cortex  (T = 4.58, pFWE-corr = .001) and posterior cingulate cortex/precuneus (T = 4.59, pFWE-corr = .049), the left temporoparietal junction/angular gyrus (T = 4.44, pFWE-corr < .001) and the left inferior temporal gyrus (T = 4.26, pFWE-corr = .048) (Figure 2). These associations were highly consistent across the clinical and non-clinical groups. Correlations between education level and increased brain activation in these areas during vicarious pain (i.e., beta values) were significant in both healthy controls (all r’s > .33, all p’s < .01) and clinical participants (all r’s > .28, all p’s < .01), with no reliable group differences (all difference p’s > .22). We observed additional associations between the activation of some of these regions during vicarious pain and empathy-related variables. In the whole sample, activation in the midcingulate cortex was negatively associated with vicarious pain unpleasantness during the task (r = -.18, p = .04), whereas activation in the posterior cingulate cortex/precuneus was negatively associated with empathic personal distress (r = -.21, p = .01) as measured with the IRI. In contrast, yearly household income was not associated with brain activation during the vicarious pain task.

Conclusion

The findings identify education as a specific socioeconomic predictor of social-affective brain function that may operate independently of clinical diagnosis. In this sample, higher educational attainment was associated with lower emotional reactivity to others pain, reflected in reduced unpleasantness during the task and lower trait personal distress. In contrast, yearly household income showed no meaningful association with empathy related responses. This dissociation suggests that it may be useful to separate educational and material components of socioeconomic status when studying social-emotional functioning5. Education seems to be more closely linked to internal social cognitive resources, while income may reflect external living conditions that have a weaker impact on empathic processing in adulthood.

At the brain level, education was associated with stronger activation of regions that are consistently implicated in social cognition and self/other processing, including the midcingulate cortex, posterior cingulate cortex/precuneus, temporoparietal junction/angular gyrus, and inferior temporal gyrus (Figure 2). These regions support processes such as mentalizing, perspective taking, appraisal and emotional regulation when observing others3,4. The associations were stable across healthy controls and clinical groups, indicating that educational influences on vicarious pain processing may be present regardless of whether a person lives with chronic pain or mood symptoms. Brain activation within some of these regions (i.e, midcingulate and posterior cingulate cortices) also related to behavioral measures of empathy, suggesting that higher activity may reflect more adaptive engagement of social cognitive systems in response to vicarious pain.

Overall, these findings point to education as a potential resilience factor in social-affective contexts. Educational experience may involve repeated exposure to diverse ideas, social reasoning and problem solving, which could promote cognitive flexibility and support regulation of affective responses to others suffering. The present results also raise important conceptual questions about the pathways through which socioeconomic factors influence brain function. Rather than assuming that all socioeconomic indicators operate through similar neural processes, it may be more accurate to consider the specific learning environments, cognitive demands and interpersonal experiences associated with different socioeconomic variables.

From a translational perspective, these results suggest that educational opportunities, cognitive enrichment and lifelong learning may represent promising targets to support social-affective functioning in both healthy and clinical populations. Interventions that strengthen mentalizing skills might help individuals regulate empathic responses in everyday life, especially in conditions involving chronic pain or emotional vulnerability. Future research should explore longitudinal designs to determine whether changes in education or cognitive enrichment led to corresponding changes in brain responses to others pain, and whether similar patterns appear in more diverse samples that vary in gender, cultural background and socioeconomic context.
