Introduction

Sleep consists of neurologically distinct stages. Based on electroencephalography (EEG) and physiological measures, a sleep cycle progresses through three non–rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep (N1, N2, N3) and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep: typically, it begins with N1, transitions to light sleep (N2), which constitutes the largest portion of the night, and then moves to deep slow-wave sleep (N3) before returning to N2 and/or entering REM sleep.1

Each sleep stage is thought to be associated with different cognitive roles: in N1, the brain begins to process memories for further consolidation in the following NREM stages; during N2, selected memories start to stabilize and strengthen with brief bursts of brain activity (sleep spindles); in N3, memories are further consolidated, and information in the hippocampus is thought to be gradually integrated into the cortical areas for long-term storages through slow oscillatory activity; finally, REM sleep is often linked with emotional memory processing and the integration of experiences into broader knowledge structures.2

Despite such well-established stage-specific knowledge about sleep, whole-brain collective neural dynamics during each stage remains poorly understood. Here, to address this, we apply the energy landscape analysis to publicly shared whole-brain fMRI data recoded during sleep and investigate how the collective brain dynamics change between distinct sleep stages.3,4

Methods

We analyzed open fMRI data obtained during sleep from the OpenNeuro repository (ds005127).5 This dataset included 12 healthy young adults (aged 18–35 years; 8 females), who completed two nights of overnight scanning. Functional MRI images were acquired on a 3 T Siemens Prisma scanner using a T2-weighted echo-planar imaging sequence (TR = 3 s, TE = 36 ms, 2.5 mm3). EEG data were simultaneously recorded during the fMRI acquisition using MR-compatible EEG caps and amplifiers (Brain Products) with 61 scalp electrodes plus electro-oculography, electrocardiography, and electromyography channels (impedances < 20 kΩ). This EEG dataset was used to score sleep stages for each 30-second epoch based on standard AASM criteria. These scores were provided in the original dataset.

To investigate collective brain dynamics during sleep, we applied energy landscape analysis to the fMRI data in the same manner as in our previous resting-state fMRI studies.3 Essentially, the fMRI data underwent realignment, slice-timing correction, spatial normalisation to the MNI space and spatial smoothing. Afterwards, we removed the confounding effects of head motion and signals of the white matter and cerebrospinal fluid. Next, we extracted time series data from 264 regions of interest (ROIs) and performed despiking and temporal band-pass filtering (0.01- 0.1 Hz). The ROI signals were then averaged within seven large-scale functional networks (Figure 1A) - the default mode, frontoparietal, salience, attention, somatosensory and motor, visual, and auditory network. Finally, we conducted spatial normalisation and temporal normalisation before binarizing the signals (+1 or -1) using the network-wise temporal averages as the thresholds. We categorized these network-wise fMRI signals based on EEG-based sleep stages, which were concatenated in the recording.

To calculate brain dynamics during each sleep state, a pairwise maximum entropy model (MEM) was then fitted separately to the data classified into each stage. Based on the fitted model, we calculated energy values for all the possible activity patterns and identified energy landscape structures (disconnectivity graphs) with local minima. As in our previous resting-state fMRI studies, we labeled the two most stable (i.e., the two local minima with the lowest energy values) as major states and the others as minor states.3

To quantify the dynamic movements among these states, we conducted a random-walk simulation on the hypothetical energy landscape using a Markov chain Monte Carlo method with the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm. For each sleep stage, random walks were initiated from random starting patterns and simulated for 10000 steps across 1000 trials, with an initial burn-in period discarded. From the resulting trajectories, we estimated dwell times within each basin and transition probabilities between different basins. 

Results

The MEM fitting accuracy was high across all sleep stages (all rD > 0.9), indicating that the MEM provided a reliable approximation of the observed network dynamics. Across all the sleep stages (N1, N2, N3, REM), energy landscape analysis consistently identified four recurrent basins, including two major states and two minor states. These basins showed highly similar network activity patterns across stages, indicating a stable set of canonical whole-brain states that persist throughout overnight sleep (see Figure 1A for the disconnectivity graphs demonstrating basins’ energy levels; see Figure 1B for the basin activity patterns for every stage; see Figure 1C for dwell times derived from random walk simulations; see Figure 1D for transitional frequencies between basins).

The most stable major basin (Major state 1) was characterized by strong co-activation of frontoparietal, salience, and attention networks, with moderate engagement of the default mode network and low sensory network activity (i.e., visual, auditory, somatosensory and motor networks). The second major basin (Major state 2), complementary to the first. These configurations indicated the segregation between the top-down control networks and sensory processing during sleep. Consistent with the stable energy values, the major states showed prolonged dwell times, that were also modulated by sleep stages. In particular, dwell in the major states remained elevated from N1 through N3 and increased further in REM sleep.

In addition to the major states, two minor states were consistently observed across all the sleep stages. The first minor state (Minor state 1) was characterized by higher default mode network engagement and reduced activation across control and sensory networks. The second minor state (Minor 2) showed the opposite pattern. Compared with the two major states, these minor states were unique in their decoupling between the default mode network and sensory-related networks. In term of temporal stability, the minor states showed minimal variation in dwell time across sleep stages, remaining close to baseline levels.

Beyond these four states shared across all the sleep stages, N2 and REM demonstrated stage-specific minor states. During N2 sleep, two additional minor states emerged. One was only present in N2 sleep, characterized by strong default mode network, auditory, and sensorimotor activation, moderate attention engagement, and suppressed visual and salience activity (Minor 4). The other state that emerged in N2 and REM was characterized by activation of the default mode network, sensorimotor, auditory activation, moderate attention and frontoparietal networks, alongside relatively weak activation of visual and salience networks (Minor 3). These two minor states showed similar dwelling time as the other minor states.

Finally, random walk results revealed the indirect transitions between major states (via minor states) were relatively similar across wake and sleep stages, with only a slight increase during N2 and N3, compared to the other stages. In contrast, direct transitions dropped in N2, suggesting that state switching in N2 is less likely to occur as direct major-to-major jumps and instead shifts toward indirect trajectories through intermediate states, which likely was due to the emergence of stage-specific transitional minor states (Figure 1D).

Conclusion

Together, these findings demonstrate that a core set of canonical whole-brain states was preserved across all the sleep stages. However, as sleep deepened, the stability of these common states varied. In particular, the direct transition frequency between two major states showed a significant reduction in N2, and the overall stability of brain dynamics was enhanced during the stage. Such unique brain dynamics may support N2-specific cognitive functions, such as consolidation of selected memory. 

Our results report whole-brain collective neural dynamics in different sleep stages. This framework motivates future work examining how stage-specific brain states are directly linked to learning and memory consolidation outcomes.
