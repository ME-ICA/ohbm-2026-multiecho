Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) often begins during late childhood and adolescence and is associated with recurrent episodes, chronic functional impairment, and substantial public health burden. Early risk stratification requires understanding how familial vulnerability and depressive symptoms alter large-scale functional connectivity (FC). Depressive genetic risk and symptoms are critical factors for children developing into MDD. However, previous studies targeted specific brain regions, neglecting whole-brain FC and its neural mechanisms. Current evidence remains insufficient to disentangle the distinct and shared FC alterations arising from genetic risk versus depressive symptoms, and how brain patterns map onto behavioral and cognitive difficulties.

To address these questions, we investigated whole-brain FC alterations and associations with behavior and cognition in children grouped into four by genetic risk and symptoms. Leveraging a large multisite cohort with harmonized imaging and clinical data, the present study aimed to (1) characterize whole-brain FC differences across children stratified by depressive genetic risk and symptoms, and (2) identify multivariate behavior-FC and cognition-FC associations that reveal early vulnerability markers.

Methods

We included 2,206 children from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study, using linear mixed-effects models to assess FC differences and sparse canonical correlation analysis to explore behavior/cognition–FC associations. Participants were categorized into four groups: high-risk with symptoms (HR-DS), high-risk without symptoms (HR-HC), low-risk with symptoms (LR-DS), and low-risk healthy controls (LR-HC).

Missing data were imputed, and resting-state fMRI underwent distortion correction, registration, motion censoring, global signal regression, and temporal filtering. FC matrices were generated using 352 regions of interest (ROIs), including 333 ROIs from Gordon atlas and 19 subcortical and cerebellar regions. Then we calculated Fisher-z transformation to form zFC.

Linear mixed-effects models included child age, sex at birth, race/ethnicity, pubertal status, parental education, parental marital status, family annual income, and mean framewise displacement (FD) as fixed effects, with family ID nested within MRI scanner as a random effect to account for both scanner-related variability and non-independence among siblings. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) based on linear mixed-effects models was first used to test overall FC differences among the four groups (HR-DS, HR-HC, LR-DS, LR-HC). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were then restricted to FCs that showed significant omnibus ANOVA effects, examining HR-DS, LR-DS, and HR-HC each versus LR-HC, as well as HR-DS versus LR-DS and HR-DS versus HR-HC. To further probe independent effects of genetic risk and depressive symptoms with larger effective sample sizes, we additionally compared an HR group (HR-DS and HR-HC) with an LR group (LR-DS and LR-HC) while including DS status as a fixed-effect covariate, and compared a DS group (HR-DS and LR-DS) with a non-DS group (HR-HC and LR-HC) while including HR status as a fixed-effect covariate. All FC group comparisons were corrected for multiple testing using a false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, with PFDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant. To examine multivariate brain–behavior/cognition associations, we applied a two-step pipeline in the HR and DS groups. First, based on the high dimensionality of the FC matrices (61,776 edges), we used the ReliefF algorithm on covariate-residualized FC data to rank all connections and retained the top 1% positively weighted features in each group (308 FCs in the HR group and 254 FCs in the DS group) for subsequent analysis. Second, sparse canonical correlation analysis (sCCA) was performed separately in HR and DS groups to construct behavior–FC and cognition–FC models, aiming to identify multivariate associations between functional features and clinical features. Behavioral variables included eight syndrome scales from the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL): anxiety/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, somatic complaints, attention problems, social problems, thought problems, aggressive behavior, and rule-breaking behavior. Cognitive measures were derived from the NIH Toolbox and covered attention, executive function, processing speed, language, episodic memory, and working memory, allowing us to probe shared and distinct FC patterns linked to behavioral and cognitive deficits in HR and DS children.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics differed across the four groups. Compared with LR-HC children, the HR-DS group showed higher social and internalizing scores and poorer episodic and working memory, while age and head motion did not differ significantly among groups.

ANOVA identified 16 FCs differing among groups (PFDR < 0.05), mainly within the default mode network (DMN), between the DMN and temporal regions, and within the temporal gyrus. Post-hoc comparisons showed that, relative to LR-HC, the HR-DS group exhibited abnormalities in all 16 FCs, with a comparable number of increased and decreased connections. The increased FCs in HR-DS were mainly located within the DMN, between the DMN and the dorsal attention network (DAN), between the DMN and the temporal gyrus, within the temporal gyrus, and between the salience network (SN) and the cerebellum, whereas the decreased FCs were observed within the DMN, between the DMN and the sensorimotor network (SMN), and within/between the temporal lobe and SMN. These patterns indicate that co-occurring genetic risk and depressive symptoms are associated with the broadest disruptions in DMN-centered and temporal circuits, and that FC alterations in DMN, which is involved in self-referential processing, may underlie difficulties in self-evaluation and persistent self-focused rumination.

To further disentangle the independent effects of genetic risk and depressive symptoms, we collapsed groups by factor. At the same level of depressive symptoms, the HR group showed relatively limited FC changes compared with the LR group, characterized by increased FC between the middle and inferior temporal gyri (MTG–ITG) and decreased FC between the right temporal pole and left paracentral lobule (PCL); these two connections also remained significant in symptom-matched contrasts (HR-DS vs LR-DS and HR-HC vs LR-HC), suggesting a robust effect of genetic risk on temporal lobe–related FC. In contrast, when groups collapsed by depressive symptoms, the DS group showed 15 significant FC differences compared with the non-DS group, largely overlapping with the HR-DS vs HR-HC and LR-DS vs LR-HC results, indicating that depressive symptoms are associated with more extensive whole-brain FC alterations than genetic risk alone.

sCCA revealed multivariate associations between FC and social, internalizing, and memory. In the HR group, social problems were mainly linked to frontoparietal network (FPN) FC, with a prominent negative association between social problems and FPN–SMN FC, whereas internalizing problems involved SMN connectivity with CON, FPN, and attention networks. In the DS group, both social and internalizing problems were primarily anchored in DMN-centered FC, with positive loadings for intra-DMN and DMN–subcortical/DMN–auditory connections and negative loadings for FPN–SMN and FPN–auditory FC. Across both HR and DS groups, social problems consistently showed negative associations with FPN–SMN FC, and internalizing problems were related to CON–VAN FC with opposite directions of loading in the two groups.

For cognition, working memory and episodic memory in both HR and DS groups were associated with FC involving the DMN, FPN, and CON, with a consistent negative relationship between memory scores and DMN–CON FC. Both groups exhibited FC patterns associated with memory. DMN hyperconnectivity may reflect persistent self-reflection, while hypoconnectivity corresponds to episodic memory deficits. The FPN, which supports working memory and declarative memory, was disrupted in both groups, potentially impairing executive control and facilitating repetitive negative thinking. Taken together, these multivariate results suggest that shared behavioral and cognitive difficulties in HR and DS children are underpinned by partly overlapping but also distinct FC configurations across DMN, FPN, CON, SMN, and temporal networks.

Conclusion

This study used linear mixed-effects models to evaluate functional connectivity (FC) patterns in children with different combinations of familial genetic risk and depressive symptoms, and to further investigate the neural mechanisms underlying how genetic risk and symptoms alter brain function. We identified 16 significant FC abnormalities across the four groups, mainly involving within-DMN connections, connections between the DMN and the temporal lobe, and connections within the temporal lobe. When groups were collapsed to examine a single factor, we found that the influence of genetic risk on children’s brain function was relatively limited, whereas children with depressive symptoms showed much more extensive FC abnormalities. This finding was further supported by post-hoc comparisons performed under matched levels of genetic risk or depressive symptoms. In DS children, widespread FC alterations primarily involved regions related to the DMN and the temporal lobe, which largely overlap with key nodes of the theory-of-mind network, suggesting the presence of emotional–cognitive bias, particularly reflected in abnormal processing of self-related mental states.

In addition, we identified the typical associations between FC alterations and behavioral problems and cognitive function in children with high genetic risk or depressive symptoms by applying sCCA. Results showed that both HR and DS children exhibited FC patterns associated with social problems, internalizing problems, and memory function, and these patterns were significantly associated with both shared and distinct FC features across the two groups. These findings provide multivariate insights into the behavioral and cognitive deficits observed in children with genetic risk or depressive symptoms and their corresponding brain functional profiles. This study highlights depressive risk as a potential MRI biomarker of vulnerability and demonstrates the widespread impact of depressive symptoms on brain function in children. Furthermore, by advancing understanding of the neurobiological mechanisms underlying behavioral and cognitive difficulties in children with genetic risk or depressive symptoms, our results may contribute to early screening of depression in high-risk children and support the development of targeted interventions focused on behavioral and cognitive impairments.
