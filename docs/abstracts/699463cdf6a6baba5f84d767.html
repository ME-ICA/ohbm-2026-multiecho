Introduction

Understanding how resting-state EEG (rsEEG) reflects individual differences in cognition is a key step towards identifying neural markers of cognitive efficiency in healthy adults. A set of rsEEG characteristics, including oscillatory power, connectivity measures, and aperiodic dynamics, has been proposed as potential biomarkers. However, it remains unclear which features are most sensitive to specific cognitive domains. Furthermore, cognitive performance in healthy middle-aged individuals shows clear interindividual variability, while the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying these differences are not well understood.^1

In this study, we examined the relationship between rsEEG and cognition in a cohort from the Barcelona Brain Health Initiative (BBHI), a community-based project on brain health in older adults. This analysis focused on a set of rsEEG features from functionally and anatomically predefined cortical regions. Our goal was to identify interpretable neural markers that could add value to future predictive models of cognitive functioning in normative aging.

Methods

Participants were healthy middle-aged adults from the BBHI (N = 126; mean age 53.9 ± 7.1 years; 45% women).^2

Resting-state EEG was recorded using an ActiChamp system (Brain Products GmbH, Munich, Germany) with 64 active electrodes mounted on an ActiCAP slim cap and positioned according to the international 10–20 system. The ground electrode was placed at Fpz, and signals were referenced online to AFz. Electrode impedances were kept below 5 kΩ. For the present analyses, only the eyes-closed resting-state condition was considered.

EEG data were filtered, downsampled, re-referenced to the average, and cleaned by ICA-based artifact rejection. Power spectral density was estimated using Welch’s method with a 0.5-second Hamming window, 50% overlap, and a frequency resolution of 0.5 Hz. The resulting spectra were parameterized using the FOOOF Python package to separate periodic oscillatory components from the aperiodic background activity. For connectivity analysis, amplitude envelope correlations were computed in the theta, alpha, and beta bands, from which functional connectivity node strength was derived.

From these calculations, a set of resting-state EEG features was obtained. These feature categories included relative power in five spectral bands, aperiodic offset and exponent, and node strength in the three frequency bands mentioned above. All features were averaged within five commonly accepted regions of interest (ROIs): frontal, central, parietal, and occipital; as a result, we obtained a total of 50 candidate predictors.

Cognitive performance was grouped into four domains based on a total of 15 neuropsychological tests: memory (RAVLT immediate recall, RAVLT delayed recall, RAVLT recognition, WAIS-IV digit span forward, Corsi block tapping), executive function (WAIS-IV digit span backward, Number–letter sequencing, Trail making Test B, Phonemic verbal fluency, Spatial updating), reasoning (WAIS-IV matrix reasoning, Block design), and processing speed/attention (WAIS-IV digit symbol substitution, Cancellation test, Numerical speed false alarms). All test scores were converted into z-scores before aggregation within each domain.

Statistical analysis followed a stepwise regression approach (stepwiselm, MATLAB). For each cognitive domain, we fitted a model, which included age and sex as covariates, with all rsEEG measures. The stepwise algorithm (penter = 0.05, premove = 0.10) identified the most parsimonious subset of predictors that best explained each domain. This allowed us to determine which rsEEG features contributed unique variance beyond demographic factors.

Results

Regression analyses revealed differences between cognitive domains. In terms of memory and processing speed/attention, performance was explained exclusively by age.

The results were more relevant for executive function, where the final model explained a proportion of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.18). Three rsEEG measures were retained: relative frontocentral delta power showed a positive association with executive performance (β = 0.023, p = 0.004), while frontal gamma power (β = −0.074, p = 0.002) and occipital theta connectivity (β = −2.51, p = 0.013) were negatively associated. Therefore, better executive performance was characterized by higher frontocentral delta power, lower frontal gamma power, and lower occipital theta band connectivity.

The associations observed for delta and gamma appear to be consistent with the previous literature linking slow oscillatory activity to top-down control and large-scale regulatory processes, and increased gamma at rest with greater neural noise or basal hyperexcitability.^3 However, the finding related to delta should be interpreted with caution, as delta band activity is also related to neurophysiological processes in healthy adults.^4

Occipital theta connectivity showed a negative association with executive performance. Lower theta band connectivity in posterior regions has previously been linked to higher levels of alertness, clearer alpha rhythm dominance, and greater cortical efficiency in adults.

In the reasoning domain, only central aperiodic offset remained in the final model (β = 0.84, p = 0.001). This model showed the best overall fit (adjusted R² = 0.27). From a physiological perspective, higher offset values have been associated with greater basal neural activity or greater availability of cortical resources, which may favor performance on reasoning tasks.^5

Across domains, rsEEG predictors did not generalize uniformly. This dissociation suggests that different cognitive domains are supported by partially distinct neurophysiological signatures, supporting the idea that a suite of rsEEG markers is needed to capture cognitive performance across all domains. 

Conclusion

This study identified a set of resting-state EEG characteristics that allow tracking of interindividual differences in cognition in healthy middle-aged adults. Specifically, frontocentral delta relative power, frontal gamma relative power, and occipital theta connectivity were associated with executive function, while central offset emerged as a predictor of reasoning. 

In the executive domain, greater frontocentral delta power and lower frontal gamma power were associated with better performance. In this context, increased delta power may reflect more effective top-down control, while reduced gamma power may indicate a more regulated state of basal excitability. Lower occipital theta connectivity was also associated with better executive performance, which could reflect that greater local coupling or synchronization in this lower frequency band during rest could reflect a state of reduced cortical excitation; however, this effect should be interpreted with caution.

Regarding reasoning, the association with central aperiodic offset is promising, although it requires further investigation. The offset parameter has been interpreted as an index of overall neural resource availability. Greater offset in central regions, which encompass cortices that act as integration centers, may reflect a system with greater basal readiness. In this framework, increased central offset could favor reasoning tasks by facilitating efficient information transfer. 

Although rsEEG measures explain a proportion of the variance in executive function and reasoning, their physiologically interpretable associations highlight their potential translational value. Taken together, these characteristics may contribute significantly to the creation of models aimed at monitoring cognitive trajectories, identifying early deviations from normative aging, and supporting individualized longitudinal assessment.
