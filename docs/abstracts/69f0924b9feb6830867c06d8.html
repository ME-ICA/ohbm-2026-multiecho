Introduction

The neurochemical architecture of the brain emerges from the intricate interplay between neurotransmitters and their receptors. Hereby different receptor types differentially influence neuronal activity levels mediating neuronal circuit dynamics.  At larger scales, receptors influence cortical and subcortical information processing, supporting cognitive functions. Receptor distributions are not uniform, their densities and types vary across synapses, cortical layers and areas (i.e., its receptor fingerprint). This organization delineates cortical and subcortical boundaries and distinguishes areas associated with distinct functional systems1. Balance between receptor systems is thus critical for healthy brain function.

Despite advances in mapping structural and functional organization, major gaps remain in our understanding of their neurochemical underpinnings. Whole-brain receptor mapping is limited as it is challenging to capture an integrated picture of multiple receptor systems simultaneously. To address this gap, this project integrates macaque whole-brain multi-receptor distribution using a trade-off analysis framework based on Pareto Task Inference (ParTI)2.

The trade-off concept is based on the idea that biological systems, shaped by evolution, often exhibit observable phenotypic trade-offs as they optimize competing objectives, e.g., minimizing wiring cost while maximizing network efficiency. ParTI models such trade-offs by fitting the data into a low-dimensional polytope, whose vertices represent archetypes (Arch), points of extreme specialization. All other data points are modeled as convex mixtures of them, reflecting trade-off. This framework has revealed key organizational principles ranging from morphology to gene expression2. By applying ParTI to receptor architectures for the first time, we aim to identify fundamental organizational principles and trade-offs that structure receptor fingerprint balance across the macaque brain.

Methods

We used 14 averaged 3D-reconstructed volumes from 2D-receptor autoradiography images in macaque, including glutamatergic, GABAergic, cholinergic, adrenergic and serotonergic receptor types3. After each receptor volume was voxel-wise min-max normalized, receptor densities were extracted and averaged within each of the 246 cortical regions of the MEBRAINS atlas4, yielding a 14x246 receptor-by-region matrix (Fig1.A). This matrix was first projected into a lower-dimensional space using Principal Component Analysis. The optimal number of Arch was determined with Explained Simplex Variance method. A minimum-volume simplex was then fitted using Principal Convex Hull Analysis, estimating Arch as the polytope vertices representing the most extreme receptor fingerprints, with all cortical regions expressed as convex mixtures of these Arch (Fig1.B). Geometric significance was assessed using the t-ratio (simplex vs. convex hull volume), and permutation-based null models. 

To interpret the polytope, we generated Arch distance maps by computing, for each Arch, the similarity measure (inverted Euclidean distance) from each cortical region to each Arch. Arch maps allow to identify which cortical regions lie closest to each Arch, i.e., specialist and which regions fall between them, i.e., generalist. A Specialist-Generalist (Spe-Gen) map was also generated by computing, for each region, its maximum similarity to any Arch, distinguishing specialist from generalist regions. Enrichment analyses were then performed by correlating Arch maps with receptor maps to determine the multi-receptor pattern underlying Arch specialization (Spearman ρ and Spin-tests for significance). Finally, we examine the large-scale resting-state networks (RSN)5 associated with specialist and generalist regions. Differences between RSN were quantified using Cohen’s d effect sizes and permutation tests. 

Results

ParTI significantly fit the receptor data into a tetrahedron defined by four Arch (Fig 2.A). Arch1 and Arch2 were closer with regions located in the visual and premotor cortices respectively (e.g., higher visual area V6l for Arch1 and premotor cortex area F4s for Arch2; Fig 2.B), whereas Arch3 and Arch4 captured associative regions located in the cingulate and prefrontal cortices (e.g., anterior cingulate area 24b for Arch3; dorsolateral prefrontal area a46df for Arch4; Fig 2.B). These specialist regions are also represented in yellow on the Spe-Gen map (Fig 2.B). Correlation analyses between Arch maps and receptor maps revealed significant complementary patterns: Arch1 and Arch2 maps were negatively correlated with most receptor maps (ρ ranging from -0.94 to -0.58) while Arch3 and Arch4 maps were positively correlated (ρ ranging from 0.94 to 0.52). Note that nicotinic receptor did not correlate with any Arch, 5-HT₂ correlated with Arch2 and Arch3 and M2 correlated exclusively with Arch1. Accordingly, regions closest to Arch1 and Arch2 exhibited low receptor density across most receptor types whereas regions closest to Arch3 and Arch4 showed the opposite trend, with consistently higher receptor densities (Fig 2.C, e.g., AMPA). Functional interpretation revealed a significant dissociation between externally oriented networks (Somatomotor and Visual) and internally oriented networks (Default Mode, Limbic), aligning respectively with the Arch1/Arch2 and Arch3/Arch4 (Fig 2.D, e.g., Arch1 and 3). Arch2, Arch3 and Arch4 also presented a significant difference between Somatomotor and Visual networks.

Conclusion

ParTI’s tetrahedral geometry reveals that macaque multi-receptor architecture is structured around 4 archetypes. This organization distinguishes externally oriented unimodal regions, which have a relatively lower neurochemically plasticity, from internally oriented associative regions that display a higher neurochemically plasticity. These findings illustrate how receptor architecture underpins the hierarchical organization of cortical information processing and provides a mechanistic link between molecular composition and functional network specialization.
