Introduction

Social touch is an underappreciated regulatory behavior that contributes to social bonding. Apart from previous research on slow, gentle stroking, whether additional sensory factors contribute to the affective experience of social touch or the underlying neural mechanisms is unknown. Based on animal models of social bonding, temperature and pressure are two primary sensory components of social touch that promote social bonding behavior and thus may be under-appreciated contributors to social touch in humans. Indeed, previous research in humans suggests the temperature and relative pressure of touch—specifically warm, deeper forms of pressure, such as those found during hand holding, hugging, and swaddling—may enhance socioemotional experience. Additionally, although the neural mechanisms that link warmth, pressure, and social context remain poorly understood, there is evidence that warmth and pressure might share a common neural pathway, focused especially on the ventral striatum (VS) and middle-insula (MI). Both regions are involved in reward, emotion, and the processing of interoceptively generated feelings, and share anatomical connections with regions that contribute to pleasant affective experience.1 Given findings that warmth and pressure share similar emotional effects and neural mechanisms, and that they are intrinsically linked in regulatory social behaviors like huddling and kangaroo care, their combination might enhance affective experience in the context of close others. Specifically, warmth and pressure together may have greater or cumulative impacts on neural (e.g., VS and MI) and affiliative emotions compared to each of the two alone. The current study investigates discrete components of social touch to examine how temperature, pressure, and social context alter affective experience and neural activity, and to explore a possible novel sensory-affective pathway underlying social bonding.

Methods

Participants

66 participants completed the study. One participant was excluded from all analyses due to incomplete and low-quality data leaving a final analytic sample of 65 participants (M age = 21.37, 75.4% women). For the self-report data only, an additional three participants were excluded. 

 

Sensory Task

MRI scans took place in a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma with a 30-channel head coil. In the scanner, participants completed a Sensory Task in which they viewed provided images of close others and gender-, age- and race-presentation matched strangers under four different combinations of temperature (warm, neutral) and pressure (deeper, lighter). Temperature was manipulated using instant hot packs (21.11°C to 22.61°C range, M = 21.48°C, SD = .453°C). Packs were deactivated (room temperature) for the neutral condition. Pressure was manipulated with weighted blankets that differed only by weight; a 20 lb blanket was used for deeper pressure and a 3 lb blanket was used for lighter pressure. 

Temperature packs were placed on the dorsal left forearm and the blanket was draped up to the participant’s chest. Two pictures were presented back-to-back of either a close other or a stranger followed by a break during which the pack and blanket were removed. Participants completed 32 trials over 2 runs in a randomized order with each of the 4 combinations matched with each of the 4 different close others and 4 strangers. Each run lasted approximately 12 minutes (Figure 1). 

Affiliative Emotions

The focus of post-task ratings of the Sensory task was low arousal, pleasurable emotions that might also foster social connection. Retrospective ratings of emotions felt while under the different combinations of temperature and pressure were completed on a 1 (“Not at all”) to 7 (“Very much so”) Likert scale. For each of the four conditions, participants were asked, “Remember back when you were in the scanner. While you experienced the [e.g., warmer pack and heavier blanket], to what extent did you feel ___?”. Items included still, comfortable, calm, connected to the people in the photos, disconnected from the people in the photos (reversed), secure around the people in the photos, and anxious (reversed; Cronbach’s alphas ranged from .813 - .847). 

 

Data Analysis

Affiliative feelings were analyzed with 2 (temperature: warmth vs. neutral) x 2 (pressure: deeper vs. lighter) repeated measures ANOVA in SPSS v.29, IBM. Follow-up pairwise comparisons assessed feelings to warm deeper pressure compared to each other condition.  

MRI Preprocessing

MRI data were preprocessed using the fMRIPrep pipeline. Analyses were constrained to activity within the VS and MI. Structural ROIs were defined using the Automated Anatomical Labeling Atlas (AAL), and VS and MI ROIs were combined into a single mask. Using MATLAB, parameter estimates were pulled for the specified regions for each condition compared to baseline. Next, repeated measures ANOVAs were run to examine a 3-way interaction among temperature, pressure, and social target within subjects. Repeated measures ANOVAs were then run separately for social target. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted for significant interactions. Last, Pearson correlations between the VS-MI parameter estimates and affiliative feelings were calculated.

Connectivity Analyses

Analyses of fMRI data were performed using the CONN toolbox following standard procedures. Functional connectivity (FC) between the VS and MI ROIs was estimated. FC strength was represented by Fisher-transformed bivariate correlation coefficients from a weighted general linear model, modeling the association between their BOLD signal timeseries. Two-tailed paired samples t-tests were used to assess differences in Fisher z-transformed functional connectivity values between the warm deeper pressure condition and all other conditions. Paired t-tests compared VS-MI connectivity in the warm, deeper pressure to close others condition to that in each other condition.

Results

Manipulation Checks, Relationship Demographics, and Closeness Ratings

As intended, the warm packs used throughout the experiment were warmer than the neutral packs (t(64) = 124.82, p < .001, 95% CI: 30.88, 31.83). The warm packs ranged from 39.42°C to 44.90°C (M = 42.24°C, SD = 2.09°C) and the neutral packs ranged from 23.22°C to 27.17°C (M = 24.82°C, SD = 1.51°C).       

Submitted pictures of close others used in the Sensory Task included parents, siblings, grandparents, cousins, friends, romantic partners or spouses, and roommates. The average ratings of closeness toward the close others ranged from 7.50 and 10 (M = 9.058, SD = 0.586) with higher ratings indicating greater closeness. The amount of time individuals reported knowing their close others ranged between 7 months and 30.145 years (M = 148.837 months (12.40 years), SD = 74.188 months (6.18 years)). 

Affiliative emotions to warm pressure

Temperature (warm vs. neutral) pressure (deeper vs. lighter) repeated measures ANOVA assessed the effect of warm deeper pressure on affiliative emotions to the Sensory Task. There were main effects of both temperature (F(1, 61) = 31.285, p < .001) and pressure (F(1, 61) = 29.023, p < .001), with affiliative emotions higher to the warm than neutral temperature and deeper than lighter pressure. Main effects were, however, qualified by a marginal interaction (F(1, 61) = 3.541, p = .065). Given primary hypotheses regarding the combination of warm deeper pressure, the interaction was further interrogated to explore the direction of effects.

Affiliative emotions to the combination of warmth and deeper pressure were higher compared to all other conditions (warm deeper pressure: M = 5.88, SD = .90, warmth: M = 5.32, SD = .87, pressure: M = 5.30, SD = .85, neutral condition: M = 4.88, SD = .91; warm deeper pressure compared to: warm lighter pressure (t(61) = 5.700, p< .001, 95% CI: .294, .824), neutral deeper pressure (t(61) = 5.893, p< .001, 95% CI: .317, .855), neutral lighter pressure (t(61) = 7.765, p < .001, 95% CI: .659, 1.355), Fig. 2A)). Thus, warm deeper pressure increased retrospective ratings of affiliative emotions more than warmth or pressure on their own and compared to the neutral condition.  

VS and MI activity to warm pressure

As hypothesized, a three-way interaction among temperature, pressure, and social target emerged for activity in the VS and MI (F(1, 64) = 4.199, p = .045). Breaking down the interaction by social target revealed a marginal interaction between temperature and pressure for VS and MI activity to viewing images of close others (F(1, 64) = 3.913, p = .052; Fig. 2C). Mirroring the pattern from the affiliative emotions, post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that warm deeper pressure increased neural activity compared to all other conditions (neutral deeper pressure: (t(64) = 2.599, p = .012, CI: -.005, .306), warm lighter pressure: (t(64) = 3.074, p = .003, CI: .023, .339), neutral lighter pressure: (t(64) = 2.869, p = .006, CI: .011, .322). That is, warm pressure, compared to warmth or pressure alone or neutral lighter pressure, increased VS and MI activity to images of close others. There was no interaction between temperature and pressure for neural activity to images of strangers (F(1, 64) = .610, p = .438). Thus, warm deeper pressure selectively increased neural activity to close others, but not strangers. There were no associations between affiliative emotions and VS and MI activity to the task (F(1, 227.8)= 0.004; p = 0.947).

Connectivity between VS and MI during warm pressure 

Connectivity between the VS and MI was higher during warm deeper pressure to close others as compared to neutral lighter pressure to strangers (t(64) = 3.217, p = .002; Fig. 2D). Most other comparisons trended in the same direction but did not reach significance (all p values > .05, except for warm lighter pressure to strangers, p = .053). 

Conclusion

Results suggest a novel sensory-affective pathway underlying the socioemotional processes that support social bonding. Specifically, the combination of warmth and pressure – sensory aspects present in social behaviors such as hugging and cuddling – selectively enhanced ventral striatum and mid-insula activity to close others, but not strangers, and increased affiliative emotions as compared to warmth or pressure presented on their own. Additionally, warm pressure (compared to neutral lighter pressure) increased connectivity of the VS and MI, suggesting there may be stronger coupling of these regions during warm pressure. These findings lend possibility to the existence of a novel sensory-affective pathway in which the VS and MI contribute to both the sensory and emotional components of social bonding. 

Results from the current study illuminate how sensory aspects and categorically different forms of social touch help to flesh out our understanding of the neural pathways involved in processing these sensations as psychologically meaningful. Findings that warmth and pressure together, in the context of close others, have the greatest neural and affective impacts point to the possibility of a ‘neural short-cut’ in which social touch directly initiates warm, close states, bypassing the need to seek physical warmth restoration.2 It is likely that multiple neural pathways work alongside one another to process, facilitate, and maintain social touch and its affective affiliates. For instance, while slow gentle stroking is related to activity in the posterior insula,3 pressure4 and warmth5 activate the mid-insula. It is possible that both forms of pleasant touch (i.e., stroking and warm pressure) converge on the insula—a hub for interoceptive processing—to be processed internally. Furthermore, greater connectivity between the MI and VS during warm pressure to close others (compared to a neutral condition) suggests warm pressure prompts the coupling of interoceptive with salience-related regions. 

This study includes several limitations. Although the current study manipulated warmth using a temperature that matches previous studies, there are individual differences in warm perception. While using a within-subject design controlled for these individual differences, calibrating warmth to each participant in future studies may increase the strength of findings. Additionally, the temperature stimuli were placed on participants’ dorsal forearms to mirror the stimulation site used in prior research on slow stroking and CT-afferents. Warmth during naturalistic social touch like hugging and hand holding stimulates other body sites that were not manipulated in the current study (e.g., hands, chest, shoulders). Future research could investigate different types and locations of stimulation to determine if either factor alters the effects seen here. Finally, the post-task ratings of affiliative emotions were not validated and do not originate from a validated questionnaire or scale. Although the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Chronbach’s α ranged from .813 to .847 across conditions), the pattern of results awaits replication.  

In sum, the current study suggests the combination of warmth and pressure may influence social bonding through a sensory-affective pathway separate from other forms of touch. Furthermore, specificity of the positive impacts of warm pressure toward close others (compared to strangers) support the idea that social touch is important in the maintenance and facilitation of close social bonds. Overall, future research should continue to explore this pathway across other sensory stimuli, different presentations of warmth and deep pressure, and across additional social contexts.
