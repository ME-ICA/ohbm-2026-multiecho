Introduction

Understanding the neurobiological basis of mental health disorders remains a central goal in psychiatric neuroimaging. However, identifying robust brain-psychopathology associations has proven difficult, in part due to substantial heterogeneity within and comorbidity between diagnostic categories. Transdiagnostic bifactor models aim to characterise this structure by separating shared from unique symptom variance, yielding more reliable and potentially more accurate latent dimensions of psychopathology1. However, the extent to which these behavioural models improve the predictive utility of neuroimaging remains largely uncharacterised. Using the Child Behaviour Checklist (CBCL), we systematically investigate whether bifactor-derived factor scores yield higher phenotypic reliability and stronger or more distinct associations with whole-brain structure and function compared to typical CBCL summary scores. To this end, we evaluate the multivariate prediction accuracy and feature weights of summary scores and factor scores from 11 previously published bifactor model solutions2 using the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study (ABCD3).

Methods

We used items from the CBCL to fit 11 bifactor models in the ABCD3 (n = 7526, ages 9-10 at baseline, mean retest interval = 10 months). CBCL items were configured to load on a single general factor and a varying number of specific factors as defined in the literature (Hoffmann et al., 2022). Test-retest reliability was then assessed using correlation, after regressing out participant age, timepoint and their interaction. In order to avoid data leakage resulting from bifactor model fitting on the entire dataset, all 11 models were fit separately on demographically matched samples4 (n=3,525 and n=3,447) in the ABCD using Mplus. Resting-state functional connectivity and cortical thickness were used to predict general and specific factors as well as summary score T-scores. Predictions were performed with linear ridge regression within a nested 2-fold cross-validation scheme using the matched samples. Their significance was tested using a permutation test. Preprocessed resting-state fMRI data and cortical thickness maps were acquired from the ABCD BIDS Community Collection4 and parcellated using the Glasser atlas. Connectivity was then calculated using Pearson correlation between the time series of all parcels. Feature weights (indicating which edges contributed more to predictions) were Haufe-transformed to improve interpretability. To examine the similarity between the Haufe-transformed feature weights for each construct, we calculated Spearman correlation and evaluated their significance using a spin test.

Results

Overall, most constructs could be significantly predicted using functional connectivity (Fig. 1A). The accuracy and reliability of corresponding constructs (e.g., externalising factors vs externalising summary score) were highly similar between summary scores and factor scores (Fig. 1B and C). Cortical thickness showed a similar pattern; however, many factors and summary scores could not be significantly predicted. Connectivity-based predictive models revealed broadly distributed neural signatures that were highly similar between corresponding constructs (Fig. 2A - highlighted diagonal elements) with general factors and total problems approaching numerical equivalence (r = 0.98), likely reflecting their almost perfect phenotypic association (r = 0.94; Fig. 2B - highlighted diagonal). However, the pattern of neural correlates across constructs (e.g., p-factor vs. externalising vs. internalising) was less correlated for factor scores (rho = -0.60 - 0.48; Fig. 2A - bottom right) compared to summary scores (rho = 0.28 - 0.86; Fig. 2A - top left), likely resulting from low phenotypic correlations between factors (Fig. 2B) due to orthogonalisation of general and specific dimensions.

Conclusion

We found no consistent evidence that bifactor-derived factor scores improved reliability or strengthened brain-psychopathology associations relative to standard CBCL summary scores. Across all constructs, feature weights from whole-brain predictive models were broadly distributed across the connectome and consistent with prior theories5, emphasising higher-order networks (default mode, frontoparietal, and cingulo-opercular networks). One potential advantage of bifactor models over summary scores was that the neural patterns across constructs (e.g., p-factor vs. externalising vs. internalising) were more distinct. Overall, these findings suggest that existing CBCL bifactor models of psychopathology do not systematically strengthen the predictive utility of neuroimaging for psychiatry. Rather, fundamental limits in the proportion of symptom variance captured by macro-scale brain features may constrain prediction regardless of the scoring method. While bifactor models may aid in separating neural correlates across constructs, improving phenotypic assessment depth, rather than alternative modelling, may provide more tangible improvements moving forward.
