Introduction

Working memory is responsible for the temporary storage and manipulation of information under attentional control, typically lasting only a few seconds. It is considered essential for a variety of complex mental activities1. However, it can be easily inhibited by things such as emotional information. Emotional information serves as a double-edged sword, which can facilitate cognitive processing, but also can interfere with it. 

Previous studies have demonstrated that the congruency effect, which refers to the facilitation effect, can be observed in the modified Stroop paradigm using the word-face pairs2. Nevertheless, when emotional stimuli are inserted in the maintenance phase of working memory, they serve as emotional distractors, which can elicit the interference effect3.

Emotional words are semantic stimuli characterized by affective connotations, which can be described along two dimensions: valence and arousal. Facial expressions are another common type of emotional stimuli, possessing features of automatic and mandatory processing. Regardless of the educational or workplace context, working memory plays a crucial role in completing tasks. Meanwhile, language with emotional content is typically processed within social contexts, including emotional faces. Therefore, this study is concerned with how the interaction effect of emotional words and emotional faces influences working memory performance.

Additionally, due to the features of limited capacity and brief duration of working memory, it is inevitably associated with cognitive load. Consequently, this study would like to explore whether cognitive load can modulate this interaction effect. Specifically, this study proposes two research questions: (1) How do facial expressions influence emotional word processing within the working memory? (2) Would the effects of facial expressions on emotional word processing be modulated by cognitive load? 

Methods

To address our research questions, we designed a dual-task paradigm called an emotional distraction delayed response working memory task, consisting of a primary word memory task and a secondary face memory task. We recruited a total of 21 college students (mean age = 22.32 ± 3.48 years) as our participants, including 10 males and 11 females. There were 450 two-character Chinese words from the Affective Words System (CAWS), with 150 words each for negative, neutral, and positive. We also selected 60 facial expressions from the Chinese Facial Affective Picture System (CFAPS), comprising 20 angry, neutral, and happy faces. 

We employed the NIRSport 2 recording oxyhemoglobin (HbO) and deoxyhemoglobin (HbR). The system was built using 16 sources and 16 detectors. The light source probe operated at wavelengths of 760 nm and 850 nm, with the recording sampling frequency configured at 10.2Hz. There were 52 channels in our placement, including 44 long channels and 8 short separation channels. 

The experiment was implemented with the software Psychopy (2024.1.3). Each trial began with a fixation cross displayed for 0.5 seconds, followed by a memory set consisting of words categorized into two conditions: low (2 words) and high cognitive load (6 words). Following the words, an inter-stimulus interval (ISI) jittered between 0 and 1 second. Subsequently, a facial expression (angry, neutral, or happy) was then presented during the maintenance phase, requiring participants to memorize it. Another ISI of 0 to 1 second preceded the probe, during which two words were displayed. Participants were instructed to identify which word was presented in the previous memory set by pressing either the left or right key. Following an additional 0-1second ISI, two facial expressions were shown, and participants were required to identify which facial expression was presented earlier during the maintenance phase by pressing the corresponding key.

For behavioral data, due to the high accuracy (ACC for word memory task: 93.87% ± 2.68%; ACC for face memory task: 96.6% ± 1.60%), we only focused on reaction times (RTs). Given the role of the face recognition task in merely ensuring facial‑expression processing during the maintenance phase, only the RTs from the word recognition task were used as the dependent variable. The mean RTs were subjected to a general linear model (GLM). To investigate the main effects and interaction effects of word valence (negative/neutral/positive), cognitive load (low/high), and face valence (angry/neutral/happy) on RTs (dependent variable), we chose Type III Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Significant effects were further analyzed using post hoc tests with Tukey’s HSD and Holm corrections.

For fNIRS data, we consider only HbO as the dependent variable for fNIRS data analysis. fNIRS raw data were preprocessed using the HOMER2 package in MATLAB. All the raw signals were converted to optical density, then corrected for motion artifacts. Next, the optical density data were bandpass filtered between 0.01 and 0.5 Hz. After that, all the optical density data were converted to oxyhemoglobin (HbO) and deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) concentration by using the modified Beer-Lambert law. To effectively reduce physiological interferences in hemodynamic response estimation, we utilize the short separation channels with the highest correction, corresponding to the selected long separation channel, as a regressor. There were 18 conditions, such as the combination of negative words, low cognitive load, and an angry face. The GLM modeled a canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF) based on the onsets and durations of the eighteen conditions, from which standardized beta coefficients were estimated. A three-way repeated-measure ANOVA was conducted to assess potential main and interaction effects. Significant effects were followed up with additional analyses corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method.

Results

For behavioral results, the type III ANOVA revealed significant main effects for word valence (LR χ² (2) = 7.39, p = 0.025), cognitive load (LR χ² (1) = 9.65, p < 0.001), and face valence (LR χ² (2) = 6.18, p = 0.045). However, no two-way and three-way interaction effects were detected. 

For fNIRS results, the three-way repeated-measure ANOVA results conducted on the 18 conditions yielded no significant main effects or three-way interaction effect. However, significant two-way interactions emerged between word valence × cognitive load, cognitive load × face valence, and word valence × face valence in certain channels.

The analysis revealed distinct significant patterns across different channels. Word valence and cognitive load interactions yielded significant effects at Ch8 (right premotor cortex, BA6) and Ch27 (right frontopolar cortex, BA10). Three channels showed significant responses to the word valence and face valence interactions, namely Ch8 (right premotor cortex, BA6), Ch27 (right frontopolar cortex, BA10), and Ch34 (left pars triangularis, BA45). The cognitive load and face valence interaction elicited stronger cortical responses, encompassing six channels, including Ch7 (right primary auditory cortex, BA41/42), Ch8 (right premotor cortex, BA6), Ch27 (right frontopolar cortex, BA10), Ch34 (left pars triangularis, BA45), Ch39 (left premotor cortex, BA6), and Ch48 (left pars opercularis, BA44).

A significant word valence × cognitive load interaction was found in the right premotor cortex (BA6) and right frontopolar cortex (BA10), as shown in Figure 2(b). Under the low cognitive load, positive words elicited decreased activation in the right premotor cortex and right frontopolar cortex, while these regions were strongly activated under the high cognitive load. Statistical comparison revealed significant differences between these two cognitive loads for both the right premotor cortex (t(20) = -2.975, p = 0.0075) and right frontopolar cortex (t(20) = -2.297, p = 0.0326). 

Figure 2(c) shows a significant interaction between word valence and face valence was observed in the right premotor cortex (BA6), right frontopolar cortex (BA10), and left pars triangularis (BA45). The right premotor cortex showed stronger activation during negative word processing when distracted by happy faces than when distracted by angry faces (t(20) = -3.157, p = 0.005). At the same time, relative to negative words, the impact of happy faces on neutral (t(20) = 2.179, p = 0.0415) and positive words (t(20) = 2.793, p = 0.0112) significantly activated the right frontopolar cortex. When positive words were paired with happy faces, compared to angry faces, the left pars triangularis produced greater activation (t(20) = -3.019, p = 0.0068).

As shown in Figure 2(d), a significant interaction between cognitive load and face valence was identified in the right primary auditory cortex (BA41/42), right premotor cortex (BA6), right frontopolar cortex (BA10), left pars triangularis (BA45), left premotor cortex (BA6), and left pars opercularis (BA44). When cognitive load was low, exposure to happy faces, compared to angry faces, resulted in increased activation in the right primary auditory cortex (t(20) = 2.655, p = 0.0152), right premotor cortex (t(20) = 3.225, p = 0.005), and left pars opercularis (t(20) = 3.886, p = 0.0013). Distraction by happy faces under the low cognitive load, as opposed to high cognitive load conditions, was associated with increased activation in the right premotor cortex (t(20) = 2.859, p = 0.0097) and left pars opercularis (t(20) = 2.323, p = 0.0309). During low cognitive load, happy faces also resulted in significantly greater activation in the left premotor cortex compared to angry (t(20) = 2.744, p = 0.0125). Under high cognitive load, happy faces elicited significantly greater activation in the left pars triangularis relative to neutral faces (t(20) = -2.425, p = 0.0249) and angry faces (t(20) = -2.592, p = 0.0174).

Conclusion

One noteworthy pattern is that some regions overlap. The right premotor cortex and right frontopolar cortex are activated in the three two-way interaction effects, suggesting that these regions function as core hubs for coordinating and balancing emotion and cognition. Furthermore, the left pars triangularis is recruited in the interactions involving word valence with face valence and cognitive load with face valence. This involvement indicates that language processing requires additional executive control when confronted with complex emotional stimuli, especially when integrating emotional information from two modalities, words and faces. Taken together, these overlapping activation patterns indicate that the brain relies on a limited set of core regions to handle multiple complex tasks.

In addition, stimuli with positive valence are processed preferentially. Across both low and high cognitive load scenarios, more brain regions are activated by happy faces than by angry faces. Under low load in particular, happy faces additionally recruit regions such as the right primary auditory cortex, bilateral premotor cortex, and left pars opercularis. Even though cognitive load is high, and the overall activation is lower than under low cognitive load, happy faces still elicit greater activation in the left pars triangularis than angry faces. This finding indicates that positive valence engages broader neural networks when cognitive resources are sufficient, supporting deep, cross-modal, embodied processing. Even when the cognitive resources are insufficient, the brain reduces extensive processing but continues to sustain essential word processing. At this stage, the brain may still process the positive valence of happy faces and compare it with the valence of words during encoding. These results also suggest that the modulatory effect of cognitive load is specific to valence.

A further observation is that the left and right hemispheres show specializations during task performance. The right hemisphere mainly shows strong responses under the low cognitive load and positive valence conditions. In contrast, the left hemisphere engages more emotion-language integration processing when cognitive load is high. When the cognitive load is low, the right hemisphere can effectively process positive information, especially the recognition and integration of facial expressions. However, under the high cognitive load conditions, the advantage of processing language in the left hemisphere becomes crucial, especially as the left pars opercularis and left pars triangularis play a vital role in language comprehension. This left-right collaboration pattern may ensure the optimal efficiency of emotion-language integration under different cognitive demands.

A related consideration is that the observed effects are related to valence congruency, but the patterns are intricate. Positive words paired with a happy face elicit strong activation in both the left pars triangularis and right frontopolar cortex, while incongruent pairs (e.g., negative words paired with a happy face) produce strong activation in the right premotor cortex. These patterns suggest that the brain prioritizes the processing of similar emotional information. Although emotional information comes from different modalities, when they share the same valence, the brain can enhance the processing efficiency. The congruent combination of positive words and happy faces activates left language-related regions, suggesting enhanced semantic integration. In contrast, incongruent valence activates right executive control areas, indicating that the brain needs extra cognitive resources to resolve the conflict between the two stimuli. This advantage of valence congruence not only improves the efficiency of emotional recognition but also provides more available resources to process complex information.
