Introduction

The neural basis underlying memory aging remains elusive but is likely multifactorial. Current understanding of risk factors includes early Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology, neural specificity decrease, network integrity reduction, and hippocampal atrophy and function decline (1). Functional MRI (fMRI) analysis has revealed that the default mode network (DMN) is highly involved in episodic memory through supporting memory encoding and retrieval, and its connectivity decreases in aging and dementia. A recent study has identified that young adults with increased resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) in higher-order DMN but decreased lower-order sensory and motor network (SMN) have better performance in a variety of behavioral measures, predominantly cognition and memory tasks (2). We here combine resting-state and task-based fMRI of a memory task and explore how this cross-hierarchical contrast rsFC is linked to memory aging.

Methods

We included 16 young adults (YA; 24.6 ± 3.6 years; 7 females) and 42 cognitively normal older adults (OA; 76.8 ± 6.9 years; 24 females) in the Berkeley Aging Cohort Study. All participants sequentially underwent structural MRI, resting state fMRI, and task-based fMRI of an object-scene-pair encoding task (1). We subtracted the averaged rsFC within lower-order regions from higher-order ones, and compared the cross-hierarchy contrast of rsFC between the young and old groups. In each age group, the rsFC contrast metrics were compared between the males and females, and further correlated with age and memory task performance (age- and sex-adjusted). Brain function contrast between the higher- and lower-order regions may also manifest as the temporal difference (or delay) of cortical activation, as the propagating wave along cortices with different hierarchy, evaluated with principal gradient of rsFC (3). We referred to previous studies (4) to extract the events of propagating wave between lower- and higher-order regions. The propagating number of each direction (“High-to-low” or “Low-to-high”) was correlated with memory task performance and activation in three key cortical regions (i.e., anterior-temporal [AT], posterior-medial [PM], and hippocampal [HPC] regions) related to memory during task-fMRI (5). The Spearman’s rank correlation was used due to non-Gaussian distribution.

Results

The cross-hierarchy contrast of rsFC appeared to be marginally higher in YA than OA group (p = 0.058). In the OA group, the rsFC contrast decreases with aging (Spearman’s ρ = - 0.32, p = 0.040) and in females (p = 0.002, two-sample t-test). These age and sex effects were not found in the young participants (both p > 0.64). OAs with lower cross-hierarchy contrast of rsFC had worse memory performance during retrieval (ρ = 0.36, p = 0.019; Fig. 1). The memory scores were not correlated with mean rsFC within either the higher-order regions or the lower ones (both p > 0.26). When examining the events of propagating waves between lower- and higher-order regions, the number of propagating activities along the “Low-to-high” direction decreased in the older group. A higher number of such propagating was associated with better memory and lower task-evoked activation in the AT, PM, and HPC regions (all ρ strength > 0.35, all p < 0.030; Fig. 2).

Conclusion

The resting-state FC contrast between higher- and lower-order regions is closely associated with aging and relevant episodic memory decline. This cross-hierarchy contrast between cortical activation timing manifests as a slow propagating wave of brain activity transiting between regions with distinct hierarchies. The propagating wave traveling from lower- to higher-order regions is positively related to better memory and may serve a preparatory function, readying the brain for memory formation, a process that is affected by aging.
