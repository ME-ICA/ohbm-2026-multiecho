Introduction

Cognitive fatigue (CF) is one of the most common and disabling symptoms in multiple sclerosis (MS), with a substantial impact on quality of life. It is typically defined as a subjective sense of mental exhaustion that arises or worsens during prolonged cognitive effort and is often accompanied by an objective decline in performance and/or increased variability on demanding tasks. CF is only partially accounted for by white-matter lesion load and can be prominent even in patients with relatively preserved physical functioning, highlighting its partly independent clinical trajectory1. Its pathophysiology remains only partially understood: current theories point to a multifactorial origin, involving neuroimmune and neuroendocrine dysregulation, along with structural and functional alterations in distributed cortico-subcortical networks. Interoception has been increasingly proposed as a key mechanism in CF, particularly in MS. Interoceptive processing relies on a network centered on the anterior insula and anterior/mid cingulate cortex, which integrates afferent bodily signals with motivational and regulatory demands. Several models of CF suggest that abnormal weighting or interpretation of these internal signals may generate a persistent sense of effort, even in the absence of overt performance decline. In MS, imaging has shown that CF specifically associates with reduced interoceptive accuracy, together with structural and functional alterations in key interoceptive hubs. Resting-state and task-based studies suggest a disrupted integration of bodily state, effort monitoring and motivational value, converging with broader “fatigue network” proposals that place the interoceptive–salience system (insula, cingulate) and its interactions with cortico–striatal circuits at the center of CF2-3, providing a bridge between subjective exhaustion, altered cost–benefit computations, and relatively preserved cognitive performance.

Methods

Here, we investigate how brain activity within the interoceptive network relates to different levels of CF during a task requiring prolonged mental effort (AULSS2 Ethical approval protocol #1332). 30 relapsing-remitting MS patients have been enrolled and divided into two groups with different CF level (low vs high; LF vs HF) according to neurologists’ clinical evaluation. 15 healthy participants (HC) have been also included, with age and education levels similar to MS groups (HC: mean 45.3 y, sd 4.1; LF: mean 41.9 y, sd 3.5; mean HF: mean 45.3 y, sd 3.8). All participants underwent a comprehensive cognitive and mood assessment: CF was assessed with Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS), Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), Piper Fatigue Scale (PFS) and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Cognitive functioning was further characterized by the Cognitive Reserve Index questionnaire (CRIq) and the Brief Repeatable Battery of Neuropsychological Tests in Multiple Sclerosis (BRB-NT). Mood and anxiety were evaluated with Beck Depression Inventory–II (BDI-II) and State-Trait Anxiety Inventory 1 e 2 (STAI-Y) and sleep quality was assessed with the Pittsburg Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). ANOVA and post-hoc t-tests were conducted on normally distributed variables, while Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn’s test were adopted for non-normally distributed data. 

All participants took part in a functional MRI study asking participants to perform a modified version of the PASAT while being scanned. A 3T Siemens VIDA scanner, equipped with a standard Siemens 64-channel coil, was used to acquire functional images with a gradient-echo, echo-planar (EPI) T2*-weighted sequence in order to measure blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) contrast throughout the whole brain (50 contiguous axial slices, ascending interleaved sequence, 2.5 mm × 2.5 mm × 3.0 mm resolution, Field of View, FOV = 219 mm × 219 mm, flip angle = 70°, TE = 30 ms). Volumes were acquired continuously with a repetition time (TR) of 2.8 s. The fMRI measurement lasted 11 min, during which three 3-minutes task blocks alternated with 30-second rest periods. During task block, participants were presented with number sequences via MR-compatible goggles, and a button press was required whenever the sum of two consecutive digits equaled 10. During rest, a white fixation cross on a black background was shown. Only participants whose accuracy level was above 70% were included. SPM124 was used to perform both data preprocessing and analysis. Preprocessing included slice timing, realignment, normalization according to the MNI template implemented in SPM and finally smoothing (FWHM, 6 mm isotropic). For each participant, first-level analysis was performed by dividing each 3-minutes task block into six 30-second time bins; signal related to each single time bin was averaged across the three task blocks and then rest-related activation was subtracted (e.g., [(time bin1/block1 + time bin1/block2 + time bin1/block3)/3] –[(rest1+rest2+rest3)/3]). This procedure led to the creation of 6 contrast images, which were entered into a second level mixed effect analysis, conducted using a flexible factorial design, to better capture inter-subject variability. GROUP (HC, LF and HF) acted as a between-subjects factor (independence and unequal variance assumed), while TIME BIN (1 to 6) was considered as a within-subject factor (dependence and equal variance assumed). For the subject factor, independence and equal variance were assumed, and its interaction with the group factor was selected. The resulting design matrix was 270*18. Since the focus of the work was on the role of interoception in CF, analyses were restricted to regions known to play a key role in enteroception: the insula, the anterior and mid-cingulate cortex, the superior, middle, inferior and orbital frontal gyri, and the basal ganglia. A binary mask was created merging all these regions and was used as an explicit mask. Results were FWE-corrected (p<.05, k > 30). 

Results

Neuropsychological assessment: Analyses were performed using ANOVA with one-tailed post-hoc t-tests for normally distributed variables, and Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by one-tailed Dunn’s post-hoc comparisons for non-normally distributed data (verified with Shapiro-Wilk test). The HC>LF comparison showed largely overlapping profiles across nearly all measures, including direct indices of fatigue (MFIS, FSS). Nevertheless, the LF group exhibited signs of psychological distress, with significantly poorer coping abilities (CRIq, z= -2.330, p = 0.01) and higher state and trait anxiety levels (STAI-Y1, z: -1.916, p = 0.028; STAI-Y2, z= -2.123, p = 0.017) compared with HC. In contrast, the HC>HF comparison revealed a pronounced impairment in the HF group across all assessed domains, encompassing mood (BDI, z: 3.307, p = 0.001; STAI-Y1, z: 1.769, p = 0.038), coping (CRI, z: -3.158, p < 0.001), fatigue measures (FSS, z: 3.992; MFIS, z: 4.571; and PFS, z: 3.879 - all p < 0.001), cognitive performance (PASAT, z: 2.719, p < 0.001), and sleep quality (PSQI, z: 2.258, p = 0.011). Consistently, the HF group also performed significantly worse than the LF group on nearly all measures, with the exception of coping (BDI, z: -4.147; STAI-Y1, z: -3.685; Y-2, z: 3.369; FSS, z: -4.499; MFIS, z: -4.822; PFS, z: -4.791and PASAT, z: 2.300, all p < 0.001; PSQI, z: -2.258, p = 0.011). No additional significant differences were detected. 

fMRI analyses: a significant main effect of Group was observed, involving the midcingulated cortex (MNI: -1, 13, 41; F: 33.33, p<.001, k: 91), the  bilateral caudate (LEFT: MNI: -14, 3, 17: F: 28.7, p >.001, k: 31; RIGHT: MNI: 24, 15, 2: F: 24.2, p >.001, k: 36) and superior frontal gyrus (LEFT: MNI: -14, 38, 38: F: 26.47, p >.001, k: 142; RIGHT: MNI: 19, 46, 35: F: 25.2, p >.001, k: 124) and the [CB1] right medial segment of the superior frontal gyrus (MNI: 12, 51, 14; F: 20.88, p<.001, k: 34). Time and the Group × Time interaction did not reach significance. Post hoc tests revealed higher activation in the right middle (MNI: 32, 49, 29; t: 6.38, p<.001, k: 91) and superior frontal gyri (MNI: 9, 49, 17; t: 6.08, p<.001, k: 36) for HC>LF. For HC>HF, this pattern extended also to the left hemisphere involving the superior frontal gyrus (MNI: -14, 38, 38; t: 7.19, p<.001), the basal nuclei and the anterior insula (MNI: -14, 3, 17, T: 7.46, p <.001, k: 61), and midcingulate cortex (MNI: -1, 13, 41, t: 8.13, p<.001, k: 220). The contrast LF>HF showed increased activity in the right basal nuclei (MNI: 24, 15, 2, t: 6.58, p<.001, k: 38). Although Time did not reach significance, activation patterns across bins were examined exploratorily: HC and LF did not differ in any bin, whereas at time bin 1 HC>HF showed reduced activation in the left basal ganglia (MNI 24, -2, 11, t: 7.46, p<.001, k: 46), superior frontal gyrus (MNI 14, 36, 38, t: 6.14, p<.001, k: 75), and midcingulate cortex (MN -1, 13, 41 t: 7.20, p<.001, k: 87), with differences in the latter two regions persisting in bins 2 and 3. At time bin 1, HF showed activation weaker than LF  too, in right basal nuclei (MNI 24, 15, 2, t: 6.89, p<.001, k: 35), anterior insula (MNI 19, 18, 2, t: 6.89, p<.001, k: 35), midcingulate cortex (MNI 2, 13, 41, t: 5.49, p=.001, k: 32), and in the left superior frontal gyrus (MNI -16, 26, 47, t 5.71, p<.001, k: 33). No further differences emerged at the chosen threshold.

Conclusion

Taken together, the results support the view that cognitive fatigue in MS may be related to dysfunction of an interoceptive–motivational network rather than to a global cognitive deficit. HF presented a broad clinical burden, whereas LF, despite resembling HC on direct fatigue measures, already exhibited psychological distress, consistent with reports of a partial dissociation between subjective fatigue and cognitive performance in MS. At the neural level, reduced activation in middle/superior frontal gyri, basal ganglia, anterior insula, and midcingulate cortex in HF relative to HC, and to a lesser extent LF, fits with models implicating fronto–striatal and cingulo–insular circuits in interoception, effort monitoring, and fatigue. The graded pattern (HC > LF > HF) suggests that higher CF may be associated with progressively weaker engagement of these regions during sustained task performance, with the temporal profile indicating that the core problem concerns the initial up-regulation of effort/valuation processes rather than a progressive breakdown or depletion of resources as the task proceeds5. This supports interoception-based accounts of CF, in which aberrant weighting of internal signals within insula–cingulate–striatal networks biases cost–benefit computations and generates chronic subjective fatigue even in the presence of relatively preserved objective cognitive performance
