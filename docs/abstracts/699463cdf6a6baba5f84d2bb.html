Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a severe psychiatric disorder defined by persistent energy restriction, fear of weight gain and disturbed body image, with broad cognitive–affective and medical sequelae1. Onset typically occurs in adolescence, a period of high metabolic demand and cortical remodeling, making nutritional deprivation a potential constraint on neurodevelopment. Structural MRI studies show widespread cortical gray matter reductions in acute AN, with cortical thickness partially normalizing after weight restoration, implicating nutritional state but not excluding longer-lived alterations2. Abnormalities in gyrification, sulcal morphology and surface area also appear, with partial normalization and age-related limits on recovery, suggesting multiple biological processes rather than a single reversible effect of malnutrition2. From a systems perspective, the cortex is organized as a hierarchical network in which metabolically costly hubs are particularly exposed to stressors, and disease-related maps often follow this architecture3. Despite this convergent evidence, most structural MRI studies in AN have relied on single-feature morphometry, fragmenting signal across modalities and limiting interpretation. Here we adopt Morphometric INverse Divergence (MIND), a multivariate framework that models each cortical region via the joint distribution of multiple vertex-wise features and quantifies interregional similarity from their divergence4. We applied MIND to test whether AN shows reduced morphometric similarity, preferential involvement of metabolically demanding hub systems, and spatial correspondence between regional alterations, structural connectivity, and cortical gradients of neurotransmitter systems involved in appetite regulation and reward5.

Methods

Dataset description and processing of imaging data: 

A total of 90 female participants were included (50 with anorexia nervosa, mean age = 20.4 ± 5.68 years; BMI = 15.69 ± 0.59, and 40 healthy controls, mean age = 25.3 ± 3.17 years; BMI = 20.23 ± 1.82). All patients met DSM-5 criteria, and healthy controls were screened to exclude any lifetime eating disorder. Exclusion criteria comprised neurological illness, psychosis, substance use disorders, hypomanic or manic episodes, MRI contraindications, and age <14. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Each participant underwent a multimodal MRI protocol including structural MRI for the estimation of cortical profile similarity and diffusion MRI for structural connectivity mapping. Data were acquired on a 3T Philips scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. High-resolution T1-weighted images were processed with FreeSurfer for cortical surface reconstruction. Vertex-wise morphometric features - cortical thickness, mean curvature, surface area, sulcal depth, and volume - were extracted according to the Schaefer 100 regions 7 networks atlas (Figure 1a). Morphometric similarity was quantified using MIND4 (Figure 1b). For each pair of regions, MIND modeled the multivariate distribution of these morphometric features and computed the Kullback–Leibler divergence between distributions; divergence values were then inverted and rescaled to yield a similarity metric between 0 and 1. This procedure captured shared structural patterns across multiple morphometric dimensions. Diffusion MRI (multi-shell, 2 mm isotropic) underwent denoising, motion, eddy-current and susceptibility-distortion correction, followed by registration to the T1-weighted image. Whole-brain probabilistic tractography was performed using anatomically constrained tractography (ACT) and multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution. Streamline weights were adjusted using SIFT2 to reduce biases. Structural connectivity matrices were generated using the Schaefer atlas and subsequently used to assess network-driven effects on regional alterations of morphometric similarity.

Statistical analyses: 

Group differences in global, network-level, and regional MIND strength were assessed using generalized linear models including age and total intracranial volume (TIV) as covariates. Associations between BMI and MIND strength were examined with partial correlations controlling for the same variables. To test whether regional MIND reductions followed structural connectivity architecture, we correlated regional alterations with the mean alteration of each region’s structural neighbors. Significance was assessed using two classes of null models: (i) spatial autocorrelation-matched surrogate maps (BrainSMASH, 10,000 null maps), and (ii) degree- and edge-length-preserving random structural connectomes (1,000 iterations). Network-Based Statistics (NBS) identified subnetworks showing reduced MIND connectivity in AN and subnetworks whose edge strength correlated with BMI (primary threshold p < 0.001; 5,000 permutations; component-level p < 0.05). Finally, spatial correspondence between MIND alterations and PET/SPECT receptor density maps (18 neurotransmitter targets) was quantified via Pearson correlation.

Results

Global MIND connectivity strength was significantly reduced in AN (t = 2.10, one-sided p = 0.019, Figure 1c). Within the AN group, global strength was positively associated with BMI (t = 2.47, p = 0.017, partial r = 0.35), indicating that reductions in overall cortical similarity scaled with the severity of undernutrition (Figure 1d). No association was observed in HC (t = −1.28, p = 0.21, partial r = −0.21), suggesting that this relationship was illness-specific.

At the network level, reductions in connectivity strength emerged selectively in higher-order systems (Figure 1e). Significant decreases were detected in the limbic (t = 3.15, FDR p = 0.008), FPN (t = 2.70, p = 0.015), DMN (t = 2.37, p = 0.024) and DAN (t = 2.15, p = 0.03) networks, with only a trend in salience/VAN (t = 1.77, FDR p = 0.06). SMN and Vis networks showed no significant differences (all FDR p > 0.20). Exploratory analyses within the AN group highlighted positive associations between BMI and MIND strength in FPN (r = 0.36, p = 0.012), DMN (r = 0.32, p = 0.026) and DAN (r = 0.31, p = 0.033), although none survived FDR. These effects nonetheless suggested that the degree of network-level disruption tracked nutritional status, particularly in transmodal systems supporting cognitive–affective integration. At the regional level, ten regions showed significantly reduced node strength after FDR correction (all p < 0.05), with effects concentrated in DMN and limbic territories. BMI-related analyses within AN revealed significant partial correlations in VAN, Vis and DMN regions (partial r = 0.43–0.52, all p < 0.05, Figure 1f). These findings indicate that lower BMI was associated with disproportionately reduced morphometric similarity in several distributed networks, suggesting that metabolic stress amplifies regional vulnerability beyond the transmodal cortex alone. Spatial correspondence with PET/SPECT maps revealed significant associations between the HC > AN t-map and receptor densities for 5-HT4 (r = 0.40), 5-HT1A (r = 0.37), CB1 (r = 0.36), μ-opioid (r = 0.35) and D2/3 (r = 0.29), all FDR p < 0.05 (Figure 2b). These results point to a close alignment between AN-related structural alterations and neuromodulatory systems involved in appetite regulation, reward processing and affective control.

Network-driven effects were observed: regional t-values correlated with the mean alteration of structural neighbors (r = 0.42). Spatial-autocorrelation-preserving nulls (p = 0.0001) and network-geometry nulls (p = 0.005) confirmed that this pattern could not be accounted for by spatial autocorrelation or by the brain’s geometric embedding, indicating that the observed reductions in structural covariance were compatible with propagation along white-matter pathways (Figure 2a). Lastly, NBS identified one significant HC > AN component (12 edges, p = 0.025, NBS-corrected, Figure 2c) at a primary threshold of p < 0.001, with edges showing consistently higher covariance in HC (mean t = 3.57, range = 3.41–3.91). A distinct BMI-related NBS component (32 edges, p = 0.022, NBS-corrected, Figure 2d) showed consistently positive correlations (mean r = 0.47, range = 0.43–0.55), indicating that lower BMI was associated with weakened long-range structural covariance, particularly between limbic–orbitofrontal areas and Vis, VAN and DMN networks.

Conclusion

The present results indicate that cortical organization in AN is disrupted across multiple spatial scales when assessed with a multivariate morphometric similarity framework. Global reductions in MIND strength, together with their positive association with BMI, suggest that large-scale structural coordination is sensitive to the degree of undernutrition. Although the cross-sectional design limits conclusions about state dependence, these findings extend prior evidence from univariate morphometry and highlight the potential of MIND to capture neurostructural variation related to metabolic status.

Alterations were not uniformly distributed but selectively involved links belonging to transmodal networks, including limbic, DMN, FPN and DAN systems, which occupy metabolically demanding and highly integrative positions within the cortical hierarchy. Their preferential involvement is consistent with hub vulnerability models and offers a plausible structural substrate for cognitive rigidity, heightened self-monitoring and altered emotional control in AN. Regional effects mirrored this pattern, with reductions concentrated in DMN and limbic territories. BMI-related associations in VAN, Vis and DMN regions further indicated that metabolic severity modulates morphometric similarity beyond the transmodal cortex, affecting attentional and visual–perceptual regions as well.

Network-Based Statistics also pointed to preferential disruption of long-range integrative pathways. The most affected edges linked dorsal-attention and limbic–prefrontal systems, whereas BMI-related components predominantly involved connections between limbic–orbitofrontal regions and visual or attentional systems. These findings suggest that undernutrition primarily weakens large-scale integrative communication, while primary sensory networks remain comparatively preserved. Additionally, we found that MIND alterations followed structural connectivity architecture: regions showing reduced morphometric similarity tended to cluster along their anatomical neighbors, indicating that the pattern of disruption spread preferentially along existing white-matter pathways rather than appearing as isolated, spatially scattered effects.

The spatial distribution of MIND connectivity loss further aligned with receptor density gradients for serotonergic, dopaminergic, cannabinoid and μ-opioid systems. Because these neuromodulatory pathways support appetite regulation, reward processing, interoception and affective control, their correspondence with morphometric alterations suggests that cortical vulnerability in AN reflects an interaction between metabolic constraints and neurochemical architecture. Together, these findings support a metabo-connectomic framework in which energetic demand, structural embedding and receptor-mediated signaling jointly shape cortical organization in AN.
