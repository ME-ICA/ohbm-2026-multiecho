Introduction

Nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) is highly prevalent in young adults and adolescents, closely linked to sex, socioeconomic, and environmental risk factors.1 Crucially, self-injurious behaviors exist on a severity continuum, yet it remains unclear whether neural disruptions represent a binary marker of clinical disorder or instead track a progressive spectrum detectable in subthreshold NSSI (sNSSI) as an early stage of NSSI. Furthermore, while fMRI studies have reported aberrant regional activity and altered functional connectivity in NSSI2, most prior work relied on undirected measures, ignoring the direction of information flow. To address this, we apply transfer entropy (TE), a nonlinear information-theoretic metric of directed information transfer3, to (1) characterize progressive alterations in directed functional interaction across the NSSI spectrum (HC, sNSSI, and NSSI); (2) relate these alterations to clinical and behavioral measures, including NSSI history, severity, stressful life events, anxiety symptoms, depressive and emotional symptoms, and affective states; and (3) examine large-scale network reorganization.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI was acquired from NSSI (n = 62, age=15.42±1.90 yrs, 11M/57F), sNSSI (n = 53, age=16.46±1.82 yrs, 22M/23F), and HC (n = 51, age=16.46±2.22 yrs, 18M/34F) participants using a uMR 790 3T scanner (West China Hospital, Chengdu, China). Preprocessed time series were parcellated into 232 regions of interest (ROIs). Pairwise TE was computed to yield a 232x232 directed connectivity matrix per subject.4 Edge-wise two-sample t-tests were performed to identify connections with group differences (p < 0.001). For significant edges, we assessed brain-behavior relationships using correlations with a battery of clinical and behavioral scores (p < 0.05, FDR corrected). To probe modular organization, we applied the Louvain community-detection algorithm for directed graphs to group-averaged TE matrices and quantified similarity to canonical functional networks using Dice overlap coefficients (Kong 2025).5 The overall analysis pipeline is shown in Figure 1A.

Results

Relative to HCs, 65 of 68 altered connections in NSSI showed reduced TE, as did 47 of 50 in sNSSI. NSSI further exhibited 18 of 25 significant reductions relative to sNSSI (Figure 1B-D). Both NSSI and sNSSI exhibited widespread decreases in directed connectivity, primarily between somatomotor and visual regions, subcortical structures (thalamus, basal ganglia), and default-mode and salience/ventral-attention networks. Compared with sNSSI, NSSI showed additional reductions in connections linking lateral prefrontal control regions to visual/somatomotor/dorsal‐attention areas and thalamus, as well as limbic OFC/PHC and cortico-striatal nodes, indicating more severe disruption of control–sensorimotor and thalamo-limbic circuits. Of 143 altered directed connections, 65 were significantly associated with behavioral measures (Figure 1E), with higher life stress, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and impulsivity generally related to lower TE. Community analyses revealed marked reorganization of directed network topology in both NSSI and sNSSI: (1) HCs showed eight well-defined communities, whereas NSSI showed seven communities with altered topology; and (2) sNSSI displayed distinct reconfiguration with blurred community boundaries and single community overlapping multiple canonical networks, producing a diffuse Dice-overlap pattern (Figure 2).

Conclusion

Using transfer entropy, we demonstrate that both NSSI and sNSSI are characterized by widespread reductions in directed connectivity, especially among somatomotor–visual circuits, thalamo–basal ganglia pathways, and default-mode and salience/ventral-attention networks. Nearly half of the abnormal edges were behaviorally relevant, with greater symptom burden associated with weaker directed connectivity. At the system level, both groups exhibited a breakdown of modular organization, evidenced by fewer and less distinct communities and reduced modular specificity, suggesting that NSSI represents a spectrum of progressively disrupted directed communication across large-scale brain network.
