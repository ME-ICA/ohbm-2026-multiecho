Introduction

In the last decades, substantial progress has been made in the development of upper limb prostheses with multiple degrees of freedom (DoF). These prostheses offer promising solutions to restore hand functionality and facilitate reintegration into the social and working life of individuals with limb loss. However, prosthesis abandonment rates remain very high in this population, mainly because of the high cognitive demands and prolonged training sessions required to achieve sufficient control.1 More recently, the need to develop tailored training programs based on integrated approaches has been highlighted. Virtual reality (VR) provides an immersive and motivating environment that can support prosthesis learning, by enabling realistic, repetitive practice with real-time feedback. High-frequency transcranial random noise stimulation (hf-tRNS) has shown potential in enhancing sensory-motor encoding and facilitating cortical excitability. Despite these advances, the combined effects of hf-tRNS and VR-based on motor learning is still poorly investigated in this field. To address this gap, we conducted a study in healthy volunteers to examine whether the integration of hf-tRNS with EMG-based VR training would foster neuroplasticity and improve motor performance.

Methods

Twenty-four healthy volunteers (mean age = 25.25 ± 3.60 years, mean school age = 15.17 ± 2.82 years, 12 male and 12 female) were included in the study. They were all right-handed, as assessed with the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (mean laterality score: 0.81 ± 0.13), and they were randomly assigned to active (6 male, 6 female) or sham (6 male, 6 female) groups.

Participants completed three daily 20-minute EMG-based VR motor training sessions (Figure 1), during each the active group received hf-tRNS stimulation over the right primary motor cortex, whereas the sham group received placebo stimulation. VR training consisted of an adapted version of  the Minnesota Manual Dexterity Test (MMDT), requiring participants to progressively grasp virtual pegs and place them into designated slots within a target grid. In order to correctly complete MMDT, volunteers must learn to control a 4-DoF virtual prosthesis with their non-dominant hand. Performance metrics from the VR environment were recorded during training sessions and behavioral analyses on task completion rate were subsequently conducted using custom scripts implemented in MATLAB (2025a).

Before and after VR motor training, participants underwent a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) session including an event-related body representation task (Figure 1). Participants were asked to judge the laterality of hands, eyes (body stimuli) and objects (control stimuli), presented at different orientations.

Functional MRI data were acquired on a 3T GE Signa Architect system (TR = 1500 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 3 mm, 46 slices 2.7 mm each with 3 mm gap, 80 x 80 matrix, flip angle = 70). Preprocessing and statistical analyses were performed using SPM25, following standard procedures for slice timing, realignment, coregistration with high-resolution T1-weighted 3D anatomical image of each subject, normalization to MNI space and smoothing (i.e., 4 × 4 × 6 mm FWHM), and applying general linear model approaches at the single-subject and group levels. fMRI results were corrected for multiple comparisons using a combined voxel-level and cluster-extent threshold corresponding to an experiment-wise α < 0.05, computed via AFNI’s 3dClustSim with the –acf option (AFNI version 24.2.06).

Results

Behavioral data extracted from the VR motor training sessions revealed a robust improvement in task performance across days in both groups. In the sham group, performance significantly increased from Day 1 to Day 2 (Δ = +98.0%, p = 0.047) and from Day 1 to Day 3 (Δ = +137.9%, p = 0.024), while the Day 2–Day 3 difference was not significant (Δ = +39.9%, p = 0.284). On the other hand, the active group showed a comparable but overall larger progression, with significant improvements from Day 1 to Day 2 (Δ = +112.0%, p = 0.037), Day 1 to Day 3 (Δ = +244.8%, p = 0.001), and Day 2 to Day 3 (Δ = +132.9%, p = 0.011).

Concerning fMRI results, in paired t-test analysis on the body representation task, the sham group showed significantly higher BOLD responses in the pre- versus post-training contrast for the body versus control condition (corrected α < 0.05, k > 32; Figure 2). Increased activity was localized bilaterally in the middle cingulate cortex (MCC; peak coordinates in MNI space X = -4, Y = -14, Z = 38, k = 104), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC; peak coordinates in MNI space X = 4, Y = -44, Z = 29, k = 32), and precuneus (peak coordinates in MNI space X = -10, Y = -62, Z = 32, k = 55). No significant pre– versus post-training signal changes were observed in the active group for the same contrast.

Conclusion

Behavioral results showed that both groups improved their performance across VR training sessions, but the active group with a significantly larger change. Additionally, only the active group exhibited a significant additional gain on the last session. This pattern suggests that neuromodulation may have enhanced motor learning, by facilitating the consolidation of task-related motor strategies. Importantly, the fMRI findings provide convergent neural evidence supporting this behavioral advantage. In the body representation task, the sham group showed significantly higher brain activity in the precuneus, MCC, and PCC only at baseline, suggesting that these multisensory and self-related regions reduced their engagement after training. In contrast, the active group showed no such pre-to-post decrease, indicating a sustained engagement of body representation networks.2 This sustained activity may reflect enhanced sensorimotor integration and a more stable updating of the body representation, consistent with hf-tRNS-induced facilitation of neuroplasticity.3

Taken together, these preliminary findings suggest that combining hf-tRNS with EMG-based VR training simultaneously enhances motor performance and preserves activity within body representation networks.4 Moreover, this integrated training paradigm demonstrated promising efficacy in healthy participants, providing a solid foundation for translating this protocol to amputees, with the goal of improving prosthesis-related skills and ultimately enhancing their quality of life.
