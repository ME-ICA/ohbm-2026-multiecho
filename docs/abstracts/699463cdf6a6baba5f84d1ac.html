Introduction

The circumventricular organs (CVOs) represent a particularly compelling yet understudied interface for brain-body communication1,2. These specialized structures lack the typical blood-brain barrier, providing direct access points between blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and neural tissue1,2. As such, this unique anatomy renders CVOs sensitive to signaling molecules like hormones, peptides or cytokines circulating in the bloodstream while simultaneously enabling them to release molecules into fluid compartments that reach the entire brain and periphery1. 

Given their strategic position as bidirectional gateways for non-synaptic signaling, CVOs provide a unique opportunity to study how circulating signals interface with central neural systems. However, despite their established physiological relevance, a systematic characterization of human CVOs across anatomical, molecular, and functional scales has been lacking. Here, we present an integrated investigation of human circumventricular organs by combining a standardized neuroanatomical atlas with transcriptomic profiling, in-vivo PET imaging, and spatial analyses of neurodegenerative vulnerability. Our multimodal approach enables an initial characterization of CVO specialization and highlights their potential relevance for large-scale brain organization and disease-associated patterns of atrophy.

Methods

CVO atlas 

Building on previously validated manual delineations of circumventricular organs (CVOs) from 20 healthy individuals2, individual masks were nonlinearly warped into MNI152 space. Voxelwise overlap across participants was summed and normalized to generate probabilistic maps for each CVO. Maps were thresholded at 25% overlap to define atlas regions, with all analyses repeated using a more conservative 50% threshold to assess robustness. Based on canonical classifications, the area postrema (AP), organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis (OVLT), and subfornical organ (SFO) were grouped as sensory CVOs, while the median eminence (ME), neurohypophysis (NH), and pineal gland (PG) were grouped as secretory CVOs.

Transcriptomic profiling

Gene expression data were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas3 (n = 6 donors; 1 female; ages 24–57). Samples located within 10 mm of each CVO centroid were assigned to atlas regions. Microarray probes were filtered based on probe intensity, differential stability, and concordance with RNA-seq data from the same donors. Gene expression values were estimated using distance-weighted interpolation from nearby probes and averaged across donors for each CVO.

Gene set enrichment analysis

To characterize molecular differences between sensory and secretory CVOs, gene expression profiles were averaged into secretory and sensory groups and analyzed using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis, implemented in the GESTALT toolkit. Gene Ontology biological process terms were used as the functional reference set. Enrichment scores were normalized against null distributions generated by 1000 gene label permutations. Statistical significance was assessed using non-parametric P-values with false discovery rate correction (α = 0.05). Only significant terms after FDR-correction were inspected.

Time-activity curves from PET imaging

Seventeen healthy adults (n = 17; 10 women; mean age 33 ± 7 years) underwent a 90-minute test–retest PET protocol with the COX-2 radiotracer [¹¹C]MC1 (OpenNeuro: ds004869)4. Participants were screened for medical and psychiatric illnesses through medical history, physical examination, electrocardiogram, and laboratory blood tests, with exclusion of those who had taken nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs within the prior two weeks. Dynamic PET data were parcellated using the CVO atlas, and time-activity curves were extracted and averaged within sensory and secretory groups. Mean population curves were computed separately for test and retest sessions, with standard errors estimated via bootstrap resampling across participants. 

Disease vulnerability

Atrophy vulnerability hotspots were derived from voxel-based morphometry analyses of T1-weighted MRI scans from 186 patients with histopathologically confirmed dementia and 73 cognitively normal controls5. Patients were stratified into pathology groups including early-onset Alzheimer’s Disease (n=68), late-onset Alzheimer’s Disease (n=29), presenilin-1 mutation carriers (n=10), Dementia with Lewis Body (n=25), 3-repeat-tau (n=11), 4-repeat-tau (n=17), TDP43A (n=12), and TDP43C (n=14). Voxelwise patient–control contrasts were parcellated using cortical (Schaefer 400) and subcortical (Melbourne S4) atlases and thresholded to highlight the top decile of affected regions as disease-specific hotspots. Euclidean distances between hotspot centroids and CVO centroids were compared against 10000 null models accounting for the spatial autocorrelation of the contrast maps, yielding Z-statistics that quantified the proximity relative to chance.

Results

Figure A: Atlas of circumventricular organs in MNI152 space

Circumventricular organs (CVOs) are specialized brain structures characterized by a permeable blood–brain barrier, enabling bidirectional interaction with circulating signals1. Despite their importance, no comprehensive atlas of human CVOs in standard space has been available. Using manual anatomical delineations from 20 healthy individuals in native T1-weighted space2, we generated a probabilistic atlas of six CVOs warped into MNI152 space: the area postrema (AP), organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis (OVLT), subfornical organ (SFO), median eminence (ME), neurohypophysis (NH), and pineal gland (PG). Consistent with established classifications, AP, OVLT, and SFO were grouped as sensory CVOs, while ME, NH, and PG were grouped as secretory CVOs.

Figure B: Gene set enrichment analysis identifies differential roles of sensory and secretory organs

To characterize molecular differences between CVO subtypes, we mapped samples from the Allen Human Brain Atlas3 to atlas-defined CVO regions and compiled a publicly available gene expression library (osf.io/jtgsp). Averaged expression profiles for sensory and secretory CVOs were analyzed using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis. Sensory CVOs showed enrichment for gene sets related to epithelial development and chemical stimulus detection, consistent with their established role in monitoring circulating signals. In contrast, secretory CVOs were enriched for gene sets associated with immune-related processes, metabolism, and neurotransmission. These transcriptomic differences suggest distinct molecular specializations aligned with the canonical functional roles of sensory versus secretory CVOs.

Figure C: Functional differences reflected in PET dynamics

We next assessed whether molecular distinctions between CVO subtypes were reflected in in-vivo functional imaging. Using our atlas of circumventricular organs, we extracted time–activity curves of [¹¹C]MC1 PET uptake, a marker of COX-2 expression and baseline neuroimmune activity4. Sensory CVOs exhibited significantly higher tracer uptake than secretory CVOs across timepoints (functional paired t-test: T = 5.76, P < 0.001), an effect that was replicated in an independent retest session (T = 4.65, P < 0.001). These findings indicate stable functional differences between CVO subtypes consistent with their divergent molecular profiles.

Figure D: Vulnerability to pathologies is organ-dependent

Finally, we examined whether CVOs are spatially associated with regions vulnerable to neurodegeneration. Using population estimates of regional atrophy from MRI-based voxel-based morphometry differences between controls and patients from six dementia phenotypes5, we identified disease-specific atrophy hotspots and quantified their Euclidean distance to each CVO. Distances were compared against 10000 null models accounting for the spatial autocorrelation of the hotspot maps to assess significance. We observed non-random proximity between specific CVOs and disease-related atrophy patterns. For example, late-onset Alzheimer’s disease hotspots were closer to the sensory area postrema than expected by chance (Z = −3.32, P < 0.001), whereas early-onset Alzheimer’s disease hotspots were closer to the secretory pineal gland (Z = −3.18, P = 0.001). These results suggest disease-specific spatial associations between CVOs and neurodegenerative vulnerability, without implying causal mechanisms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we present a comprehensive atlas of human circumventricular organs in standard MNI space and demonstrate its utility for multimodal investigation of these specialized brain structures. Our work establishes the first comprehensive study of these critical structures in humans, profiling their genetic fingerprint, uncovering the functional differences between secretory and sensory organs, and mapping their vulnerability to disease. Together, these results highlight the value of a standardized CVO atlas for probing neuroimmune and neurovascular interfaces in the human brain and provide a foundation for future studies investigating the role of CVOs in health and disease.
