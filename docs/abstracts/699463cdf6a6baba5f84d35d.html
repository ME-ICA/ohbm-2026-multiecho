Introduction

Fast optical signals (FOS), or event-related optical signals (EROS), are millisecond-scale responses measured with near-infrared light. EROS have been observed in human occipital cortex during visual stimulation and in frontal cortex.¹-² They are thought to reflect membrane-potential–related changes in refractive index and scattering and thus be more closely linked to neuronal electrical activity than classical hemodynamic near-infrared signals, helping bridge the spatiotemporal gap between electrophysiology and optical imaging.³ 

However, the reproducibility and origin of FOS remain debated. Their amplitude is much smaller than hemodynamic signals, leading to low signal-to-noise ratio and high susceptibility to cardiac, respiratory, and superficial artifacts, and different laboratories have reported inconsistent findings, including failures to detect robust FOS under tightly controlled conditions. These issues cast doubt on FOS as a stable, generalizable marker, and single-modality optical data are often insufficient to separate neural from non-neural components. Simultaneous multimodal acquisition provides a route to validating FOS: if, within the same task and subject, FOS align in latency and waveform with EEG/MEG event-related potentials/fields, this supports a neuronal origin. Previous work mainly used dual-modality EEG–FOS paradigms. 

Here, we use an integrated tri-modal system combining optically pumped magnetometer MEG (OPM-MEG), electroencephalography (EEG), and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) to assess occipital FOS during a checkerboard reversal paradigm. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of simultaneous magnetic, electrical, and fast optical event-related responses in the same participants and task, enabling direct comparison of latencies, waveforms, and frequency dependence and providing multimodal evidence for the existence and neural correspondence of FOS relative to classical visual evoked responses.

Methods

We used a homemade simultaneous multimodal neuroimaging system that integrates a 32-sensor triaxial OPM-MEG module (Neuro-1, Quspin, USA), a 64-channel custom EEG module, and a custom NIRS module with 32 detectors and 32 sources emitting light at 680 nm and 850 nm, all operated inside a custom horizontal cylindrical magnetic shielding enclosure. In the present experiment, only 2 EEG channels, 1 OPM-MEG sensor, and 8 NIRS sources with 8 detectors were activated. These sensors were embedded in a subject-specific helmet fabricated based on high-resolution anatomical 3D models. All electrodes and optical channels were positioned over the occipital region; the two EEG electrodes were placed symmetrically over the left and right hemispheres, and the MEG sensor was located along the midline between them. All subsystems were synchronized and controlled via a unified PXIe platform (National Instruments, USA).

Two healthy right-handed male volunteers participated in the study. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. During the experiment, participants lay supine on a sliding bed inside the magnetic shield and viewed a projection screen via a mirror to perform a visual stimulation task. The visual stimulus consisted of a black-and-white checkerboard reversal paradigm with three reversal frequencies: 1, 2.5, and 5 Hz. At the beginning of each block, a black screen was presented for 4–5 s, followed by a centrally presented checkerboard whose contrast polarity alternated at the specified frequency for 30 s, and then a 30 s black screen for rest before the next block. For the 1 Hz condition, 12 blocks were presented; each polarity reversal was defined as one trial, yielding 720 trials in total. For the 2.5 Hz condition, 5 blocks were presented (750 trials in total); for the 5 Hz condition, 3 blocks were presented (900 trials in total). Participants were instructed to keep their eyes open and to minimize eye and head movements during checkerboard stimulation.

All MEG, EEG, and NIRS data were processed offline in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA). For all modalities, individual checkerboard reversals were treated as events. Data were segmented within predefined time windows and averaged across trials to obtain event-related responses. MEG and EEG data were first filtered using a 1–30 Hz zero-phase band-pass filter to remove slow drifts and high-frequency noise, and then aligned to the stimulus triggers and averaged within the −50 to 500 ms window to obtain ERF and ERP, respectively. NIRS fast optical signals were resampled to 200 Hz, and channels over the occipital cortex were selected based on predefined source–detector configurations. Using the median intensity during the initial resting period as baseline, signals were expressed as relative changes (ΔI/I₀·100%) and filtered with a 0.5–18 Hz zero-phase band-pass filter. To remove cardiac artifacts, a data-driven heart-rate–related modeling and regression procedure was applied to estimate and regress out components within the 0.5–5 Hz cardiac band. Finally, single checkerboard reversals were used as events for segmentation and averaging in the −50 to 500 ms window to obtain EROS. During rest periods, pseudo-triggers were generated at the same temporal rate as during stimulation, and the same event-related averaging procedure was applied to provide a resting baseline for comparison. For each participant, we quantified the trial-wise optical response by averaging the signal within the P100 time window (92 ± 5 ms). Trial-wise values from stimulation and rest periods were then compared using a paired-samples t-test (stimulation vs. rest) to assess within-subject FOS contrast.

Results

After adaptive filtering of the multimodal recordings to remove cardiac artifacts, we analyzed data from two participants under different checkerboard reversal frequencies. Stable fast optical responses were observed in a subset of near-infrared channels, primarily those formed by sources located over the central visual cortex and their neighboring detectors. Event-related analyses were performed separately for the three modalities, and, for each condition, approximately 700–900 trials were averaged to obtain MEG event-related fields (ERF), EEG event-related potentials (ERP), and near-infrared–based event-related optical signals (EROS). Figure 1 presents the results from one representative participant.

As shown in Fig. 1A, MEG, EEG, and FOS all exhibited temporally aligned peaks at approximately 92 ± 5 ms and 130 ± 5 ms post-stimulus, corresponding to the canonical P100 and N135 components of visual evoked potentials, respectively. This temporal correspondence suggests that FOS is closely linked to the same underlying synchronized neuronal current flow that gives rise to the EEG and MEG signals. To compare stimulation and rest, data from the 30 s rest periods within each trial were segmented into epochs using pseudo-triggers. As shown in Fig. 1B, within the time window corresponding to the P100 (92 ± 5 ms), a within-subject, trial-wise paired-samples t-test revealed a significant difference in optical responses between stimulation and rest (p = 0.038).

Fig. 1C summarizes EROS waveforms obtained from the effective fast-optical channels under the three checkerboard reversal frequencies. For the 1 Hz and 2.5 Hz conditions, clear peaks were consistently observed around 100 ms after stimulus onset. In contrast, at 5 Hz, the waveforms gradually transitioned to a nearly sinusoidal steady-state oscillatory pattern. For EEG and MEG visual responses, increasing the stimulation frequency does not abolish single-trial VEP components such as P100, but the reduced inter-stimulus interval causes strong temporal overlap of these transient responses. As a result, the recorded signal shifts from isolated VEP components to a sinusoidal steady-state visual evoked potential (SSVEP), in which a distinct P100 peak is no longer easily separable.4 The fast optical signals exhibited a similar transition from transient to steady-state responses across frequencies, further supporting the correspondence between fast optical, electrical, and magnetic signals in the temporal dynamics of visual processing.

Conclusion

In this study, we used a tri-modal OPM-MEG, EEG, and NIRS system to experimentally validate fast optical signals in the occipital cortex. Under 1 Hz and 2.5 Hz checkerboard reversal conditions, EROS showed latency peaks around 95 ms and 130 ms that closely matched the canonical P100 and N135 components in the ERP/ERF, and exhibited significant differences between stimulation and rest, indicating robust neural relevance. As the stimulation frequency increased to 5 Hz, the optical responses evolved from discrete event-related components into a steady-state oscillatory pattern, mirroring the transition from VEP to SSVEP in EEG/MEG. Taken together, these findings provide convergent evidence from magnetic, electrical, and fast optical measurements supporting the existence of occipital fast optical signals and their close temporal coupling to the postsynaptic neuronal currents that underlie EEG and MEG. This strong correspondence is consistent with the hypothesis that FOS primarily reflect membrane-potential–related neuronal activity, and demonstrates the potential of tri-modal imaging for probing fast neuro-optical mechanisms and guiding future large-sample, multi-task studies.
