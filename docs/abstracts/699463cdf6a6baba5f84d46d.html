Introduction

Addictive disorders, including both substance and behavioral addictions, present significant public health challenges, with craving and compulsive use as core symptoms. Although transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) has shown potential in alleviating craving, existing studies exhibit high methodological heterogeneity. Prior meta-analyses have not incorporated stimulation parameters or brain-level mechanisms, limiting clinical applicability. This study aimed to construct an evidence-based, whole-brain causal map linking tDCS interventions to craving reduction, identify optimal stimulation targets, and explore the underlying neurobiological mechanisms.

Methods

A systematic review included 57 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving tDCS interventions for various addictions, yielding 97 effect sizes. Of these, 39 studies provided sufficient stimulation parameter detail for simulation-based analysis. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to assess the efficacy of tDCS. Electric field simulations were performed using individualized head models derived from 120 healthy participants. The electric field strength and its normal component were computed at each cortical vertex based on stimulation protocols. These were correlated with study-specific effect sizes to calculate the Perturbation Effect Index (PEI), which quantifies the causal association between stimulation at a specific brain location and symptom improvement. PEI maps were statistically assessed via permutation testing. Functional and anatomical decoding of PEI maps was conducted using NeuroSynth. Additionally, spatial correlations were computed between PEI maps and multi-modal brain features, including gene expression, receptor distribution, metabolic gradients, and functional topographies. To validate network-level mechanisms, subject-specific virtual brain models were constructed from resting-state fMRI data of 120 methamphetamine use disorder (MUD) patients using a neural perturbational inference (NPI) framework. Simulated stimulation was applied to peak PEI regions, and the resulting whole-brain responses were analyzed. Finally, optimized stimulation montages were computed using distributed target optimization algorithms based on PEI maps.

Results

Meta-analysis confirmed the efficacy of tDCS in reducing craving across addictions (P < 0.001), with significant effects observed in methamphetamine, opioid, and cannabis users (P < 0.01). PEI mapping identified the middle frontal gyrus, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, angular gyrus, and superior parietal lobule as regions most associated with craving reduction. Functional decoding revealed that these regions are involved in executive control, working memory, reasoning, task switching, and language processing (FDR < 0.001). High-PEI regions correlated positively with expression of genes related to synaptogenesis, the 5-HT1B (5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 1B) and monoamine oxidase receptor distribution, and with whole-brain glucose metabolism and key cortical functional gradients (FDR < 0.05). Virtual stimulation of the middle frontal gyrus in MUD patients activated the frontoparietal control network, dorsal and ventral attention networks, bilateral caudate nuclei, right nucleus accumbens core, and right thalamus (Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.05). Two optimized electrode montages (4-electrode and 8-electrode) were developed using the MNI152 template, balancing practical applicability with precision, and constrained within safe stimulation parameters (<2 mA).

Conclusion

This study presents the first whole-brain, causal map linking tDCS to craving reduction across multiple addictive disorders. It identifies the middle frontal gyrus as an optimal target and uncovers multiscale mechanisms involving cognitive control networks, synaptic and receptor-level neurobiology, and metabolic architecture. The findings support the development of individualized and precision-targeted neuromodulation strategies for addiction treatment.
