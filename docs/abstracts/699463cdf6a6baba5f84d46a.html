Introduction

Human vision extends beyond the passive reception of optical signals; it involves an embodied "feeling" of sensory input that shapes sensory valuation. Psychological research suggests that individuals rate prototypical exemplars of objects more positively than atypical ones, a phenomenon often attributed to processing fluency. While this relationship is established for simple stimuli, quantifying the typicality of complex, natural sensory information remains a challenge. We hypothesize that the "universality" of neural representation—defined as the extent to which a visual representation is shared across different individuals—indexes the typicality of perceptual encoding1. In this study, we introduce a measurement termed Representation Agreement (RA), calculated as the inter-subject correlation of representational patterns. The primary objective of this research is to determine whether this universality of representation within the human visual system predicts the sensory valuation of natural images, thereby linking shared neural processing to sensory valuation.

Methods

We utilized the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD), a high-resolution 7T fMRI dataset collected from 8 healthy adult subjects2. This massive dataset represents a significant advancement in sampling depth, with each participant undergoing 30–40 scan sessions over the course of a year. Data were acquired using a 7T Siemens Magnetom scanner at the Center for Magnetic Resonance Research (CMRR), employing high-resolution 1.8-mm isotropic voxels and a whole-brain sampling rate (TR) of 1.6 seconds to capture fine-grained cortical responses. Each subject viewed 10,000 unique natural scene images drawn from the COCO dataset, engaging in a continuous recognition task to maintain attention. A critical subset of 907 images was shared across all subjects, with these images presented multiple times to facilitate robust inter-subject representational similarity analysis and reliability estimation2.

The fMRI time-series were preprocessed using the GLMsingle toolbox, an advanced modeling framework designed to derive accurate single-trial beta estimates by mitigating specific sources of fMRI noise. This pipeline involved three key optimized stages: (1) identification of optimal Hemodynamic Response Functions (HRFs) for each voxel from an empirically derived library of 20 candidate functions; (2) application of GLMdenoise, which utilizes Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on task-unrelated voxels to derive cross-validated noise regressors; and (3) estimation via Fractional Ridge Regression, which applies voxel-wise L2 regularization (shrinkage fractions ranging from 0.05 to 1.0) to stabilize beta estimates for closely spaced trials. This approach has been shown to significantly improve the test-retest reliability and representational quality of single-trial responses compared to standard GLM approaches.

We computed human representational agreement (RA) by calculating the mean pairwise correlation of representational similarity matrices across eight subjects. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined using surface-based mappings provided by the NSD, based on an additional population receptive field (pRF) experiment, a functional localizer (fLoc) experiment, the Wang et al. probabilistic visual area atlas, and the HCP-MMP1 multimodal parcellation. Using this ROI scheme, we examined multiple levels of the visual processing hierarchy, including early visual cortex (pRF-visualrois), place-selective regions (fLoc-places), ventral stream regions grouped into early, mid-level, and high-level stages, as well as regions within the default mode network (DMN), specifically the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC).

To link neural data with sensory value, we utilized average enjoyment ratings from an independent large-scale dataset, BOLD50003, which comprises approximately 5,000 natural images and shares a subset of images with the NSD dataset used in this study. We relied on ratings collected by Zhou et al.4, in which 50 participants rated each image on enjoyment, symmetry, and complexity using a 5-point Likert scale. We averaged enjoyment ratings to establish a robust "ground truth" for general sensory valuation. We further investigated the geometry of neural representations by performing PCA on the image-by-vertex matrices to correlate RA indices with dominant perceptual dimensions (PC scores).

Results

Our analysis revealed that the universality of neural representation significantly predicts sensory valuation, though this relationship is spatially specific1. Qualitative inspection of images eliciting high representational agreement revealed a prevalence of animate subjects and open spatial configurations, whereas images with low agreement typically depicted inanimate objects and closed spaces. Dimensionality reduction analysis provided mechanistic insight: Human RA was found to approximate the raw Principal Component (PC) scores of the representational space. This indicates that high inter-subject representation agreement effectively captures the dominant perceptual dimensions shared across the population (e.g., animacy and spatial openness).

In terms of ROI-specific effects, we observed distinct patterns across the visual hierarchy. We found significant negative correlations between ROI agreement and sensory value (after controlling for complexity) in place-selective areas, mid- and high-level ventral streams, and the PCC (Figure 1). This implies that higher representational universality in these regions is associated with lower sensory valuation. In contrast, early visual areas (V1-V4), the early ventral stream, and the mPFC did not show significant associations. This suggests that the representational universality relevant to sensory valuation emerges at later stages of visual processing where semantic and holistic scene features are encoded, along with high-level evaluative areas like the PCC.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that universality of representation in the human brain is a robust predictor of sensory valuation for complex natural images. We conclude that brain representation agreement is not a uniform metric but specifically captures the most dominant dimensions of perceptual representation—specifically aligning with evolutionarily conserved biases toward animate and open-space environments. This pattern is consistent with a preference for resource-rich environments and supports the view that human sensory valuation is rooted in shared, efficient neural encoding of biologically relevant visual features. Expanding upon recent work by Hosseini et al.5, which examined representation agreement within artificial neural networks at specific network layers, our study extends this framework to human fMRI data by evaluating RA across multiple brain regions of interest. Importantly, this extension includes both the visual hierarchy and higher-order networks such as the default mode network (mPFC, PCC), implicating high-level sense-making and self-relevance processes in universal sensory valuation.

Crucially, our observation of relationship between representational universality and sensory valuation offers a more nuanced perspective on statistical learning theories1. While Dotsch et al. suggest that prototypicality defined by high statistical likelihood is often preferred in social traits such as faces, our results for complex natural scenes reveal a different pattern: highly universal(i.e., typical) representations are associated with lower sensory valuation. This dissociation may reflect a form of "novelty seeking" in natural scene perception, whereby human vision favors a moderate degree of unfamiliar or distinctive features that support adaptation and flexibility to dynamic environments. The prominent involvement of the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), a core hub for the integration of self-referential information, further suggests that sensory valuation of natural scenes relies on diverse self-interpretations evoked by dynamic visual stimuli within spatial openness environment. Taken together, these findings suggest a dual role for human representational agreement: it not only indicate the degree of typicality but also embodies the "typical subspace" of perceptual representation (e.g., animacy). This coexistence implies sensory valuation reflect both visual novelty and typical information in visual environments. Such coexistence may facilitate representation learning of the visual world, enabling cognitive systems to maintain processing fluency while continuing to learn in changing environments.
