Introduction

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder that is characterized by recurrent seizures. In adults, temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is the most common form of focal epilepsy. Although TLE is often associated with mesiotemporal atrophy, research has shown that cortical atrophy often extends well beyond the affected temporal lobe, supporting the view of TLE is a disease of atrophy1,2. Yet, not everything shrinks. Recent studies have indeed complicated the story, reporting patterns of cortical hypertrophy alongside widespread cortical atrophy, but these findings appear sporadically in the literature and have not been examined systematically3,4. Our goal in this study is to characterize both cortical atrophy and cortical hypertrophy in individuals with TLE.

Methods

Participants. We studied 351 adult patients with pharmaco-resistant TLE (154 males, age ± SD = 34.6 ± 10.5 years, 173 right-sided focus) and 97 healthy controls (37 males, age ± SD = 38.8 ± 11.7 years) from the IDEAS dataset 5 . Healthy controls were older than patients (t-value = -3,119 p = 0.002), while sex distribution was similar between groups (χ² = 0,75, p = 0.39). All participants underwent a T1-weighted MRI scan.

Cortical thickness w-maps. We built a normative model of cortical thickness in healthy adults using surface-based, vertex-wise linear models. We then used the resulting β-maps for age and sex, together with the residual variance, to compute vertex-wise w-scores for cortical thickness in each participant. We grouped patients into ipsilateral and contralateral relative to the seizure focus by flipping the right-sided TLE patients so the left hemisphere always represented the seizure side. 

Group-level differences. We compared cortical thickness w-maps between individuals with TLE and controls using BrainStat (https://brainstat.readthedocs.io/). Results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR).

Individual-level maps. To assess the prevalence and spatial distribution of cortical atrophy and hypertrophy at the individual level, we thresholded each patient’s w-map to retain the 10% most atrophic and the 10% most hypertrophic vertices. Thresholded vertices were only retained if they belonged to a cluster of at least 50 connected vertices. We then binarized these vertices and overlaid the maps across patients.

Results

At the group level, individuals with TLE showed marked cortical atrophy in bilateral fronto-parietal cortices, and mesial occipital cortex (all t-values < −3.27, pFDR < 0.05). Individuals with TLE also showed trends for cortical hypertrophy in bilateral orbitofrontal cortex, inferior temporal cortex, and insular cortex (all t-values > 3.25, pFDR < 0.10; Figure 1a).

At the individual level, cortical thickness changes mirrored the group-level patterns. Cortical atrophy across individuals with TLE peaked in the left superior parietal lobule (maximum overlap in 31.1% patients). In contrast, cortical hypertrophy peaked in the bilateral orbitofrontal cortex and left inferior temporal cortex (maximum overlap in 29.1% patients; Figure 1b).

Conclusion

Temporal lobe epilepsy is associated with both cortical atrophy and cortical hypertrophy that extend beyond the temporal lobe. Using a normative model of cortical thickness in a large sample, we found hypertrophic regions alongside widespread atrophy. These results challenge the long-standing view that TLE is defined mainly by cortical atrophy.
