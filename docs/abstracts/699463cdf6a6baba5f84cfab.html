Introduction

The increasing adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in neuroimaging has amplified the need for large, diverse, and well-annotated datasets. However, sharing of neuroimaging data across institutions and borders remains constrained by privacy laws, ethical obligations, and technical limitations of anonymization. Synthetic neuroimaging data—produced by generative models such as variational autoencoders (VAEs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), and diffusion models—has emerged as a promising approach to mitigate these barriers.1 Such data can reduce privacy risks and facilitate open collaboration while maintaining analytical value. Yet, key challenges persist regarding validity, reproducibility, and regulatory recognition of synthetic data as anonymized or de-identified.

Methods

We conducted an integrative review of current generative approaches in neuroimaging, including both 2D and emerging 3D models. The review synthesized literature from machine learning, neuroimaging methodology, and data governance to assess (1) common applications (e.g., data augmentation, missing-modality reconstruction, and cross-site harmonization) and technical challenges (e.g. generating 4D data, memorization), (2) ethical and scientific concerns (e.g., validity, bias, reproducibility, and transparency), and (3) legal frameworks governing data protection, particularly the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the U.S. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), and the Common Rule.

Results

Generative models have demonstrated significant potential for augmenting training datasets and improving model generalization, but key technical challenges include the lack for standardized metrics to assess the validity of data, the difficulty of modeling high-dimensional (3D/4D) datasets, the scarcity of large training datasets for generative models, and the risk of memorizing identifiable features from training data.2 Ethical analysis indicates that failure to reproduce the full demographic and biological diversity of real-world populations in synthetic data can propagate hidden biases and limit generalizability. It further underscores the importance of transparency and model interpretability in sustaining public trust. Without clear reporting on training data, parameter choices, and validation methods, the reproducibility of scientific results derived from synthetic datasets remains uncertain.3, 4 Regulatory analysis reveals a critical gray zone: major frameworks do not clearly define whether synthetic biomedical data qualify as anonymized. This ambiguity complicates institutional review, informed consent procedures, and the secondary use of derived datasets in global collaborations. A consistent, cross-jurisdictional framework for synthetic biomedical data governance is urgently needed.3-5

Conclusion

Synthetic neuroimaging data present a pivotal opportunity to advance open and equitable neuroscience by reducing privacy barriers and broadening access to data. Nonetheless, significant technical and ethical challenges remain, and their responsible use requires close collaboration among neuroscientists, ethicists, and legal scholars. Establishing best practices for model documentation, evaluation metrics, and transparency is essential to sustain scientific trust and integrity. Policymakers also need to clarify the legal status of synthetic data and develop governance frameworks that balance innovation with the protection of individual privacy. Future efforts should prioritize benchmarking, the creation of community standards, and the development of privacy-preserving methods that uphold both ethical rigor and scientific utility.
