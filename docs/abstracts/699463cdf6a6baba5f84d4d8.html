Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) is increasingly conceptualized as a disorder of brain network dysconnectivity, characterized by abnormal anatomical neural connectivity and a consequent decoupling of the brain's integrative thought processes.1 While conventional neuroimaging studies have extensively mapped aberrations in connection strength,2 the fundamental principles governing how information is communicated across these disrupted networks remain poorly understood. Communication models from network neuroscience, such as shortest-path routing, navigation, and random walk, offer a principled framework to quantify the efficiency of information flow beyond static connectivity.3 This study investigates whether SZ involves alterations not only in the brain's wiring but also in its high-level communication strategies. We employed a multi-strategy framework incorporating shortest-path, navigation, random walk, and a hybrid model4 based on multi-modal imaging data from patients and healthy controls (HC). Our aims were to: 1) compare communication efficiency with different strategies across multiple scales, 2) examine routing strategy bias in the context of the hybrid model, 3) correlate communication metrics exhibiting significant between-group differences with cognitive performance, and 4) evaluate their potential for classification. By integrating these analyses, we aim to address a central question: does SZ involve a fundamental disruption in the brain's communication mechanisms, beyond mere static connectivity, that can explain cognitive deficits and serve as a biomarker of the disorder?

Methods

T1-weighted images, resting-state functional MRI (R-fMRI) data and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) data of 58 SZ and 74 HC were obtained from the Centers of Biomedical Research Excellence Project (COBRE). Specifically, the detailed MRI scan parameters and specific information can be found on the website: http://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/retro/cobre.html. The cognitive function of these subjects was evaluated using the Measurement and Treatment Research to Improve Cognition in Schizophrenia (MATRICS) Consensus Cognitive Battery. R-fMRI data were preprocessed as follows: slice timing correction; head motion realignment; structural image segmentation; spatial normalization to MNI space; spatial smoothing; nuisance regression; and band-pass filtering (0.01 - 0.08 Hz). DWI data preprocessing included brain extraction, eddy-current and motion correction, diffusion tensor estimation, and fractional anisotropy (FA) calculation. We employed the Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas with 90 regions (AAL90) for parcellation. Functional connectivity (FC) was quantified as the Fisher z-transformed Pearson correlation coefficient between regional time series, while structural connectivity (SC) was defined by the fiber density between each pair of nodes. For each subject, we computed three communication efficiency matrices using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox, corresponding to the three communication strategies: shortest-path routing, navigation, and random walk, respectively. Each communication matrix is a 90 by 90 matrix in which every element represents the communication efficiency between two brain regions. In addition, following the hybrid routing strategy algorithm proposed by Liang et al.,4 we further computed a regional-level hybrid strategy (represented as a 90-dimensional vector) and a 90 by 90 hybrid communication efficiency matrix for each subject. 

To investigate whether significant between-group differences exist in brain network communication efficiency between the HC group and the SZ group across different communication strategies and at various scales, independent two-sample t-tests were performed for the four aforementioned communication strategies at four distinct scales: global average, regional level, Yeo 7-network level,5 and within-/between-network level. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied accordingly. Subsequently, for those items that exhibited significant between-group differences, we computed Pearson correlation coefficients with each of the eight MATRICS scores separately and performed FDR correction. Furthermore, for the hybrid routing communication strategy, we conducted independent two-sample t-tests to investigate at which Yeo 7-networks significant between-group differences existed in regional communication strategy bias between the HC group and the SZ group. Finally, we applied five different machine learning classification models—including logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), XGBoost, and multilayer perceptron (MLP)—to predict the class of HC or SZ based on six types of features (FC, SC, hybrid routing strategy, and three types of between-network efficiency). This was conducted to investigate which metrics can effectively distinguish between HC and SZ.

Results

For the results of the statistical analysis, as shown in Figure 1A, the communication efficiency of the three strategies—shortest-path routing, navigation, and random walk—significantly decreased in the SZ group compared to the HC group at the global level. At the regional level, no brain regions exhibited significant differences in communication efficiency for any of the communication strategies after FDR correction. At the Yeo 7-network level, as shown in Figure 1B and 1C, both shortest-path efficiency and navigation efficiency significantly decreased in the visual network in the SZ group compared to the HC group. As shown in Figures 1D and 1E, at the within-/between-network level, the communication efficiency between the visual and limbic networks was significantly reduced in the SZ group compared to the HC group for both navigation and random walk strategies. Synthesizing the results from all between-group efficiency comparisons, we found that the communication efficiency of the hybrid routing strategy showed no significant group differences across all scales examined. However, as shown in Figure 1F, in the between-group comparisons of strategy bias, we found significant between-group differences in the visual, somatomotor, and limbic networks. More specifically, compared to the HC group, the SZ group showed a significant increase in bias for shortest-path routing strategy in the visual and limbic networks, a significant increase in bias for random-walk strategy in the somatomotor network, and a significant decrease in bias for random walk strategy in the limbic network. Regarding the correlation results with MATRICS scores, all metrics—except for all between-network efficiency—showed significant positive correlations with the social cognition score (r = 0.270, p-FDR < 0.05 for shortest-path routing efficiency; r = 0.255, p-FDR < 0.05 for navigation efficiency; r = 0.297, p-FDR < 0.01 for random walk efficiency; r = 0.287, p-FDR < 0.01 for shortest-path routing efficiency in visual network; r = 0.324, p < 0.001 for navigation efficiency in visual network, respectively) and the overall composite score (r = 0.230, p-FDR < 0.05 for shortest-path routing efficiency; r = 0.248, p-FDR < 0.05 for navigation efficiency; r = 0.257, p-FDR < 0.05 for random walk efficiency; r = 0.264, p-FDR < 0.01 for shortest-path routing efficiency in visual network; r = 0.361, p < 0.001 for navigation efficiency in visual network, respectively).

For the results of machine learning (Figure 1G), the best performance was achieved by FC using SVM (accuracy = 0.701 ± 0.026). Among SC and the four communication metrics, the between-network navigation efficiency achieved best performance (accuracy = 0.644 ± 0.025), while the hybrid routing strategy achieved the worst performance (accuracy = 0.614 ± 0.021), demonstrated comparable levels of classification performance. The overall classification performance was suboptimal, which may be attributed to overfitting resulting from the limited sample size.

Conclusion

Our multi-strategy, multi-scale analysis provides a nuanced view of network communication disruptions in schizophrenia. The significant reduction in global communication efficiency across three fundamental strategies (shortest-path, navigation, random walk) robustly supports the dysconnectivity hypothesis, indicating a pervasive deficit in information integration. Crucially, this impairment is not uniform but exhibits network specificity. The pronounced deficits localized within the visual network and, notably, in the connectivity between the visual and limbic networks. Furthermore, the observed positive correlations between these communication efficiency metrics and scores in social cognition underscore the functional and clinical relevance of our network measures, linking more efficient communication to better cognitive performance.

A novel insight is the dissociation between communication efficiency and strategy bias revealed by the hybrid model. Although the overall hybrid communication efficiency stayed untouched, the regional preference for specific routing strategies was significantly altered within the visual, somatomotor, and limbic networks. This finding suggests the presence of active, compensatory strategic reconfiguration—a rerouting of information flow—to maintain functional capacity despite underlying neurobiological disturbances. 

In terms of diagnostic potential, while FC yielded the best classification accuracy in our preliminary machine learning analysis, the communication strategy metrics demonstrated comparable, albeit slightly lower, performance. This suggests that metrics capturing the principles of network communication offer valuable, complementary information to traditional FC, potentially reflecting different aspects of the pathophysiology. 

In conclusion, our study indicates that SZ involves not only a reduction in brain network communication efficiency but also a fundamental alteration in the high-level routing strategies that govern how brain networks communicate, offering a more insightful framework for understanding the disorder.
