Introduction

As we age, the brain undergoes numerous changes, including alterations in brain structure, function and connectivity between brain regions. These alterations are accompanied by age-related changes in executive functions (EF), which have a substantial effect on various aspects of daily life, especially during older ages1. Prior studies have linked age-related EF differences to alterations in large-scale functional brain networks, particularly to reduced network segregation and local efficiency in older adults2. However, these findings predominantly stem from analyses of static functional connectivity (sFC), which average connectivity metrics across the entire scan and therefore do not capture the time-varying nature of neural communication. Dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) addresses this limitation by quantifying fluctuations in connectivity over time, which may help to explain EF-related variability in older adults3. Previous dFC studies report that older adults spend less time in dFC states that are characterized by higher segregation between networks. Moreover, a more pronounced switching between states as well as higher prevalences of segregated dynamic states, have been positively associated to EF in young to middle-aged adults4. Hence, these findings suggest dFC to be sensitive metrics to explore age and EF-related differences in functional brain network dynamics that cannot be unraveled using averaged sFC metrics. Therefore, the current study aimed to unravel the hypothesized benefits of using dFC over sFC to reveal age- and EF-sensitive temporal dynamics in a large population-based sample of older adults.

Methods

We analyzed 812 adults (375 females; 55–85 years, MeanAge = 67 ± 7 years) from the 1000BRAINS cohort 5. Resting-state fMRI time series were extracted from all subjects using the Schaefer-100 parcellation. EF was defined by four cognitive tests assessing different facets of executive functions. These included a figural fluency test (i.e. Fünf-Punkte-Test [Jülich version]), a problem-solving test (i.e. Subtest 3 from the Leistungsprüfungssystem 50+), a concept shifting test (i.e. Trail Making test [part B-A]) and a susceptibility to interference test (i.e. Farb-Wort-Interferenztest [Jülich version], similar to Stroop). sFC was computed as parcel-wise Pearson correlations. dFC was estimated using a sliding-window approach, and hierarchical clustering identified groups of FC patterns as clusters for sFC and as recurring states for dFC. For dFC, we extracted four dFC metrics for each subject: 1) state frequency (the proportion of windows assigned to each dynamic state), 2) mean dwell time (the average duration spent in a state before switching), 3) number of transitions (the total count of shifts between states), and 4) the transition probability matrix (the likelihood of moving from one state to another). We assessed pairwise associations between age, EF, and brain metrics measures using covariate-adjusted partial correlations, controlling for sex and education in age–EF and age–brain analyses, and additionally for age in brain–EF analyses. Subjects were divided into younger (N=405, MeanAge=61± 3 years) and older groups (N=407, MeanAge=73± 4years) based on the median age (67 years), and group differences were assessed using robust ANCOVA. To quantify the correspondence between static and dynamic connectivity organization, we computed cross-state similarity by correlating each dFC state centroid with the centroids of the sFC-derived clusters using Pearson correlation. We then plotted these values across subjects and color-coded them by sFC cluster membership to inspect how the sFC clusters correspond to the expression of the same pattern in dFC.

Results

In this study we identified three dFC states (Fig. 1a): State 1: dual-control state with a strong overall connectivity, especially in visuomotor and cognitive-affective control systems; State 2: globally weakly connected state and State 3: attention-action integration state with strong connectivity between attentional-somatomotor networks. Compared with younger adults, older adults spend less time and showed less stability in the dual-control State 1 while they spend more time in the weakly-connected State 2. Furthermore, they showed fewer transitions between these two states (Fig. 1c) and the probability of remaining in the weakly-connected State 2 was higher in the older group (Fig. 1d). Younger old adults showed the opposite pattern and displayed a higher probability of remaining in, and returning to, the dual-control State 1 from the two other states (Fig. 1d). Additionally, we found an age-related decrease in likelihood to remain in the dual-control State 1 or to switch to it from the weakly-connected State 2 (Fig. 1d). This increased likelihood was furthermore positively associated with EF performance (Fig. 1e).

Analogously to dFC, sFC of all subjects were also clustered into three clusters (Fig. 1b). These clusters showed highly similar network architectures to the three dFC states, and Pearson correlations confirmed a clear one-to-one correspondence between them (Fig. 1f). Despite this correspondence, the distribution of these patterns differed markedly between the two approaches: While the low-connectivity pattern (corresponding to dFC State 2) was the most dominant dFC state (53.2%) it appeared least often in the sFC (14.3%). This mismatch motivated a post-hoc comparison of how this configuration is represented using the two methods. Thereby, we examined the relation between sFC cluster assignment and the duration of dFC State 2 (the low-connectivity state; Fig. 1g). Subjects with a highly dominant State 2 in dFC were assigned to the corresponding sFC Cluster 2. In contrast, those subjects spending less time in this state were more often assigned to sFC Cluster 3 or Cluster 1. 

Conclusion

Our findings revealed age-related alterations in dynamic connectivity patterns from control-dominant to low-connectivity states in older adults, potentially due to a reduced flexibility in functional connectivity dynamics in the course of aging. Thereby it is important to note that older old adults not only showed a reduced dominance of the strongly connected dual-control state (State 1) and increased dominance of the globally weakly connected state (State 2), aging was also accompanied by reduced flexibility in dynamic connectivity: Transitions between dual-control State 1 and weakly connected State 2 occurred less frequently with increasing age. In turn, older old adults showed a higher probability of remaining in the low-connectivity configuration, potentially hinting at a process of network dedifferentiation. In line with this, younger adults more often entered or returned to the dual-control state, indicating a greater dynamic access to strongly connected network configurations, a prerequisite for higher cognitive functioning. Hence, the current results not only support previous studies showing higher connectivity to be related to better cognitive performance, but also extend these by showing that transition patterns would be meaningfully related to EF performance, i.e. recurrent access to dual-control State 1 may support cognitive functioning in older adults.

Building on these dynamic findings, our comparison of sFC and dFC highlights the additional insights into brain network configurations that can be captured by dFC. While dFC analysis showed the frequency of entering this low-connectivity configuration across time, sFC summarized the entire scan into a single, static connectivity profile. As a result, only subjects who spent a large proportion of time in the low-connectivity configuration were assigned to the corresponding sFC low-connectivity Cluster 2. However, subjects showing substantial but not permanent periods of low overall connectivity in dFC, were likely to be classified into other sFC clusters (Cluster 1 and 3). This observation directly supports the use of dFC for sensitively detecting transient fluctuations in coupling and might serve as an explanation for the partially contradictory results obtained when examining age-related differences in network configurations using sFC versus dFC 3. Taken together, these findings indicate that dFC captures the full dynamic range of connectivity patterns in aging and may therefore be better suited to detecting subtle connectivity alterations associated with EF.
