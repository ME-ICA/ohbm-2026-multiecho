Introduction

Migraine is a chronic neurological condition characterized by severe headaches and symptoms like photophobia, phonophobia, allodynia, and gastrointestinal issues such as nausea.1 Many attacks are preceded by prodromal signs like fatigue and neck tightness, which, when identified early, can help reduce headache severity. Diagnosis follows ICHD-3 criteria, with patients typically experiencing unilateral, pulsating headaches, worsened by physical activity and accompanied by nausea and light/sound sensitivity.2 About a third of patients experience an aura, while most have migraines without aura (MO). Migraine affects over a billion people globally, is the second leading cause of disability, particularly in individuals under 50, and contributes 45.1 million disability-adjusted life years annually.

Migraine treatment involves medications, with traditional drugs like topiramate and beta-blockers having limited efficacy and low patient adherence. New therapies, such as monoclonal antibodies targeting calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), show promise, though safety concerns remain in certain populations.1 Non-pharmacological treatments like transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) are also being explored for reducing migraine frequency and intensity by modulating brain activity in specific regions.3

Digital twin brain model, which simulates brain activity through computational models, holds potential for testing treatments before clinical trials.4 The Brainnetome Atlas, which integrates multi-scale connectivity data, supports these simulations, offering insights into brain structure and function.5 This study aims to build a digital twin brain model for migraine without aura to predict external stimulation response ,identify optimal treatment targets and provide non-invasive, effective therapies for the future.

Methods

This study included 109 participants: 65 migraine patients (12 male, mean age 39.29) and 44 healthy controls (15 male, mean age 46.55), all recruited from Yongzhou Central Hospital and diagnosed with migraine without aura based on ICHD-3 criteria. Written informed consent was obtained, and the study was approved by ethics committees.

MRI data were collected using a Siemens Skyra 3.0T scanner, including T1-weighted, resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). DWI preprocessing involved motion correction and brain extraction using FSL. For rs-fMRI, the first 10 time points were discarded, followed by slice timing correction, head motion realignment, and spatial normalization to MNI space. Noise reduction was performed using regression modeling and band-pass filtering (0.01 - 0.08 Hz) via the BRANT toolbox. Structural connectivity was assessed by co-registering T1w images to the b0 image and performing probabilistic fiber tracking using BEDPOSTX and PROBTRACKX. A 246-region Brainnetome atlas was used to derive a connectivity matrix, and functional connectivity was evaluated by extracting mean BOLD time series from each ROI and computing a 246×246 correlation matrix.

In this study, we used two metrics to characterize brain dynamics and measure intergroup differences: Probabilistic Metastable Substates (PMS) and Metastability. PMS quantifies brain state dynamics using Leading Eigenvector Dynamics Analysis (LEiDA) to assess dynamic functional connectivity, with phase coherence matrices computed at each time point to measure synchrony between regions. An unsupervised clustering algorithm identifies brain states, and PMS is calculated based on the proportion of time spent in each state. Metastability measures the temporal variability of brain network synchronization, reflecting a non-equilibrium state, and is quantified by the standard deviation of the Kuramoto order parameter, which indicates the brain's flexibility in shifting between functional states while maintaining structural integrity

In digital twin brain research, modeling BOLD signals and virtual modulation is essential. We used the supercritical Hopf bifurcation model with Landau-Stuart oscillators to simulate the dynamic characteristics of BOLD signals. The model simulates local brain region dynamics and couples the oscillations of individual nodes using structural connectivity derived from DWI data. When the model for MO patients and healthy control were built, we obtained the response of each brain region by iteratively increasing the bifurcation parameter a for each region. The response can be defined as the differences between the stimulated brain states and the target brain states.

Results

In this study, we used PMS and Metastability to quantify the differences between the MO and healthy control. Figure 2 shows that there was two significantly different PMS states between the MO and healthy control groups (when the k-means clustering was set to 5 clusters), the probability distribution for the MO group is [0.4011, 0.1490, 0.1596, 0.1483, 0.1420] and the probability distribution for the healthy control group is [0.3996, 0.1809, 0.1575, 0.1480, 0.1140]. For State 2 and State 5, there is significant difference between MO and healthy control (pstate2 = 0.0492, pstate5 = 0.0423). And for the metastability, the MO group’s value (0.1600) is significantly lower than that (0.1753) of the HC group (p = 0.0485).

To quantify the differences between the stimulated BOLD signal and the healthy control BOLD signal, we defined the Perturbation Impact on Recovery (PIR) for each brain region. PIR reflects the difference between the optimal model (before perturbation) and the model after perturbation, using the respective metrics. In this study, we utilized the two metrics to building model and stimulating virtually. To quantify the comprehensive stimulating results, we defined the Average Normalized Ratio (ANR) metric. Figure 2 shows that among the top five most effective brain regions for stimulation, four were located in the occipital cortex (MVOcC-196, LOcC-199, MVOcC-198, and LOcC-207), with the exception of the Amygdala-212.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides strong evidence supporting the occipital cortex as a potential therapeutic target for migraine without aura. Through the use of Hopf model and digital twin brain simulations, we demonstrated that the occipital cortex plays a central role in modulating global brain dynamics, with key high-efficiency perturbation nodes located in the occipital visual cortex. These findings align with existing structural and functional neuroimaging studies, as well as neurostimulation evidence targeting the occipital cortex. Moreover, the occipital cortex’s abnormal excitability and its involvement in cortical spreading depression (CSD) further reinforce its significance in the pathophysiology of migraine. This unique role highlights its potential for targeted intervention, supported by clinical studies showing the efficacy of TMS in modulating visual cortex excitability and alleviating migraine symptoms. Moving forward, individualized neurostimulation and serotonergic receptor modulation strategies targeting the occipital cortex may offer a promising therapeutic approach for MO patients, providing a more precise and mechanistic basis for treatment.
