Introduction

Sleep plays a crucial role in a wide array of brain functions that shape brain health and cognition, yet aging significantly impairs the ability to both initiate and maintain it. Older adults take longer to fall asleep, experience more frequent awakenings, and have lighter, more fragile sleep1. These changes collectively suggest age-related alterations in the neural mechanisms governing sleep-wake transitions. Despite the prevalence of these complaints, their mechanistic origins are not well characterized, especially in the human brain. Animal studies have identified a network of subcortical brain regions, including the brainstem, thalamus and other subcortical nuclei, in sleep-wake control, and recent evidence suggests that hyperexcitability of neurons in wake-promoting  circuits drives greater sleep fragility in older mice2. However, similar evidence for hyperexcitability or circuit-level changes in human aging is lacking. Human studies of whole-brain dynamics during sleep are limited but suggest older adults exhibit higher static functional connectivity (FC) between regions involved in attention and higher-order cognition, potentially impeding the sensory disengagement necessary for sleep3. Crucially, static FC averages over long periods, obscuring rapid, transient fluctuation. Because many age-related changes center on transitions in arousal state – either between sleep and wake or between different sleep stages, these static analyses miss dynamic connectivity changes that likely mediate age-related sleep impairments in older adults. To address these gaps in knowledge, we used simultaneous EEG-fMRI during nighttime sleep to first examine age-related changes in brain dynamics both locked to moments of behavioral awakening. We then explored age-related differences in dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) during sleep as assessed using a Gaussian Linear Hidden Markov Model (GLHMM)4.

Methods

We collected a simultaneous EEG and fast fMRI dataset to investigate how aging impact sleep brain dynamics in young (18-39, n=35, 22 F) and older(60-85, n=42, 22 F) adults during nighttime sleep. BOLD fast fMRI data was acquired at 3T (TR=378 ms, TE=31 ms, 2.5 mm3 voxels, 40 slices). EEG data was acquired using a 32-channel MR compatible EEG cap. Subjects were asked to perform a self-paced button press task while awake in the scanner but allowed to transition in and out of sleep which resulted in the loss and regaining of behavioral responses. We defined behavioral arousals as the first button press after 30 seconds of inactivity (Fig 1AB), and excluded arousals with excessive motion (>0.3mm). We calculated the occipital EEG spectrogram around each arousal and extracted alpha power (8-13 Hz) around arousal (Fig 1D). We extracted the fMRI time series 30 s before/after each arousal to calculate the mean fMRI response for each group across 81 cortical and subcortical regions. To assess dFC, we used a GLHMM4 trained on both parcellated fMRI data and power in notable EEG frequency bands. We selected 6 as the optimal number of states based on fractional occupancy (FO) and across-iteration repeatability measures (Fig 1E-G). We used across-subject permutation testing to test for group differences in state occupancies.

Results

Consistent with prior work5, we identified significant subcortical activation and broad cortical deactivation locked to behavioral arousals (Fig 2A). Regions of the cingulate cortex were significantly activated in both groups (Fig 2B), also consistent with prior work; however, the strength and time-course of arousal-locked cingulate activity is significantly altered with age (Fig 2BC). Other cortical regions including auditory and somatomotor regions also exhibited age-related changes in arousal-locked activity (Fig 2BC). Subcortical activation is weaker right around the moment of arousal, specifically in regions such as brainstem, caudate, and putamen; however, subcortical activity remains elevated afterward, most notably in the caudate and putamen (Fig 2C). This reflects an impaired return to baseline and potentially points to hyperexcitable subcortical circuitry following behavioral arousal state-transitions. A GLHMM analysis also identified significant age-related alterations to whole brain dFC during sleep, exemplified by differences in FO of certain states (Fig 2E). These GLHMM states were coupled to behavioral state: two become significantly more active leading up to and around the moment of arousal (states 3 & 6), while two others (states 4 & 5) become more active post-arousal (Fig 2F). Notably, older adults spent significantly more time in state 6 (Fig 2F), associated with transitioning arousal state, demonstrating that older adults spend more time in an arousal-like brain state. States 4 and 5, which likely represent the previously observed post-arousal dip (Fig 2D), have significantly different FOs for the two groups, potentially reflecting two distinct post-arousal brain states preferential to different age groups.

Conclusion

Aging significantly alters the spatiotemporal dynamics of behavioral awakenings and the temporal characteristics of brain state fluctuations, reflecting how long and how often older adults occupy distinct brain states. Arousal-promoting subcortical and cingulate regions showed significantly weaker but prolonged activation around behavioral awakenings in older adults. Many regions of the somatomotor and default mode network also exhibited age-related changes to their arousal locked activity. Furthermore, we identified an arousal-related brain state that older adults spend more time occupying and two distinct post-arousal brain states that might reflect distinct post-arousal states for young and older adults. Ultimately this work identifies key networks vulnerable to age-related changes during sleep, advancing our understanding of the neural mechanisms of impaired sleep in the aging human brain.
