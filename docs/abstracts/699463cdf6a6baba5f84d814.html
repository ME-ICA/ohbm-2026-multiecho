Introduction

The reproducibility crisis in neuroimaging underscores how analytical flexibility can profoundly shape reported findings.¹ With hundreds of defensible pre-processing and modelling choices, minor methodological variations can yield divergent conclusions, threatening the realization of cumulative and replicable science. Multiverse analysis addresses this challenge by systematically mapping the outcome space of plausible analytic decisions rather than selecting a single best pipeline.2

In this study, we applied a multiverse analysis framework to evaluate the robustness of a debated claim derived from Barbey’s Network Neuroscience Theory, that general intelligence (‘g’) emerges from the small-world topology of brain networks and their dynamic reorganization in support of system-wide flexibility and adaptation.3 While some less recent studies reported positive associations between intelligence and small-world topology,4 subsequent work found no such association.5 This discrepancy offers an ideal case for evaluating how analytic variability affects hypothesis testing by investigating whether this association reproduces across a multiverse of analytic choices.

Our objective was twofold: (1) to quantify the relationship between ‘g’ and small-world brain networks across a multiverse of 72 analytic pipelines using resting-state fMRI data, and (2) to evaluate the robustness of these results across two independent measurement sessions collected on different days. Beyond examining effect magnitude, we highlight how a multiverse approach functions as a reproducibility audit, showing when conclusions reflect analytic robustness rather than methodological arbitrariness.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI and behavioral data from 827 participants were drawn from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult dataset. Participants underwent two 15-minute resting-state scans collected on separate days. Preprocessing followed the HCP minimal pipeline, with subsequent analyses branching into a systematically guided multiverse of 72 pipelines (Figure 1) varying five key decisions. The full multiverse was applied independently to both scanning sessions (144 pipelines):

  1. Global signal regression (included/excluded)

  2. Temporal filtering (bandpass vs. lowpass)

  3. Functional connectivity estimation (Pearson, partial, or wavelet)

  4. Graph representation (binary vs. weighted)

  5. Thresholding (10%, 30%, 50% of strongest connections)

For each pipeline, whole-brain functional connectivity matrices were computed, and small-worldness was quantified using Networkx sigma (σ) and small world propensity measure - Networkx omega (ϕ), along with the average clustering coefficient and characteristic path length. General cognition (g) scores were derived via confirmatory factor analysis of 15 cognitive tasks from the NIH Toolbox and Penn Battery. Correlations between each network measure and g were computed separately for both scan sessions.

To understand how different analytic choices shaped the results, a set of complementary approaches was used to map variability in correlation outcomes across the multiverse. The distribution of correlation coefficients (r) was summarized using descriptive statistics (mean, SD, and range), and a specification curve was constructed to visualize the full outcome space and assess whether any subset of pipelines produced systematically stronger or distinct correlations relative to the rest of the multiverse. Cosine similarity matrices quantified similarities among pipeline outcome patterns, and six embedding algorithms—Locally Linear Embedding (LLE), Spectral Embedding (SE), t-SNE, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), Potential of Heat-diffusion for Affinity-based Transition Embedding (PHATE), and Principal Component Analysis (PCA)—were applied to project relationships among pipelines into a two-dimensional space. Reliability across sessions was evaluated using cross-run correlations between effect sizes (r and r²) obtained from the two independent resting-state scans.

Results

Across the 144 analytic pipelines (72 per session), correlations between g and small-world network metrics were consistently weak and near to zero. For Networkx sigma (σ), correlation coefficients ranged from –0.10 to +0.08 (mean = –0.009 ± 0.039 in Run 1; –0.063 ± 0.056 in Run 2). Comparable patterns of null results were observed for omega (ϕ) (–0.07 to +0.09; mean ≈ 0.00 ± 0.04), average clustering coefficient (–0.11 to +0.09; mean ≈ 0.02 ± 0.05), and characteristic path length (–0.10 to +0.11; mean ≈ 0.01 ± 0.05). Effect-size estimates were uniformly small, with all small-world metrics explaining less than 1% of the variance in general cognition (r² = 0.0014–0.0070). Cross-session reproducibility was low (r = –0.07 to 0.19), which likely reflects methodological limitations of small-worldness measures rather than the absence of a genuine association with general cognition. As meaningful cross-session correlations require robust underlying effects, the observed instability most likely reflects the limited robustness of the small-worldness estimates.

The specification curve (Figure 2) visualized how correlation effect sizes varied across the analytic multiverse. Correlation estimates were symmetrically distributed around zero, with the 95% bootstrap confidence interval forming a narrow band centered at the null. Nominally significant correlations appeared infrequently and were dispersed across pipelines rather than concentrated within particular analytic choices. This pattern indicates that analytic flexibility did not systematically shift results toward positive or negative effects and that the observed null relationship is robust across preprocessing, connectivity estimation, graph construction, and thresholding decisions.

Low-dimensional embeddings using six algorithms (LLE, SE, t-SNE, PHATE, PCA, and UMAP) illustrated the broader structure of analytic variability. All methods showed substantial overlap among pipelines, consistent with the high pairwise similarity observed in cosine similarity matrices. UMAP provided the clearest separation, showing partial clustering of weighted versus binarized networks, which may reflect differences in how these representations encode small-world structure. However, this organization did not correspond to differences in correlation strength with g. Together, these findings indicate that although analytic parameters influence the geometric arrangement of pipeline outcomes, they did not alter the general agreement towards null findings across the multiverse. 

Conclusion

The guided multiverse analysis showed that small-world network topology is not a neural correlate of general intelligence across a wide range of defensible analytical choices. This outcome challenges earlier positive findings and aligns with more recent null results, indicating that under commonly used operationalizations of small-worldness, the association with general cognition is weak and not consistently detectable. These results do not necessarily refute the theoretical link between global brain efficiency and intelligence but instead suggest that the specific metrics of small-worldness commonly used (e.g., Networkx sigma and omega) may not capture the relevant network properties. Within the operational definitions and analytic variations tested here, this relationship was not detectable.

The multiverse framework provided a principled way to examine whether analytic flexibility could produce divergent conclusions or obscure a genuine effect. Across all pipelines, effect estimates remained near zero, and no analytic branch yielded a meaningfully different pattern of results. This strengthens confidence that the null association reflects characteristics of the data rather than idiosyncrasies of pre-processing, connectivity estimation, graph construction, or thresholding. Cross-session consistency further supports this interpretation, demonstrating that the negligible effects are not specific to a single imaging session but may instead reflect the limited robustness of small-worldness metrics themselves.

At the same time, the multiverse revealed how analytic decisions shape the structure of the outcome space even when they do not change the final inference. Low-dimensional embeddings, for example, UMAP showed partial separations between weighted and binarized graphs, highlighting that analytic choices can influence the outcome. However, this did not correspond to differences in correlation strength with general intelligence. By making these patterns explicit, the multiverse framework helps identify when theoretical claims are genuinely unsupported rather than obscured by the idiosyncrasies of a single pipeline.

This study illustrates how a guided multiverse can be applied as a robustness analysis tool in neuroscience. Mapping the analytic decision space exposes where methodological choices matter, where they do not, and how robust an empirical conclusion truly is. In this case, the convergence toward a stable null across 144 analytical specifications suggests that the absence of an association is not an artifact of analytic arbitrariness but a robust property of the metrics examined in the dataset at hand. As the field moves toward more transparent and cumulative science, multiverse approaches can resolve theoretical ambiguities, clarify the limits of commonly used metrics, and support cumulative theory-building. They also help identify decision clusters that influence reproducibility and guide the optimization of analytic workflows.
