Introduction

Dysphagia affects a substantial proportion of individuals following acute ischemic stroke and contributes to serious complications including aspiration pneumonia, malnutrition, and prolonged hospitalization. 1,2 Swallowing depends on distributed cortical, subcortical, brainstem, and descending motor pathways, yet the specific lesion patterns that explain elevated dysphagia risk remain incompletely understood. Prior lesion-mapping studies suggest that middle cerebral artery (MCA) territory injury and disruption of corticobulbar and corticospinal projections are common contributors, but most investigations have not examined arterial territories and white-matter pathways simultaneously while adjusting for lesion volume and age. To address this gap, we analyzed regional lesion burden in a large acute stroke cohort using an arterial-territory atlas and tract-based segmentation derived from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) atlas. We aimed to identify whether specific regions and pathways were associated with dysphagia beyond overall lesion volume to support earlier risk stratification and guide clinical management in the acute setting.

Methods

Participants were drawn from the Stroke Outcome Optimization Project (SOOP),3 a dataset of patients hospitalized with acute stroke in the upstate region of South Carolina. Inclusion criteria included ischemic stroke, high-quality structural T1-weighted MRI, manually traced lesion masks, and dysphagia status documented during hospitalization. The final sample included 595 participants (mean age = 67 years; 306 female). Lesions were manually delineated in MRIcroGL, converted to binary NIFTI masks, and quantified for regional and overall lesion volume. Regional lesion burden was extracted from arterial-territory maps4 and HCP white-matter tracts. 5 Dysphagia was coded as a binary clinical measure (failed vs. passed dysphagia screen). We used statistical models to examine associations between regional lesion burden and dysphagia status. Analyses incorporated relevant clinical covariates and were structured to evaluate both overall and region-specific contributions.

Results

GLMs indicated that regional lesion burden predicted dysphagia beyond the baseline model for both arterial territories and HCP tracts. In preliminary tract-level analyses, dysphagia shows significant positive associations with lesion burden in major descending pathways which include the right corticobulbar tract, superior corticospinal segments, and several frontopontine and pontine pathways (all p < .05). These associations remained even after controlling for total lesion volume, suggesting a region-specific contribution rather than overall lesion burden. Territory-level analyses revealed complementary findings, with increased dysphagia risk associated with lesions in select MCA subregions. Ongoing analyses include refinement of model specificity and evaluation of associations with downstream complications, including aspiration pneumonia.

Conclusion

 In this large acute stroke cohort, dysphagia was associated with lesions in specific arterial territories and descending motor pathways, even after accounting for lesion volume and age. These findings support a network-based model of swallowing impairment and highlight the relevance of corticobulbar, corticospinal, and frontopontine pathways in mediating early dysphagia risk. These regions can be quantified from routine clinical imaging, and these metrics may offer an objective tool to enhance early dysphagia screening and guide decisions regarding swallow evaluation and airway management. Future work integrating diffusion tensor imaging, as well as instrumental swallowing assessments, may further improve these models and clinical prediction.
