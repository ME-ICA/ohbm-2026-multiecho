Introduction

Brain blood vessels change their physical properties with aging due to a variety of mechanisms, including atherosclerosis and arteriosclerosis, affecting measurable changes in their gross morphology. Vascular risk factors that affect these changes, such as diabetes, hypertension, and atrial fibrillation are also associated with dementia risk in aging. Therefore, non-invasive assessment of brain blood vessel morphology is important in studying brain aging. Time of flight magnetic resonance angiography (TOF-MRA) offers a view of large brain blood vessels providing information about aging-related changes to blood vessel morphology. However, studying the role of changes to vascular morphology in cognitive aging and neurodegenerative diseases is presently limited, because studies that pertain to brain aging often do not collect MRA. Fortunately, information about brain vessel morphology is also visible in other MRI contrasts, such as T1w and T2w that are more commonly acquired, but information about blood vessel morphology is not routinely extracted and quantified from these measurements in standard neuroimaging processing pipelines. Here, we introduce a novel tool – the anat2vessels neural network model – that segments and quantifies brain blood vessel morphology in routinely-collected T1w and T2w MR images. 

Methods

We used three datasets: (1) The IXI dataset (https://brain-development.org/ixi-dataset/) was used to train a deep learning model on T1w, T2w and blood vessel masks extracted from matching time of flight (TOF) MRA scans. The dataset contains 582 subjects, of which 568 have T1w, T2w, and MRA imaging present (ages: 19-86, 277 male) with images acquired in 3 different scanners. (2) The Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN) dataset1. Here, 653 subjects (ages 18-88, 322 male) were used, for which T1w and T2w MRI - but no MRA - are available. Data were all collected on the same Siemens 3T Tim Trio scanner, using one set of acquisition parameters. Assessments of the Mini Mental State Exam (MMSE) and cardiovascular measurements were also accessed. (3) The third generation of the Open Access Series of Imaging Studies (OASIS3)2 . The full dataset includes measurements from 1,098 individuals (ages 42-95, 487 male) of T1w and T2w MRI as well as TOF-MRA. Acquisition parameters varied widely, as the data was acquired in more than 10 different scanners (both 3T and 1.5T) over a long period of time. Participants in this study also underwent extensive clinical evaluation and neuropsychological assessments.

The IXI dataset was used to train a model to produce binary segmentations of vessels from T1w/T2w inputs (Fig 1a). For every subject in this dataset, blood vessel segmentation masks were obtained using the COSTA cerebrovascular segmentation deep learning network, which was trained on a multi-site, multi-vendor TOF MRA dataset3. This algorithm produces binary masks of the vascular tree for each input MRA image at an identical resolution to the input. We chose the COSTA model after comparing it with an expert-segmented dataset, as we found that it produced more reliable and accurate results. We trained the nnU-Net ResEnc L model 4 on paired T1w/T2w/T1w+T2w and blood vessel segmentations derived from the MRA images, where T1w and T2w images were resampled to the MRA resolution with linear interpolation. For training and evaluation, we randomly split the IXI dataset into train (N=427) and test (N=106) samples, with an even split of each scanner in each set. Following standard nnUNet procedure, 5-fold cross-validation was conducted within the training set, stratified by scanner. The model was trained with an initial learning rate of 0.01, and linear decay to zero over 1,000 epochs. The batch size was 35, and the patch size was 64x320x320. 

To produce interpretable and anatomically-based predictions of age and disease from the vessel segmentations produced by anat2vessels we extracted a number of features from the masks (Fig 1b). To do so we first produce a “skeleton” of the mask using the Lee thinning algorithm. This is used to define the center-line of each blood vessel, as well as to compute the number of bifurcation points and endpoints in the vessel map. We can also compute an estimate of the radius at each point by computing a distance transform from the center-line to the edge at each point on the centerline. This centerline is then broken up by Bifurcations into a set of individual vessels. We then measure the total length of each vessel as well as the length between the endpoints of the vessel. We use these measurements to calculate tortuosity for each vessel as: length of vessel/distance between endpoints. Finally, we calculate the total volume of a vessel mask by summing the number of voxels in the mask and scaling by voxel size to produce an estimate in mm3. 

In the validation datasets (CamCan, OASIS3), we used anat2vessels to process T1w + T2w images. In parallel, we used Freesurfer to extract cortical thickness features in the Desikan-Killiany atlas parcels. Statistical evaluation of anat2vessels features was done by comparing models that included (1) Cortical thickness features and (2) Cortical thickness features + anat2vessels features, both with age and sex as covariates.

Results

The anat2vessels model produces accurate vessel segmentations. An example test-set demonstrates a close match (Fig 2a) between ground truth (red) and anat2vessels segmentations (green). Test-set Dice scores are: T1w + T2w: 0.522 ± 0.15 (SD); T1w only: 0.35±0.14; T2w only: 0.517±0.15, significantly higher for the T1w+T2w model both in comparison to the T1w model (t426=26.6, p<0.001) and in comparison to the T2w model (t426=1.97, p<0.05). Dice coefficients are not correlated with age: r2=0.01 (T1w), 0.02 (T2w only) and 0.01 (T1w+T2w). Because the model trained with T1w and T2w provides the most accurate results, all subsequent analyses are based on the outputs of the T1w+T2w model. Morphological features extracted from model-generated vessel masks also have very high correlations with morphological features extracted from ground-truth (Fig 2c).

In CamCan, we assessed the contribution of anat2vessels-based features to models of individual variability in several characteristics. First, we asked whether blood vessel features derived with anat2vessels improves models of brain aging. As a baseline we fit a model to cortical thickness features. This model accounts for a substantial portion of variance in aging (adjusted R2=0.6), but a model that contains both cortical and vessel features has a substantially higher adjusted R2=0.69. Analysis of variance between the cortex-only model and the cortex-and-vessels model finds that the model R2 differs significantly, even when accounting for the difference in the number of parameters (F11, 575=13.5, p<0.001). We tested whether vessel features could explain individual differences in participant attributes related to cardiovascular and cognitive aging. We found that vessel features significantly improved the model for systolic blood pressure (F11, 485=2.37, p<0.01), but not for MMSE or diastolic blood pressure (both p>0.05). However, when the data was split into older (above 60 years old) and younger (60 and under) participants, we found that the addition of vessel features improved the model of MMSE in older individuals (F11, 186=1.93, p<0.05) and the addition of vessel features improved modeling of both diastolic (F11, 264=1.91, p<0.05) and systolic (F11, 264=3.13, p<0.001) blood pressure in younger individuals. Furthermore, a logistic regression model that includes both cortex and vessel features classifies individuals with hypertension –  defined as diastolic blood pressure above 90 mmHg or systolic blood pressure above 140 mmHg – significantly better than a model with only cortical features (Fig 2d, F11, 650=3.1, p<0.001).

In OASIS3 a model of aging that included anat2vessels-derived vessel features accounted for significantly more variance (R2=0.53) than a model that only included cortical features (R2=0.49) and this difference was statistically significant (F11, 642=3.9, p<0.001). In addition, a logistic regression model distinguishing individuals that are diagnosed with AD from healthy controls accounted for more variance with cortex and vessel features (pseudo-R2=0.49) than cortical features alone (pseudo-R2=0.46) and this difference was also significant (F11, 666=2.9, p<0.001). Similar results are obtained for a model that distinguishes individuals with impaired cognition from participants without cognitive impairment (pseudo-R2=0.46 with cortex only and pseudo-R2=0.48 with cortex-and-vessels; F11, 666=2.9, p<0.001). We replicated the finding that a model of diastolic blood pressure in participants under 60 is improved by adding anat2vessels features (F11, 31=2.4, p<0.05). We do not replicate the same finding in systolic blood pressure:even though a model adding anat2vessels features results in a substantial increase in model accuracy (from R2=0.7 to R2=0.87), the difference is not statistically significant (F11, 31=2.2, p<0.06). There are only 10 participants under 60 with hypertension in OASIS3, so we could not adequately replicate the logistic regression model findings from CamCan.

Conclusion

We introduce a novel tool for quantification of blood vessel tissue properties in neuroanatomical images, which provides information about individual differences in blood vessel morphology features. The tool that we developed explains variance in age and in other characteristics linked to brain blood supply. Taken together, these evaluations demonstrate that the method has potential for high utility across a range of different datasets pertaining to brain aging, vascular health and the intersection between them. Code is openly available at https://github.com/nrdg/anat2vessels and model weights at https://huggingface.co/huggingbrain/AnatomicalVesselSeg.
