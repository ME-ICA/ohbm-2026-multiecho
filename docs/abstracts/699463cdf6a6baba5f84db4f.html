Introduction

Although surgical resection is widely regarded as an effective resort for treating drug refractory temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), approximately 40% of patients fail to achieve sustained seizure freedom after surgury.1 The thalamus serves as a critical hub within the limbic–temporal circuitry and is thought to play a key role in seizure maintenance, bilateralization, and network propagation, making it a plausible node-level marker of network load and surgical failure risk. Indeed, poor surgical outcome of TLE has been associated with increase in presurgical thalamic nodal hubness measured by resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) TLE.2

Glutamate-glutamine cycle impairment in hippocampal sclerosis has been evidenced by 13C labeling, showing slow rates of glutamate-glutamine cycling, decreased glutamine content, and a relative increase in glutamate content.3 Glutamate and glutamine composite (Glx) levels has been found to increase in the thalamus of patients with idiopathic generalised epilepsy, indicating epilepsy related excitotoxicity.4 Although multiple studies have attempted Glx detection in the hippocampus, thalamic Glx levels have not been characterized in TLE. In this study, we leverage the recent advances in ultrafast Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopic Imaging (MRSI) enabled by SPICE (SPectroscopic Imaging by exploiting spatiospectral CorrElation) technology5 to simultaneously map brain-wide neurometabolite and neurotransmitter levels, such as N-acetyl aspartate (NAA), creatine (Cr), choline (Cho), and Glx, at 3-millimeter resolution, and to investigate whether changes in thalamic Glx levels could predict postsurgical outcome for TLE.

Methods

Data acquisition: 

In this IRB approved study, eighty-three healthy volunteers and seventy-three drug resistant unilateral TLE patients underwent MRSI on a 3.0 T PET/MR system (Biograph mMR; Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Detailed demographic characteristics are presented in Figure 1. All subjects were required to fast 4-6 hours before the scan and remain awake with their eyes closed during the scan. The MR experimental protocols included T1-weighted anatomical images using MPRAGE (1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3), T2-weighted FLAIR (0.4 × 0.4 × 3.0 mm3, coronal view), high-resolution MRSI scans using SPICE (nominal resolution 2.0 × 3.1 × 3.8 mm3, FOV = 240 × 240 × 160 mm3, TR/TE = 160/1.6 ms, 12 min 35 sec), and resting-state fMRI images (3.0 × 3.0 × 3.0 mm3,TR/TE = 3000/30 ms, matrix size = 64 × 64, 35 slices, 200 time points). FDG PET images were obtained at 30–50min post a bolus injection of 3.7 MBq/kg of 18F-FDG with the following parameters: 128 slices per slab, gap = 0.5 mm; matrix size = 344 ×344, reconstructed with 3D OSEM iterative reconstruction (subsets =21, iterations = 4) and post-filtered with an isotropic full-width half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel of 2 mm. Patients were followed up post-surgically for outcome evaluation using Engel score.


Data processing: 

Reconstruction of the MRSI spatiospectral functions was performed as described in previous publications5, which generated metabolite maps of NAA, Cho, mI, Cr, and Glx. The estimated metabolite concentrations were normalized using the water reference to compensate for the B1 inhomogeneity of the head coil used in the experiment. 

The individual Desikan-Killiany parcellation and thalamic nuclei segmentations were obtained from T1-MPRAGE using FreeSurfer v7.0. T1-weighted images and ROI masks were affine transformed to the MRSI and fMRI spaces, using the high-resolution SPICE water image and the time-averaged fMRI image as references via the ANTs toolkit (http://stnava.github.io/ANTs/). Metabolic signals were then extracted from the corresponding ROIs. Regional metabolic measures were standardized in native space: for each ROI, MRSI-derived metabolites and FDG-PET SUVRs in TLE patients were converted to z-scores relative to the HC group.

Resting state fMRI data underwent standard preprocessing including removal of the first 10 time points, slice timing correction, motion correction, regression of nuisance covariates, and band-pass filtering (0.01–0.10 Hz). Functional connectivity was estimated by computing the Pearson correlation coefficients between the mean time series of each pair of ROIs.

Statistical analysis

For group statistical analysis, right TLE (RTLE) cases were left–right flipped so that the seizure focus corresponded to the left hemisphere, and ipsilateral/contralateral values were defined accordingly. In each patient, regional metabolic measures were derived and asymmetry indices (AI =(ips−con)/(ips+con)) were computed for all ROIs. The paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess within-subject differences. Mann–Whitney U test was used for between group comparisons.

Outcome prediction was performed within a nested cross-validation framework combining LASSO-based feature selection with logistic regression. The outer loop used 5-fold stratified cross-validation, and within each training fold a 3-fold inner loop was used to fit a LASSO–logistic model, with class weights set to 0.5/0.5 for good versus bad outcome to mitigate class imbalance. This nested procedure was repeated 20 times (100 outer folds in total). For each feature, we calculated the proportion of outer folds in which it was selected, and on the selected subsets we further fitted ridge-penalized logistic models to obtain average coefficients as a measure of feature importance. The N most stable features were then used to train a final ridge-logistic model, whose performance was evaluated by leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) in all TLE patients with available outcome data.

Results

On the left side of Figure 2A illustrates a representative whole-brain MRSI metabolic map from a patient with unilateral hippocampal sclerosis (HS), alongside the corresponding structural MRI, FLAIR, and 18F-FDG PET images. The ipsilateral hippocampus showed reductions in NAA/Cr and Glx/Cr, whereas Glx/NAA ratio was elevated. Notably, contralateral thalamus exhibited elevated Glx/Cr on the medial side, and elevated Glx/NAA. Among TLE patients, NAA/Cr levels showed significant decrease in the ipsilateral hippocampus and thalamus compared to HC (p<0.05) and compared to contralateral side (pair-wise p < 0.05); FDG uptake levels in the bilateral hippocampi and thalami showed an overall decrease compared to HC (p < 0.001), but ipsilateral FDG is significantly lower than contralateral side only in hippocampus (p < 0.001), not in the thalamus. Glx/Cr did not show significant changes in hippocampus but was significantly decreased in the ipsilateral thalamus compared to contralateral side (pair-wise p < 0.01).

Among patients with post-surgical follow up (N = 31), poor outcome group had elevated Glx in the contralateral thalamus compared to HC (p < 0.05), and to their ipsilateral side (pair-wise p < 0.05); whereas good outcome group showed decreased Glx in contralateral thalamus compared to HC (p = 0.10, n.s.), and to the contralateral thalamus of poor outcome group (p < 0.01). Neither NAA/Cr levels or FDG uptake levels showed distinguishing power towards surgical outcome (Fig. 2B). 

To further investigate whether contralateral thalamic Glx/Cr elevation reflected broader network engagement, we compared resting-state functional connectivity between good- and poor-outcome groups. Relative to good-outcome patients, those with bad outcome showed generally stronger connectivity within temporal lobe–centered networks, with particularly pronounced increases in (i) intra-thalamic connectivity within and between thalamic subnuclei across both hemispheres, (ii) thalamo–thalamo connectivity between contralateral thalamic subnuclei and the ipsilateral thalamus, and (iii) thalamo–temporal connectivity between subcortical nuclei and contralateral temporal cortex (Fig.2C).

Guided by feature selection frequency and average importance in the nested cross-validation, we ultimately retained five stable predictors for outcome modeling: contralateral thalamic Glx/Cr, hippocampal asymmetry index of Glx/NAA, ipsilateral medial thalamic Glx/Cr, ipsilateral lateral thalamic Glx/NAA, and asymmetry index of medial thalamic Glx/NAA. Used individually, these markers yielded AUCs of 0.58–0.82 for distinguishing good from poor outcome (Fig.2D). Combined in a ridge-logistic model, the 5-feature classifier achieved an AUC of 0.95 under LOOCV, highlighting the strong discriminative value of hippocampal–thalamic Glx-based measures for individual outcome prediction.

Conclusion

In this study, we have demonstrated thalamus-centered glutamatergic network reorganization in TLE and its close association with postsurgical outcome by integrating whole-brain MRSI with multimodal imaging. The findings from this study suggest that presurgical elevation of Glx/Cr in the contralateral thalamus can serve as a promising biomarker for predicting surgical outcomes in TLE patients. The combination of metabolic and functional connectivity measures adds a valuable dimension to our understanding of how thalamic involvement impacts the overall seizure network. Abnormally increased Glx in the contralateral thalamus was associated with poorer postsurgical outcome, suggesting deep involvement of this structure in a more diffuse epileptic network. Consistently, resting-state functional connectivity analysis revealed stronger intra-thalamic and thalamo–temporal coupling within temporally anchored networks in the bad-outcome group, in line with the MRSI-detected Glx elevation and supporting the role of the contralateral thalamus as a hyperexcitable network hub sustaining postoperative seizures. These findings suggest that preoperative Glx imbalance within the thalamus and limbic–temporal circuitry may serve as a network-level biomarker useful for supporting more personalized prognostic assessment and surgical decision-making in drug-resistant TLE.
