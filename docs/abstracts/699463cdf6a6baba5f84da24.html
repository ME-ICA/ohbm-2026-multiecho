Introduction

Introduction: Brain hubs are regions with high connectivity that coordinate information flow across large-scale brain systems, spanning both cortical and subcortical structures¹. Graph-theoretical analyses have shown that hubs play a central role in the integration, efficiency, and robustness of brain networks. This conventional approach to hub mapping characterizes hub status using graph metrics estimated from a connectome matrix, providing limited insight into the internal functional organization that may support integrative processing. For dimensionality reduction purposes, connectome matrices are usually estimated on a low-dimensional parcellation of the whole brain (typically 100 parcels), therefore also limiting the overall spatial resolution of these investigations. Furthermore, hubs themselves have been suggested to be functionally multidimensional, composed of multiple overlapping functional components rather than a single dominant process 1. Benefitting from sparse multivariate decomposition of resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) data that explicitly models overlap between networks 2, we estimated how many (sparse number < 10) networks interact within each voxel and identified the corresponding interacting networks. In this work, we tested whether different hub classes—the so-called connector and provincial hubs estimated using conventional graph theory—differ not only in how they are connected to other brain regions, but also in how their internal functional activity is organized, including how many distinct functional components they express, how evenly these components contribute to parcel-level activity, and which large-scale brain networks they are interacting with.

Methods

Methods: Resting-state fMRI data from 50 healthy adults (25 male; age 22–30), each completing two runs (~14.4 min; TR = 720 ms; voxel size = 2 mm isotropic), were selected from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) database 3. Data were minimally preprocessed using the micapipeline (v0.2.3)4, including ICA-FIX denoising and nuisance regression, and aligned to MNI space at 2 mm isotropic resolution. Functional time series were extracted using a whole-brain composite atlas combining the Schaefer 100-parcel cortical atlas 4 and the Tian subcortical atlas 5, providing coverage of cortical, subcortical, and thalamic regions. Using conventional connectome approaches, parcel-wise functional connectivity matrices were first used to compute the participation coefficient, in order to identify connector hubs, which are characterized with broad between-network connectivity and provincial hubs, which are predominantly characterized with within-network connectivity. To do so, participation coefficients were estimated assuming an overall organization of brain activity in the 7 canonical networks defined by the Yeo atlas. Then, to further characterize functional modes associated with these hubs, we applied the Sparsity-based Analysis of Reliable k-hubness (SPARK)³, a method developed by our group which decomposes fMRI data into spatially sparse, overlapping functional and interacting components (“atoms”). SPARK explicitly models overlap by allowing each voxel to express contributions from a small number of distinct functional components, yielding subject-level voxel-wise k-hubness maps (k typically ranging from 1 to 10 interacting networks). To ensure robustness, visually inspected atoms showing clear noise-related spatial patterns or physiological artifacts were excluded prior to downstream analyses. Subject-level k-hubness maps were aggregated over our population to generate group-level probability maps for regions exhibiting k-hubness = 1, 2, 3, and ≥4. Corresponding spatial atoms obtained for each subject (typically ranging from 100 to 300 atoms per subject) were then assigned to specific parcels using a composite measure of spatial overlap and functional amplitude. All atoms, pooled across subjects (typically ranging from 100 to 300 atoms per parcel), were clustered to derive parcel-specific functional modes. The same approach was considered for both connector hubs and provincial hubs identified using the conventional participation coefficient. Finally, for each parcel of interest, mode contributions were summarized using three complementary parcel-level metrics derived from subject-level mode probability maps.

(i) The number of modes was defined as the number of distinct parcel-specific functional modes identified through atom clustering.

(ii) The balance of mode contributions quantified how evenly different functional modes contributed to a parcel, as reflected by the distribution of subject-level mode probabilities averaged within the parcel.

(iii) The diversity of mode contributions was quantified using normalized Shannon entropy of the same parcel-level mode probability distribution.

To assess the large-scale network affiliations of these modes, mode probability maps were further decomposed with respect to atlas-defined canonical networks, yielding parcel-level measures that characterize whether functional modes preferentially engage a single network or are distributed across multiple networks.

Results

Group-level k-hubness probability maps indicated that connector hubs more frequently exhibited multimodal configurations (k ≥ 2), whereas provincial hubs were more often characterized by unimodal organization (k = 1; Fig. 1A). This important result highlights overall concordance between conventional graph theory using participation coefficient and SKARK k-hubness. Moreover, our results suggest that connector hubs are more likely supporting overlapping functional components, even at the voxel level.  At the parcel level, connector hubs expressed a larger number of functional modes with more evenly distributed contributions across modes and higher entropy, reflecting greater internal functional diversity (Fig. 1B). In contrast, provincial hubs were typically supported by fewer modes, with activity dominated by one or two components, consistent with a more specialized functional organization.

Analysis of the large-scale network affiliations of these modes further showed that functional modes within connector hubs were distributed across multiple canonical cortical and subcortical networks, whereas modes within provincial hubs were more strongly associated with a single dominant network (Fig. 1C). Together, these results indicate that connector hubs differ from provincial hubs not only in their connectivity topology, but also in how their internal functional components are organized and distributed across brain networks.

Conclusion

These findings show that graph-defined hub categories differ not only in connectivity topology but also in the internal structure and network composition of their functional modes. Connector hubs exhibit organization supported by multiple overlapping functional modes drawn from several large-scale networks, consistent with integrative processing, whereas provincial hubs display more constrained functional architectures supported by fewer, more specialized modes. By linking graph topology with sparsity-derived functional mode organization, this multiscale framework provides a principled approach for characterizing hub organization and may help explain the selective vulnerability of connector hubs in disorders involving disrupted large-scale integration, including epilepsy and other neuropsychiatric conditions.
