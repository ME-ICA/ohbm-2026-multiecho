Introduction

A unique human ability is to express ideas through language in various modalities, such as spoken, written, and signed. Sign language offers a unique and natural opportunity to explore the cognitive and neural mechanisms of language processing, as its linguistic structure is encoded in movement and space and decoded through vision. Yet, the neural network supporting sign language processing is poorly understood compared to spoken and written language. Existing research suggests that sign language is configurationally equivalent to spoken language.1 Evidence for this comes from findings that signed and spoken language comprehension engage similar cognitive processes—such as lexical, semantic, and phonological mechanisms2—and recruit largely overlapping left-hemispheric cortical networks, including the left occipitotemporal cortex, superior temporal sulcus, inferior frontal gyrus, and inferior parietal lobule.3 

Using a dynamic causal modeling (DCM) approach and building on established DCM models for spoken4 and written5 language comprehension, this study constructed a DCM model to characterize the interacting neural regions during sign language comprehension. The model includes six left-hemisphere regions of interest (ROIs): the inferior occipital (iO) gyrus, the ventral occipitotemporal (vOT) cortex, the posterior and anterior portions of the superior temporal sulcus (pSTS, aSTS), the pars orbitalis of the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), and the inferior parietal lobule (IPL). We expected to observe multiple pathways from the visual occipital cortex to the semantic frontal cortex. This study also compared the neural network supporting sign language comprehension with those for spoken and written language, which would provide insights into what aspects may be universal and what aspects are affected by modality for language organization in the human brain. 

Methods

Participants. Three groups of hearing native Spanish-speaking participants with varying experience in Spanish Sign Language (LSE) took part in the study: 22 proficient native signers (4 males; mean age = 40 years), 20 proficient L2 signers (5 males; mean age = 35 years), and 22 non-signers (4 males; mean age = 40 years). 

Stimuli. Three types of stimuli were designed for each modality: sign stimuli (signs, pseudosigns, scrambled signs), spoken stimuli (words, pseudowords, rotated speech), and written stimuli (words, pseudowords, scrambled words). Each stimulus type in each modality consisted of eighty experimental items and sixteen additional items (eight practice items, eight response items with a superimposed pink arrow). 

Procedure. An event-related fMRI design was used, with stimuli from all types in each modality intermixed across runs. Within each run, each trial lasted for two seconds, during which the stimuli were presented, and then the screen remained blank for the remaining time. Participants were instructed to finish a go/no-go task: viewing or listening to the experimental items passively (no-go trials), or for the response items indicating the direction of the arrow (go trials). The go trials were discarded from the analysis. 

Data collection and preprocessing. Whole-brain MRI data were acquired using a 3T MRI scanner. The fMRI data were preprocessed in SPM8, including slice-timing correction, realignment, volume repair, normalization to MNI space, spatial smoothing, and high-pass filtering. Individual-level general linear models modeled each trial with six head motion covariates, and three contrasts per modality (All > Null, Wordlike > Null, Word > Baseline) were submitted to group-level one-sample t-tests (FDR corrected p = 0.05) to identify brain regions during language comprehension. 

DCM analyses. DCM investigates how brain regions interact with one another during different experimental contexts, which estimates three sets of parameters: (A) intrinsic connectivity reflects context-independent coupling between regions; (B) modulatory connectivity measures modulation on intrinsic connectivity by experimental variables; (C) driving input quantifies the direct influences of experimentally designed inputs on specific brain regions. DCM analyses were performed using SPM12 due to a far more robust optimization scheme than SPM8. We first established a dynamic causal model with six left-hemispheric ROIs for sign language (Figure 1A): The iO region for primary visual processing; the vOT cortex for complex visuospatial processing; the pSTS and aSTS for phonological and semantic processing, respectively; the IFG for higher-order semantic integration and control; and the IPL for the hand configuration and spatial processing. This model was then adapted for written language by removing the backward connections of pSTS→IPL→vOT due to the absence of specific phonological and spatial processing (Figure 1B), and adapted for spoken language by excluding the left iO region due to the lack of visual input, making the left pSTS the driving input instead (Figure 1C). Next, to identify coordinates of the six ROIs at the group level, we applied small volume correction (p < 0.05) for spheres of 10 mm radius centered on coordinates based on previous DCM studies45 in the equivalent contrasts across three modalities (iO was identified in the All > Null contrast; vOT, pSTS, and IPL from the Wordlike > Null contrast; left aSTS and IFG from the Word > Baseline contrast). Then, for each participant, the time series of each ROI was extracted as the first principal component of all voxel time series within a 4-mm spherical radius centered on the individual local maximum. Finally, the three models were estimated, and the posterior estimates (i.e., driving inputs and intrinsic connectivities) were entered into one-sample t-tests to test whether they are significantly different from zero by using SPSS 20.

Results

All parameters of the models were reported under a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (corrected p-value = 0.05/number of connections in each model). 

Sign language model. The random effects analysis (i.e., one-sample t-tests) on the parameters (driving input, intrinsic connectivity) of the sign language model for signers (i.e., native signers and L2 signers) yielded the following results (corrected p-value = 0.0031). As shown in Figure 1A, the parameter for the driving input into the left iO was not significantly different from zero (mean = 0.008 Hz; t(35) = 0.668, p = 0.509). The parameters for 10 (out of 16) forward intrinsic connections were significantly greater than zero (ts ≥ 3.170, ps ≤ 0.003), indicating that the visual cortex (iO) activation flows into higher-order semantic area (IFG) via multiple pathways: iO→vOT→pSTS→IFG and iO→pSTS→IFG. Importantly, the connections of vOT↔IPL←pSTS were significant (ts ≥ 3.170, ps ≤ 0.003), while the IPL→pSTS connection was marginally significant (mean = 0.034 Hz, t(35) = 2.732, p = 0.010). In addition, there were multiple connections reflecting feedback activation: the significant connection of pSTS→vOT (mean = 0.035 Hz, t(35) = 3.421, p = 0.002), marginally significant connections of pSTS→iO (mean = 0.051 Hz, t(35) = 3.125, p = 0.004) and IFG→aSTS (mean = 0.032 Hz, t(35) = 2.929, p = 0.006), and nonsignificant connections of aSTS→pSTS and IFG→pSTS (ts ≤ 0.001, p > 0.003). All of the intrinsic connection parameters were positive, suggesting that activation in one of the brain regions flows into its connected regions, thus increasing the activation level there. 

Written language model. The random effects analysis (i.e., one-sample t-tests) on the parameters (driving input, intrinsic connectivity) of the written word model for all participants yielded the following results (corrected p-value = 0.0035). As shown in Figure 1B, the parameter for the driving input into the left iO was significantly different from zero (mean = -0.059 Hz; t(58) = -4.502, p < 0.001), which suggests that the input of written words exerts activation in the left iO. The parameters for all forward intrinsic connections were significantly greater than zero (ts ≥ 8.330, ps < 0.001), showing that the visual cortex (iO) activation flows into higher-order semantic areas (IFG) via multiple neural routes: iO→vOT→aSTS→IFG, iO→vOT→pSTS→IFG, iO→pSTS→IFG, and iO→vOT→IPL→pSTS→IFG. The connections reflecting feedback activation, including IFG→aSTS→pSTS→vOT and IFG→pSTS→iO, were also significant (ts ≥ 6.288, ps < 0.001). All of the intrinsic connection parameters were positive, indicating that activation in one of the brain regions flows into its connected regions, thus increasing the activation level there.

Spoken language model. The random effects analysis (i.e., one-sample t-tests) on the parameters (driving input, intrinsic connectivity) of the spoken word model for all participants yielded the following results (corrected p-value = 0.0045). As shown in Figure 1C, the parameter for the driving input into the left pSTS was significantly different from zero (mean = -0.025 Hz; t(59) = -3.020, p = 0.004), indicating that the input of auditory words exerts activation in the left pSTS. The parameters for all forward intrinsic connections were significantly greater than zero (ts ≥ 6.254, ps < 0.001), indicating that the auditory cortex (pSTS) activation flows into higher-order semantic areas (aSTS and IFG) via multiple neural routes: pSTS→IFG, pSTS→vOT→aSTS→IFG, and pSTS→aSTS→IFG. In addition, the connections reflecting feedback activation, including IFG→aSTS→pSTS, IFG→pSTS, and vOT→pSTS, were significantly different from zero (ts ≥ 18.982, ps < 0.001). All of the intrinsic connection parameters were positive, suggesting that activation in one of the brain regions flows into its connected regions, thus increasing the activation level there.

Conclusion

In summary, this study investigated the neuronal network that underlies the comprehension of sign language by using the DCM approach, and also compared the neuronal network with those supporting spoken and written language comprehension. There are four main findings. 

First, the left pSTS region is the critical hub that transmits activations to other regions in the sign language model. This indicates that comprehending sign language preferentially activates pSTS—driving higher-level processing in the semantic aSTS and IFG after receiving low-level input from the visuospatial iO and vOT regions—thereby reflecting the crucial phonological processing involved in sign language comprehension. 

Second, in the sign language model, there are at least two pathways from the visual cortex (iO) to the higher-order semantic area (IFG): iO→pSTS→IFG and iO→vOT→pSTS→IFG. These two pathways suggest that semantic information (in the IFG region) can be accessed from the visual cortex (in the iO region) directly or via a visuospatial interface (in the vOT cortex) when comprehending sign language. 

Third, the dynamic causal models for three modalities are largely overlapping, although there are slightly different connections. This suggests that the neural network supporting sign language comprehension shares the same architecture with spoken and written language comprehension, that is, multiple pathways from perceptual to higher-order semantic areas. This further implies that sign language processing, despite its visual-spatial modality, relies on similar cognitive and neural mechanisms to spoken and written language. 

Fourth, the sign language model shows a slightly different pattern of connectivity from those for the other two modalities. Specifically, an extra backward connection of pSTS→IPL→vOT reflects specific roles of the left IPL (e.g., highly interactive processes of phonological assembly and visuospatial components) in sign language processing. The weak connection of aSTS↔IFG suggests that sign language processing may involve alternative routes for semantic integration compared to the other two modalities. The other weak connections (pSTS→iO, IPL→pSTS) and absence of connections between regions (from IFG/aSTS to pSTS) may reflect weaker top-down influences from higher-level to lower-level processing during sign language comprehension compared to spoken and written modalities.

Overall, the most striking finding of this study is that the model for spoken or written language also accounts for the neural architecture underlying sign language processing, with multiple links between the processing of perceptual input and phonological or semantic processing. While the nodes of the models are the same, the differences in connectivity provide new avenues for exploring how modality shapes language processing. This study therefore provides important insights into the connectivity architecture of neural substrates involved in sign language processing, and also confirms the existence of a cross-modal architecture for language comprehension.
