Introduction

As social beings, our survival depends on the ability to perceive and interpret the behaviors of others. This capacity relies on the integrated functioning of distributed brain systems, particularly the action observation network (AON), which supports kinematic and motor representation, and the mentalizing network (MN), which underlies inferences about others’ intentions and mental states1. While these systems have often been studied separately, real-world social interaction perception likely requires their complementary engagement: the AON contributes to decoding how an action is performed, whereas the MN supports reasoning about why it is undertaken. How these networks dynamically interact to guide judgments about social behavior remains an open question.

Here, we examined how the AON and MN cooperate when observers judge social action or infer the underlying intentions behind it. Furthermore, building on prior evidence that increasing perceptual ambiguity drives social processing toward a more centralized decisional regime, we asked whether the contributions of the AON and MN are modulated by the level of stimulus uncertainty. Specifically, we test whether their functional interplay shifts under heightened ambiguity, potentially reflecting adaptive neural strategies for resolving uncertain social cues.

Methods

We designed a magnetoencephalography (MEG) experiment involving 11 participants, utilizing Gaussian-blurred images containing social information derived from a previously established and widely implemented paradigm2. This approach was developed to differentiate between the AON and the MN, thereby creating an experimentally operational paradigm amenable to quantitative analysis. Participants were presented with an image depicting two individuals engaged in an interaction. Each image was presented with two corresponding questions, asking participants to either judge the type of social interaction (e.g., “Is he smiling?”) or infer the underlying intention behind the interaction (e.g., “Is he helping someone?”). Ambiguity was systematically controlled by the sigma(σ) level of the Gaussian blur: 8, 16, and 24, generating three ambiguity levels.

In the analysis, using MNE-Python, we segmented each trial as an epoch between -700 ms and 0 ms relative to participants’ judgment corresponding to decision making. In source localization, first, the repeated trials were averaged into five pseudo-trials through a random selection to obtain a high signal-to-noise ratio for source-level decoding. Then, the entire brain was parcellated into 181 regions based on the HCP multi-modal parcellation 1.0 atlas3, with the corresponding regions in the left and right hemispheres integrated. Finally, based on multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA), we quantified the extent to which neural activity patterns could decode participants' judgments of social interactions (How task) and intentions (Why task) across all region-of-interest (ROI) cluster combinations (AON alone, MN alone, and AON+MN) under varying ambiguity levels (low, medium, high).

Results

The behavioral results indicate that increasing the sigma of the Gaussian blur effectively created low, medium, and high levels of stimulus ambiguity, leading to longer response times and reduced accuracy.

MEG findings revealed neural collaboration mechanisms underlying the perception of social behaviors. In the social interaction type judgment task, we found that activity within either the AON or the MN alone was sufficient to decode task judgments under low and medium levels of ambiguity. However, as ambiguity increased, MN's contribution gradually decreased. In this high ambiguity condition, AON alone achieved higher decoding accuracy than the AON+MN combination. The results of the intention inference task exhibited a similar pattern: both networks independently decoded judgments under low and medium levels of ambiguity. With increasing ambiguity, AON's contribution gradually diminished, while MN played a more prominent role.

Conclusion

This MEG study reveals adaptive neural mechanisms underlying social perception, demonstrating that stimulus ambiguity could modulate decision strategies. As ambiguity increases, task-specialized brain networks gain dominance while reducing reliance on auxiliary networks, leading to a shift from cooperative processing to centralized processing in the brain's decision-making mode. Taken together, these results revealed how two networks collaborate in different contexts to complete cognitive tasks.
