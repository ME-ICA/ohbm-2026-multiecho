Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder and is characterized by motor symptoms such as bradykinesia with rigidity, rest tremor, and postural instability. Non-motor symptoms also impose a substantial impact on quality of life. Although subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN-DBS) is effective in improving motor symptoms, its effects on neuropsychiatric outcomes remain inconclusive.1

Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) reflects dynamic interactions among brain regions by detecting spontaneous low-frequency fluctuations in the blood oxygen level–dependent (BOLD) signal. Most prior rs-fMRI studies have examined connectivity changes related to motor symptoms in the on- and off-DBS states. However, few investigations have explored how intrinsic disease-related connectivity patterns and surgery-related connectivity changes contribute to postoperative non-motor outcomes.2

Using rs-fMRI acquired from PD patients before and after DBS surgery, as well as from healthy controls, this study aims to elucidate (1) intrinsic connectivity alterations associated with PD and (2) connectivity changes attributable to the surgical procedure that relate to short-term neuropsychiatric outcomes following STN-DBS.

Methods

       Preoperative and one-month postoperative MRI scans were obtained from PD patients who underwent bilateral STN-DBS at Taichung Veterans General Hospital (VGHTC) between October 2018 and June 2024. Preoperative rs-fMRI images were acquired using a 3T Philips MRI scanner, whereas postoperative MRI examinations were conducted on a 1.5T Siemens MRI scanner due to safety considerations following DBS implantation. Healthy control participants were recruited, divided equally into the 3T MRI and 1.5T MRI groups between Aug 2024 and Jul 2025.

Resting-state functional data were collected using a gradient echo–planar imaging sequence with axial orientation (3T Philips: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 35 ms, flip angle = 90°, 34 slices with 4-mm thickness and 0.4-mm gap, voxel size = 2.4 × 2.4 × 4.4 mm³; 1.5T Siemens: TR = 3000 ms, TE = 50 ms, flip angle = 90°, 37 slices with 4-mm thickness and 0-mm gap, voxel size = 3.4 × 3.4 × 4 mm³).

High-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE images were also acquired (3T Philips: TR = 6.4 ms, TE = 3.7 ms, flip angle = 8°, 190 slices, voxel size = 0.7 × 0.7 × 1.0 mm³; 1.5T Siemens: TR = 5 ms, TE = 2.8 ms, flip angle = 8°, 176 slices, voxel size = 0.8 × 0.8 × 1.0 mm³).

All PD patients underwent a series of neuropsychiatric assessments before surgery and again six months after DBS implantation. The test battery included the Cognitive Abilities Screening Instrument (CASI), Beck Depression Inventory–II (BDI-II), Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), and the Questionnaire for Impulsive-Compulsive Disorders in Parkinson’s Disease–Rating Scale (QUIP-RS).

       All rs-fMRI data were analyzed using the CONN toolbox (https://web.conn-toolbox.org/home) following four main steps: preprocessing, denoising, first-level analysis, and group-level comparison/regression. Seed-based connectivity (SBC) maps and ROI-to-ROI connectivity (RRC) matrices were generated at the first level, with each voxel evaluated for both short- and long-range connections to the rest of the brain. Group comparisons were performed between healthy controls and PD-DBS patients for both the 3T and 1.5T MRI cohorts. Regression analyses were conducted to examine associations between brain connectivity patterns and each clinical measure separately, controlling for age and sex.

Results

In the 3T MRI cohort, rs-fMRI scans were obtained from 20 healthy controls and 26 PD patients prior to DBS surgery, with no significant differences in age (62.30±4.64 vs. 61.88±7.60 years, p = 0.965) or gender distribution. In the 1.5T MRI cohort, 20 healthy controls and 38 PD-DBS patients were included; although healthy controls were younger (60.10 ± 5.16 vs. 63.28 ± 6.88 years, p = 0.049), gender distribution was comparable between groups.

After adjusting for age and sex, several seed-based connectivity patterns that differed between PD-DBS patients and healthy controls in the 3T dataset were associated with neuropsychiatric outcomes. Connectivity between the left supplementary motor cortex and the right parietal operculum cortex and right central opercular cortex correlated with post-DBS changes in CASI scores (Fig. 1A,B). In addition, connectivity between the left planum temporale and the right paracingulate gyrus/anterior cingulate gyrus correlated with changes in QUIP-RS scores after DBS (Fig. 1C,D).

In the 1.5T MRI cohort, connectivity differences between healthy controls and PD-DBS patients were also correlated with postoperative changes in CASI and QUIP-RS scores. For CASI, postoperative cognitive changes were associated with connectivity between the left precentral gyrus and the left superior division of the lateral occipital cortex (Fig. 1E,F); the right postcentral gyrus and both the left superior division of the lateral occipital cortex and the posterior division of the cingulate gyrus; the left superior division of the lateral occipital cortex and both the left temporo-occipital inferior temporal gyrus and the left inferior division of the lateral occipital cortex; and the connectivity between the right putamen and the left precentral gyrus (Fig. 1I,J).

Post-DBS changes in QUIP-RS scores were correlated with connectivity between the left cerebellar region 7 and the right frontal pole (Fig. 1G,H), as well as connectivity between vermis region 3 and vermis region 4/5, right cerebellar region 4/5, and right cerebellar region 3 (Fig. 1K,M). All reported correlations remained statistically significant after adjusting for age and sex.

Conclusion

In the 3T MRI cohort, healthy controls showed stronger connectivity between the left supplementary motor cortex and the right parietal operculum cortex and right central opercular cortex, and this connectivity pattern correlated with less cognitive decline six months after DBS surgery. This finding suggests that disease-related alterations in connectivity may influence cognitive preservation following DBS implantation. In the post-surgical 1.5T MRI cohort, healthy controls similarly demonstrated stronger connectivity than PD-DBS patients, and a comparable pattern emerged for the connectivity between the left precentral gyrus and the left superior division of the lateral occipital cortex. In contrast, connectivity between the right putamen and the left precentral gyrus was associated with poorer postoperative cognitive outcomes. Taken together, these results imply that the preservation of specific functional networks may confer cognitive benefits after surgery, whereas reduced intrinsic connectivity in PD may reflect compensatory mechanisms aimed at maintaining cognitive resilience.3 The microlesion effects induced by the surgical procedure may transiently enhance these compensatory network responses, contributing to favorable cognitive outcomes in some patients.

Connectivity between the left planum temporale and the right paracingulate gyrus/anterior cingulate gyrus in the 3T MRI cohort, as well as connectivity between the left cerebellar region 7 and the right frontal pole in the 1.5T MRI cohort, was relatively stronger in heathy controls than PD-DBS patients. Both connectivity patterns were negatively correlated with postoperative impulsive–compulsive symptom severity, suggesting that altered network activity in PD may predispose patients to greater vulnerability to impulsive–compulsive behaviors compared with the general population.4 In the postoperative MRI cohort, PD patients also demonstrated stronger connectivity between vermis region 3 and vermis region 4/5, right cerebellar region 4/5, and right cerebellar region 3 than healthy controls. This connectivity pattern showed a positive correlation with increasing QUIP-RS scores, potentially reflecting mechanisms underlying de novo impulsive–compulsive disorders observed in some patients after DBS surgery, as reported in previous studies.

The results of this study indicate that both pre- and post-DBS functional MRI provide valuable information for predicting neuropsychiatric outcomes, including cognitive and impulsive–compulsive domains. These findings suggest that outcome variability may reflect a combination of intrinsic disease-related connectivity characteristics and connectivity changes induced by the surgical procedure.
