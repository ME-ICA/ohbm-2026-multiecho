Introduction

Brain maps describe the spatial variation of different properties (e.g., structural, functional, and genetic) across cortical and subcortical regions.^1 Quantifying the covariation between these maps has revealed important associations between diverse brain phenotypes. A well-known feature of these brain maps is their smoothness, which is commonly attributed to spatial autocorrelation. Numerous statistical methods, such as the seminal spin test, have been developed to control false positives arising from autocorrelated data when testing associations between brain maps.^2

However, the intrinsic smoothness does not solely reflect spatial autocorrelation. Spatial trends—large-scale, coordinate-dependent, deterministic changes in the mean—also generate smooth patterns that fundamentally differ from local, distance-dependent, stochastic autocorrelation.^3 Conflating these two concepts and interpreting spatial trends as autocorrelation may lead to biased inferences: overestimated spatial autocorrelation, underestimated effective sample size, and inaccurately quantified effect size ultimately increase the risk of false discoveries.

Here, we distinguish spatial trend from autocorrelation and provide empirical evidence that spatial trends are present in brain maps. We demonstrate simulation evidence of how overlooking spatial trend can distort the results of spatial association tests and present methods to account for trending confounds. We apply our methods to brain map association tests and report the empirical impact of spatial detrending. Our results highlight the need to account for such overlooked spatial trends in neuroimaging studies.

Methods

A spatial process X observed at coordinates s can be decomposed into a mean component m and an autocorrelated stochastic term ε (Eq 1). Spatial trend describes the situation where the mean m is coordinate dependent and systematically change across the space (Eq 2, i.e., change as a function of coordinates s). In contrast, spatial autocorrelation characterizes the covariance structure of ε, typically assumed to decrease with increasing spatial separation h (or lag distance) between observations (Eq 3). Studies associating brain maps often assume a constant mean of observed brain properties across the cortical and subcortical space (i.e., spatial trend is absent). Under this assumption, the covariance converges to 0 at long distances, and observations eventually become independent, characterized by the presence of plateau (sill) in the variograms (Fig 1A). However, when spatial trends are present, variograms may not exhibit a sill, indicating a persistent, large-scale data structure (Fig 1B).^3,4

Spurious autocorrelation between two spatial maps can arise when their trends are not appropriately modelled. Spatial coordinates may act as confound factors (when both maps show coordinate dependent trends), where related patterns between maps of interest can be driven by a common, third spatial process. These uncaptured confounds can alter effect sizes and drive false discoveries, like how false conclusions can be drawn in neuroimaging studies when age, sex, and site factors are not appropriately controlled.

To account for trends and spatial confounds in brain map association tests, we propose to decompose the brain map X into spatial trends m and autocorrelated residuals ε. Spatial trends are derived using a Laplacian regularized estimation m=(I + λK)^(-1)X, where K is the cotangent Laplacian, and the non-negative λ is a regularization parameter to be optimized.^5 The detrended residual is computed by ε=X-m. Setting λ=0 (i.e., no regularization) yields m=X, whereas an appropriate choice of λ allows removal of low-frequency trends while retains meaningful, high-frequency, local autocorrelated structure in residuals. For empirical brain maps, we fit trends and residuals using λ = 10^[-1, 0, 1, 2, 3] and optimize λ by inspecting the variograms of residuals and selecting the value corresponding to a stable sill indicative of absence of trends (Fig 1C-1E).

In the results section, we first demonstrate empirical evidence of spatial trends in representative brain maps by examining their variograms and spatial distributions. We then present simulation evidence that uncaptured spatial confound can lead to false discoveries. Our proposed method is applied to detrend brain maps, and associations between detrended residuals are evaluated using spin test and compared with results derived from raw brain maps.

Results

We examined five representative brain maps, including the gene expression PC1, neurosynth, T1/T2 ratio, cortical thickness, and the first functional cortical gradient (Fig 2A). Variogram sills are absent in all maps, and a continuously increasing semivariance beyond sample variance at large lag distances is observed (Fig 2B). In addition, brain map values are found to systematically change with spatial coordinates (Fig 2C). These findings suggest the presence of spatial trends in brain maps.

We demonstrate the consequence of overlooking trends in spatial association tests using an ecological simulation (Fig 2D). Consider two species x and y that coexist, and their true distributions ε(x) and ε(y) in space are independent (or negatively associated due to competition). However, both species also positively depend on a third spatially distributed driver z—such as rainfall and other environmental resources—which induces a shared trend between x and y across the space. Failure to model this underlying, spatially dependent confound will lead to an inaccurate effect size estimation and potential false discoveries (e.g., significant positive association between x and y in our simulation). The same scenario also applies in neuroimaging studies when x, y, and z are spatially distributed brain phenotypes.

One option to control for spatial trends is to fit a trend surface and evaluate the detrended residuals. The optimal λ for Laplacian regularized trend estimation is selected such that a stable sill plateauing near the sample variance is evident in the variogram of residuals, indicative of trend-free autocorrelation. Trends and autocorrelated residuals are estimated for each representative brain map (Fig 2E-2H), and associations between residual maps are evaluated using spin test and compared with associations between raw brain maps (Fig 2I).^2 While most results are consistent, associations between gene expression and functional gradient, and between cortical thickness and functional gradient, turn nonsignificant when trend effects are controlled. In addition, decreases in effect sizes (absolute value of Pearson correlation coefficient) are evident, suggesting shared spatial trends among maps.

Conclusion

Spatial trends that represent systematic change in mean across space are evident in diverse brain maps. We propose Laplacian-regularized trend estimation to account for trend effects in brain map association tests, improving the validity of spatial inference in neuroimaging research.
