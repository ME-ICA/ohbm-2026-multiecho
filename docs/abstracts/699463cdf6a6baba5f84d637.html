Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a common progressive cognitive disorder and a leading cause of dementia. Conventional structural MRI features include reduced cortical thickness and hippocampal atrophy.1 However, these macroscopic measures exhibit limited sensitivity in the early stages of the disease. While biomarkers of β-amyloid (Aβ) and tau are essential for defining molecular pathology, comprehensive disease monitoring requires assessing the concomitant cellular microstructural injuries, which are more directly linked to tissue loss and functional decline.

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) is a non-invasive imaging technique that probes brain microstructure in vivo by measuring the random motion of water molecules. By incorporating the diffusion time dependency with multi-compartment models, time-dependent diffusion MRI (td-dMRI) offers an approach to estimate cellular microstructures such as cell size and intracellular volume fraction.2,3 The clinical feasibility and diagnostic capability have been extensively explored in tumors. Nevertheless, due to the complex pathological environment where neurons and glial cells coexist and degenerate, the potential for revealing the cellular microstructure of AD remains unexplored.

This study investigated the primary hypothesis that td-dMRI could capture microstructural alterations at different clinical stages, thereby providing complementary cellular-level insights into macroscopic pathological phenomena. We also assessed whether microstructural features were associated with cognitive function and amyloid deposition.

Methods

Study Participants

A total of 93 participants were prospectively enrolled at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University between August 2023 and October 2025, including 47 AD dementia, 30 mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and 16 cognitive normal (CN) participants. All participants underwent time-dependent diffusion MRI scans. The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was used to assess global cognition. 18F-Florbetapir PET/CT scans were conducted in a subgroup of 22 dementia patients to quantify regional amyloid load. Ethics committee approval was granted by the hospital research ethics board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study design was shown in Figure 1A.

Image Acquisition

MRI was performed on a 3-T scanner (uMR790, United Imaging Healthcare) with a maximum gradient of 100 mT/m and maximum slew rate of 200 mT/m/ms. T1-weighted structural images were acquired using a sagittal 3D accelerated gradient-echo sequence with 1×1×1 mm3. Diffusion images were acquired using an in-house OGSE diffusion MRI sequence with trapezoid-cosine gradients and echo-planar imaging acquisition. OGSE data were acquired at oscillating frequencies of 50 Hz (effective td=5 ms, two cycles, b=250 and 500 s/mm2) and 25 Hz (effective td=10 ms, one cycle, b=250, 500, 750, and 1000 s/mm2), and PGSE data were acquired at diffusion duration δ of 10 ms, and diffusion separation Δ of 30 ms and 50 ms (td =26.7/46.7 ms, b=0, 250, 500,750,1000 s/mm2), respectively. The following parameters were used for both sequences: four diffusion directions; TR/TE, 5000/116 ms; field of view, 200×200 mm; in-plane resolution, 2.5×2.5 mm; number of slices, 20; and slice thickness, 5 mm. Amyloid-PET imaging were acquired using Siemens Bio-graph mCT. Participants received a bolus injection of 10 mCi 18F-Florbetapir (AV-45), and a 10-min static scan was acquired at 50-min post-injection.

Image Analysis

T1 image processing was performed using an automated reconstruction pipeline in FreeSurfer. Volumetric T1 data were obtained with segmentation for all 34 cortical regions and the hippocampus using the Desikan-Killiany atlas. Individual b0 images were registered to each subject's corresponding T1 images. The td-dMRI data was fitted using the JOINT model in Matlab 2018a. Intracellular volume fraction fin, extracellular diffusivity Dex, cell diameter d, and cellularity (fin/d·100) were estimated with a Bayesian algorithm. Additionally, ADC values were fitted using a log-linear fitting according to the formula S/S0 = exp(-b∙D), using all b-values for td at 46.7 ms. We used PETsurfer for PET image processing, including registration with individual T1-weighted images. PET data quantification was performed as the standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR), using the cerebellar gray matter as the reference region.

Statistical analysis

Group differences in microstructural metrics across hippocampus and 34 cortical regions were analyzed using ANCOVA (FDR-corrected) followed by post hoc t-test with Tukey's HSD method, controlling for age, sex, and education. Partial correlation analyses were performed to examine the coupling between microstructural metrics and cognitive scores and Aβ deposition. Bootstrapped mediation analysis was conducted to investigate the mechanistic pathway linking amyloid pathology, microstructural damage, and cognitive decline. To address multicollinearity, we employed Ridge logistic regression for classification tasks, utilizing task-specific microstructural feature subsets (e.g., diameter and Dex for MCI vs. CN; fin and Dex for AD vs. MCI; cellularity and Dex for AD vs. CN) and structural (thickness and volume) and ADC features. The diagnostic performance of single-modality and multi-modal fusion models were assessed by the area under the curve (AUC) with 5-fold cross-validation.

Results

Demographic characteristics and clinical assessments

Figure 1B presented the demographic and clinical characteristics of all participants. The CN subjects were younger than the MCIs and ADs (p=0.03). No differences were found in sex (p=0.98) and education (p=0.55). According to the neuropsychological assessments, the AD group had worse MMSE scores than the MCI and NC groups.

Group differences in macro- and microstructure

As shown in Figure 2A, compared to the CN group, individuals with MCI exhibited a significant increase in mean cell diameter within the hippocampus and across widespread cortical regions (all adjusted p<0.05), suggesting early astrocyte activation and neuroinflammatory swelling, while fin remained largely preserved. As individuals progressed from MCI to AD dementia, a pronounced reduction in fin and cellularity was observed, particularly within the frontal and parietal lobes, indicative of neuronal loss. Concurrently, Dex showed a progressive increase from CN through MCI to AD (all adjusted p<0.05), reflecting a continuous expansion of the extracellular space. Notably, the effect sizes of Dex and d exceeded those of cortical thickness and ADC, underscoring that microstructural pathology is more sensitive and precedes the conventional MRI markers.

Associations of microstructural measures with cognition

In the whole cohort analysis (Figure 2B), lower MMSE scores were significantly correlated with reduced cellularity and fin, as well as increased cell diameter and Dex (all adjusted p<0.05). Notably, Dex showed the strongest negative correlation with cognition (partial R=0.30-0.59), serving as a sensitive marker of disease severity. Regionally, the cellularity decreases in the frontal and parietal areas and the diameter showed widespread increases, particularly in frontal, parietal and temporal lobes.

Associations of microstructural measures with amyloid deposition

In the Aβ-positive AD subgroup, cross-regional analysis demonstrated a significant spatial correspondence between amyloid burden and microstructural change (Figure 2C): regions with higher Aβ SUVR exhibited more pronounced increases in cell diameter and reductions in fin. To further elucidate the pathway linking pathology to clinical symptoms, we conducted a mediation analysis focusing on the rostral middle frontal cortex—a region identified as a hub of pathological accumulation and cognitive decline in our analysis and previous studies.4 The analysis indicated that changes in cell diameter mediated the relationship between local amyloid deposition and cognitive decline (ACME=–7.38, 95% CI [–15.22, –0.32], p=0.038).

Microstructural measures can improve diagnosis performance

Across different diagnostic tasks, microstructural metrics performed comparably or superior to structural and conventional ADC measures, with multi-scale combination further enhancing overall diagnostic performance (Figure 2D). For distinguishing patients with AD from controls, the AUC of combined microstructural and structural model reached up to 0.947. In early detection task (MCI vs. CN), this model achieved an AUC of 0.873, outperforming the microstructure-only model (AUC=0.856) and structure-only model (AUC=0.846), revealing the synergistic value of integrating cellular-level and tissue-level markers. Notably, in the most challenging task of differentiating AD and MCI, the microstructural model (AUC=0.726) significantly outperformed the structural and ADC model (AUC: 0.693; 0.675).

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated the efficacy of time-dependent diffusion MRI as a non-invasive tool for probing cellular microstructural alterations in Alzheimer’s disease. Our findings provide three key insights into AD pathophysiology and diagnostics. First, we revealed a two-phase microstructural damage, characterized by early cellular swelling (increased diameter) in MCI and subsequent neuronal loss (decreased fin and cellularity) in AD. Second, these microstructural metrics are spatially coupled with amyloid pathology and serve as a mediator linking Aβ deposition to cognitive decline. Third, diffusion-based microstructural features demonstrate superior sensitivity over conventional structural atrophy in monitoring disease progression.

The two-phase microstructural evolution aligns with distinct cellular pathologies. In the MCI stage, we observed a widespread increase in mean cell diameter without significant changes in fin. This pattern is consistent with early-stage neuroinflammatory responses, specifically astrocytic hypertrophy and microglial activation, which are known to occur in the presence of soluble Aβ oligomers before overt neuronal death. In contrast, progression to AD dementia was characterized by a decline in cellularity and fin, reflecting the stage of irreversible neuronal loss and neuropil atrophy. Importantly, the elevated Dex indicates extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation driven by tau pathology, where reactive astrocytes internalize proteoglycans and upregulate degrading enzymes, thereby reducing structural tortuosity in the extracellular space.5 As neuroinflammation and soluble tau oligomers diffuse widely through the interstitial fluid, this "de-matrixing" causes a global diffusivity increase preceding macroscopic atrophy. Furthermore, the strong negative correlation with MMSE scores identified Dex as a robust surrogate marker for global cognitive decline.

Integrated PET imaging revealed a spatial correspondence between elevated Aβ burden and increased cell diameter, providing in vivo evidence supporting Aβ-induced glial reactivity. Mediation analysis further identified cell diameter as a significant mediator between amyloid deposition and cognitive decline in the rostral middle frontal cortex. This suggests that Aβ pathology drives cognitive impairment primarily by triggering microstructural alterations, such as neuroinflammation, rather than direct toxicity. Consequently, preserving microstructural integrity may represent a promising therapeutic strategy, even in the presence of amyloid pathology.

The diagnostic modeling confirmed the incremental value of integrating microstructural metrics with conventional MRI. For classifying AD or MCI against cognitively normal controls, the multimodal model combining microstructural and structural parameters enhanced diagnostic performance. More importantly, in distinguishing AD from MCI, microstructural metrics alone surpassed structural measures. This performance gap can be attributed to the "lag effect" of brain atrophy, where significant tissue loss occurs later than cellular dysfunction. Therefore, td-dMRI metrics provide a more temporally precise gauge of disease severity than volumetric measurements.

Several limitations should be considered. First, our cohort was relatively young and focused on relatively early stage of AD. A large cohort with multicenter enrollment would be ideal to validate the clinical value of the cellular microstructural markers in AD. Second, our biophysical model relies on simplified geometric assumptions (e.g., spherical cells) that may not fully capture complex cellular morphology of neurons and glia. Advanced models involving explicit neurite compartments (sticks or cylinders) and orientation dispersion may be developed in the future to better disentangle soma swelling from neurite dystrophy, providing a more comprehensive characterization of neurodegeneration.
