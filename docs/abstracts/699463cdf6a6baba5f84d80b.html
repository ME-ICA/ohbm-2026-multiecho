Introduction

Late gestation and early infancy are critical periods for human brain development, because major structural brain networks are maturing and provide the foundation for emerging functional activity networks, which, in turn, dynamically self-organize, guided by endogenous neural activity and environmental input. These networks are critical for many lifelong brain functions and support fundamental vigilance states, which shape information processing, the allocation of cognitive resources, and interactions with the physical and cognitive environment. In the developing brain, vigilance states emerge gradually and become increasingly distinct from one another. In infants, this is clearly reflected in rapidly changing sleep EEG patterns, with distinct signatures in active versus quiet sleep. However, many studies do not account for vigilance state and instead pool active and quiet sleep into a single 'sleep' EEG condition. This work studies the developmental differentiation of functional connectivity networks underlying active and quiet sleep.

Methods

We analyzed 124-channel EEG data collected longitudinally from N = 136 preterm infants during daytime sleep from 33 to 45 weeks postmenstrual age (N = 306 recordings overall). These data were classified into active sleep (AS) and quiet sleep (QS), and artifact-free 5-minute epochs were selected for further analysis. Next, the EEG signals were band-pass filtered into four frequency bands of interest (low delta [0.4–1.5 Hz], high delta [1.5–4 Hz], theta [4–8 Hz], and alpha [8–13 Hz]) and source-reconstructed into 58 parcels using an infant head model. Functional connectivity between regions was computed as phase-phase coupling (PPC; using the weighted phase lag index) and amplitude-amplitude coupling (AAC; using correlations between amplitude envelopes of mutually orthogonalized signals). Similarity Index (SI) of connectivity profiles within each subject between active and quiet sleep was computed as the Spearman correlation between vectorized connectivity matrices. Overlap between the top 10% and 20% strongest connections (network backbones) of the same matrices was estimated using the Jaccard index (J). Both similarity metrics were then correlated with postmenstrual age at EEG recording for both connectivity modalities (AAC and PPC) and across four frequency bands using Spearman’s rank correlation. Results were also validated against pooled surrogate values from permutation tests for each frequency band and modality (threshold was 99th percentile).

Results

For the AAC modality, SI between state-specific networks did not change across development at lower frequencies (see Fig.1A; 0.4–8 Hz, R ≈ 0, p ≥ 0.2). However, in the alpha band, SI decreased with age (8–13 Hz, R = −0.3, p < 0.0001), indicating increasing divergence between states. In contrast, for the PPC modality, SI increased significantly between states across all frequency bands (see Fig.1B; 0.4–13 Hz, 0.17 ≤ R ≤ 0.29, p < 0.004 for all). Similarly, overlap of the state-specific network backbones for AAC did not change with age at lower frequencies (0.4–8 Hz; R ≈ 0, p ≥ 0.23 for the top 10% and R ≈ 0, p ≥ 0.52 for the top 20%), but decreased in the alpha band (R ≤ −0.27, p < 0.0001 for both thresholds). For PPC, backbone overlap between states increased with maturation across all frequency bands (0.13 ≤ R ≤ 0.33, p < 0.028 for both thresholds).

Conclusion

Our previous work showed that functional connectivity networks at term-equivalent age differ between active and quiet sleep. Here, we extend these observations by demonstrating that state-specific connectivity patterns start to diverge already earlier in development. Notably, for AAC, state-specific networks diverge only in the alpha band, while networks in other frequency bands remain relatively stable. In contrast, for PPC, both connectivity profiles and network backbones become increasingly similar between states across all frequency bands with maturation. This PPC convergence may reflect a developing, more precisely organized structural scaffold able to support selective and temporally precise phase-based interactions and is largely shared across active and quiet sleep, leading to greater similarity between states. Overall, these findings suggest that active and quiet sleep networks are not developmentally stable for connectivity analysis and should therefore be treated separately rather than pooled into a single 'sleep' condition.
