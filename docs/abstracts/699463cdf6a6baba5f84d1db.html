Introduction

Since treatment response in major depressive disorder (MDD) remains highly heterogeneous1, and reliable biomarkers for guiding antidepressant selection are lacking2, numerous studies have sought to find biomarkers associated with individual treatment outcomes3. While several antidepressants have been investigated for neural correlates of their treatment response4, mirtazapine, despite its widespread use, has received limited attention in this regard. There is no study that has looked into the association between pre-treatment neuroanatomical features and treatment response to mirtazapine. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between pre-treatment brain structural features and treatment response to mirtazapine in patients with MDD.

Methods

Sixty-eight patients with MDD enrolled in the OptiMD-SP5 study were included in the present analysis. The OptiMD-SP5 is a bicentric naturalistic study on optimized treatment of depression conducted at Heidelberg University Hospital and the University of Regensburg Hospital. Patients were assigned to receive monotherapy with mirtazapine (n=23) or one of the comparator antidepressants: agomelatine (n=20) or a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (n=25). Prior to initiation of antidepressant treatment, all participants underwent high-resolution T1-weighted structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) using a 3T scanner. Clinical outcomes were evaluated following a minimum of four weeks of treatment, and patients were classified as responders or non-responders based on a comprehensive clinical evaluation, with change in Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) score as the primary outcome measure. Using a factorial model of response status by treatment arm in voxel-based morphometry (VBM), we first examined differences in regional gray matter (GM) volume between responders and non-responders to mirtazapine. With this model, we also assessed whether these response-related differences in patients treated with mirtazapine were absent in patients who received other antidepressants, in order to determine the specificity of the GM characteristics associated with mirtazapine response. Finally, we included additional MRI data from 21 healthy controls (HCs) in the prior Genomic Imaging Göttingen (GIG) cohort, who were demographically matched to patients treated with mirtazapine, and examined whether the GM characteristics associated with mirtazapine response reflect deviations from healthy brain structure in either responders or non-responders. For the VBM analyses, the significance level was set at p < .005 (uncorrected), and cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < .05 was additionally assessed as a sensitivity check.

Results

Significant differences in GM volume were observed between responders and non-responders to mirtazapine across several brain regions (Figure 1). Responders showed greater GM volume in the ventral striatum (right [R], t = 3.42; left [L], t = 2.88), angular gyrus (R, t = 3.62), and posterior cingulate gyrus (L, t = 3.01) relative to non-responders. In contrast, non-responders showed greater GM volume in prefrontal and parietal areas, including the superior (L, t = 3.66) and inferior frontal (R, t = 3.33) gyri, the anterior intraparietal cortex (IPC; R, t = 3.75; L, t = 4.13), the posterior IPC (L, t = 4.64), and the superior parietal lobule (R, t = 3.74).

Compared with HCs, non-responders showed reduced GM volume in the ventral striatum (R, t = 3.00; L, t = 2.69), angular gyrus (R, t = 3.54), and posterior cingulate gyrus (L, t = 3.67). Non-responders exhibited higher volumes in the inferior frontal gyrus (R, t = 3.08) and the posterior (L, t = 2.52) and anterior IPC (R, t = 3.09; L, t = 4.03) relative to HCs, whereas responders displayed reduced volume in the superior frontal gyrus (L, t = 2.60) and superior parietal lobule (R, t = 3.21) relative to HCs.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that mirtazapine response in MDD is associated with distinct neuroanatomical profiles. Responders showed preserved GM volume in the ventral striatum and posterior parietal–cingulate regions, along with reduced GM volume in the medial prefrontal cortex and superior parietal lobule relative to non-responders. These structural patterns were not observed with other antidepressants, supporting their specificity to mirtazapine. Comparisons with healthy controls further confirmed the pathological nature of these GM differences. Together, these findings suggest that these GM profiles may represent neuroanatomical characteristics of an MDD subtype responsive to mirtazapine, distinguishing responders from non-responders.
