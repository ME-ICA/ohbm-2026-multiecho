Introduction

Understanding how the brain engages cognitive tasks requires identifying mechanisms that allow dynamic adjustment of its computational state1. Central to this process is the balance between neural excitation and inhibition (EIB), which supports flexible switching between intrinsic and task-driven modes. EIB is traditionally assessed using Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (MRS), the only noninvasive method providing regional glutamate and GABA+ estimates, but this approach is limited by coarse spatial resolution and restricted coverage. An emerging alternative uses whole-brain measures of neural temporal dynamics from fMRI. The Hurst exponent (H) quantifies the scale invariance and long-range temporal memory of BOLD signals and is thought to reflect inhibitory constraints that stabilize neural dynamics against stochastic noise. However, the neurobiological specificity of task-related changes in H remains unclear, and its sensitivity to acquisition parameters and time-series length complicates the interpretation of its relationship to underlying neurochemical processes.

Here, we examine the relationship between load-dependent suppression of H during tasks with varying cognitive demands and MRS-derived measures of EIB in the same participants. Using parametric task designs that systematically vary cognitive load, we address two aims: (i) characterize how cognitive load modulates H across task conditions, establishing H as a marker of task-driven complexity changes, and (ii) assess the biological validity of H as an EIB proxy by testing its association with concurrent Glx and GABA+ estimates in the same brain regions. This work seeks to establish H as a spatially distributed marker of load-dependent EIB modulation with broad applicability in cognitive neuroscience.

Methods

To validate H estimation, we generated stationary (fGn; H=0.25–0.75) and nonstationary (fBm; H=1.25–1.75) simulations, alongside NiSO4 phantom data (3600s) scanned with the in-vivo BOLD protocol. The in-vivo dataset included 36 healthy adults2 (3T Siemens Prisma) with T1-weighted, resting-state BOLD EPI, and MEGA-PRESS GABA-edited MRS (left DLPFC) to quantify EIB undergoing four sessions of increasing parametric working memory load (i.e., Rest, 0-Back, 1-Back, 2-Back). Standard preprocessing was applied, with estimators tested on varying segment lengths (100–10,000 points) to assess sensitivity.

We employed an extended Hurst framework3,4, with H exponents ranging up to 2, to unify stationary and nonstationary assessment. Four estimators: Continuous and Discrete Wavelet Transforms (CWT, DWT), Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (DFA), and Fractionally Integrated Noise (FIN) were systematically benchmarked for accuracy and stability on synthetic and phantom data. The validated methodology was then applied to the in-vivo dataset to investigate task-dependent reconfigurations of temporal dynamics. H was estimated voxelwise and across 100 cortical functional Schaefer parcels. To characterize the task effect and its relationship to neurometabolism, we employed linear mixed-effects models predicting H from the interaction between EIB and session, accounting for subject- and parcel-specific variability via random intercepts. Additionally, we performed voxel-wise (within session) one-sample t-tests and parcel-wise (between sessions) repeated measures ANOVA to evaluate whole-brain patterns of H changes within and across sessions.

Results

Simulations and phantom analyses identified DFA and FIN as the most robust estimators for short (Fig.1), fMRI-like timeseries, yielding the lowest errors and unbiased estimates for stationary noise. In-vivo validation confirmed high inter-method consistency (r>0.7) and subject reliability (ICC~0.6). While both methods accurately tracked non-stationary transformations (ΔH~1), unlike CWT, DFA was selected for subsequent analyses due to its superior versatility across voxelwise and parcelwise scales.

Mixed-effects models (Fig.2) in the left DLPFC revealed distinct, state-dependent mechanisms. For EIB, the negative resting-state association (β= -0.10) significantly inverted during the task (Interaction β= [0.16, 0.19], all p<0.001), marking a shift where higher excitation predicts greater signal persistence rather than randomness. Conversely, the positive resting-state influence of GABA+ (β=0.34) diminished under load (Interaction β= [-0.39, -0.29], all p<0.001)), indicating a decoupling of baseline inhibitory tone from temporal scaling during task performance.

Voxelwise analysis confirmed significant non-zero H patterns across all sessions (pFWE<0.05 voxelwise and pBonferroni<0.01 across sessions, TFCE-corrected). Crucially, parcel-wise repeated measures ANOVA revealed widespread task-induced modulation of temporal dynamics (pFDR<0.05, Fig. 2). In these regions, H consistently decreased from rest to task. The cortical distribution of effect sizes highlights the spatial specificity of this adaptation, demonstrating a robust reconfiguration of temporal structure under cognitive load.

Conclusion

This study establishes the Hurst exponent as a robust and interpretable signal processing parameter for capturing the scale-free, temporally correlated structure intrinsic to neural timeseries data. Methodological validation across simulations and in-vivo reliability testing reveals that H estimation performance is signal-dependent. DFA and FIN demonstrate superior accuracy and reproducibility across fMRI-relevant timescales (199 points), while CWT shows systematic degradation at low H values and DWT exhibits significantly reduced accuracy on non-stationary signals.  Controlled phantom experiments confirmed the consistency of H estimates across varying noise regimes, demonstrating their sensitivity to the signal’s intrinsic temporal organization rather than extrinsic preprocessing influences. In-vivo inter-technique consistency and inter-subject reliability confirm that simulation-derived performance rankings translate to real fMRI data, with DFA and FIN maintaining strong agreement. Method-specific bias profiles differ systematically: DFA slightly overestimates H while FIN slightly underestimates, providing complementary bounds. However, DFA’s explicit detrending confers robustness to non-stationary properties that compromise likelihood-based estimators like FIN. 

The observed inverse relationship between H and EIB provides a biologically grounded link between large-scale fractal organization and neurometabolic regulation. Critically, DFA resilience is essential since task-induced cognitive load alters EIB, which fundamentally changes the statistical moments of neural timeseries (e.g. variance alterations). These state-dependent shifts in neurometabolic regulation violate the constant autocorrelation structure assumption required by likelihood-based methods that assume a single, invariant generative process. 

Moreover, we characterized the relationship between temporal scaling and cognitive load, demonstrating a widespread suppression of H across specific cortical parcels during task sessions. The dynamic modulation proved to be chemically state-dependent: under cognitive load, the relationship between H and EIB inverted, while the influence of GABA+ diminished. This suggests a functional reconfiguration where neural dynamics shift from an inhibition-stabilized5 resting regime to an excitation-driven persistence essential for cognitive processing. Taken together, these findings position H as a reliable methodological marker of temporal organization in neural signals, while motivating further exploration of generalized multifractal estimators to capture richer hierarchical scaling properties of brain activity.
