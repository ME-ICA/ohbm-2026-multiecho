Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a highly heterogeneous condition, with neurobiological mechanisms that vary substantially across developmental stages. Structural–functional connectivity (SC–FC) coupling reflects the anatomical constraints shaping large-scale neural dynamics and has emerged as a key feature of network dysregulation in MDD. However, it remains unclear whether SC–FC coupling abnormalities follow a continuous trajectory across the lifespan or instead diverge into stage-specific spatial phenotypes. Moreover, the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying these macroscopic network alterations remain largely unexplored. Here, we test whether SC–FC coupling disruptions in MDD exhibit distinct developmental versus aging-related patterns and delineate their multiscale biological substrates.

Methods

We analyzed multimodal MRI data (dMRI and fMRI) from three distinct cohorts of MDD patients and matched healthy controls (Ntotal=9225), comprising an adolescent cohort (10-18 years, N=269) and two large-scale adult cohorts derived from the UK Biobank: Middle-aged (40-59 years, N=3,358) and Elderly (≥60 years, N=5,598). The brain was parcellated into 246 regions using the Brainnetome Atlas. SC-FC coupling was quantified for each participant using a multilinear regression model, where structural properties were used to predict regional functional connectivity strength. The correlation between predicted and empirical FC served as the coupling index. We generated group difference maps (d-maps; MDD vs. controls) for each age group, controlling for age, sex, head motion, and intracranial volume. To systematically quantify the topographic overlap of abnormalities, we performed cross-age spatial similarity analyses at both whole-brain and network levels (Yeo-7 networks), with significance determined via 1,000 spin-permutation tests to rigorously account for spatial autocorrelation. Finally, macroscopic d-maps were decoded using: (1) cognitive terms from Neurosynth; (2) 20 neurotransmitter receptor/transporter density maps from neuromaps; and (3) cell-type abundance profiles estimated by integrating single-nucleus RNA-sequencing signatures with the Allen Human Brain Atlas.

Results

Across all cohorts, MDD was associated with widespread alterations in SC–FC coupling, yet their spatial distributions differed markedly by age. Cross-age similarity analysis revealed a clear “developmental versus aging” dichotomy, arguing against a simple continuous lifespan trajectory. Specifically, adolescent abnormalities showed negative spatial correlations with both middle-aged and elderly maps, whereas middle-aged and elderly groups exhibited significant positive spatial convergence at the whole-brain level (r = 0.40，pspin-FDR < 0.01). This convergence was particularly remarkable within the somatomotor (r = 0.78，pspin-FDR < 0.001) and salience/ventral attention (r = 0.51, pspin-FDR < 0.001) networks, indicating a shared aging-related network phenotype. Multiscale decoding linked adolescent MDD SC-FC coupling alterations to cognitive terms related to mood regulation and risk processing, whereas the middle-age and elder depressive patients was associated with fear, pain, and memory-related processes. Despite macroscopic convergence in adulthood, the underlying neurochemical associations with abnormal SC-FC coupling remained age-specific in MDD. Specifically, adolescents were most strongly associated with serotonergic 5-HT2A receptor distributions, middle-aged exhibited patterns linked with dopaminergic markers (FDOPA, D2), while elderly MDD were most correlated with serotonergic 5-HT1B and serotonin transporter (5-HTT) profiles. Cell-type decoding further revealed a robust cellular dissociation with SC-FC coupling in MDD, with adolescents selectively associated with layer 4 intratelencephalic (L4 IT) excitatory neurons, whereas the convergent adults were dominated by inhibitory interneurons (LAMP5, VIP) and deep-layer excitatory neurons (L5 ET).

Conclusion

This study focuses on MDD lifespan brain patterns of SC-FC coupling across 3 age stages: adolescents, middle-aged, and older adults, and their multi-scale mechanistic differences at the cognitive, neurochemical and cellular levels, by analyzing multimodal MRI data from 9,225 participants. These findings demonstrated a fundamental divergence between a “development-specific phenotype in adolescence” and a “convergent aging-related network signature” in middle-aged and elderly patients. This macroscopic dichotomy is supported by distinct multiscale mechanisms, spanning cognitive, neurochemical, and cellular levels, and suggests that similar large-scale network abnormalities in adulthood may arise from different biological substrates across the lifespan. Together, our results bridge macroscale connectomics with microscale neurobiology and underscore the necessity of age-specific, biologically informed precision strategies for understanding and treating MDD.
