Introduction

Cannabis has historically been linked to a range of adverse outcomes, including academic underachievement, unemployment, psychiatric morbidity, suicidal ideation, cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome, and cardiovascular complications1. Nevertheless, shifting societal attitudes, expanding legalization, and increasing recognition of potential therapeutic applications have contributed to a substantial rise in cannabis consumption in recent years. Cannabis now ranks as the third most commonly used psychoactive drug after alcohol and tobacco, with older adults representing the fastest-growing group of users2. This demographic is increasingly turning to cannabis to manage chronic medical and psychiatric symptoms, and prevalence estimates have increased markedly over the past decade—from 10.0% to 11.9% among adults aged 45–64 and from 4.1% to 5.9% among those aged ≥653. Cognitive aging, a major health concern in later life, shares several neural substrates with cannabis use4; however, the consequences of cannabis exposure for the aging brain and cognitive trajectories remain insufficiently characterized. To address this gap, we leveraged large-scale data from the UK Biobank (>25,000 participants) to systematically investigate associations among cannabis use, normative aging processes, and cognitive function.

Methods

This study applied the fully automated, data-driven NeuroMark framework5 to characterize connectome-level signatures associated with cannabis use, brain aging, and cognitive performance in older adults. Our goal was to determine whether cannabis use influences healthy cognitive aging by altering large-scale functional network organization. By comparing correlated FC patterns and their corresponding effect maps, we quantified the degree of convergence between cannabis-related and age-related connectivity alterations (as shown in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2). We hypothesized that cannabis use may modulate brain aging and cognition through cannabinoid-mediated neuroprotective processes, potentially supporting cognitive resilience via reduced neural dedifferentiation and compensatory network reorganization.

Results

Linear mixed-effects analyses revealed that cannabis use and normative aging are associated with overlapping but directionally opposing patterns of functional network connectivity, particularly within subcortical–sensorimotor and subcortical–cerebellar circuits. At the FNC level, the t-values for cannabis use and age were significantly inversely correlated (r = –0.3384, p < 10⁻⁵; Fig. 1A and C). When examining whole-brain connectivity within functional-domain representations, we observed an even stronger negative correspondence between cannabis- and age-related models (r = –0.6851, p < 10⁻⁴; Fig. 1B and C). Behaviorally, cannabis users demonstrated enhanced performance across multiple cognitive domains, and these cognitive associations were reflected in convergent neural patterns. Cannabis-related t-values showed robust positive correlations with cognitive performance at the FNC level (r = 0.4254–0.6826, all p < 10⁻⁵; Fig. 2), with the strongest association observed for the tower rearranging task (r = 0.6826, p = 1.01 × 10⁻¹⁸⁹). These positive brain–behavior correlations indicate that cannabis-related alterations in connectivity parallel corresponding enhancements in cognitive function.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal significant associations among cannabis use, chronological age, and cognition, reflected in overlapping and widespread alterations in large-scale brain network connectivity. Cannabis use was linked to increased functional connectivity within and between subcortical and cerebellar networks, stronger coupling between sensorimotor and visual systems, and reduced connectivity between subcortical/cerebellar regions and sensory cortices. These connectivity patterns parallel age-related neural changes and may reflect a compensatory or neuroprotective effect of cannabis that mitigates neural dedifferentiation.
