Introduction

The scientific community is pivoting toward agentic AI: LLM-driven systems that autonomously chain tools, select parameters, and report results, promising effortless neuroimaging workflows. We argue this trend directly threatens reproducibility. Agentic orchestration is probabilistic: each decision point (tool choice, parameter selection, parsing, reporting) multiplies risk across a branching directed acyclic graph (DAG, a tree-like workflow with no cycles). Small per-call errors, combined with parameter fan-out, yield exponential effective call counts and silent failure rates that quickly exceed acceptable thresholds. In neuroimaging, where pipelines already span multiple modalities and plugins, this “small error × big branch” dynamic is dangerous: silent, plausible-looking outputs can contaminate downstream analyses without warning.

We formalize this risk, align it with early observations, and frame a mitigation: intent-level mediation that collapses the error surface. Contributions: (1) a branching DAG failure model for agentic orchestration; (2) simulated projections for realistic pipelines showing how failure and misreport rates explode with branching depth; (3) a concrete mitigation (intent-action server, IAS) and an empirical plan on ChRIS. ChRIS is an open neuroimaging platform with a hypermedia API (Collection+JSON) and a CLI for constructing workflows; we use this as an empirical test bed to build a FreeSurfer task via API and shell, and to count agentic errors and silent misreports. Our stance is oppositional to the “agents will fix usability” narrative: without guardrails, agentic AI is a reproducibility anti-pattern. We position this work as a warning and a design pattern for anyone considering agentic orchestration in clinical or research neuroimaging, especially as new model releases accelerate and “works on my run” becomes a moving target. The goal is to keep usability gains while preventing silent erosion of trust in computed results.

Methods

We model agentic orchestration as walking a branching directed acyclic graph (DAG) of choices. Even a small branching factor per step creates geometric growth in effective calls; visible depth can be shallow while the number of underlying calls explodes. We distinguish “near-path” errors (plausible-but-wrong parameters) from “far” errors (overt breaks). Total failures include both; silent failures (“misreports”) come only from near-path deviations that the agent labels as success. Compounding drift is represented by a correlation factor: nonzero for unconstrained agents, essentially zero when constrained. We compare two scenarios: a baseline agent with higher branching, higher per-call error rates, and drift; and an IAS-constrained scenario with branching near one, far errors nearly eliminated, and lower near-path error and misreport rates.

Experimental substrate: ChRIS is an open neuroimaging platform with a hypermedia API (Collection+JSON) and a CLI; clients must choreograph multi-call “intent” sequences (fetch data, create feeds, attach FreeSurfer plugins, monitor). Early experiments show current agents (claude-cli, OpenAI Codex-style models, latest Google Gemini) cannot reliably orchestrate the raw API end-to-end. A “shell model” is being developed that exposes internal apps as a Linux-like directory tree of executables to simplify orchestration; early agent runs on this shell model already exhibit failure rates comparable to our predictions, with silent failure modes still being collected. Due to anticipated LLM updates, we will generate a historical trace of model-version ability on these tasks, acknowledging rapid model updates and the “works on my run” drift.

Conceptual mitigation (IAS): To mitigate these failure modes, agents should be removed from orchestration and used only as translators between external intents and a library of pre-vetted, deterministic, auditable workflows. The Intent-Action Service (IAS) provides that translation and bounded orchestration: a single intent endpoint, a small validated parameter set, and structured outputs instead of free-form agent reports. IAS collapses branching, strips out most far errors, validates at the intent boundary, and produces typed, cacheable outputs. Agents ask “what”; IAS controls “how” using constrained, tested steps. IAS can be deployed as a standalone microservice that clients call over HTTP, or as a language-specific library exposing programmatic intent APIs (e.g., Python/Rust/JavaScript), but in both cases the intents are identical, pre-vetted, and auditable. This keeps orchestration logic in one place and makes it testable, logged, and versioned.

Simulation plan and figure: We sweep workflow depths from 1 to 100 (log-scaled x-axis), using representative parameters (baseline higher branching and error; IAS near-unity branching, lower error, no drift). We produce one figure here: failure versus depth, with 5% and 20% thresholds marked, to show how quickly risk rises in baseline agentic runs and how IAS flattens and delays threshold crossings. A misreport figure is presented in Results. The figure and its thresholds let readers map their own pipeline depth/branching to risk (e.g., typical 5–15-call workflows already hit double-digit silent failure in baseline agentic scenarios). We will also show how small parameter reductions, achievable via IAS constraints, shift the curves to safer regions.

Empirical plan: We  catalog measured failure and misreport rates from agent-driven FreeSurfer volumetric workflows on ChRIS, covering both raw API traversals and shell-model orchestration. We maintain a historical log of multiple model versions (claude-cli, OpenAI/Codex lineage, Google Gemini) and maintain a historical trace to reflect shifting model behavior. Silent failures are surfaced by comparing reported “success” runs against ground truth and schema validation at the intent boundary, and by examining near-path deviations that agents marked as successful.

Results

Simulations: In baseline agentic runs (high branching, high per-call error, nonzero drift), total failure climbs quickly: within a few levels (roughly 2–5), the chance of an outright wrong run surpasses a few percent, and by 3–5 levels it enters a high-risk band. Silent failures (the most dangerous case, where outputs are wrong but reported as success) exceed 10% in that same depth range. In the IAS-constrained runs (branching near one, far errors near zero, low misreport rate, no drift), outright failure stays below alarm thresholds until much deeper (around 6 levels for a few percent risk, around 16 for a higher band), and silent failures remain low, rising slowly and staying under ~4% across the modeled range. These bands map to typical neuroimaging intents of 5–15 coordinated calls; baseline agentic behavior is already in unsafe territory there, while IAS remains within conservative risk limits.

Fig 1 (introduced in Methods) shows total failure vs. depth. Baseline appears with two lines: “Baseline (independent)” with no drift and “Baseline (correlated)” with drift/parameter cascade. Both climb sharply; a few extra decision points can flip a “looks fine” run into a high-probability failure. The IAS curve is flatter and shifted right by roughly an order of magnitude in depth; for many practical pipelines it remains below a few-percent line, indicating bounded risk and a delayed onset of unacceptable failure probability.

Fig 2 (log misreport vs. depth, presented here) focuses on silent failures; esp dangerous since they present as “success.” Baseline shows two lines (independent and correlated); both rise into double digits after only a few branching decisions, as plausible-but-wrong parameter choices pass through and get reported as correct, creating false assurance and contaminating downstream analyses. IAS bends that curve down: by constraining branching and enforcing validation at the intent boundary, it keeps silent failures in the low single digits over the same depths. The IAS trace underscores that removing compounding effects and enforcing intent validation flatten the silent-failure risk.

Practitioner reading: If your typical workflow spans around 10 calls with any meaningful branching, an unbounded agent can silently return wrong results at rates that would invalidate downstream analysis. With IAS constraints, the same workflow sits in a much lower-risk band, where a small, inspectable surface remains to be checked by humans or schemas. Silent failures are especially insidious because logs and “success” markers look normal; without intent validation, typed outputs, or versioned intent contracts, these errors are hard to detect after the fact. The plotted thresholds are parameterized for realism but are meant to be read qualitatively: modest improvements in per-call error or branching (as IAS enforces) shift curves down and right; modest degradations push silent failures into unacceptable territory even at shallow depths. Readers can map their own pipeline depth and branching to these bands to decide whether unbounded agentic orchestration is tolerable.

Forthcoming empirical results: Early agent runs (claude-cli, OpenAI/Codex lineage, Google Gemini) on the ChRIS shell model show orchestration failures consistent with the simulation; silent failure cases are being cataloged with ground-truth comparisons. We present a historical trace of model-version performance on raw Collection+JSON traversal and shell-model orchestration for FreeSurfer volumetrics, comparing unbounded agentic vs. IAS-mediated intent calls. We will report observed silent-failure rates, false-assurance rates (reported success but wrong outputs), and whether newer models materially change risk without intent-level constraints. The expectation is that, even as models improve, unbounded agentic orchestration retains a high rate of silent errors unless bounded by IAS-style controls.

Conclusion

The field is in an agentic AI craze: LLM-driven agents are being promoted as the easy path to “smarter” neuroimaging pipelines. This paper is a voice against that trajectory. Unbounded agentic orchestration is the enemy of reproducibility in neuroimaging. The danger is not just visible failures but “success-labeled wrong outputs” that slip through when branching decisions multiply small per-call risks. Our modeling and early observations show that plausible-but-wrong runs become common after only a few decision points in typical workflows, silently contaminating downstream analyses. The field is already grappling with reproducibility; unbounded agents amplify that crisis by making it harder to detect when things went wrong.

Key findings: Simulations show that in baseline agentic runs, outright failure and silent failure cross unacceptable bands within just a few decision points typical of neuroimaging intents. In contrast, when branching is collapsed and validation is enforced (IAS-like constraints), total failure stays below common alarm thresholds much deeper into the graph, and silent failures remain in low single digits. The misreport curves are the warning signal: without guardrails, agents can confidently return wrong results often enough to invalidate downstream conclusions; with intent-level constraints, that risk is bounded and visible.

The intent-action server (IAS) offers a pragmatic countermeasure: remove agents from orchestration and use them only as translators of human intent into a bounded, pre-vetted library of deterministic workflows. IAS collapses branching, strips out most far errors, validates parameters at the intent boundary, and emits typed, auditable outputs. It can be deployed as a microservice over HTTP or as language-specific intent libraries, but in all cases the orchestration logic lives in one place, logged, versioned, and testable. This turns “agentic magic” into a reproducible, inspectable interface where the error surface is small and failures are visible rather than silent.

For practitioners and reviewers: if a pipeline spans around 10 calls with any meaningful branching, an unbounded agent can silently deliver wrong results at rates that invalidate conclusions. IAS-style mediation keeps risk in the low single digits and makes it feasible to audit. Treat unbounded agentic orchestration as a default anti-pattern in clinical and research neuroimaging. Require intent-level mediation, structured outputs, and human- or schema-based checks at the intent boundary before accepting agent-produced results. This applies equally to API/CLI orchestration and emerging “shell” abstractions that expose internal apps as executables: without intent-level control, silent failures accumulate. Logs and “success” markers are not enough; typed outputs, validation hooks, and versioned intent contracts are necessary.

Broader implications: usability and reproducibility must be co-designed. Agentic AI can help with intent capture and translation, but orchestration belongs in constrained, testable services. By isolating agents to the “what” and keeping the “how” in a validated intent layer, we gain convenience without sacrificing trust. As models evolve rapidly, this separation becomes more important: “works on my run” is a moving target, and reproducibility depends on bounding and auditing the decision surface, not on hoping the next model update will “just get it right.” If the community wants to adopt agentic AI responsibly, it must pair agents with intent-action layers that keep failure surfaces small, observable, and correctable. This is a call for design discipline: celebrate usability wins from agents, but insist on intent-level guardrails to avoid a reproducibility crisis accelerated by automation. The alternative is a fast path to irreproducible science masked by friendly UIs and confident agent replies.
