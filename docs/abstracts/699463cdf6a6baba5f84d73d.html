Introduction

Associative memory is the ability to link and retrieve information together and is essential for everyday life functioning. In a social context, face-name learning provides a sensitive and ecologically valid example of this process. The medial temporal lobe (MTL), and particularly the hippocampus, is known to support associative binding, while early face learning engages occipito-temporal regions and affective components are encoded in the amygdala.1 Face-name associations thus depend on a distributed network with hippocampal integrity at its core2 and is hence particularly vulnerable in ageing and neurological conditions involving MTL, such as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and traumatic brain injury (TBI). Non-invasive brain stimulation offers a promising approach to counter these memory impairments by modulating neural plasticity, however, conventional techniques are limited in their ability to reach deep brain structures. Transcranial temporal interference stimulation (tTIS) may overcome this limitation by enabling focal modulation of subcortical regions.3 Importantly, response to stimulation has been described to be variable across individuals, particularly in clinically heterogeneous populations where structural damage, cognitive profile, and residual plasticity may influence responsiveness.4 Understanding this variability, and identifying behavioural markers of responders and non-responders, is therefore critical to optimise stimulation protocols and moving towards personalised interventions. Here, we tested whether intermittent theta-burst tTIS targeting the left hippocampus enhances face-name associative memory in healthy older adults, MCI and TBI patients, and characterised individual differences in stimulation response. 

Methods

Three cohorts were tested in this randomised, triple-blind, sham-controlled cross-over study (total N = 45): healthy older adults, MCI patients and TBI patients. Each participant completed six sessions, including consenting and baseline neuropsychological assessment (MoCA and FAB), as well as a structural MRI, followed by two experimental blocks. During each experimental block, participants completed a face-name association task during either active or sham hippocampal stimulation, followed by two follow-up sessions at 90 minutes and 24 hours. During the encoding phase, 14 unfamiliar face-name pairs were presented serially, whilst during retrieval, these faces were shown with five name options, from which participants had to select the correct one “as fast and as accurate as possible”. tTIS targeting the left hippocampus was delivered using two electrode pairs positioned according to previous work by Violante and colleagues.5 In the active condition, stimulation followed an intermittent theta-burst (iTBS) protocol, aligning with hippocampal oscillatory dynamics and LTP-like effects, whereas the sham stimulation consisted of a ramp-up and ramp-down. Task data were analysed using mixed-effects models, with stimulation condition and session as fixed effects and subject as a random intercept. Accuracy was modelled with a binomial distribution, reaction time (RT) with a gamma distribution, and confidence with linear models. Structural MRI and diffusion-weighted imaging were used to quantify grey matter volume and structural connectivity within a predefined face-learning network. To characterise individual responsiveness to stimulation, subject-level improvement delta scores were calculated by contrasting performance changes between active and sham conditions and participants were classified as responders or non-responders accordingly. Multimodal features, including baseline cognitive scores, grey matter volume, ROI to ROI structural connectivity, and simulated individual e-fields were entered into a linear support vector machine (SVM) classifier with 5-fold cross-validation to identify predictors of stimulation responsiveness. Feature stability was assessed based on 200 bootstrap resamples and responder classifications were behaviourally validated on the raw data using non-parametric tests.

Results

At the group level, no main effects of stimulation were observed for RT or accuracy. Across all participants, RTs became significantly faster and accuracy and confidence significantly higher at both 90-minute and 24-hour follow-ups compared to training, reflecting robust learning and consolidation effects. Clear cohort differences were present throughout, with MCI and older adults showing slower responses and lower accuracy than the TBI group. Despite the absence of group-level stimulation effects, substantial inter-individual variability was observed. Classification performance of the SVM models was lower for accuracy outcomes but showed above-chance prediction for RT improvement at the 24-hour follow-up, reaching a mean balanced accuracy of 64% and an AUC of 0.68. Predictive performance was weaker at the earlier follow-up. The most consistently selected predictive features were bilateral grey matter volume of the hippocampus, grey matter volume of the right amygdala, the MoCA score, and structural connectivity between the right parahippocampus and hippocampus. Feature stability analysis demonstrated particularly high consistency for left hippocampal volume and the MoCA score. Behavioural validation of the responder classification confirmed that responders differed from non-responders in raw task performance specifically under active stimulation. Responders showed higher accuracy at both follow-ups and faster RTs at the 24-hour follow-up during iTBS, whereas no consistent differences were observed under sham stimulation. This pattern indicates that responder status reflected genuine sensitivity to hippocampal stimulation rather than an arbitrary thresholding of the improvement delta scores.

Conclusion

tTIS targeting the left hippocampus did not lead to uniform behavioural benefits at the group level in a face-name association task in healthy older adults, MCI and TBI patients. Instead, substantial inter-individual variability in responsiveness to hippocampal stimulation was evident, despite comparable task-related learning and consolidation across cohorts. Importantly, SVM models showed that improvement with active tTIS was systematically related to baseline cognitive and neuroanatomical factors, i.e. reaction-time gains at the 24-hour follow-up session were predictable from specific baseline features. Hippocampal grey matter volume and the MoCA score emerged as particularly stable predictors, in line with the finding that MCI patients profited the least from the stimulation and concomitantly had the lowest grey matter volume and MoCA scores. Validation using the raw behavioural scores, compared to the deltas used in the SVM prediction, confirmed that responders differed from non-responders specifically under iTBS-tTIS stimulation, supporting the interpretation that these differences reflected sensitivity to neuromodulation rather than arbitrary cut-offs. In sum, these results suggest that hippocampal neuromodulation may benefit individuals whose structural and cognitive profiles support effective engagement of the targeted hippocampal network. The absence of consistent group-level effects underscores the need to move beyond average treatment responses and towards stratified approaches that incorporate individual markers of stimulation sensitivity. Future work should refine prediction models with increased sample sizes and potentially integrating other sets of multimodal biomarkers to support personalised applications of non-invasive brain stimulation in populations at risk of associative memory decline.
