Introduction

A central question in affective neuroscience is whether the perceptual aspects of emotion arise from a set of core regions within the cerebral architecture, or from the dynamic integration of distributed brain systems. Although current evidence favors a distributed, dynamically interacting network perspective, most studies still rely on pairwise functional connectivity analyses, a dyadic approach that may fail to capture the higher-order, multi-way interactions that support evolving emotional experiences.
Here, we address this gap by applying a dynamical higher-order framework [1] to naturalistic fMRI data. Rather than focusing on simple co-fluctuations between pairs of regions, we examine whether emotion dynamics can be better described when considering higher-order interactions (HOIs). By linking temporal fluctuations in emotion to dynamic brain configurations, we show that emotional complexity is best captured by the topological structure of these HOIs. Using multivariate partial least squares correlation (PLSC), we further demonstrate that HOI metrics explain substantially more shared variance between brain activity and emotional features than standard pairwise metrics, suggesting that higher-order frameworks offer a more nuanced description of the neural basis of human emotion.

Methods

We analyzed the Emo-FilM dataset [2], which includes fMRI data from 30 healthy participants watching 14 emotionally evocative short films (average duration ≈ 7 minutes). For each film, the dataset provides continuous ratings of 50 emotion dimensions at every repetition time, capturing the moment-to-moment evolution of emotional experience. To characterize brain dynamics beyond standard pairwise connectivity, we applied a time-resolved higher-order framework based on a recent topological data analysis method [3]. From the fMRI time series, we extracted four types of time-varying metrics: (i) regional BOLD activity, (ii) edge co-fluctuations between pairs of regions (respectively corresponding to nodal and pairwise dynamics), (iii) triangle-level measures reflecting interactions among triplets of regions, and (iv) a homological scaffold encoding mesoscopic topological structure accounting for the presence of group interactions. To compare the temporal organization of brain activity with that of emotional experience, we constructed recurrence plots (time–time correlation matrices) for both neural and emotional metrics. We then applied Louvain-based consensus clustering to these matrices to identify temporal communities (i.e., segments of time with similar patterns). The correspondence between neural and emotional partitions was quantified using Element-Centric Similarity (ECS) [4], which ranges from 0 (completely dissimilar) to 1 (identical partitions).
Finally, to identify the specific connectivity patterns associated with distinct emotional states, we applied multivariate Partial Least Squares Correlation (PLSC) at the level of the consensus-defined “emotion clusters” (approximately 130 clusters across all films). PLSC extracts Latent Variables (LVs) that maximize covariance between emotional profiles and brain network configurations. The stability of these LVs was evaluated using a bootstrap procedure with 30 iterations of subject resampling.

Results

Higher-order metrics better track emotional dynamics. The homological scaffold consistently demonstrated the strongest brain-emotion alignment, achieving the highest ECS scores and outperforming all metrics in 11 out of 14 films (Fig. 1d). In contrast, BOLD activation consistently exhibited the lowest similarity. Crucially, the scaffold’s superior performance persisted even after accounting for low-level audiovisual features (e.g., saturation, brightness, spectral flux), suggesting that it captures intrinsic neural dynamics tied to emotional processing rather than sensory inputs.

Higher order interactions provide nuanced information about emotions. PLSC analysis revealed a clear hierarchy in the neural encoding of emotion. While node-level BOLD signals typically yielded only 1 significant latent variable and pairwise edges 2 LVs, the scaffold consistently identified a richer organization (4 LVs in 80% of bootstrap iterations). This suggests that higher-order topology accesses a more complex view of neural information, effectively expanding the dimensionality of the explainable brain-emotion space. We characterized latent variables identified by PLSC by aggregating the brain weights onto the 7 Yeo functional networks (plus subcortical structures) and examining the associated emotional profiles (Fig. 2b-c). While both pairwise and higher-order methods produced distributed spatial patterns, they revealed distinct functional priorities. Moreover, compared to edges, the scaffold spatial profiles captured a more complex variety of mixed emotional subgroups. Remarkably, we anticipate that, while both methods appeared sensitive to arousal, the homological scaffold provides a superior link to valence/power dimensions (results not shown). This enhanced sensitivity is reflected in the scaffold’s LVs, which do not merely cluster simple categories but rather mirror the intertwined nature of these affective dimensions through distributed spatial patterns.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that the homological scaffold metric captures the temporal dynamics of emotional experience with significantly greater fidelity than traditional pairwise or node-based metrics. By effectively tracking the clustering of emotional states during film viewing, higher-order topology reveals a layer of neural information that remains inaccessible to conventional functional connectivity. Crucially, our multivariate analysis suggests that dynamic higher-order structures are required to support the rich, multi-dimensional taxonomy of complex human emotions. Together, this work establishes dynamical higher-order models as a powerful extension of network neuroscience, advancing our understanding of how the brain integrates distributed information to construct the nuanced flow of subjective experience.
