Introduction

1. INTRODUCTION

Normal aging is accompanied by widespread alterations in large-scale functional networks, reflected in reduced within-network coherence and increased cross-network interactions.1,2 Although these patterns are often interpreted as compensatory, most work has centered on cortical systems. As a result, it remains unclear how coordinated alterations across cortical, subcortical, and cerebellar circuits jointly contribute to aging, limiting our understanding of lifespan network reorganization as a distributed process.

Brain-age prediction using resting-state functional connectivity has become a powerful framework for quantifying system-level aging. Deep learning models are particularly promising because they capture nonlinear, multiscale patterns beyond the reach of traditional methods. However, the high dimensionality of connectivity data and modest sample sizes make dimensionality reduction essential. Linear approaches such as PCA offer computational efficiency, but used in isolation, they are not sufficient to capture nonlinear aging-related structure, motivating more flexible representations.

To overcome these limitations, we integrate a semi-supervised autoencoder with an interpretability strategy designed to reveal biologically meaningful aging patterns.3,4 Autoencoders address the shortcomings of linear dimensionality reduction by learning compact, nonlinear representations that capture distributed connectivity alterations across cortical, subcortical, and cerebellar systems. Yet their predictive strength is still limited by poor interpretability. To address this gap, we incorporate a two-step SHAP framework combined with PCA that attributes model predictions to latent features and maps these contributions back onto network-level interactions. This combined approach isolates age-related components of whole-brain functional connectivity and identifies the network interactions most strongly shaping brain-age estimates.

Methods

2. METHODS

2.1 PARTICIPANTS, PREPROCESSING, AND FUNCTIONAL CONNECTIVITY CONSTRUCTION

Resting-state fMRI data from 599 adults in the HCP-Aging cohort were analyzed to construct whole-brain functional connectivity (FC) matrices spanning cortical, subcortical, and cerebellar regions. Data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep, and FC was computed from a combined Brainnetome–SUIT parcellation. All FC values were Fisher z-transformed. To ensure unbiased normalization, the mean and standard deviation were derived exclusively from the training split in each fold and subsequently applied to the test data.

2.2 BRAIN-AGE PREDICTION FRAMEWORK

To model age-related connectivity patterns, we developed a supervised autoencoder termed the Orthogonal Auxiliary-Guidance Convolutional Autoencoder (OAG-CAE). OAG-CAE decomposes the input FC matrix into two disentangled latent subsets: an age-related space shaped by auxiliary age-supervision signals, and an age-irrelevant space constrained by orthogonality. Both the auxiliary supervision and disentanglement objectives are incorporated into the overall training loss, explicitly guiding the learning of age-informative latent representations. This design encourages the model to encode aging-specific information into a dedicated latent representation while suppressing nuisance variability.

The age-related latent features were then passed to a multi-scale convolutional regressor incorporating gated convolutions, channel-wise attention, and multi-kernel filters, enabling the extraction of complementary age-relevant patterns at different representational scales. A bias-shift correction and linear post-hoc calibration reduced systematic prediction errors.

Model training used five-fold cross-validation stratified by age and gender, with 80% of subjects assigned to training/validation and 20% to testing in each fold. The Adam-Weight optimizer and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) loss were used to optimize performance, and early stopping was applied to prevent overfitting. Batch sizes were harmonized across folds to ensure stable gradient updates.

2.3 INTERPRETABLE MAPPING OF FUNCTIONAL CONNECTIVITY WITH SHAP

To interpret the contribution of FC patterns to brain-age estimates, we developed a PCA-enhanced two-step SHAP attribution strategy. In the first stage, Kernel SHAP was applied to the trained regressor using the age-related latent representations as input, yielding contribution scores for each latent dimension. In the second stage, these latent-level SHAP weights were projected onto principal components derived from the FC matrices, producing PCA components ranked by their aggregated SHAP importance.

The highest-ranked components were back-projected into FC space, generating spatial attribution maps that indicate which functional connections most strongly influence model predictions. A knee-point detection algorithm was subsequently applied to these spatial attribution scores to identify the most highly age-relevant functional connections in a data-driven manner.  This procedure mirrors the model’s predictive hierarchy in reverse: whereas prediction involves information flowing from FC to latent representations and ultimately to predicted age, interpretation traces contributions back from predicted age to latent representations and, via PCA, to the FC space, providing an efficient and biologically grounded explanation of age-sensitive connectivity features. PCA served solely as a post-hoc interpretive tool and was not involved in model training.

Results

3. RESULTS

3.1 BRAIN-AGE PREDICTION PERFORMANCE

Across five-fold cross-validation, the proposed OAG-CAE achieved robust prediction accuracy, with a mean MAE of 6.10 ± 0.54 years and a Pearson correlation of r = 0.84 (p < 0.001) between chronological and predicted age. Errors were stable across the adult lifespan, showing no inflation in older age groups. These results demonstrate that the model provides accurate and age-invariant estimates of brain age.

3.1.2 COMPARISON WITH BASELINE MODELS

Performance was benchmarked against traditional machine-learning and deep-learning models. Linear Regression, Ridge, Lasso, and SVR produced MAEs of 6.94–6.74 years, while XGBoost performed markedly worse (MAE = 8.66 years). Deep learning baselines (VGG, ResNet, Transformer Net) failed to converge on FC matrices and produced near-mean predictions (MAE > 12).

3.1.3 ABLATION STUDY

Ablation experiments confirmed the contribution of each architectural component. A simple Autoencoder combined with linear regression produced the weakest performance (MAE = 8.35 ± 0.76). Replacing it with a classic Convolutional Autoencoder reduced error to 7.43 ± 0.40. Adding auxiliary classification and regression signals yielded further improvements (best auxiliary model: MAE = 6.47 ± 0.58).

3.2 INTERPRETABLE DEEP LEARNING ANALYSIS

3.2.1 SHAP-BASED ATTRIBUTION

Among the ten principal components derived from the FC space, the PCA-enhanced two-step SHAP analysis identified three components (PCA-1, PCA-3, and PCA-6) as the most influential for brain-age prediction. PCA-1 exhibited positive SHAP values, indicating that stronger FC contributed to older predicted age, whereas PCA-3 and PCA-6 showed negative associations. Together, these components consistently accounted for the majority of SHAP-derived importance across cross-validation folds, indicating both their dominant contribution and stability.  

3.2.2 NETWORK-LEVEL REORGANIZATION

Connections with positive SHAP contributions, indicating features positively associated with increasing age, were primarily located between the cerebellum and higher-order cortical systems, including the default mode, frontoparietal, and attention networks, as well as between the default mode and visual networks. In contrast, connections with negative SHAP contributions, indicating features negatively associated with increasing age, were mainly distributed across somatomotor, ventral attention, basal ganglia, and thalamic networks, together with reduced within-network and between-network coupling among the default mode, frontoparietal, ventral attention, and limbic systems. In addition, the relative SHAP importance of the PCA components highlighted which latent dimensions contributed most strongly to the brain-age predictions.  

3.2.3 REGION-LEVEL CONTRIBUTIONS

Age-related alterations in functional connectivity extended across cortical, subcortical, and cerebellar systems. Age-increasing connections were mainly observed along the cortico–cerebellar axis, linking prefrontal, temporal, and parietal association cortices with posterior cerebellar regions. Enhanced coupling was detected between the middle and superior frontal gyri and the right cerebellar lobule VIIIa, between the precuneus and cerebellar lobule VIIIa, and between the middle temporal gyrus and cerebellar lobules I–V and VIIIb.

Age-related decreases in connectivity formed two major clusters. One cluster was centered on the anterior insula and included reduced coupling within the salience network, as well as weakened connections with the anterior cingulate cortex, precentral gyrus, inferior parietal lobule, and basal ganglia. A second cluster involved fronto-limbic and fronto-parietal connections, characterized by reduced connectivity between the inferior and orbital frontal gyri and limbic and midline regions, including the hippocampus, amygdala, and precuneus.

Conclusion

4.Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate that integrating an orthogonal-auxiliary-guidance convolutional autoencoder with a SHAP-based interpretability framework enables accurate and interpretable estimation of brain age from whole-brain functional connectivity. This approach not only improves prediction performance but also reveals coherent, biologically meaningful patterns of age-related network reorganization.

Aging was associated with reduced connectivity within and between somatomotor, ventral attention, basal ganglia, and default mode systems, suggesting diminished integration among lower-level sensorimotor and salience-related circuits. In contrast, increased coupling among default mode, dorsal attention, frontoparietal, cerebellar, and visual networks indicated enhanced cross-network integration in higher-order cortico–cerebellar pathways.

Together, these findings point to a lifespan shift in the balance between functional segregation and integration, characterized by weakened sensorimotor–attentional circuits alongside strengthened higher-order cortico–cerebellar interactions. By combining deep learning with interpretable network-level attribution, our framework provides a powerful tool for mapping age-sensitive connectivity patterns and advancing our understanding of how large-scale brain systems reorganize across adulthood.
