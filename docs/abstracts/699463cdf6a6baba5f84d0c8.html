Introduction

Repeated blast brain injury (rBBI) in military personnel is increasingly recognized as a distinct form of traumatic brain injury (TBI) with pathological features not commonly observed in civilian blunt TBI.1 Biomechanical and neuropathological studies highlight concentrated mechanical stress, astroglial activation, and interface astroglial scarring at the gray matter-white matter (GM–WM) junction in rBBI.2 However, conventional MRI lacks sufficient sensitivity to characterize such subtle microstructural changes. Ultra-high-gradient ex vivo diffusion MRI (dMRI) provides a platform to probe microstructure at spatial and diffusion encoding regimes not feasible in vivo, allowing direct comparison between microstructure measures modeled from dMRI signals and underlying pathophysiologic processes. This is underscored by recent ex vivo multidimensional MRI studies that successfully mapped astrogliosis in rBBI cases with high specificity.3 The Soma-And-Neurite Density Imaging (SANDI) model offers improved specificity beyond anatomical or tensor-based metrics by separating soma-, neurite-, and extracellular-related diffusion signals.4 We hypothesized that the neurite signal fraction shows a larger depth-dependent gradient between the GM-WM junction and the pial surface in brains donated by Special Operations Forces (SOF) personnel with repeated blast exposure (RBE) than in civilians with TBI due to blunt head impact. This hypothesis stems from reports of reactive astrocytosis and glial remodeling at the GM–WM junction in rBBI, which may manifest as increased neurite-like diffusion signal. To test this hypothesis, we performed SANDI analysis of ultra-high-gradient ex vivo dMRI in postmortem human brains to compare cortical depth-dependent microstructural profiles between SOF and civilian TBI cases.

Methods

Data acquisition:

Fixed ex vivo whole brain specimens from five brain donors (mean age 44±16 years, all males) were obtained from the University of Washington BioRepository and Integrated Neuropathology (BRaIN) Laboratory and the Uniformed Services University Brain Tissue Repository: three brains from civilians with chronic neuropsychological symptoms after isolated moderate-to-severe impact TBI events (mean age 40±17 years) and two brains from SOF personnel with extensive RBE. Table 1 summarizes demographic details, TBI exposure, and medical history for each specimen. Postmortem MRI was performed at Massachusetts General Hospital via a separate research protocol approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board.

After at least 1 month of fixation in 10% formalin, we acquired dMRI data utilizing the 3T Connectome 2.0 scanner (MAGNETOM Connectom.X, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a custom-built 64-channel whole-brain receive array coil designed for ex vivo dMRI with an embedded forced-air cooling and temperature monitoring system. A 3D multi-shot EPI sequence was used (Gmax = 500 mT/m; SRmax = 487 T/m/s; TR/TE = 500/51 ms; 0.6 mm isotropic resolution; b-value = 2.0/4.5/8.1/12.7/18.3/25 ms/μm2 in 32/32/32/32/32/64 directions; Δ/δ= 17.5/11.5 ms, 6 shots, echo spacing = 1.2 ms, phase partial Fourier = 6/8). Total acquisition time was ~46 hours. Room temperature air was circulated throughout the coil housing during the scan to maintain a steady-state temperature throughout each acquisition.

Data processing:

The dMRI data were preprocessed using MP-PCA denoising, TOPUP, EDDY, and gradient non-linearity distortion correction. The SANDI model with a dot compartment to account for immobile water in fixed ex vivo brain tissue was fitted to obtain maps of soma radius (Ris), intra-soma signal fraction (fis), intra-neurite signal fraction (fin), dot component signal fraction (fdot), intra-neurite diffusivity (Din), and extra-cellular diffusivity (Dec) using random forest regression. All model parameters were projected on cortical surfaces reconstructed using FreeSurfer (v7.4.1). The cortical depth-dependent analysis was conducted from the pial surface (0%) to GM-WM surface (100%) with 5% intervals. For each SANDI metric (θ), a depth-dependent gradient between GM-WM and pial surface was acquired: Δθgw-p = θGM-WM - θpial. Additionally, we performed region of interest (ROI) analysis using cortical parcellation based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas from FreeSurfer. Δθgw-p values were averaged within each ROI, resulting in Δθgw-p,roi. For statistical analysis, we combined all Δθgw-p,roi from all brain specimens, segregated into two groups (i.e., SOF RBE vs. civilian TBI), compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and calculated Cliff’s δ to check the effect size between groups.

Results

Brains donated by SOF personnel demonstrated a consistently larger gradient in fin from the GM-WM surface to the pial surface (Δfin,gw-p) compared with civilian TBI specimens (Figure 1A). The gradient in fin was positive for both SOF and civilian TBI, consistent with recent layer-specific SANDI analysis of healthy subjects.5 In ROI analysis, Δfin,gw-p pooled across all 54 ROIs (both left and right hemispheres) for each group was significantly larger in SOF than civilian TBI group [median Δfin,gw-p  = 0.17 (SOF) vs. 0.09 (civilian), p<0.001] with a Cliff’s δ of 0.73, representing a “very large effect” (Figure 1B). The transverse temporal cortical region showed the largest difference between Δfin,gw-p,roi  of SOF and civilian group [median Δfin,gw-p,roi =0.15 (SOF) vs. 0.05 (civilian)] (Figure 1C). In a lobar-scale analysis, the largest difference between the groups was found in frontal lobe [median Δfin,gw-p,roi  = 0.17 (SOF) vs. 0.10 (civilian)]. Δθgw-p of other SANDI metrics, including Ris, fis, fec (=1- fis- fin- fdot), Din, and Dec, were all negative. Absolute values of them (|Δθgw-p|) were significantly larger in SOF than civilian TBI group (p<0.05) with absolute Cliff’s δ of 0.41, 0.67, 0.55, 0.57, and 0.13, respectively. The largest effect size was observed in fin, and the smallest effect size was observed in Dec.

Conclusion

Ultra-high-gradient ex vivo dMRI combined with SANDI modeling revealed distinct cortical-depth microstructural profiles associated with RBE in SOF personnel. The larger GM-WM-to-pial gradient in fin observed in repeated blast-exposed brains supports the hypothesis that astroglial remodeling at the GM–WM junction contributes to increased neurite-like signal. These findings align with histopathological reports of prominent reactive astrocytosis in rBBI and are further supported by ex vivo multidimensional MRI studies showing that increases in both relaxation times and diffusivities—consistent with reduced axonal packing and density secondary to highly reactive astrocytes3—can elevate neurite-like signal fractions. The observed GM-WM-pial gradient pattern thus indicates a potential microstructure signature of RBE, pending histopathological validation and replication in larger samples. Future work integrating histology, expanding sample sizes, and comparing with other non-invasive imaging biomarkers (e.g., multidimensional MRI3) may allow identification of rBBI in military personnel, making the “invisible wounds of war” visible.
