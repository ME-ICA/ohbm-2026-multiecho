Introduction

Spatial navigation is essential for everyday life and shows large individual differences shaped by both genetic and environmental influences. The hippocampus is a key neural substrate of navigation, but links between hippocampal volume and navigation in healthy samples are mixed.¹ The hippocampus also shows protracted maturation across childhood and adolescence.² Studies using virtual navigation tasks suggest that hippocampal morphology relates to how people navigate, including strategy use.³ Recent work has emphasized that task design and analysis choices can change the observed association and has called for methodological consistency.⁴ We tested whether hippocampal volume measured in childhood predicts MiniCity performance up to 16 years later, and how prediction compares when hippocampal volume is measured at baseline versus at the time of navigation testing.

Methods

We analyzed T1-weighted MPRAGE structural MRI from 630 participants (316 female, 314 male) who had at least one scan before age 15; baseline was the earliest scan ≤15 years (age 4.12–14.99). All T1w scans (baseline and test-time) were processed with FreeSurfer⁵ (v7.4.0) to derive left and right hippocampal volumes and estimated intracranial volume (ICV). Hippocampal volume was left+right hippocampus. Covariates were estimated intracranial volume (ICV), scanner/site, and sex.

Years later, 109 of these participants (70 female, 39 male) completed a laptop-based MiniCity navigation task at 16.50–29.97 years (M = 19.33, SD = 3.28). The gap from baseline MRI to testing was 5.63–16.29 years (M = 11.58, SD = 2.45). MiniCity included 3 minutes of map study and 7 minutes of biking in a virtual environment to locate 8 targets. Each target found counted as 1 point; after the first 8 targets, the same set was repeated within the time limit. Observed scores ranged 0–10 (M = 6.77), and no participant exceeded 10. These 109 participants also had an MRI visit in the same wave as MiniCity (test-time), which provided a second hippocampal volume and ICV measure.

We fit generalized additive models (GAMs) to model baseline hippocampal volume in the full MRI sample (n = 630) from ICV, scanner/site, sex, and a smooth term for baseline age. In the MiniCity subset (n = 109), we fit 3 prediction models: baseline-only, test-time-only, and baseline+test. All MiniCity models included sex and a smooth term for age (baseline age for baseline-only and baseline+test; test-time age for test-time-only), plus the corresponding ICV covariate(s). Baseline models also included time since baseline MRI (years). MiniCity models were fit with maximum likelihood to compare fixed effects across models. We correlated MiniCity score with residual hippocampal volume after covariate adjustment. We compared models using AIC/BIC and repeated 10-fold cross-validated RMSE (5 repeats) to summarize fit and predictive performance. For the joint model, we also ran sensitivity analyses using only baseline ICV or only test-time ICV.

Results

In the baseline-only model, baseline hippocampal volume predicted later MiniCity score (t = 2.15, p = 0.0339). Notably, baseline ICV, a proxy for total brain size, was not associated with MiniCity score (p = 0.280), nor was the time between the baseline MRI and the MiniCity test (p = 0.702). Baseline residual hippocampal volume, computed from the baseline GAM in the full MRI sample (n = 630), correlated with MiniCity score (r = 0.22, 95% CI [0.03, 0.39], p = 0.0216). Test-time residual hippocampal volume showed a smaller, non-significant association (r = 0.15, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.33], p = 0.118), and in the test-time-only model, test-time hippocampal volume was marginal (t = 1.83, p = 0.070). Finally, in the joint model, test-time hippocampal volume explained unique variance (t = 2.21, p = 0.029), while baseline hippocampal volume did not (p = 0.657), consistent with shared variance between the two scans. Prediction improved slightly, with cross-validated RMSE (lower is better) of 2.24 for baseline-only, 2.18 for test-time-only, and 2.14 for baseline+test. The test-time hippocampal effect remained significant in joint-model sensitivity analyses using a single ICV covariate (p = 0.0066 using baseline ICV; p = 0.0157 using test-time ICV).

Conclusion

Hippocampal volume measured in childhood and adolescence predicted navigation performance up to 16 years later. This long time gap suggests that a simple structural measure from early life, in a region with protracted development, can capture stable individual differences relevant for later navigation. A test-time scan added modest predictive value, but the childhood scan alone performed nearly as well. When both scans were modeled together, the test-time measure captured the unique variance, consistent with correlation between repeated hippocampal volumes. Because hippocampal volume is macrostructural, the association may reflect maturational differences in synaptic processes or tissue properties that support navigation ability.
