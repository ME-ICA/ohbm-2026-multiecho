Introduction

Early infancy is a critical period of brain development marked by rapid maturation of anatomical architecture and large-scale functional organization. During this critical window, emerging cognitive abilities arise from the interplay between developing anatomical pathways and dynamic functional activities. Understanding how functional signals are organized relative to evolving structural networks is therefore essential for uncovering core principles of early cognitive development. 

Classic structure–function coupling studies rely on time-averaged connectivity similarity and therefore fail to capture moment-to-moment neural dynamics. Recent advances in graph signal processing offer a complementary framework by modeling BOLD activity at each TR as signals defined on subject-specific structural networks and decomposing them into components that are either aligned with anatomical connectivity or liberal, reflecting structured deviations from anatomical constraints and enabling the characterization of dynamic, signal-level structure–function interplay beyond static connectivity measures.1 Biologically, aligned signals reflect efficient, anatomy-guided coordination across connected regions, whereas liberal signals capture flexible, transient reconfigurations not strictly dictated by structural topology. In adults, this balance between alignment and liberality is behaviorally meaningful, with excessive liberality associated with poorer cognitive flexibility,2 suggesting that optimal cognition depends on controlled deviation rather than unconstrained functional freedom. 

Here, by combining DTI with resting-state fMRI, we examined how aligned and liberal signals evolve during infancy. We identified developmental trajectories of alignment and liberality, and demonstrated that excessive liberality relative to age norms predicts lower cognitive ability, highlighting the importance of developmentally appropriate structure–function integration during early cognitive development.

Methods

A total of 91 subjects whose DTI and resting-state MRI were available (124 scans) were included in this study. All participants were enrolled in the UNC/UMN Baby Connectome Project, ages ranged from 105 to 861 days old. Cognitive assessments using the Mullen Scales of Early Learning  were obtained concurrently with imaging visits. The Early Learning Composite T-score was used as an index of each subject’s cognitive ability.

An infant-specific preprocessing pipeline was applied to all MRI scans.3 Structural images were processed using the iBEAT toolbox, which segments the brain into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and reconstructs topologically correct, geometrically accurate cortical surfaces. Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed with FSL, including removal of the first 10 volumes, motion correction, and band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz). After initial preprocessing, resting-state fMRI images were warped to T1-weighted images using boundary-based registration. Mean signals from white matter, CSF, and 24 motion parameters were regressed out. The preprocessed fMRI data were then projected onto the reconstructed mid-cortical surface and spatially smoothed with a 2 mm FWHM kernel. The Schaefer-200 ROI atlas was used to extract BOLD signals using surface-based registration.4  DTI data were preprocessed using QSIPrep,5 followed by deterministic tractography and SIFT refinement. Structural connectivity matrices were generated by normalizing fiber counts between Schaefer-200 atlas-defined brain regions by the product of their respective volumes.

To quantify the correspondence between functional dynamics and structural architecture, regional BOLD signals at each TR were modeled as a graph signal defined on subject-specific anatomical networks reconstructed from diffusion tractography (Figure 1). For each subject, the anatomical network was represented by a weighted, symmetric adjacency matrix Α∈RNxN, where N denoted the number of brain regions, and Aij encodes the strength of white-matter connectivity. At each TR, the BOLD signal was represented as a graph signal x(t)∈RN, with each entry corresponding to the BOLD activity of a brain region. We applied the Graph Fourier Transform (GFT) to project these signals onto the eigenvector basis of the anatomical network. Specifically, the adjacency matrix was decomposed as A=VΛVT, where Λ contains the ordered eigenvalues, and V contains the corresponding eigenvectors. The GFT of the BOLD signal at time t was then defined as xGFT(t)=VTx(t), yielding a spectrum of graph-frequency components. Components associated with high smoothness (large eigenvalues) reflect BOLD signals aligned with structural connectivity, whereas those with low smoothness (small eigenvalues) capture liberal signals that deviate from anatomical constraints.  Aligned and liberal signals were reconstructed via the inverse GFT using graph filters to isolate the highest- and lowest-smoothness components, respectively: xA(t)=VHAxGFT(t), xL(t)=VHLxGFT(T) , where HA and HL are diagonal filter matrices selecting high- and low-smoothness components. Following prior work,2 we retained the top and bottom 10 components to define most aligned and liberal signal regimes. This decomposition provided a principled framework for assessing the extent to which moment-to-moment functional activity conformed to or diverged from underlying structural topology.

To characterize temporal activity patterns, we computed the mean absolute magnitude of the decomposed signals for each brain region, following prior research,1 because this measure provides a robust summary of signal strength over time by avoiding cancellation of positive and negative fluctuations. We then averaged these values across functional networks or the entire brain to derive system-level measures.

Results

Based on the age distribution of the cohort, scans were grouped into four postnatal age bins centered at 200, 400, 600, and 800 days. The spatial distributions of regional functional alignment and liberality are shown in Figure 2a. Regions within the occipital cortex, prefrontal cortex, and temporal pole consistently exhibited high alignment across age groups. Across development, functional alignment exhibited a pronounced global increase from 200 to 400 days, followed by a relatively stable spatial pattern at later ages. This trend was also reflected in whole-brain and network-level developmental trajectories (Figure 2b). 

In contrast, functional liberality showed a more focal and heterogeneous spatial pattern, with elevated values concentrated in specific higher-order functional regions, including the middle frontal cortex, insula, and intraparietal sulcus (Figure 2a). While whole-brain functional liberality remained relatively stable across development, network-level liberality exhibited distinct developmental trajectories (Figure 2c). Specifically, the visual and ventral attention networks showed a continuous decrease in liberality, whereas the somatomotor network demonstrated a sharp early decline followed by stabilization around 600 days. The limbic network exhibited a modest decrease until approximately 550 days, followed by a slight increase. In contrast, higher-order networks—including the dorsal attention, control, and default mode networks—showed a rapid increase in liberality during the first 600 days, after which levels stabilized.

Individual deviations from these developmental trajectories were further examined in relation to Early Learning Composite scores from the Mullen Scales of Early Learning. While no significant association was observed for functional alignment, deviations in whole-brain functional liberality showed a significant negative correlation with early learning performance (r=-0.28, p=0.001, Figure 2d). Multiple linear regression across the seven functional networks confirmed the predictive role of functional liberality (R2=0.217, p=0.0001), with significant negative effects observed in the visual, somatomotor, control, and default mode networks (Figure 2e).

Conclusion

In this study, we identified distinct developmental trajectories of functional alignment and liberality, revealing substantial heterogeneity across functional systems.

We observed an early increase in functional alignment followed by stabilization at later ages, suggesting the rapid establishment of structure-guided communication as anatomical connectivity develops during infancy and the subsequent emergence of a more mature, anatomically constrained functional organization. This pattern likely reflects increasing myelination, strengthening of long-range white-matter pathways, and improved signal transmission efficiency that together promote coordinated activity across structurally connected regions. Consistently high alignment in the occipital cortex, prefrontal cortex, and temporal pole likely reflects their strong and persistent underlying anatomical connectivity, supporting early visual processing and the formation of integrative circuits relevant for emerging cognitive and socio-emotional functions, respectively. Collectively, these findings suggest that early brain development prioritizes stabilization of structure-guided functional coordination, providing a robust anatomical foundation for later cognitive flexibility.

In contrast, functional liberality exhibited network-specific developmental trajectories, underscoring system-specific maturation of structure–function interplay. Early decreases in liberality within visual and somatomotor networks likely reflect rapid consolidation of anatomy-guided signaling as these systems mature early and become increasingly constrained to support stable sensory and motor functions. This reduction may indicate a transition from initially flexible, exploratory functional organization toward more efficient and reliable processing supported by well-established structural pathways. Although the ventral attention network also showed a decreasing trajectory, its relatively high liberality compared with other networks suggests a sustained role in flexible, stimulus-driven reconfiguration during development, potentially facilitating rapid detection and reorienting to salient environmental inputs during early learning. In contrast, increasing liberality in higher-order networks may reflect the prolonged maturation of association cortices, where flexible, anatomy-deviant functional configurations enable dynamic integration across distributed systems and support the emergence of higher-order cognitive processes, including attention regulation, executive control, and internally oriented mentation. Together, these patterns indicate that developmental changes in functional liberality reflect a coordinated redistribution of functional flexibility rather than a uniform global shift.

Importantly, the association between elevated functional liberality and lower early learning performance suggests that excessive deviation from anatomical constraints may reflect inefficient or unstable network organization during a critical developmental window, potentially disrupting reliable information integration across brain systems. Such excessive liberality may index immature structure–function coupling, leading to increased variability or reduced coherence in functional signaling that undermines early learning. This finding is consistent with prior adult studies linking excessive functional liberality to poorer cognitive flexibility.2 Together, these results indicate that optimal early cognitive development depends not on globally increased or decreased functional freedom, but on achieving a developmentally appropriate balance between structurally aligned communication and flexibly organized functional activity that supports both stability and adaptability.
