Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder imposing a growing socioeconomic burden. Since clinical diagnosis remains difficult for atypical, preclinical, or prodromal cases, robust biomarkers are urgently needed. Advances in psychoradiology1 offer promise for identifying such biomarkers through the noninvasive analysis of brain structure and function.

Brain connectivity can be measured noninvasively in vivo by various neuroimaging methods: functional MRI (fMRI), positron emission tomography (PET), electroencephalography (EEG), and magnetic encephalography (MEG), diffusion MRI (dMRI), structural MRI (sMRI). Graph theoretical analysis (GTA) provides a powerful tool to quantify the global topological organization of brain networks (the ‘connectome’), whose alterations underlie many neuropsychiatric disorders. 

PD is increasingly conceptualized as a disconnection syndrome2. Although many studies have reported brain network abnormalities in PD, findings are inconsistent3. Meta-analyses can identify consistent patterns. Our prior study4 found robust alterations in PD but was limited to a single modality (dMRI) and ignored within-study statistical dependencies. To address these limitations, we used multilevel random-effects models to account for statistical dependencies, conducting a multimodal GTA meta-analysis for a comprehensive assessment of brain dysconnectivity across both structural and functional dimensions. We also used diagnostic meta-analysis to evaluate the diagnostic performance of GTA metrics combined with computational techniques.

This study aimed to identify consistent multimodal global brain network alterations in PD and to assess the diagnostic accuracy of GTA metrics. We conducted both diagnostic and case-control meta-analyses. For the latter, we additional examined cognitive impairment to account for distinct network alterations across symptom subtypes.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted according to the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).

Search strategy, study selection and graph theoretical metrics interpretations

For our search strategy, we used terms related to disease, GTA, and structural or functional neuroimaging. Articles included fulfilled the following criteria: 1) original research investigating network alterations at the whole-brain level applying GTA methods to multimodal neuroimaging; 2) reporting global topological parameters; 3) comparing PD with HC; and 4) reporting sufficient statistics for calculation of effect sizes. Studies eligible for diagnostic meta-analysis reported GTA metrics for PD diagnosis and provided outcomes such as sensitivity (SE) and specificity (SP), used to calculate 2 × 2 contingency tables. 

This study analyzed network segregation, integration, and small-worldness. Segregation (e.g., clustering coefficient, modularity) reflects specialized processing, while integration (e.g., path length, global efficiency) measures communication efficiency. Small-worldhess captures the balance of high clustering and short paths. We also examined complementary metrics—assortativity, strength, degree, and density—to characterize network resilience and connectivity levels. Together, these metrics quantify topological alterations in structural and functional networks.

Data extraction and quality assessment

For the case-control meta-analysis, we extracted demographic data of PD patients and healthy controls, clinical manifestations, MRI data acquisition, preprocessing, and network construction methods, as well as global graph theoretical parameter outcomes. The quality and risk of bias of the included studies were assessed using a modified version of the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. For the diagnostic meta-analysis, we extracted reported diagnostic accuracy data, including sensitivity (SE), specificity (SP), area under the curve (AUC), true positive (TP), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), and false negative (FN). The risk of bias and applicability of all included studies were assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-AI (QUADAS-AI) criteria. When multiple studies derived samples from the same open database, only the study with the largest sample size was included.

Data analysis

For the case-control meta-analysis, effect sizes for each metric were calculated using Hedges’ small sample correction, and pooled results were obtained using a multilevel random-effects meta-analysis. We performed meta-regression to analyze categorical factors such as medication status (off-state/drug-naïve vs. on-state), data acquisition, preprocessing, and network construction methods. Additionally, continuous-variable meta-regression was employed to evaluate the moderating effects of mean patient age, percentage of males, disease duration, years of education, LEDD, UPDRS-III scores, MMSE scores, and H&Y stage.

For the diagnostic meta-analysis, we applied a bivariate random-effects model accounting for the intrinsic correlation between sensitivity and specificity to estimate pooled values of AUC, SE, and SP. Using a three-level meta-analysis, the log-transformed diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) was modeled, incorporating random effects at both the "study" and "table" levels to examine variability within and between studies. Between-study variance (τ²) was estimated using the DerSimonian-Laird method, while overall heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q, I², and τ². To identify sources of extreme heterogeneity, we performed subgroup and moderator analyses based on algorithm type (AI algorithms vs. other algorithms), imaging technique (functional vs. structural imaging techniques), feature selection (whether combined with other network features), and threshold (proportion vs. non-proportion) factors.

Results

1. Characteristics of the included studies

Figure 1 outlines the study selection process. The case-control meta-analysis incorporated 80 studies (42 fMRI, 25 dMRI, 10 EEG, 4 sMRI, 3 others) with 3736 PD patients and 2384 healthy controls. Ten articles (43 contingency tables) qualified for the diagnostic meta-analysis.

2. Primary results in case-control meta-analysis

The different modalities detected somewhat different patterns of alteration in PD: dMRI revealed deficits in both network segregation (lower clustering coefficient and local efficiency) and integration (lower global efficiency and higher characteristic path length); fMRI showed mainly changes in segregation (lower clustering coefficient and modularity); and sMRI and EEG reported no consistent abnormalities (Figure 2). These findings represent partially overlapping but modality-specific patterns of network disruption, rather than cross-modality convergence on a single underlying biology.  

Taking the modalities in turn, dMRI finds a breakdown in both local specialization and global integration in PD vs HC. Specifically, clustering coefficient (g = -0.328, P = 0.002), local efficiency (g = -0.272, P = 0.007) and global efficiency (g = -0.445, P < 0.001) were significantly lower in PD, while characteristic path length (g = 0.396, P = 0.001) and normalized clustering coefficient (g = 0.245, P = 0.026) were significantly higher in PD. By fMRI, PD mainly shows lower network segregation, while measures of network integration remain largely unchanged. Specifically, PD vs HC showed a significantly lower clustering coefficient (g = -0.351, P = 0.004) and a trend falling short of formal statistical significance toward lower local efficiency (g = -0.217, P = 0.066), though global efficiency (P = 0.844) and characteristic path length (P = 0.996) showed no significant differences. Modularity was significantly higher in PD (g = 0.217, P = 0.036). By sMRI and EEG there were no significant case-control differences in GTA metrics. 

Compared to HC, both cognitively normal and cognitively impaired PD patients showed reduced network segregation, as reflected by lower clustering coefficient (Cognitive normal: g = -0.623, P = 0.015; Cognitive impairment: g = -0.993, P = 0.047) and local efficiency (Cognitive normal: g = -0.782, P = 0.008; Cognitive impairment: g = -1.293, P = 0.053). Patients with cognitive impairment had significantly lower local efficiency vs those with normal cognition (g = -0.508, P = 0.051). In contrast, there were no significant differences in network integration (characteristic path length and global efficiency) between PD subtypes (with and without cognitive impairment) or between each subtype and HC. Overall, the studies were of moderate to high quality.

In meta-regression analysis, drug and network construction strategies were identified as potential moderating factors.

3.Primary results in diagnostic meta-analysis

In the bivariate meta-analysis, the pooled SE, SP and area AUC were 0.77 (95% CI: 0.70-0.82), 0.81 (95% CI: 0.76-0.86) and 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83-0.89) respectively. High heterogeneity was observed, with an I² of 89.15% (95% CI: 86.62–91.61%) for SE and 88.86% (95% CI: 88.25–91.47%) for SP. To address the dependency among multiple outcomes within studies, a three-level meta-analysis was performed, yielding a pooled diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of 16.4 (95% CI: 6.1–44.8, prediction interval: 0.40–666.5, P < 0.001). Significant heterogeneity was identified (Q (42) = 524.409, P < 0.0001), including significant between-study variance (τ² = 1.530, I2 = 44.202%, n = 10 studies) and within-study variance (τ² = 1.081, I2 = 51.751%, k = 43 tables).

In meta-regression analysis, studies using GTA Plus (i.e. both GTA metrics and other network metrics) vs GTA Only metrics for distinguishing PD from HC showed higher SE, SP and DOR (P < 0.01), and studies using non-proportional thresholds vs proportional thresholds showed higher SE and SP (P < 0.05).

Conclusion

1. Structural networks are more severely disrupted than functional networks in PD

We found that, in dMRI studies, patients with PD showed consistently lower network segregation and integration compared with HC, whereas fMRI studies revealed selectively reduced segregation only. Notably, this pattern is not unique to PD and has also been reported in other neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease18 and focal epilepsy19. This may be attributable to the higher plasticity of functional networks: functional networks can flexibly reorganize through indirect, dynamic, or context-dependent interactions, whereas structural networks are constrained by the relatively fixed architecture of white-matter tracts.

2. Network segregation was lower in both cognitively normal and cognitively impaired PD patients, with more pronounced abnormality in the latter

Our findings suggest that topological disorganization of brain networks may represent an inherent characteristic of PD, regardless of cognitive status, but that the severity of network disruption increases with the emergence of cognitive impairment. Lower clustering coefficient and local efficiency in the cognitively impaired subgroup may reflect a breakdown of local communication and reduced capacity for functional specialization, consistent with other evidence linking segregation deficits to executive dysfunction and AD4,5. These results support the growing view that cognitive impairment in PD is underpinned by network-level disorganization, and emphasize the importance of stratifying patients by cognitive status in future network analyses. While UPDRS-III scores and education were related to specific network metrics, these associations did not correspond to significant case–control differences, indicating limited robustness of these effects. All the studies included in the current meta-analysis were of cross-sectional design, which cannot explicitly elucidate the causal relationships between brain network alterations and disorder; longitudinal follow-up studies would help clarify these issues.

3.GTA metric exhibits promising but preliminary diagnostic potential for PD

Our meta-analysis demonstrated that the GTA metric exhibits promising but preliminary diagnostic potential for PD, with a pooled AUC of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.89) and a pooled DOR of 16.36 (95% CI: 6.07–44.8). However, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously given the limited external validation and methodological heterogeneity across studies. Significant heterogeneity was primarily attributable to feature selection, as "GTA Plus" studies (combining GTA metrics with other network features) showed significantly higher both AUC and DOR vs "GTA Only". This supports the integration of multimodal features for enhanced PD diagnosis. Although the meta-regression analysis showed that thresholds significantly affect SE and SP, the same impact was not observed for DOR. While the meta-regression and moderator analysis did not reveal statistically significant differences, studies using AI algorithms showed higher DOR, AUC, SE and SP vs non-AI studies, highlighting the potential advantages of automated AI-based diagnostic methods for PD.

Our findings suggest that PD involves distributed disruption of brain network topology, with reduced segregation and impaired integration. Because graph-theoretical metrics are sensitive to acquisition and analysis choices, harmonized protocols, clear reporting of atlases, preprocessing and thresholds, and open code sharing are crucial for reproducible research. Clinically, these metrics may serve as imaging biomarkers, but they require validation in large longitudinal cohorts and integration with structural, functional and molecular data for PD diagnosis and stratification.
