Introduction

Dopaminergic signaling within cortico-striatal circuits is central to cognitive flexibility, creativity, and reward processing1,2. Yet, causal human evidence remains scarce. Transcranial focused ultrasound stimulation (tFUS) allows millimeter-precise, non-invasive modulation of both cortical and subcortical hubs within the dopaminergic system, offering a unique opportunity to probe causal contributions of individual brain areas to brain function3. Here, we combine tFUS with behavioral testing and functional MRI to investigate how perturbing the caudate nucleus (Cd) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC)—two key nodes of the mesocortical dopaminergic pathway—modulates cognitive flexibility and the hedonic experience of music. This study builds on a preceding behavioral experiment in which initial analyses suggested stimulation-related modulations in music-evoked affect.

Methods

In a within-subject, single-blinded, sham-controlled, and randomized study design, healthy participants completed three tFUS–fMRI sessions targeting the right Cd, right dlPFC, or sham stimulation. Each session includes pre- and post-sonication behavioral assessments, simultaneous tFUS-rsMRI, as well as rsMRI between and fMRI during post-sonication tasks. Cognitive flexibility was measured with a fast, trial-wise switching Stroop paradigm designed to quantify switching costs and interference effects with high intra-individual reliability. Hedonic and affective responses to self-selected music excerpts were assessed using continuous ratings of emotional intensity, chills, and valence. High-resolution resting-state and task-based BOLD imaging (TR = 800 ms, multiband factor 4) allowed characterization of both localized and distributed network consequences of stimulation-induced perturbation.

To achieve precise, individualized sonication, we employed a customized preprocessing pipeline that prepares each structural MRI (T1, T2, and PETRA) for simulation and neuronavigation. PETRA-based pseudo-CT generation supported accurate modeling of skull density and acoustic transmission. Sonication planning was performed with Brainsight (Rogue Research, Montréal, Canada), and acoustic field simulations—including focal spot position, peak negative pressure, and thermal safety margins—were computed using BabelBrain4. These simulations guided trajectory definition and parameter selection to ensure consistency across subjects and conditions.

During scanning, the ultrasound transducer was held in place using customized, MR-safe tracking adapters developed in-house to maintain coil and subject alignment under bore constraints. These adapters, combined with optical tracking and repeated inline calibration, allowed stable targeting throughout the process. All ultrasound parameters adhered to established safety guidelines, with real-time monitoring and post-session verification of transducer position recorded for quality assurance.

Analytic procedures followed a preregistered framework: Bayesian hierarchical models evaluated Stroop switching costs and interference effects, while cumulative link mixed models assessed ordinal hedonic ratings. Exploratory analyses investigated stimulation-dependent changes in information flow and mesocortical network dynamics, as well as individual differences in sensitivity to neuromodulation. O-information scores were calculated to measure the balance between synergistic and redundant information between distinct brain areas.

Results

A comprehensive piloting and optimization phase established the feasibility, reliability, and targeting precision of the full tFUS workflow, including sonication, behavioral task delivery, and MRI acquisition. This phase also enabled validation of the preprocessing and analysis pipelines, ensuring that the framework could be deployed consistently across participants. Early analyses reveal significant modulation of music-evoked hedonic responses by tFUS, demonstrating that ultrasound perturbation can influence affective processing in paradigms directly aligned with the current study.

Building on this foundation, preliminary findings indicate individual condition-dependent shifts in behavioral and subjective responses following active sonication, consistent with stimulation-induced alterations in information flow and network dynamics. These effects emerge across both task domains, with patterns suggesting differential engagement under Cd- versus dlPFC-targeted stimulation. Although full statistical inference is ongoing, these preliminary trends support the sensitivity of our combined tFUS–task approach and align with the hypothesized roles of striatal and prefrontal dopaminergic hubs.

Across all completed sessions, sonication has been delivered safely and with high targeting accuracy, as verified by simulation-derived trajectory adherence and stable tracker geometry throughout scanning. To date, participants have tolerated all procedures well, and behavioral task performance has shown high compliance and low dropout. Collectively, these results confirm the robustness of the integrated neuromodulation–imaging framework and provide converging preliminary evidence for stimulation-dependent modulation of cortico-striatal function.

Conclusion

This work establishes a rigorously controlled, simulation-guided TFUS–fMRI framework for causally probing diffferent mesocortical dopaminergic pathway in humans. Early findings from the pilot study and ongoing data collection demonstrate that ultrasound perturbation of Cd and dlPFC influenced distinct cognitive control processes and music-evoked affective experiences and induced distinct patterns of network dynamics and information flow. By modulating deep and cortical nodes within the same individuals, this approach provides a uniquely powerful means of linking dopaminergic circuit dynamics to behavior.

Beyond the immediate empirical findings, the study advances methodological standards for non-invasive neuromodulation through precise targeting, multimodal validation, and preregistered analytic pathways. The integration of individualized acoustic simulation, neuronavigation, and high-temporal-resolution BOLD imaging creates a platform for future mechanistic research exploring how transient perturbations propagate through large-scale neural systems.

Together, these contributions establish a foundation for next-generation investigations into the neural basis of creativity, flexibility, motivation, and hedonia. The paradigm holds further translational promise for disorders with dysregulated dopaminergic function, offering a blueprint for safe, targeted, and mechanistically interpretable neuromodulation.
