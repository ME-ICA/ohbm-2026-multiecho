Introduction

Reading is an essential skill in our society. During reading acquisition, children progressively integrate visual, phonological, and semantic processes into an increasingly coordinated neural network. Understanding how these reading-specific processes, as well as domain-general cognitive abilities, contribute to successful reading development is crucial for identifying the mechanisms underlying typical and atypical reading acquisition. However, substantial concurrent developmental changes in cognition, language, and overall brain maturation during childhood make it challenging to isolate the specific contributions of different processes to emerging reading skills. The artificial script (AS) learning paradigm offers a powerful experimental approach for addressing this challenge: individuals learn the associations between unfamiliar orthographic characters (i.e., an AS) and phonemes within a relatively short timeframe1. Yet, despite the usefulness of the paradigm for modeling the learning of early reading processes, the neural dynamics that unfold in AS processing immediately after such short training are still not fully understood. Specifically, it is unclear whether a brief AS training already engages core reading-specific neural networks or whether learners rely predominantly on domain-general cognitive mechanisms such as attention or working memory to process the novel script. Furthermore, the temporal dynamics of brain responses during nascent orthographic processing have not been systematically characterized. Here, we aim to address these gaps by investigating the behavioral and neural correlates of AS reading after a very short (30-minute) AS learning session in typically reading adults. We investigated three key questions: Which brain regions support the processing of a newly learned AS? Do temporal response dynamics in core reading regions differ between novel and familiar scripts? How do domain-general cognitive abilities shape early orthographic processing?

Methods

Participants

61 healthy, typically reading, native German-speaking adults (24.10 years, 38 f) participated in the study. The inclusion criteria for the study were reading fluency above the 20th percentile as assessed by the Salzburger Lese-und Rechtschreibtest II, and right-handedness. 

Experimental design

All participants underwent a digit span test to assess working memory and the d2-R test of attention. During the fMRI session, participants completed three 10-minute AS training runs. Immediately after training, they performed a lexical decision task with the newly learned script while still in the MR scanner. Finally, all participants performed an AS reading fluency task outside the MR scanner. 

Artificial script learning

During the AS training, participants learned the correct associations between 12 AS letters and German phonemes (Figure 1A). For each trial, participants were presented with an AS letter-phoneme pair and had to perform a button press to indicate whether the pairing was correct or incorrect. After a jittered delay, participants received visual feedback on their performance. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of two AS letter sets. 

Lexical decision task

After training, participants performed a 10-minute lexical decision task (Figure 1B). During this task, German words and pseudowords were presented in either AS or Latin script blocks. Participants were instructed to perform a button press to indicate whether a stimulus was a word or a pseudoword. During each block, four AS or Latin script stimuli were presented for 7 seconds each. In total, 40 words and 40 pseudowords were presented per run. 

Scan parameters

MRI scanning was performed at the MR Center of the Psychiatric University Hospital Zurich using a 3 Tesla Philipps Achieva MRI scanner (Philipps, Best, The Netherlands) and a 32-channel head coil. Functional MRI images during the lexical decision task were acquired using a gradient-echo T2*-weighted planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (RT) = 1250 ms, echo time (TE) = 35 ms, flip angle (FA) = 80°, field of view (FoV) = 240 x 240 x 132 mm3, 40 slices in ascending order, multiband factor 2, slice gap of 0.3 mm, voxel size 3 x 3 x 3 mm3. The first five images were excluded from analyses and discarded as dummy scans. At the end of the scanning session, a T1-weighted anatomical image was acquired with an FA of 9°, 176 slices, a voxel size of 1x1x1 mm3, and a FoV of 197 × 197 × 138 mm3. 

Whole-brain fMRI data analyses

fMRI data during the lexical decision task was preprocessed using a standard preprocessing pipeline using SPM12 and MATLAB2024b, including realignment, coregistration, normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute space, and spatial smoothing with 6 mm full width at half maximum. For the first-level analysis, a general linear model was created with regressors for AS blocks and Latin script blocks, as well as the six motion parameters. 

Region of interest fMRI data analyses

For ROI analyses, we extracted representative time courses from key regions of interest involved in reading (based on Neurosynth). Time series were extracted as eigenvariates of the signal, i.e., the first principal component of the raw signal, and accounted for the six motion regressors. The extracted time series were averaged over all ten blocks and, additionally, over all stimuli of the same condition. Finally, for each participant, we extracted information on the timing and value of signal peaks within the mean trial time courses. Paired t-tests were performed to assess differences in peak activity during AS reading and Latin script reading, and differences in peak timing between the two conditions. 

Behavioral analyses

To assess relationships between behavioral variables, Pearson correlations were calculated. 

Results

Artificial script learning

All participants successfully learned the associations between letters written in an AS and German phonemes within three runs of 10 minutes duration each. While participants had to initially guess the correct pairings and learn from the respective feedback, they achieved an accuracy of 89% and 93% in the second and third runs, respectively.

Artificial script reading fluency

There was no significant correlation between AS reading fluency and Latin script reading fluency (r(60)=0.04, p=0.77). AS reading fluency correlated significantly with attention (r(60)=0.56, p<0.001) and working memory (r(60)=0.38, p=0.003), while Latin script reading fluency only showed a correlation with attention (r(60)=0.26, p=0.04), but not working memory (r(60)=0.10, p=0.46). Importantly, correlations between AS reading fluency and attention and working memory were significantly stronger than the correlations between Latin script reading fluency and attention and working memory (attention: z(60)=1.90, p=0.03; working memory: z(60)=1.63, p=0.05).

Performance during the lexical decision task

On average, participants performed the lexical decision task for AS stimuli with an accuracy of 75.12%, while the accuracy for Latin script stimuli was 97.56%. AS task performance (as assessed by accuracy) was significantly correlated with attention (r(60)=0.37, p=0.003), working memory (r(60)=0.28, p=0.03) and AS reading fluency (r(60)=0.79, p<0.001), but not Latin script reading fluency (r(60)=-0.05, p=0.69). Latin script task performance hit ceiling levels in many participants, so no correlations were performed. 

Whole-brain fMRI analyses

When comparing AS reading to Latin script, we observed significant activation (FWE-corrected p<0.05) in the occipito-temporal cortex, including the visual word form area (VWFA), the superior parietal lobule, supramarginal gyrus, left precentral gyrus (PCG), supplementary motor cortex, and cerebellum. Significant activation was also found in the left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), ventral tegmental area, bilateral putamen, and thalamus (see Figure 3). Large parts of the activation showed overlap with the multiple-demand network. 

Time-course analyses

To gain insights into the temporal dynamics of AS processing and Latin script processing, we investigated brain activation time courses driven by AS and Latin script stimuli in key regions of the reading network (Figure 2), namely the VWFA, superior temporal gyrus (STG), IFG, PCG, and inferior parietal lobule (IPL). The VWFA (t(44)=6.57, p<0.001), IFG (t(44)=3.63, p<0.001), PCG (t(44)=5.65, p<0.001) and IPL (t(44)=13.03, p<0.001) demonstrated significantly higher peak activation during AS reading than during Latin script reading. In contrast, the STG (t(44)=-2.57, p=0.01) showed significantly stronger peak activation during Latin script than during AS reading.

Interestingly, the peak activation during AS reading, controlled for peak activation during Latin script reading, showed significant correlations with AS reading fluency for the VWFA (r(44)=0.39, p=0.01), the PCG (r(44)=0.37, p=0.01), and the IPL (r(44)=0.47, p=0.001), but not for the STG (r(44)=0.08, p=0.91) and the IFG (r(44)=0.21, p=0.18). 

The VWFA showed a slight but non-significant delay in peak activation timing between the AS condition and the Latin script condition (t(44)=-1.82, p=0.08). Interestingly, in the STG, peak activation was reached earlier for the AS condition than for the Latin script condition (t(44)=2.70, p=0.01). For the IFG, we observed a substantial delay in peak activation during AS reading compared to Latin-script reading. By the time the AS condition reached its peak, activation in the Latin-script condition had already declined to its minimum. Finally, for the PCG and IPL no analyses could be performed for peak timing as activation during AS reading was at a constantly high level and no activation peaks could be defined.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the behavioral and neural correlates of reading a newly learned script as compared to a familiar script. 

Artificial script reading performance

In our sample, AS fluency did not correlate with Latin script fluency. One possible explanation is that decoding a novel orthography relies disproportionately on domain-general cognitive processes such as working memory and attention, whereas fluent reading of a familiar script depends primarily on automatized word recognition and well-established orthographic- phonological mappings that require minimal executive resources. In line with this account, in this study, AS reading fluency was strongly associated with working memory and attention, whereas Latin script reading fluency showed only weak or no significant associations with these measures. Further, attention and working memory also showed strong correlations with AS reading performance during the lexical decision task. These findings suggest that as orthographic expertise develops, reading performance transitions from reliance on domain-general executive processes to automatized, specialized processing mechanisms.

Artificial script reading compared to Latin script reading in the brain

Whole-brain fMRI analyses showed that the AS > Latin script contrast activated a network of regions that overlapped substantially with the multiple-demand network. The multiple-demand network is consistently recruited during tasks that require a high level of cognitive control and effortful problem-solving 2. Prior studies have shown that activation in the multiple demand network is positively correlated with working memory and attention. Thus, our finding of increased activity within the multiple demand network during AS reading emphasizes the role of domain-general cognitive processes during early reading and is in line with our behavioral findings. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the multiple demand network is rapidly engaged to support novel symbol decoding, even following brief letter-sound learning. 

Neural dynamics in key regions of the reading network

Beyond conventional whole-brain analyses, we performed temporal response profile analyses of key regions of the reading network. This approach revealed not only which regions were engaged during AS and Latin script processing, but also how their neural responses evolved over time. AS reading elicited higher activation than Latin script reading across most key regions of the reading network (VWFA, IFG, PCG, IPL), likely reflecting the increased demand for neural resources when processing a novel script compared to a familiar script. Finally, time-course analyses revealed distinct temporal dynamics across regions. In the VWFA, peak latencies were comparable for AS and Latin scripts, indicating that VWFA responses are time-locked to visual stimulus onset regardless of script familiarity or reading automaticity. This is consistent with the role of the VWFA as an early, prelexical orthographic processor that is reliably responds to letter-like stimuli across writing systems 3. In contrast, the IFG showed an earlier peak latency for Latin than AS. This temporal pattern aligns with evidence that the IFG supports phonological retrieval and articulatory planning, processes that occur more rapidly for familiar, automatized words than for newly learned orthographic forms 4. For the PCG and IPL, AS reading elicited sustained, elevated activity without distinct peaks, precluding direct latency comparisons. This sustained activation pattern reflects the substantial and prolonged cognitive demands required for phonological and motor processing of novel orthographic material in these regions. 

 Collectively, this study validates the AS paradigm as a powerful tool for isolating the neural and cognitive mechanisms underlying early reading acquisition and reveals the critical role of domain-general executive processes before reading-specific networks become automatized.
