Introduction

Early identification of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) remains a pressing clinical challenge, with disease-modifying therapies now entering clinical practice, and detecting neurodegeneration during the prodromal window before extensive synaptic loss has become paramount. However, existing screening paradigms fall short: neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid analysis are costly, invasive, or inaccessible for population-level deployment, while cognitive batteries lack sensitivity to preclinical changes.

Emerging research points to two promising biomarker domains operating through distinct biological mechanisms. Circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) carries methylation signatures from dying neurons, offering a peripheral window into central nervous system pathology. Meanwhile, motor function, specifically gait performance under cognitive load, serves as a real-time readout of distributed brain network integrity. These modalities interrogate fundamentally different aspects of neurodegeneration: cfDNA captures ongoing cellular damage at the molecular level, whereas gait reflects the downstream functional consequences on sensorimotor and executive systems.

We hypothesized that combining these orthogonal biomarker streams within a unified machine learning framework would yield diagnostic performance exceeding either approach in isolation. To test this, we developed a multimodal AI platform that integrates cfDNA methylation analysis with quantitative gait metrics extracted via computer vision in real-time. This study presents the development of our multimodal architecture and validates the gait analysis component in a pilot clinical cohort.

Methods

Platform Development: We developed a multimodal AI platform integrating two complementary biomarker streams for early AD/MCI detection. The architecture employs late fusion of modality-specific predictions, combining molecular signatures from cfDNA methylation with functional motor-cognitive biomarkers from gait analysis. The platform was implemented with modular pipelines for each biomarker stream.

cfDNA Methylation Module: The cfDNA classification model was trained on publicly available methylation datasets from the AD Knowledge Portal and NIAGADS, comprising genome-wide methylation profiles from AD, MCI, and cognitively normal subjects. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified using DMRcate (R/Bioconductor) with a significance threshold of FDR<0.05 and absolute methylation difference >10%. Preprocessing and normalization were performed using minfi (R/Bioconductor) with BMIQ normalization to correct for probe type bias. 

Prospective cfDNA Validation Protocol: Following IRB approval, we will collect peripheral blood samples (10mL EDTA tubes) from participants who completed the gait assessment. Genome-wide methylation profiling will be performed using the array targeting >850,000 CpG sites. Quality control metrics will include bisulfite conversion efficiency >95%, detection p-value filtering (<0.01), and sample identity verification.

Gait Analysis Module - Clinical Validation: We enrolled 38 participants under IRB approval from an academic memory clinic (mean age 68.0±9.1 years, range 54-90 years). Participants were classified into cognitive impairment (AD and MCI) and cognitively normal control groups based on a comprehensive clinical evaluation, including Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR). Exclusion criteria included non-AD dementias, active malignancy, musculoskeletal disorders affecting gait, and recent blood transfusion.

Gait Assessment Protocol: Participants completed a standardized dual-task gait protocol using markerless computer vision. The protocol included single-task walking at a self-selected pace and dual-task walking while performing cognitive tests. Spatiotemporal gait features were extracted, and feature engineering was performed using 42 parameters, including stride velocity, stride length, stride time, stride-to-stride variability (coefficient of variation), double support time, gait asymmetry index, and dual-task cost. All features were normalized for height and baseline walking speed.

Machine Learning Pipeline: The gait classification model employed XGBoost gradient boosting with recursive feature elimination (scikit-learn), identifying 12 key predictive features. Model training used stratified 5-fold cross-validation with hyperparameter tuning via grid search. The multimodal ensemble integrates cfDNA and gait predictions using learned fusion weights optimized on validation data. Model interpretability was assessed using SHAP values to quantify feature contributions.

Statistical Analysis: Classification performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC-ROC. Group differences in gait parameters were assessed using independent t-tests with Bonferroni correction. Correlations between biomarkers and MoCA scores were evaluated using Pearson coefficients (SciPy, statsmodels). Statistical significance was set at α=0.05.

Results

Multimodal Platform Architecture: The developed platform successfully integrates cfDNA methylation analysis and gait assessment modules within a unified prediction framework. The cfDNA module was trained on published methylation data identifying DMRs enriched in pathways related to synaptic function, neuroinflammation, and neuronal signaling. The architecture supports late fusion of modality-specific predictions, enabling flexible weighting based on biomarker availability and confidence.

Gait Analysis Validation: In the pilot cohort (N=38), the gait classification model achieved 93.8% overall accuracy in distinguishing cognitive impairment from cognitively normal controls. Significant between-group differences emerged under dual-task conditions, with participants showing cognitive impairment demonstrating elevated stride-to-stride variability and increased dual-task cost compared to controls.

Feature Importance: The minimal feature set achieving optimal performance was dominated by dual-task gait variability metrics. Top predictive features included the stride velocity coefficient of variation during cognitive load, dual-task cost for stride velocity, stride time variability, and stride length variability. Single-task features contributed less predictive power, confirming the value of motor-cognitive interference paradigms.

Dual-Task Performance: Participants with cognitive impairment showed reduced accuracy on the serial subtraction task during walking compared to controls. Dual-task gait variability showed significant negative correlation with MoCA scores, with stronger correlations observed for dual-task than single-task parameters.

Biomarker Complementarity Analysis: Theoretical modeling based on the trained cfDNA module and validated gait module suggests modest correlation between modality-specific predictions, supporting the orthogonal information hypothesis. The multimodal architecture is designed to leverage this complementarity, with cfDNA features expected to capture early molecular changes and gait features reflecting functional motor-cognitive manifestations.

Conclusion

This study presents a multimodal AI platform integrating cfDNA methylation and gait biomarkers for early Alzheimer's disease and MCI detection. Clinical validation of the gait analysis component achieved 93.8% accuracy in a pilot cohort of 38 participants, demonstrating robust discrimination between cognitive impairment and normal aging.

The platform architecture reflects the distinct biological underpinnings of each biomarker stream. cfDNA methylation captures brain-derived epigenetic signatures detectable in peripheral blood, while dual-task gait variability indexes the functional integrity of frontal-subcortical circuits underlying motor-cognitive integration. By combining molecular and functional biomarkers, the platform aims to achieve superior diagnostic performance compared to single-modality approaches.

The prominence of dual-task gait variability as the strongest predictor aligns with the motor-cognitive interference hypothesis and supports gait analysis as a proxy measure for distributed brain network integrity. The strong correlations with MoCA scores validate the clinical relevance of automated gait assessment for cognitive screening.

From a translational perspective, the platform offers a scalable, non-invasive diagnostic solution. Gait assessment requires only a 3-minute walking protocol with standard camera equipment, while cfDNA analysis uses a routine blood draw. Combined estimated cost of $200-400 per assessment compares favorably to PET imaging ($3,000-6,000) or CSF analysis ($2,500-4,000), enabling potential deployment in primary care and community screening settings.

Limitations include the pilot sample size for gait validation and the need for prospective cfDNA validation. Ongoing studies will collect matched blood samples from gait-characterized participants to validate the full multimodal pipeline and quantify the performance gain from biomarker integration.

In conclusion, multimodal AI integrating molecular and functional biomarkers represents a promising paradigm for accessible, accurate early AD detection in the era of disease-modifying therapies.
