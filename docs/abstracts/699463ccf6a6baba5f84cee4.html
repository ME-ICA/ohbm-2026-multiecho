Introduction

Mental disorders account for ~20% of years lived with disability worldwide, motivating a shift from treatment to prevention and early risk detection. Yet robust biomarkers of vulnerability remain scarce, partly because symptoms, genetics, risk factors, and neurobiology overlap across diagnoses, and because risk factors are multifactorial. A transdiagnostic, multimodal approach is therefore needed. 

Emotion dysregulation (ED), the difficulty in modulating emotional experience and responses, is a central transdiagnostic symptom characterizing several emotion dysregulation disorders (EDD: borderline personality disorder, bipolar disorder, ADHD), which share clinical features, heritability, neurobiological substrates, and high comorbidity1. Studying individuals at familial risk (offspring of EDD patients; EDDoff) offers a critical window for intervention, especially during adolescence and young adulthood when fronto-limbic circuits for emotion regulation mature. 

Converging evidence links ED to inflammation and  stress, with effects on hippocampal structure and function and large-scale networks. Beyond limbic and prefrontal systems, the sensorimotor network (SMN), which supports embodied emotion regulation, also appears altered in EDD. Disrupted hippocampus-SMN coupling may impair sensorimotor integration and appraisal of emotional stimuli, sustaining a cycle of stress, inflammation, and ED. Peripheral inflammation can compromise the blood-brain barrier and promote neurotoxic pathways, potentially damaging networks relevant to ED2. 

Despite these links, no study has jointly integrated clinical, functional and diffusion MRI, and serum biomarkers within a transdiagnostic framework in high-risk youth. Our study addresses this gap by combining clinical assessments, functional and diffusion MRI, and immune markers in EDD patients, EDDoff, and healthy controls. This multimodal approach aims to identify convergent signatures of ED to inform targeted intervention.

Methods

Participants. Three groups were recruited, as previously described3: adults with EDD (BD, BPD, ADHD), EDDoff, and healthy controls (HC). Diagnoses followed DSM-IV-TR and patients were recruited at Geneva University Hospitals. EDDoff had at least one parent with EDD. No participants were biologically related across groups. The ethics committee of Geneva approved the study (CER 13-081). All participants provided written informed consent and completed a multimodal battery covering clinical, serum, and neuroimaging measures (structural and dynamic functional connectivity).

Clinical assessment. Trained psychologists administered the Diagnostic Interview for Genetic Studies. Emotion dysregulation (ED) was indexed by the Affective Lability Scale (ALS) and the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (CERQ). Additional questionnaires assessed depression, anxiety, personality, cognition, and demographics.

Biological markers. 28 serum cytokines and growth factors were assayed with the Procarta multiplex platform, including pro-inflammatory markers such as CRP, anti-inflammatory markers such as IL-10, and neurotrophic markers such as BDNF. 

Dynamic functional connectivity (dFC). Resting-state fMRI acquisition, preprocessing, motion handling, and hippocampal seed-based micro-co-activation pattern (μCAPs) methods follow prior work4. Five large-scale μCAP networks were identified from data-driven, activity-based hippocampal parcels. For each participant, we computed μCAP occurrence measures indexing the dFC of each μCAP with its corresponding hippocampal parcel.

Structural connectivity. Diffusion data were acquired on a 3T Siemens TrioTim (TR 6300 ms, TE 84 ms, voxel 2.3 × 2.3 × 2.0 mm, 72 directions, b = 1000 s/mm², 52 slices, FoV read 230 mm, phase 100%). Processing followed ENIGMA-DTI protocols. Briefly, preprocessing steps were conducted using FSL, FreeSurfer, ANTs, and MRtrix3. This included N4 bias correction (ANTs), skull stripping (FreeSurfer), motion and eddy-current correction (FSL), and tensor estimation (MRtrix3). FA was computed via tensor2metric. TBSS was implemented with FSL, registration to the ENIGMA FA template, and ROI-wise FA extraction using the JHU white-matter atlas5. Visual quality controls were performed at each step.

Descriptive analysis: PCA. We used a two-step PCA strategy. First, a multimodal PCA on all variables from four blocks (clinical, CL; dFC; FA; serum biomarkers, BIO) identified components capturing shared variance across modalities. Retained component (RC) scores were compared across groups (EDD, EDDoff, HC) with ANOVA and Tukey HSD post-hoc test, to probe transdiagnostic patterns and vulnerability. Second, separate PCAs within each block characterized domain-specific structure, and Pearson correlations were computed between RCs across blocks to test cross-domain associations. Component retention used parallel analysis, which adjusts for small-sample bias and compares observed eigenvalues to the 95th percentile of random eigenvalues. Analyses were run in R 4.3.3 with the psych package, Varimax rotation, and Thurstone regression-based scores. Analyses were adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Predictive analysis: random forests. We trained random forest (RF) models for two aims. RF classification to discriminate HC from individuals with EDD or EDDoff using all multimodal predictors. Performance was benchmarked against a baseline multinomial ridge model. RF regression was used to predict continuous ED symptom scores (ALS and CERQ) from non-clinical features only, that is, serum and neuroimaging markers. RFs were implemented in R using the randomForest package with out-of-bag (OOB) optimization. Data were stratified by group and split into training and test sets (70-30%). Three artificial "probe" variables (Gaussian, uniform, and binomial noise) were initially included to detect overfitting. The number of drawn candidate variables in each split was set to the standard value of sqrt(p).

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

The final sample comprised 237 participants: 97 with emotion dysregulation disorders (EDD; ADHD n=30, BPD n=33, BD n=34), 67 offspring of EDD patients (EDDoff), and 73 healthy controls (HC). Groups did not differ in education or handedness (all p>0.05).

Significant group differences in PCA components related to ED symptoms, inflammation, and hippocampal connectivity

A multimodal PCA across clinical, neuroimaging, and serum variables yielded eight retained components. Two showed significant group effects. RC3, labeled “Elevated ED with hippocampal-visual-somatomotor (SMN) hypoconnectivity and limbic (LN) hyperconnectivity”, loaded on higher affective lability, greater use of non-adaptive regulation strategies, anxiety, and borderline traits, together with lower hippocampus-SMN and higher hippocampus-LN connectivity. RC3 scores were higher in EDD and EDDoff than HC (all Tukey-adjusted padj<.00001). RC5, labeled “High immune dysregulation”, was driven by pro-inflammatory markers (for example MIF, MCP1, CD62E, CRP, IL18, IL17) together with BDNF and mixed-role mediators (IL2-R, EGF). RC5 scores were higher in EDD than in EDDoff and HC (all padj<.02). These results indicate distinct clinical, functional, and biological alterations in EDD and in familial risk.

Inflammation is inversely correlated with white matter integrity, and hippocampal connectivity positively correlates with ED symptoms and inflammation

Block-wise PCAs identified 11 components overall (four BIO, three FA, three CL, one dFC). Interblock correlations revealed that a structural connectivity component (FA-RC3; higher FA in CGH, SS, EC, RLIC, PTR) correlated negatively with an immune-dysregulation component (BIO-RC1; IL12, IL4, IFNγ, IL10, IL17, IL1β, TNFα), r=-.15, t(235)=-2.38, p=.01; removal of one influential HC reduced this to a nonsignificant trend. The sole dFC component (dFC-RC1) correlated positively with a clinical “High-ED” component (CL-RC1) and with an inflammatory component (BIO-RC4 driven by IP10, GROα, VCAM, IL18, IL17), p<.01 for both, supporting links among hippocampal connectivity, ED severity, and inflammation.

A RF classifier distinguishes HC with strong accuracy

A random forest classifier using all multimodal predictors distinguished HC from EDD or EDDoff with 85.7% accuracy (95% CI 75.3–92.9, p=.001), κ=0.64, McNemar p=.34, sensitivity 93.9%, specificity 66.7%, balanced accuracy 80.3%, PPV 86.8%, and NPV 82.4%. The most influential features were clinical measures related to ED (affective lability, borderline traits, depression, anxiety, ADHD), followed by imaging features (FA in RLIC and ACR, hippocampal connectivity with SMN); key serum contributors included CRP, IL2R, and MCP-1.

 RF regression accurately predicts ED symptoms

Random forest regression models trained on biological and neuroimaging predictors alone accurately estimated ED symptoms. For ALS, performance was RMSE 0.54, MAE 0.44, R²=.23, with predicted scores significantly correlating with observed ones, r=.48, t(235)=4.45, p<.00005. High FA in PTR and low FA in ALIC were the strongest structural predictors, while lower hippocampus-SMN and higher hippocampus-LN connectivity were the strongest functional predictors. CERQ scores were also predicted accurately (r=.32, t(235)=2.73, p=.007), with structural connectivity variables being again among the most influential ones. The same predictors did not reliably estimate other symptom domains such as depression or ADHD. Overall, findings highlight a multimodal signature linking ED symptoms with hippocampal network imbalance and immune dysregulation, detectable in patients and in familial risk.

Conclusion

This multimodal, transdiagnostic study identified clinically and biologically meaningful signatures of ED vulnerability and disease across EDD patients, EDDoff, and healthy controls.

As detailed above, PCA conducted on the full dataset identified two transdiagnostic and multimodal components (one associated with ED and hippocampal connectivity, and the other associated with inflammation) that showed significant differences among groups. Subsequent modality-specific PCAs and inter-block correlation analyses revealed clinically relevant patterns of convergence across clinical, neuroimaging, and biological domains. Complementary machine learning analyses further confirmed the predictive utility of specific multimodal features for both group classification and symptom severity, extending prior literature and supporting a dimensional, transdiagnostic view of ED vulnerability.

These findings collectively suggest that hippocampal connectivity alterations and clinical symptoms may represent ED vulnerability markers, as evidenced by their disruption in both EDD patients and EDDoff compared to HC, along with their positive intercorrelation, which suggests their mutual reinforcement in early-stage vulnerability. In contrast, a pronounced pro-inflammatory state appears to emerge specifically in established EDD, suggesting that significant immune dysregulation may be more characteristic of full clinical manifestation rather than prodromal vulnerability stages.

Several multimodal features emerge as potential transdiagnostic EDD markers to be further investigated for early intervention, risk stratification, patient stratification, or diagnosis. These include white matter microstructure in visual/sensorimotor (PTR, RLIC), and emotion-regulating (ALIC, CGH) tracts, functional connectivity disruptions in large-scale networks (particularly -visual-sensorimotor, SMN, and limbic, LN, networks), and elevated levels of systemic biomarkers including BDNF and pro-inflammatory markers (CRP, MCP-1, MIF). The transdiagnostic convergence of ED, visual-sensorimotor and emotion-regulating networks dysfunction, and immune dysregulation provides novel mechanistic insights into the multi-system pathophysiology underlying EDD.

This integrated vulnerability profile carries implications for developing precision psychiatry approaches aimed at early identification and intervention in high-risk populations. Future longitudinal studies should systematically evaluate the predictive value and causal relationships of these multimodal markers for early identification and illness progression, while intervention research should investigate targeted approaches capable of modifying these transdiagnostic risk pathways. As a perspective, particularly promising in this regard may be mindfulness-based interventions, given their potential capacity to improve functional connectivity patterns, reduce inflammatory markers, and enhance white matter integrity. Hypothetically, these effects may reflect a fundamental restoration of body-mind integration, potentially addressing the observed sensorimotor dysconnectivity and its downstream clinical effects on emotional processing and dissociation symptoms in EDD patients. Hence, a multimodal, transdiagnostic, and targeted intervention of this nature could theoretically interrupt the vicious cycle sustaining ED.

This integrated approach could inform future precision psychiatry strategies, moving beyond diagnosis to the early identification and targeted, early treatment of at-risk individuals, ultimately reducing vulnerability and progression to overt psychopathology among at-risk populations such as EDDoff.
