Introduction

Visual imagery—the ability to conjure scenes in the "mind's eye" without external stimuli—varies remarkably across individuals, from those with photo-like vividness (hyperphantasia) to those lacking imagery entirely (aphantasia). This variability has profound implications: imagery underpins memory, future planning, emotion regulation, and clinical interventions from trauma treatment to motor rehabilitation. Yet despite decades of research, the neural basis of imagery vividness remains contested. A longstanding debate centers on whether early visual cortex (EVC) or high-level regions like the left fusiform gyrus—termed the fusiform imagery node (FIN)—are critical for imagery generation. Meta-analyses show consistent fusiform engagement with minimal EVC involvement, while within-subject studies demonstrate EVC recruitment when imagery is vivid. These perspectives may reflect complementary mechanisms rather than contradictory findings. Beyond visual regions, the salience network—anchored in the anterior insula—has emerged as a candidate for regulating access to internally generated content by mediating transitions between externally and internally oriented brain states. To reconcile these accounts, we moved beyond region-centric approaches to examine how brain network topology relates to imagery vividness. Using graph theory applied to structural (DWI; n=525) and functional (rs-fMRI; n=556) connectomes, we tested connectivity patterns across four networks: a task-defined imagery network, the occipital network, salience network, and default mode network. Graph metrics (local efficiency, clustering coefficient, global efficiency, betweenness centrality) quantified network segregation and integration properties. This large-sample, multimodal approach allowed us to test whether vivid imagery emerges from sensory architecture, higher-order integration, salience-based regulation, or their interplay.

Methods

Participants completed the 32-item VVIQ-2 (eyes closed) approximately one week before MRI scanning. Data were collected at two sites (Krakow, Poland; Aarhus, Denmark) using 3T Siemens scanners (Skyra and Prisma-fit) with harmonized protocols. The functional sample included 556 participants; the structural sample included 525 participants.

Resting-state fMRI comprised 18 minutes of acquisition (TR=700-801ms, TE=30-33.6ms, 2.5mm³ voxels). Preprocessing used SPM CONN toolbox surface-based pipelines including realignment, coregistration, CompCor denoising, and bandpass filtering (0.008-0.09Hz). Functional connectivity matrices (148×148 Destrieux atlas regions) were computed using Fisher-transformed correlations.

Diffusion-weighted imaging used high-angular resolution acquisition (211 directions across multiple b-shells: b=5-2500 s/mm²). Preprocessing included denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, and distortion correction. Structural connectomes were constructed using MRtrix3 with anatomically-constrained tractography (ACT), multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (MSMT-CSD), and SIFT2 filtering, selecting 10 million streamlines per connectome.

Four networks were extracted from full connectivity matrices: (1) a task-defined imagery network (22 nodes) based on prior fMRI and lesion evidence, including bilateral inferior frontal gyrus, parahippocampal and fusiform gyri, inferior temporal regions, intraparietal sulcus, precuneus, and occipital pole; (2) an occipital network (16 nodes) representing early visual areas; (3) a salience network (19 nodes) mapped from Yeo7 parcellation; and (4) a default mode network (29 nodes) from HCP atlas designations.

Connectivity matrices were thresholded across a cost range (k=0.15-0.30, interval 0.01) and binarized. Four graph metrics were computed using Brain Connectivity Toolbox: local efficiency and clustering coefficient (segregation), global efficiency (integration), and betweenness centrality (hubness). Metrics were calculated at nodal and network levels.

Statistical analysis used general linear models with VVIQ-2 as the explanatory variable, controlling for age, gender, and site. Node-level p-values were FDR-corrected (q<0.05) within each metric per network. Multivariate regression with 10-fold cross-validation assessed joint contributions of significant predictors, evaluating model performance via RMSE, R², AIC, and BIC.

Results

Mean VVIQ-2 score was 117.21 (SD=22.41, range=32-160), with no associations with age (r=-0.006, p=0.89) or gender (t=-0.08, p=0.94).

Functional connectivity: Within the task-defined imagery network, local efficiency in the left fusiform gyrus was positively associated with imagery vividness (F=3.15, pFDR=0.038, R=0.12, N=555). This was the only node-specific effect across all functional analyses. No network-level effects survived correction in functional connectomes for any network.

Structural connectivity—Occipital network: VVIQ scores were positively associated with network-level local efficiency (F=2.44, pFDR=0.015, R=0.102, N=523) and clustering coefficient (F=2.22, pFDR=0.027, R=0.098, N=525). This convergence of two segregation metrics suggests that structural organization in early visual networks supports vivid imagery through enhanced local information processing.

Structural connectivity—Salience network: Global efficiency in the right insular cortex (long gyrus and central sulcus) was positively associated with imagery vividness (F=3.09, pFDR=0.041, R=0.108, N=524). No other salience network nodes reached significance.

No significant effects were observed in the default mode network or in network-level structural analyses of the imagery network.

Multivariate modeling: Each significant predictor explained unique variance in VVIQ scores. The strongest single predictor was left fusiform local efficiency (Adj.R²=0.017, p=0.002), followed by right insula global efficiency (Adj.R²=0.008, p=0.021), occipital network local efficiency (Adj.R²=0.009, p=0.019), and occipital clustering coefficient (Adj.R²=0.008, p=0.025). The most frequently selected model across cross-validation folds combined fusiform and insula efficiency (Mean RMSE=21.50, Mean R²=0.049). The best-fitting model (lowest AIC=4496.14, highest Adj.R²=0.028) included three predictors: left fusiform, right insula, and occipital network efficiency—demonstrating that no single factor explained imagery better than a combined model integrating sensory, salience, and higher-order visual systems.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that vivid visual imagery emerges from the interplay between sensory-based structural networks, salience-based regulatory hubs, and higher-order visual integration—reconciling longstanding theoretical debates in the field.

The functional connectivity finding in the left fusiform gyrus supports the fusiform imagery node (FIN) hypothesis, suggesting this high-level visual region serves as an integrative hub for assembling internally generated visual content. Notably, the fusiform was the only region showing node-specific effects in functional analyses, lending empirical support to proposals positioning it as critical for imagery.

The structural segregation findings in the occipital network—increased local efficiency and clustering—are consistent with EVC-centered accounts emphasizing sensory architecture. However, an alternative interpretation warrants consideration: enhanced segregation may facilitate suppression of EVC regions, reducing perceptual interference that could disrupt imagery formation. This interpretation aligns with evidence showing increased EVC activation in aphantasia during autobiographical retrieval, and studies demonstrating that suppressing early visual cortex activity via tDCS enhances imagery vividness.

The novel finding of increased global efficiency in the right insular cortex extends traditional sensory models by implicating domain-general control systems. The right anterior insula serves as a gatekeeper for conscious access, mediating transitions between default mode and executive networks. Individuals with more globally efficient insular nodes may exhibit superior capacity to allocate attentional resources toward imagery even when challenged by competing sensory input. This finding aligns with theories proposing the insula as an integrative hub for interoceptive, emotional, and cognitive information, potentially enhancing the phenomenological richness of imagery.

Critically, our multivariate analyses revealed that no single factor explained imagery vividness better than a combined model. The best-fitting model integrated predictors from functional imagery networks (fusiform), structural sensory networks (occipital), and structural regulatory networks (insula)—demonstrating complementary contributions across modalities and systems. This represents the first large-scale study (N>500) examining both structural and functional network topology in relation to imagery vividness, highlighting the value of multimodal connectomic approaches for understanding individual differences in internal mental experience.
