Introduction

Tau accumulation is a key driver of cognitive decline in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), yet individuals with comparable levels of tau pathology frequently exhibit substantial heterogeneity in clinical impairment. While tau burden is closely linked to disease severity at the group level, the correspondence between biological pathology and clinical presentation is highly variable at the individual level1,2. Discrepancies between biological disease stage and clinical stage, referred to as biological-clinical mismatch, have been reported in approximately 40-45% of amyloid-positive individuals with Alzheimer-type biomarker abnormalities, indicating that a substantial proportion of patients show atypical alignment between pathology and clinical presentation3–5. This observation has gained particular relevance in the context of recently revised research criteria for AD3, which define disease severity based on biomarker levels of tau pathology while staging clinical impairment separately, without specifying how closely these dimensions should correspond. However, existing studies4,5 have largely assessed mismatch cross-sectionally, leaving unresolved whether biological-clinical mismatch reflects transient staging noise, measurement variability, or temporary disease fluctuations, or whether it represents a stable and biologically meaningful phenotype with distinct longitudinal trajectories of disease and clinical progression. If mismatch constitutes a persistent phenotype rather than a transient artifact, it may provide insight into mechanisms of cognitive vulnerability and resilience and inform prognosis and trial design.

Here, we examined whether biological-clinical mismatch constitutes a stable phenotype over time in amyloid-positive individuals, how mismatch categories are distributed across diagnostic and biological disease stages, and whether mismatch is associated with distinct longitudinal trajectories of cognition and tau accumulation after accounting for key covariates.

Methods

We analyzed 233 amyloid-positive participants from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) with longitudinal tau-PET imaging (AV1451), structural MRI, genetic data, and clinical follow-up. Amyloid positivity was defined using established PET-based thresholds. Tau burden was quantified using a meta-temporal composite SUVR, capturing an early neocortical hub of tau propagation with sufficient inter-individual variability for staging and longitudinal assessment.

Biological disease stage was defined using a validated hierarchical tau-composite framework4 spanning medial temporal, temporal neocortical, and higher-order association cortices. Regional tau positivity thresholds were derived from amyloid-negative cognitively unimpaired participants using mean + 2 SD criteria for early-stage composites and ADNI-internal distributional thresholds for higher-order neocortical regions. Clinical stage was defined using standard diagnostic categories (cognitively normal, mild cognitive impairment, dementia). Biological-clinical mismatch was computed as the directional discrepancy between biological and clinical stage, yielding three groups: Congruent, Clin > Bio (greater clinical impairment than expected for biological stage), and Bio > Clin (greater biological burden than expected for clinical stage). The cross-sectional distribution of mismatch categories across diagnostic and biological stages, along with the proportional distribution of biological stages within each diagnosis group, is shown in Figure 1A-B. Mismatch categories were unevenly distributed across diagnostic and biological stages. Within each clinical diagnosis, individuals spanned multiple biological stages, and individuals at similar biological stages were represented across different clinical diagnoses.

Longitudinal stability of mismatch categories was examined using alluvial flow analyses across available follow-up visits (Figure 2C). Transition frequencies were calculated between consecutive visits, and stability was evaluated descriptively across time. 

Longitudinal cognitive trajectories were assessed using linear mixed-effects models predicting ADAS-Cog total score as a function of time (years from baseline), mismatch category, and their interaction. Models were adjusted for age, sex, education, amyloid chronicity, APOE-ε4 dosage, and global tau SUVR, with random intercepts for participant. ADAS-Cog was selected as the primary cognitive outcome because it provides a continuous and longitudinally sensitive measure of global cognitive impairment across mild cognitive impairment and dementia stages, enabling comparison across mismatch categories defined independently of diagnosis. 

Lastly, longitudinal tau trajectories were examined using linear models predicting meta-temporal tau SUVR as a function of time, mismatch category, and their interaction, adjusting for age, sex, education, amyloid chronicity, clinical diagnosis, and APOE-ε4 dosage. Tau SUVR was not included as a covariate in these models, as it constituted the outcome of interest. Model-based predicted trajectories were estimated using marginal effects.

Results

Longitudinal tracking of mismatch categories in the full amyloid-positive sample showed that group membership was largely preserved over follow-up, with relatively few individuals transitioning between categories and transitions rarely bidirectional (Figure 2C). Across consecutive follow-up intervals of the amyloid-positive sample, mismatch transitions were infrequent. During the first four years of follow-up, approximately 9–14% of participants with consecutive observations changed mismatch category, indicating substantial longitudinal stability. Congruent individuals consistently showed the lowest transition rates (approximately 8-10% per year), whereas Bio > Clin exhibited greater apparent variability, though estimates were less precise due to smaller numbers at risk. Beyond four years of follow-up, the number of participants with consecutive observations declined markedly, limiting reliable estimation of transition rates.

In longitudinal cognitive models, mismatch category was independently associated with cognitive performance after adjustment for global tau burden, age, sex, education and APOE-ε4 dosage. Compared with Congruent individuals, Clin > Bio participants exhibited significantly worse ADAS-Cog scores (β = +2.99, p < .001) whereas Bio > Clin participants showed significantly better cognitive performance (β = −3.28, p = .002). Time-by-mismatch interaction terms were not significant (p > .05), indicating parallel rates of cognitive change across groups and stable separation of cognitive trajectories over time (Figure 2D).

In contrast, longitudinal tau-PET analyses revealed distinct tau trajectories by mismatch category. Relative to Congruent individuals, Clin > Bio participants exhibited significantly lower meta-temporal tau burden (β = −0.39, p <.001) whereas Bio > Clin participants showed significantly higher tau levels (β = +0.33, p <.001). Overall, tau increased over time (β = 0.013 SUVR/year, p = 0.04). These effects persisted after adjustment for demographics, amyloid chronicity, diagnosis, and APOE-ε4 status (Figure 2E).

Conclusion

Biological-clinical mismatch identifies a stable and biologically meaningful phenotype in AD that is not captured by diagnostic stage alone. Individuals with comparable clinical diagnoses differ substantially in underlying tau burden and cognitive vulnerability, and these differences persist longitudinally, indicating that factors beyond amyloid and tau pathology contribute importantly to clinical manifestation.

Mismatch status was associated with systematic differences in cognition and tau accumulation over time. Individuals with greater biological burden than expected for their clinical stage (Bio > Clin) exhibited better cognitive performance but accelerated tau accumulation, whereas those with greater clinical impairment than expected (Clin > Bio) showed worse cognition despite lower and more stable tau burden. 

The longitudinal stability of mismatch categories and the low frequency of transitions suggest that factors beyond amyloid and tau pathology contribute to individual disease expression. Comorbid processes such as vascular injury, neuroinflammation, co-pathologies, and systemic health factors may help explain why some individuals tolerate higher tau burden with preserved cognition while others show disproportionate clinical impairment. Incorporating these processes into the mismatch framework may improve biologically informed stratification, prognostic accuracy, and personalized intervention strategies in AD.
