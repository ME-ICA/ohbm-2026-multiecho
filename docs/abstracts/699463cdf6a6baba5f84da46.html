Introduction

Aging is commonly associated with changes in brain structure and function.1-3 Multiscale entropy (MSE) has been used to characterize age related changes in functional brain activity, whereby fine scale MSE increases and coarse scale MSE decreases with age.2  These changes are believed to reflect a shift in the balance of local and distributed network communication with age to reflect an increased reliance on local processing.4 Likewise, the brain’s structure also appears to become less integrated and more segregated with age, whereby measures of structural connectivity (SC) show decreased connector-hub inter-modular connectivity and an increased number of provincial hubs as we age.3 This study seeks to directly compare functional and structural changes in age using the Cambridge Centre for Aging and Neuroscience dataset (Cam-CAN). Robust correlations and partial least squares were used to: 1) assess the relationship between structural and functional connectivity, and 2) investigate their relationship with participant age. It was hypothesized that SC metrics of local efficiency would reflect changes in fine scale entropy and, in contrast, nodal efficiency would be related to changes in coarse scale entropy.

Methods

This study used the eyes closed resting-state MEG data and the diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) acquired for 561 participants in the CamCAN dataset. Participants’ cortical surface and boundary element models were reconstructed from their MRI data using FreeSurfer’s recon-all and watershed algorithms. The MEG data was pre-processed using a tsss filter and interpolation for rejected channels. The data was trimmed, bandpass filtered (1-90Hz) with a 50Hz notch filter. Cardiac and ocular artifacts were removed using ICA. The source space activity was resolved using a lcmv beamformer and timeseries were extracted for regions of interest (ROI) for the Schaefer200 17Networks atlas. MSE was then computed from these ROI time series.

The SC matrix for each participant was calculated for the Schaefer atlas from the dMRI data using probabilistic tractography (FSL bedpostx to fit the probabilistic model and probtrackx to perform tractography). The SC matrices were consistency thresholded at 50% and participants’ data were excluded if they had regions with no connections or had SC density >3SD away from the mean of the sample. Graph theory metrics of nodal efficiency and local efficiency were obtained for each ROI in the SC matrix. 

To test the relationship between SC and MSE, correlations between local and nodal efficiency across ROIs were correlated with MSE at each scale.  A behavioral partial least squares (PLS) analysis was used to determine patterns of maximal covariance (LV) between the data matrices representing the MSE curves and SC metrics for each participant and their age.

Results

The correlation analysis revealed two relationships between MSE and SC. Local efficiency showed a significant relationship with MSE that only existed at fine scale (Figure 1A) and, in contrast, nodal efficiency was only related with MSE at coarse scales (Figure 1B). The behavioural PLS analysis of the combined MSE curves and structural connectivity metrics returned a significant LV that was correlated with age. The expression of the LV in the MSE curves was seen as a cortex-wide relative decrease in coarse-scale entropy and a regional increase in fine-scale entropy that was largely constrained to regions in the occipital cortices and medial regions of the frontal, somato-motor and parietal cortices, replicating previous studies (Figure 1C,D&E).2 The SC metrics also loaded onto this LV with local efficiency increasing in superior regions in the frontal, somato-motor, parietal and visual cortices and decreasing in the medial regions of the frontal, somato-motor and parietal areas (Figure 1F). Changes in nodal efficiency were largely constrained to lateral frontal lobes (increased nodal efficiency) and the inferior temporal lobes (decreased nodal efficiency; Figure 1F).

Conclusion

The robust correlation analysis between MSE and SC metrics confirmed our hypothesis, whereby local efficiency is related to fine scale entropy and nodal efficiency is related to coarse scale entropy. These findings provide structural context that support the previous interpretations of MSE whereby fine and coarse scale entropy are thought to reflect local and distributed network communication.2,4 Furthermore, these quantifications of brain structure and function evolve together with age highlighting a nuanced relationship that can be used to describe changes in network communication over the adult lifespan.
