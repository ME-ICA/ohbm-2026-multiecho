Introduction

Cortico- cortical evoked potentials (CCEPs), i.e. brain responses to direct electrical stimulation pulses, have been variably classified in the literature as effective, functional and/or anatomical connectivity.1,2 This ambiguity reflects uncertainty about what CCEPs actually measure: do they primarily trace direct structural pathways, do they capture functional network organization? Clarifying this relationship is important for interpreting CCEP studies and understanding what this increasingly used approach reveals about brain organization. In this study, we directly compared functional and CCEP-derived (effective) connectivity in the primary auditory cortex using stereoencephalography (SEEG) recordings of subjects with temporal lobe epilepsy.

Methods

Participants had pharmacoresistant epilepsy undergoing pre-surgical evaluation at Hôpital de la Timone (Marseille), with electrodes covering a part of the auditory system and at least one stimulation run of at least 20 pulses performed in the first auditory cortex. They also completed a sound listening protocol (24 ms long pure tones at 500 and 1000 Hz, presented binaurally, about 100 trials each, interval inter stimuli of 1sec +/-200ms).3 We analyzed 15 datasets from 12 participants (7 female; mean age = 33 ± 13.5 years), including three with bilateral auditory cortex implantations (Figure 1). Bad channels were excluded by visual inspection and SEEG contacts were assigned according to their coordinates to regions using the AAL1-2018 atlas4. 

Functional connectivity analysis: Event-related potentials (ERPs) evoked by pure tones characterized auditory regions. The primary auditory cortex (A1) was identified by ERPs with the highest amplitude and earliest first component (~20 ms). Spectral coherence was computed between A1 and all other channels using wavelet-based Fourier transform (FieldTrip5). Neural activity (3-125 Hz, cycles: 2-11) was extracted from -1 to 1 s relative to sound onset, time-locked to stimulus presentation. The functional connectivity matrix used mean coherence across patients for each connection, then was correlated with CCEP-derived connectivity to assess concordance in the same patients and network.

CCEP analysis: CCEPs were computed from time-locked averages of A1 stimulation pulses. CCEP connectivity was computed using FTRACT methodology,2 yielding probabilistic connectivity matrices at the parcel level quantifying the probability to observe a significant CCEP component in a parcel when stimulating another one. We correlated our matrices with the FTRACT2 reference database (~1,300 patients) to validate our sample. 

Results

All A1 contacts were labeled as 'Heschl' or 'superior temporal' regions, bilaterally, in the AAL1-2018 atlas. 

We first validated that our CCEP dataset provides a representative sample of the CCEP-derived auditory network by comparing it with the FTRACT4 reference database. Connectivity matrices derived from stimulations to the same auditory cortex parcels showed high correlation (r = 0.79, p < 3 × 10⁻⁶), demonstrating that CCEPs map a stable network architecture even with smaller sample sizes.

We then compared the CCEP-derived connectivity matrix from our dataset with the functional connectivity matrix obtained from spectral coherence between A1 contacts and all other recording contacts during sound listening. This comparison revealed a strong correlation (r = 0.72, p < 2 × 10⁻⁵), indicating high concordance between CCEP-based and functional connectivity measures of the auditory network.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that CCEP connectivity patterns correlate highly with functional connectivity, suggesting that CCEP mapping includes the functional network of the targeted system and revealing a close relationship between different approaches to studying brain connectivity.

We were able to map the network of interest—in this case the auditory cortex—thanks to the direct sensory access of this system, which is conveniently targeted in sEEG functional mapping during clinical evaluation of epileptic patients. While these results would likely extend to other primary cortices given their similar hierarchical position, higher-order associative regions may show more differentiable responses to electrical stimulation vs. cognitive stimulation, requiring further investigation.

Clinically, functional mapping relies on high-frequency stimulation to identify eloquent cortex through induced symptoms, yet the network-level effects remain poorly understood. Our findings demonstrate that CCEP, produced by low-frequency stimulations that evoke symptoms more rarely, can reveal the functional architecture underlying these clinical mappings, particularly in primary sensory areas.
