Introduction

American football raises concerns about brain health from repetitive head impacts, even without traumatic brain injury. Findings on the effects of RHI exposure on brain function and structure in athletes have been mixed across acute, seasonal, and long-term stages, while the overall evidence suggests that the magnitude and frequency of head impact exposure are key factors in shaping microstructural changes.^1 Given diffusion MRI sensitivity to subtle microstructural changes in traumatic brain injury, it is particularly well-suited for investigating acute repetitive head impact effects and their heterogeneity. Investigating athletes in a natural game setting, Bazarian et al. (2024) demonstrated that dMRI can detect microstructural changes following repetitive head impacts 48 hours post-game.^2 Building on this finding, combining a within-subject design with multivariate analyses could further improve the detection of subtle white matter changes occurring in the ultra-acute phase, while accounting for individual differences in head impact exposure.

In this study, we address this gap using a pre- to post-game design with head-impact telemetry. The first aim of this study was to prospectively quantify immediate WM microstructural changes in a naturalistic setting, a university-level football game, by comparing diffusion MRI measures and relating these changes to head-impact telemetry. Our second aim was to apply a multivariate approach to overcome the curse of dimensionality often encountered in tractometry studies, where multiple comparisons can reduce statistical power and spatial averaging may obscure important information, while still capturing richer information than traditional univariate methods.

Methods

Thirty-two male football players from three Canadian varsity teams were recruited across two competitive seasons, yielding 47 distinct game assessments with paired pre- and post-game multi-shell 3T dMRI scans. Pre-game MRI was acquired within 24 hours of the game, while post-game MRI was completed immediately following the game. Participants wore specialized head-impact sensors throughout each game, capturing common impact-related biomechanical metrics.

Whole-brain tractograms were generated using TractoFlow^3, a state-of-the-art dMRI preprocessing pipeline. BundleSeg automatically extracted major WM bundles that were input to Tractometry Flow to perform along-the-tract profiling. Standard DTI, HARDI, neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) measures were computed along 20 sub-sections of each bundle. A principal component analysis was used to reduce all the metrics to three biologically interpretable indices. To assess whether pre-post game differences were associated with head impact metrics in a single model, we computed dynamic time warping–based similarity matrices for PC-derived features across all scans. We then applied similarity network fusion to generate aggregated brain-wide diffusion similarity matrices, and extracted pre-post distances for each participant.^4 Deltas of aggregate distances were computed for each pre-post scan pair, then modelled using a linear mixed-effects model to investigate whether they were related to head impact metrics.

To identify the anatomical origins of significant findings (if any) from our statistical analyses, computed in the section above, we conducted a perturbation analysis on the overall aggregated similarity matrices. Each top perturbing bundle was then each fitted with a generalized additive mixed-effects model with a scaled t distribution family using mgcv, as similarly done in Caffarra et al. (2024).^5

Results

PCA reduced the eight variables (ad, afd, md, noddi_fwf, noddi_ndi, noddi_odi, nufo, rd) with the first three components collectively explaining 92.5% of the total variance. PC1 ("Fiber Coherence") accounted for 52.4% of the variance, with highest loadings from axial diffusivity (0.461), apparent fiber density (0.419), orientation dispersion index (-0.445), and NuFO (-0.388) collectively reflecting WM fiber integrity and directional organization. PC2 ("Global water diffusion") explained 23.7% of variance and was dominated by mean diffusivity (0.651), radial diffusivity (0.557), and free water fraction (0.320), capturing global water diffusion characteristics within and around tissue structures. The third principal component (PC3: "Tissue Microstructure") explained 16.4% of the variance and was primarily defined by the three highest loadings: neurite density index (0.698), free water fraction (0.594), and orientation dispersion index (0.290), capturing tissue microstructural properties.

We fitted a linear mixed model (estimated using REML and nloptwrap optimizer) to predict the distance based on log-transformed HAE counts and 95th percentile impact magnitude. The model included athlete id as random effect. The model’s total explanatory power was substantial (conditional R2 = 0.92) and the part related to the fixed effects alone (marginal R2) was of 0.23. Conclusion

The primary finding was that the number of HAE was positively associated with greater pre–post WM dissimilarity as indexed by the overall multivariate measure. This suggests that exposure burden, rather than the magnitude of individual impacts, is more closely related to acute microstructural alterations. The log relationship further implies a nonlinear association, where the earliest impacts within a game may contribute disproportionately to observed changes, with additional impacts adding smaller incremental effects.

The use of a multivariate tractometry framework allowed for the simultaneous integration of multiple diffusion metrics across numerous WM bundles, offering a more comprehensive characterization of acute microstructural changes than traditional univariate analyses. This approach mitigated multicollinearity, preserved tract-specific profiles, and allowed robust multivariate modeling of pre–post changes, similar to prior applications in mild traumatic brain injury populations. Overall, these results suggest that multivariate frameworks may enhance the detection and interpretation of acute head-impact-related changes, particularly when effects are subtle, distributed, or directionally complex, complementing prior univariate studies.
