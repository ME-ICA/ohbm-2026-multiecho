Introduction

Gliomas are the most frequent of all primary malignant brain tumors, and are considered one of the most devastating cancers due to their locally aggressive behavior and difficult treatment.1 They tend to preferentially spread along white matter (WM) tracts and blood vessels, where they lead to pathologic demyelination and vasogenic edema.2 The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) technique called diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) exploits the diffusive properties of water, which follow certain patterns according to the tissue structure and are disturbed by various pathologies.3 Axons comprising WM tracts make water diffusion easier along them rather than perpendicular to them due to their myelin sheath.4 Information about the magnitude of water diffusion from DWI in combination with the anisotropic water diffusion inside axons is utilized by multiple specialized WM imaging techniques, such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD). The aim of this study is to investigate WM diffusion properties around brain gliomas using advanced computational methods to inspect hidden patterns of diffusivity. By doing so, the goal is to attain new insights into the behavior of gliomas in relation to their surrounding WM, deepening our knowledge about them.

Methods

For the analysis, a publicly available University of California San Francisco Preoperative Diffuse Glioma MRI (UCSF-PDGM) dataset was used, which contains data of 495 unique subjects with histopathologically confirmed diffuse gliomas. UCSF-PDGM contains three types of data: imaging data from various MRI modalities, clinical data about patients, and information about gradient directions and b-values (b0 + 55 diffusion gradients, b = 2,000 s/mm²). To conduct the analysis, methods from the domains of neuroimaging, digital image processing, statistical analysis, and machine learning (ML) were used. The image preprocessing was initialized by non-linear image registration of DWI images to the rest of MRI data using symmetric normalization (SyN) with elastic regularization, mutual information (MI) as the similarity metric, and B-splines as the resampling interpolation method. Considering the similarities between b0 DWI images and T2-weighted images (T2WIs), the registration procedure was realized using T2WI data as the target. Next, regions of interest (ROIs) were generated using morphological and set-theory operators applied to the available segmentation masks (Figure 1). To analyze the interaction between gliomas and the surrounding WM tissue, two ROIs – termed periedematous and peritumoral – were generated for each subject using the spherical (5mm radius) and cubic (2×2×2 mm) structuring elements. The ROIs were selected to inspect specific boundaries and transitions of tissue: the periedematous ROIs can be interpreted as the regions in which tumor-affected tissue transitions to healthy surrounding WM, while the peritumoral ROIs reflect the transition between tumor masses and the surrounding edemas. The final step of image preprocessing was the computation DTI and CSD models in ROIs, yielding various diffusion properties: axial diffusivity (AD), fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), normalized entropy (NE), the fODF magnitude (MAG), and generalized fractional anisotropy (GFA). The diffusion properties were aggregated in each ROI, meaning that each ROI gets quantified as a single data point in the analysis. The choice was made to represent two aspects of each diffusion property inside each ROI: the distribution central tendency and the degree of statistical dispersion, i.e., the median and the interquartile range (IQR), as there are no natural assumptions to be made about the underlying distributions. The only difference is the NE, which is computed as a single value from all fiber orientation density functions (fODFs) values in a given ROI. The analysis of computed diffusion properties started with exploratory data analysis (EDA), which implied redundancy and potential for dimensionality reduction. This was performed using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), a non-linear manifold learning technique. To select the optimal hyperparameters of UMAP, the tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE) was used for the multi-objective optimization using trustworthiness and stress, i.e., the resulting UMAP hyperparameters were chosen so that an optimal compromise between local and global retention is achieved. With the diffusion properties reduced to 2D, the cluster analysis using Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) was performed in both peritumoral and periedematous UMAP embeddings. Because the target number of clusters K is not given, three clustering quality indices were used to find optimal K: Davies-Bouldin index (DBI), Calinski-Harabasz index (CHI), and Silhouette index (SI). The relationship between identified clusters and suitable clinical markers was performed using statistical tests (α = 0.05), namely: Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, Mann-Whitney U test, Levene test, Brown-Forsythe test, χ² test, and Fisher’s exact test, depending on whether the tested attribute was qualitative or quantitative. Additionally, effect size was evaluated using Cliff’s δ and Cramér’s V.

Results

Due to a lack of tumor segmentation labels, some subjects with sufficient FA, and significant deformations introduced during image registration, the total number of available data points decreased to 384 for peritumoral ROIs and 445 for periedematous ROIs. Figure 2 shows that the peritumoral diffusion properties form two major clusters with a transition zone in the middle, while the periedematous diffusion properties are spread out more evenly. Using DBI, CHI, and SI, the optimal number of peritumoral clusters was confirmed to be 2, while the optimal number of periedematous clusters should be either 3 or 7. The peritumoral clustering was better and more consistent overall, while no consensus was made in the periedematous clustering, and so only the peritumoral clusters were analyzed further. From the available combinations of attributes, the relationship between identified clusters and patients’ age was deemed statistically significant, but very weak. The relationship between clusters and patients’ sex was found to be statistically significant and moderately strong (more precisely, the relationship is weak or strong, depending on the used Cramér’s V scale). Relationship with other testable attributes was not found to be statistically significant.

Conclusion

The results indicate that some underlying patterns of diffusivity can be observed in the peritumoral white matter, but their true meaning and clinical implications are not interpretable in the current stage of research. As it stands, the observed clusters may correspond to a known biomarker that is not available in the UCSF-PDGM dataset. The more ambitious interpretation of the findings would be that the two identified subtypes represent some previously unknown pathophysiological property of gliomas that is reflected in the way in which the tumor masses affect the surrounding WM tissue, altering its diffusion properties. Interestingly, the discovery of two clusters related to diffusion has been documented previously by Foltyn-Dumitru et al.5, albeit using different methodology and modalities, hinting towards the idea that the presented findings may indeed hold some ground. A deliberate choice was made to use a publicly available dataset together with free and open-source software to maximize reproducibility and transparency, increasing the potential for follow-up studies. Both the source code and pre-computed diffusion properties are publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/kukrma/glioma-diffusion-analysis).
