Introduction

Magnetic Field Correlation imaging (MFCi) is a quantitative MRI technique sensitive to both diffusion characteristics and microscopic magnetic field inhomogeneity. Following Jensen & Chandra (2006)1, MFCi uses a series of spin echoes (SEs) where the first is symmetric and subsequent SEs have their refocusing pulses shifted by a time shift (ts), sensitising the MR signal to reversible dephasing from local susceptibility gradients. Echo-dependent signal changes are used to estimate the apparent MFC through Eq. 1:

S(τ) = S0exp(-2γ2MFC(TE/2)ts2) (1)

Where S0 is the symmetric SE signal and TE is the echo time. The MFC reflects how diffusing water protons probe varying microscopic field gradients through Eq. 2:

MFC(τ) = γ2K(τ) (2)

Where γ is the gyromagnetic ratio and K is the temporal autocorrelation given by Eq. 3:

K(τ) = <B(0)B(τ)> (3)

MFCi has shown greater sensitivity to susceptibility-related length scales than R2, R2* and R2'2, and has previously been used to quantify changes in iron content in-vivo, but has never been applied to myelin. In Multiple Sclerosis (MS), lesions involve demyelination, axonal loss, gliosis, and oedema3, all of which alter diffusion characteristics and microscopic susceptibility gradients. MFCi may therefore provide a unique contrast for imaging changes to myelin and iron in MS. Prior simulations and ex-vivo imaging4 show that at ≥7 T, MFCi is sensitive to myelin and correlates with inhomogeneous magnetisation transfer ratio (ihMTR).

We expect that reductions in myelin and axonal density will flatten associated susceptibility gradients and increase extracellular diffusivity, resulting in a reduced apparent MFC within lesions. To evaluate MFCi's application to MS, we compare it with quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM). We hypothesise that (i) MFCi will differentiate MS lesions, normal-appearing white matter (NAWM) and healthy white matter (WM); (ii) tissue-specific MFC values will correlate with QSM-derived susceptibility estimates.

Methods

Participants: A preliminary cohort of 10 participants (5 with relapsing remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) and 5 healthy controls matched on age and sex) with mean age of 34.9±6.38 years, and a sex ratio of 2:3 male-female have been included. All MS participants met the diagnostic criteria for RRMS and all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with institutional ethics committee guidelines.

MRI Acquisition: All MRI data was acquired at 7 T using a Siemens Magnetom system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a multi-channel (8-channel transmit, 32-channel receive) headcoil. Whole-brain MFCi was acquired using a multi-echo spin-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with 1.4mm isotropic resolution, TE=[46,54] ms, refocusing pulse shifts ts=[0:3:12] ms and TR=6800 ms. Each SE was acquired twice with opposing phase encoding directions for macroscopic susceptibility distortion correction. Magnetisation-prepared 2 rapid acquisition gradient echo (MP2RAGE) volumes acquired to provide high-contrast quantitative T1 maps for anatomical registration and tissue segmentation. A multi-echo gradient-echo sequence with 0.667 mm isotropic resolution, TE=[5:5:35] ms, TR=[39]ms was used for QSM. Participants had an additional scan at 3 T using a Siemens Connectom on the same day, which included a 3D T2-weighted scan that was used for lesion identification. 

Image Preprocessing: MFCi: For each TE, ASEs were concatenated by order of ts for denoising using the MPPCA algorithm, and for Gibbs ringing removal as implemented in MrTrix3. Then, the ASEs were coregistered to their respective SSE to minimise motion effects between SEs. Topup (FSL) was used to calculate the susceptibility induced field map from the SSEs, which was then used to correct macroscopic susceptibility distortion in the corresponding ASEs. The corrected SEs were then re-concatenated and coregistered to the brain-extracted inversion 2 image from the MP2RAGE acquisition. MP2RAGE: the inversion 1 volume was brain-extracted using FSL’s BET (Figure 1a), with the binary mask then being applied to the inversion 2 and uniform volumes. GRE: Raw k-space data were processed in MATLAB using the retroMoCoBox package (https://github.com/dgallichan/retroMoCoBox) to apply FatNav-based motion-correction prior to coil combination using ASPIRE (https://github.com/korbinian90/ASPIRE). Magnitude volumes underwent Gibbs ringing removal prior to QSM processing. Downsampled magnitude volumes were co-registered to the MP2RAGE inversion 1 volumes, with the resulting transformation matrix being inverted and used for tissue segmentation on the derived QSM maps (Figure 1c).

Tissue segmentation: Tissues were segmented using Atropos (ANTs), and then eroded for more conservative masks. MS lesions were segmented using a semi-automatic method; first the lesions were identified using mindGlide5, and then manually edited by two of the authors. All masks were made mutually exclusive. 

MFCi fitting: For each of the two echo times (TEs), the logarithm of the concatenated spin-echo (ASE) volumes were fitted voxelwise using a linear regression of ln S(ts) against -2xMFC(TE/2)xts2. In order to calculate the microscopic MFC (μMFC), the short-TE MFC (Figure 1b) was subtracted from the long-TE MFC to remove the effect of molecular relaxation. 

QSM Reconstruction: QSM reconstruction consisted of an initial phase unwrapping using ROMEO, followed by a projection onto dipole fields using MEDIsuite and source-reconstructed using non-linear dipole inversion (NDI) with a threshold of 0.25.

Results

Two MS patients were excluded from the analysis, one for having measures of white matter susceptibility more than two standard deviations from the sample mean and another for having a negative μMFC within lesions. 

Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to assess differences in each MFC metric across tissue types (Figure 2a). For both MFC(TE=46 ms) and MFC(TE=54 ms) the overall test was significant (H = 6.30, N = 20, p = 0.043 for both), suggesting tissue-dependent differences, as expected. Pairwise comparisons made with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons indicated no statistically significant differences between HC WM and MS NAWM (pcorr=1) and between HC NAWM and MS lesions (pcorr>0.5), although HC WM compared to MS lesions showed a trend toward significance (pcorr=0.106). No significant differences between tissues were present for μMFC.

Pearson correlations were computed between tissue-specific MFC and QSM values (Figure 2b). In HC WM, strong negative correlations were observed for MFC(TE=46 ms) (R2=0.98, p=0.0036) and MFC(TE=54 ms) (R2=0.89, p=0.042), but no significant correlations were observed for the μMFC (R2=0.37, p=0.276). In MS NAWM, positive but non-significant correlations were observed for both MFC(TE=46 ms) (R2=0.99, p=0.095) and MFC(TE=54 ms) (R2=0.98, p=0.135), a significant strong positive correlation was observed in the μMFC (R2=1, p=0.0176). In MS lesions, negative correlations were observed but not deemed significant for the MFC(TE=46 ms) (R2=0.87, p=0.238), MFC(TE=54 ms) (R2=0.56, p=0.461) and μMFC (R2=0.88, p=0.22).

Conclusion

This work demonstrates for the first time that in-vivo human measures of MFCi reveal measures of microstructural variability in MS NAWM and lesions, and that MFCi at short TEs correlates with source susceptibility estimates in healthy white matter. The results suggest that MFCi is sensitive to tissue-specific microstructural changes associated with demyelination in MS, even in a very small sample. The Kruskal-Wallis tests indicate that MFC at both echo times (46 and 54 ms) show tissue-dependent differences, with a trend toward a reduced MFC in MS lesions compared to healthy white matter. μMFC did not distinguish between tissue types, suggesting that that the variability introduced by the small sample size may have obscured the effect.

The QSM-MFC correlations are particularly informative; In HC WM, the strong negative correlations between MFC and QSM (R² ~0.9–0.98) suggest that regions with greater apparent bulk susceptibility correspond to a reduced MFC, which is somewhat unexpected. 

MS lesions show negative but non-significant correlations, likely reflecting heterogeneous lesion pathology (variable demyelination, gliosis, oedema) and a lack of statistical power introduced by a small sample size. Pairwise differences did not reach significance, and correlations in MS tissues are highly variable, indicating that these results should be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory. Larger cohorts are needed to confirm the trends and assess the robustness of MFCi as a biomarker of MS-related microstructural changes.
