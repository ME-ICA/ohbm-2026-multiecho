Introduction

Loneliness is a significant public health challenge with high prevalence among adolescents and young adults. Loneliness is commonly defined as the subjective experience of having fewer (social loneliness) or lower quality (emotional loneliness) relationships than desired and thus reflects perceived experiences rather than objective social isolation. Loneliness is associated with negative long-term health-related outcomes, such as an increased risk for immune system dysfunction, reduced cardiovascular health, and risk for psychiatric disorders such as depression and anxiety. However, the specific mechanisms contributing to loneliness in adolescents and young adults are poorly understood.

Current neuroimaging evidence suggests that loneliness is related to structural and functional alterations in the prefrontal cortex, insula, hippocampus, amygdala, and posterior superior temporal cortex, as well as atypical activity and connectivity in attentional, visual, and default mode networks.1 However, to what extent these changes are similar between age groups remains incompletely understood. Furthermore, most prior work relied on between-group designs (lonely vs. non-lonely) or simple correlations, without cross-validated models to predict loneliness in unseen individuals.

In this study we therefore applied connectome-based predictive modelling2 to investigate the association between brain functional connectivity (FC) and loneliness using the large, high-quality, open-access dataset of the Human Connectome Project (HCP). In line with a prior study utilizing connectome-based predictive modelling to predict loneliness in another sample3 and current knowledge about the neurobiology of loneliness, we hypothesized that (1) loneliness could be predicted based on FC, and that (2) edges contributing to the association would be (between) the default mode network, visual network, attention network, and limbic brain regions.

Methods

Participants
Data from the HCP-Young Adult (2025 release, downloaded on November 13th, 2025) was utilized. As we are predominantly interested in loneliness in young adults, we only included data from participants within 22-25 years of age (N=226; subset of N=95 analyzed). We analyzed the first neuroimaging session, which included a structural scan (T1w) and two resting-state fMRI scans (opposing phase encoding direction: REST1_LR, and REST1_RL, respectively). Additionally, the National Institute of Health (NIH) toolbox on Emotion was administered, which includes a loneliness survey. Personality traits were investigated using the NEO Five-Factor Inventory.

Data acquisition and processing      
Data was acquired on a 3T Siemens scanner (Skyra). A T1w-scan was obtained for anatomical reference (3D MPRAGE: TR/TE/TI/FA: 2400ms/2.14ms/1000ms/8°, voxel size=0.7mm isotropic, FOV=224x224mm), followed by two resting-state fMRI scans (Gradient-echo EPI: TR/TE/FA=720ms/33.1ms/52°, voxel size=2mm isotropic, FOV=280x180mm, multiband factor=8, 1200 dynamics, scan duration≈15 minutes).

Functional data for all participants was minimally preprocessed according to HCP protocols, which included normalization to MNI-152 template, motion correction, and co-registration of structural and functional scans. We excluded the first three volumes and performed motion filtering4 to obtain a filtered framewise displacement (FD). We then regressed mean white matter, gray matter, and cerebrospinal fluid signals, along with 24 filtered motion regressors (rotation and translation in x, y, and z directions including their derivatives, squares, and squared derivatives) and linear and quadratic drift from the fMRI signals, followed by low pass filtering with a Gaussian kernel of 0.11Hz (in-house scripts, Matlab R2022b, Mathworks, MA). Volumes exceeding a filtered FD threshold of 0.2 mm, including the immediately preceding and following volumes, were scrubbed from the data. Connectivity matrices were then constructed using the Shen-268 functional atlas.5 Pearson correlations were computed between all 268 region-of-interest pairs, Fisher z-transformed, and averaged across the two resting-state scans to yield a single weighted connectivity matrix per participant.

Participants exhibiting high motion (<60% of volumes removed or mean FD<0.15 mm in either run) were excluded. Connectivity matrices were screened for outliers in mean correlation strength and edges with zero connections (using L2 distance metrics).

Connectome-based predictive modelling       
Connectome-based predictive modelling was used to quantify the relationship between resting-state FC and individual loneliness scores. For each edge in the connectivity matrix, we computed its association with loneliness across participants (Spearman correlations), separately for positive and negative associations. Edges showing a significant association with loneliness (p < 0.05 or P<0.01, uncorrected) were retained. For each participant, the weights of all selected edges were then summed to yield a single summary FC score per network (positive or negative association) and fed into a linear predictive model of individual loneliness scores. Given the modest sample size, leave-one-out cross-validation was employed.

Statistics
Total loneliness scores (NIH loneliness questionnaire) were used. We conducted Spearman correlations to investigate associations between loneliness scores and relevant covariates: mean FD and five personality measures: agreeableness, openness to experiences, extraversion, conscientiousness, and neuroticism (pFDR<0.05 was considered significant).

For connectome-based predictive modelling analyses, we used permutation testing to obtain p-values for any observed positive associations between observed and predicted loneliness scores (1000 permutations). To assess the robustness of our findings, we assessed the influence of relevant covariates using partial correlations.

Results

Resting-state fMRI data and loneliness scores were available for N=94 participants (36 females). Four participants were excluded due to HCP-reported processing errors, and two additional participants were excluded based on quality control of the FC matrices (N=1 for mean correlation strength and N=1 for L2 distance), yielding a final sample of N=88 (36 females). No participants were excluded based on mean FD post-scrubbing.

Mean correlation strength did not differ significantly between runs (Wilcoxon signed-rank test; W=2392, p=0.07), with post-scrubbing mean FD across runs at 0.02 mm (IQR=0.009). Loneliness scores showed a median of 51.6 (IQR=8.8), skewed toward lower values (Figure 2D), and did not differ by sex (Kruskal-Wallis test; H(1)=0.45, p=0.50). Loneliness was not associated with mean FD (r=-0.09, p=0.43) but correlated with personality traits: negatively with extraversion (r=-0.32, pFDR=0.0038; Figure 2A) and conscientiousness (r=-0.25, pFDR=0.037, Figure 2B), and positively with neuroticism (r=0.53, pFDR<0.001, Figure 2C); associations with agreeableness (r=-0.20, pFDR=0.086) and openness (r=-0.08, pFDR=0.47) were not significant.

Connectome-based predictive modeling revealed no predictive relationship between functional connectivity and loneliness (selection threshold p<0.05: r=-0.11; p<0.01: r=-0.19, Figures 2EF). Including covariates such as mean FD (p<0.05: r=-0.08; p<0.01: r=-0.11), neuroticism (p<0.05: r=-0.05; p<0.01: r=-0.08), or extraversion (p<0.05: r=-0.16; p<0.01: r=-0.23) or conscientiousness (p<0.05: r=-0.19; p<0.01: r=-0.28) did not improve prediction.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that resting-state FC did not reliably predict individual loneliness scores in this young adult cohort, even after covariate adjustment. The observed negative correlations likely reflect a lack of variability in loneliness scores, overfitting or edge-selection instability due to the limited sample size rather than meaningful inverse prediction, which is a challenge in connectome-based predictive modelling with modest samples.

Several factors may contribute to this null result. Loneliness is a subjective, state-like construct with variable measurement stability across time and contexts, potentially weakening its expression in stable resting-state networks. In the HCP sample, resting-state fMRI data were not acquired concurrently with loneliness measures, potentially decoupling transient state fluctuations in loneliness from stable connectivity patterns. Indeed, in the only prior study able to predict loneliness scores using resting-state FC, these measures were obtained on the same day3. Although there is ongoing debate about “state” versus “trait” loneliness, with chronic (“trait”) loneliness expected to be less dependent on same-day assessment, we did not observe evidence for any association between resting-state connectivity and loneliness in this sample. Furthermore, the skewed distribution toward lower loneliness scores may have reduced variance available for prediction. The modest sample size of 88 participants falls below the recommended threshold (N>100) for stable connectome-based predictive modelling performance, which may be particularly problematic for complex states such as loneliness. Given that effect sizes for clinical measures, which may be more closely related to loneliness, are typically smaller than for cognitive measures, this reduced effect size poses an additional challenge. Finally, personality covariates like neuroticism and extraversion, known loneliness correlates, did not improve the prediction, suggesting that resting-state connectivity may not capture sufficient unique variance in loneliness beyond these traits.

Future analyses will apply connectome-based predictive modelling to the full HCP Young Adult subsample aged 22-25 years (N=226). Although connectome-based predictive modelling of resting-state fMRI has previously been applied to predict a variety of behavioral measures, task-based fMRI may provide more robust predictors of loneliness than resting-state fMRI. Unlike resting-state scans, where mental states vary freely, task-fMRI constrains participants to standardized emotional stimuli, possibly reducing within-scan variability and enhancing signal-to-noise for socio-emotional processes. Given evidence of altered emotional processing in lonely individuals, connectome-based predictive modelling applied to emotion recognition task fMRI could reveal more reliable connectome signatures.
