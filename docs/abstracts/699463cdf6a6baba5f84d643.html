Introduction

Adaptive behavior in the real-world requires continuous coordination of perceptual information with goal-directed action, yet the neural organization facilitating this integration remains unclear. Previous work in this context has largely employed naturalistic tasks with passive viewing (e.g. movie watching) to reveal distributed encoding of perceptual processes, particularly to reveal neural representations of visual object features and semantic categories.1,2 Despite their high ecologically validity, such paradigms omit overt behavior, leaving it unclear how the brain organizes perception and action integration as these cognitive processes unfold simultaneously during continuous, goal-directed behavior.

Theoretical frameworks have proposed a distributed Action-Mode Network,3 encompassing cingulo-opercular and medial frontal regions, as a candidate system supporting perception-action integration. However, empirical evidence for this network has largely originated from abstract task contrasts that isolate individual cognitive processes or resting state conditions that are devoid of goal-oriented behavior. To address this critical gap, our primary objective in this study was to investigate the cortical organization of perception and action integration during naturalistic interactive gameplay using voxel-wise encoding models applied to densely sampled neuroimaging and behavioral data.

Methods

Video game paradigms enable dense logging of perceptual and behavioral data during ecologically valid, goal-directed behavior. We used the CNeuroMod Super Mario Bros dataset, in which five participants completed up to 18 hours of fMRI scanning on a 3T Siemens Prisma Fit MRI scanner while actively playing randomized sequences of video game levels (mean age = 44.6 years old, SD = 3.93, 2/3 female to male ratio). Participants received extensive training to achieve proficiency on all levels before scanning, which ensured stable gameplay during fMRI data collection. Moreover, levels were randomly ordered across runs to minimize learning effects.

Neuroimaging data were preprocessed using fMRIprep (version 20.2.5) and resampled to the 32k fsLR grayordinate space. In order to investigate the cortical organization of action and perception integration during densely sampled video gameplay, we fitted voxel-wise encoding models across approximately 91,000 cortical grayordinates using banded ridge regression with variance partitioning.4,5 Motor feature spaces captured button inputs (directional movement, jump, sprint), while visual feature spaces encoded dynamic scene content (enemy presence, environmental layout). All features were mean-centered within gameplay levels, temporally aligned to the fMRI signal (TR=1.49s), and convolved with finite impulse response filters (2-5 TR delays). Separate ridge penalties for each feature space allowed optimal regularization while enabling variance partitioning to quantify unique versus shared encoding. Models were trained and validated using leave-one-run-out (LORO) cross-validation, with approximately five training sessions and two held-out validation sessions per participant.

Using variance partitioning we decomposed total explained variance (R²) into (1) shared variance jointly explained by motor and visual features, (2) motor-unique variance explained only by motor features after removing visual contributions, and (3) visual-unique variance explained only by visual features after removing motor contributions. This approach allowed us to identify cortical regions preferentially encoding unimodal versus shared perceptual-motor information during ongoing behavior. Model fitting and variance partitioning was performed for each participant individually, and variance maps were averaged to generate group-level results.

Results

Encoding models reliably predicted cortical activity across functional networks, explaining 8-15% of variance in sensorimotor and attention systems (mean 12%, peak 49%). High prediction accuracy was observed in visuomotor integration regions: superior parietal cortex (21%, including areas V6A and V7), posterior parietal cortex (17%, including VIP and LIPv), MT motion complex (17%), and supplementary motor area (17%, including FEF and SCEF), with individual voxels reaching up to 49% explained variance in posterior parietal and visual motion regions.

Variance partitioning revealed that shared motor-visual variance accounted for the majority of the explainable variance (76%), indicating dominant conjunctive representations of action and perception during naturalistic behavior. This shared variance was spatially concentrated within the cingulo-opercular network, including supplementary motor area, anterior cingulate, and anterior insula, which collectively form core components of the Action-Mode Network.

In contrast, motor-unique variance was primarily localized to sensorimotor cortex (11%), while visual-unique variance was concentrated in occipitotemporal regions (13%). Unique and shared representations showed a distributed and overlapping spatial organization, with motor encoding extending into ventral visual areas and visual encoding present within classical motor territories. However, the strongest convergence of perceptual and motor information was consistently observed within the Action-Mode Network regions.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that perception and action integration during naturalistic behavior is supported by distributed neural codes, with unimodal visual and motor information uniquely expressed in visual and motor cortices, respectively, and shared representations preferentially expressed within the Action-Mode Network. The concentration of shared variance in these Action-Mode Network regions suggests that this system may coordinate perception and action through conjunctive representations that are otherwise distinct in unimodal systems. By extending voxel-wise encoding approaches from passive naturalistic paradigms to interactive gameplay, this work provides a principled account of how the brain organizes perception-action integration when these processes co-occur in real-world behavior.
