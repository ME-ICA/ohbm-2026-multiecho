Introduction

Contemporary neuroimaging offers an abundance of high‑quality pipelines and analysis methods for deriving functional connectivity (FC) from fMRI data. However, this methodological diversity is a double-edged sword, as variation across approaches can yield divergent results and hinder reproducibility.1,2 Currently, researchers lack a clear decision-making process for selecting methods, often relying on narrow comparisons or external phenotypic alignment. In either case, the basis of choosing methods is unlikely to be representative of the context in which the pipelines will be used. On the other hand, if it is, then the tool selection poses a circular logic challenge, with methods being chosen because of the presence of a hypothesized interaction. Even in cases where researchers seek to sample multiple points in the multiverse of methods, rather than sampling from it, there is often no clear way to draw the line between methods that merit inclusion versus those that are of insufficient quality. In this work, we begin to address this method benchmarking challenge through a case study in FC estimation. We compare several benchmarking measures, and arrive at recommendations for how to perform such exercises in a way that accommodates diverse method architectures and provides a principled basis for method selection.

Methods

We used minimally preprocessed HCP S1200 resting‑state fMRI data and the Schaefer 2018 parcellation (200 parcels) to construct a benchmark of FC estimators. Following prior work,3 time series were post‑processed with standard scaling, band‑pass filtering using even padding to reduce edge artifacts, global signal regression, and ROI‑wise averaging. Motion was assessed using framewise displacement (FD), and scans with excessive movement were excluded based on predefined thresholds, resulting in 867 subjects.

We compared two broad method families: (1) statistics of pairwise interactions (SPIs) implemented with the PySPI4 package, and (2) autoregressive connectivity implemented with our “scikit autoregressive models for functional connectivity” (skarf) package (https://github.com/childmindresearch/skarf). 284 SPIs in PySPI were profiled for runtime and memory. From these, we excluded squared and redundant variants, SPIs exceeding a 5 minute runtime, and those that required more than 4GB of memory, resulting in 149 selected SPIs spanning classical covariance/correlation, robust and regularized covariance estimators, spectral and information‑theoretic measures. 

We implemented regularized vector autoregressive (VAR) models in the skarf library, building on the equivalence between VAR and multivariate linear regression.5 Twelve skarf estimators were selected a priori, with two representatives from each of six conceptual categories. These spanned covariance-, precision-, and linear VAR-based connectivity, incorporating ridge, lasso, elastic-net, and low-rank factorized variants. For each estimator, both lag-0 (concurrent prediction) and lag-1 (one‑TR‑ahead temporal prediction) activity were evaluated.

All methods were evaluated with a common set of benchmarks, including: i) phenotypic association, ii) edge-wise and graph reliability, and iii) representational efficiency (Figure 1). For behavioral prediction of HCP cognitive and affective targets (55 measures and 4 factors), we performed kernel ridge regression with Pearson kernels, nested cross‑validation, and mean squared error as the model‑selection metric to avoid instabilities associated with correlation‑based tuning. Behavioral targets were processed with nuisance regression (age, sex, mean FD), outlier clipping, and standardization, with all transforms fit only on training data to prevent test leakage. We quantified reliability and discriminability of connectomes across runs and sessions using standard test–retest and inter‑subject metrics, and similarly performed these evaluations on gradient structures and other network-level derivatives. To examine representational efficiency, we compute information theoretic measures to assess the density of information contained within each representation.

Results

Across 149 SPIs and 12 skarf estimators, we found that no single FC method dominated across all criteria. For behavioral prediction (Figure 2), multiple methods achieved robust above‑chance performance for the cognition factor, but none convincingly and consistently outperformed an empirical covariance baseline. Autoregressive (skarf) estimators with sparse penalties naturally yielded very high edge‑level sparsity while preserving competitive predictive accuracy and canonical large‑scale network structure, but did not systematically “win” over dense similarity‑based FC when evaluated solely on cognition. Imposing a uniform 0.8 sparsity level across all methods revealed that many dense PySPI measures remain stable under aggressive thresholding, whereas some naturally sparse estimators lost comparatively less information, highlighting trade‑offs between representational efficiency and predictive performance. Reliability and discriminability metrics further showed that small numerical differences between methods can coexist with substantial differences in computational cost and sparsity, emphasizing that “best” depends on the evaluation axis. Overall, our benchmark revealed clusters of methods that behave similarly under multiple criteria and identified regimes where additional complexity (e.g., autoregressive structure) yielded nuanced, but not uniformly superior, benefits.

Conclusion

Using FC estimation as a case study, we present a comprehensive benchmarking approach that integrates behavioral prediction, reliability, and sparsity‑aware evaluation to compare heterogeneous connectivity representations on equal terms. The resulting framework provides a concrete, reproducible template for method selection that can be extended beyond FC to other neuroimaging analysis choices, moving the field toward more principled, data‑driven navigation of the methodological multiverse.
