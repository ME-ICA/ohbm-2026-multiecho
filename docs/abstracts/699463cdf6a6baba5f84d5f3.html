Introduction

In psychotherapy, the importance of acceptance, empathy, and congruence was proposed by Rogers, is shared as basic attitudes of therapists.^1 These attitudes of therapist are thought to promote self-regulation and reduce anxiety in clients, though there is limited experimental evidence to support this. In this study, we explored how the congruence could reduce clients’ anxiety using a hyperscanning fMRI setting. We defined congruence as an alignment between the therapist's mental state and what they talk to client. Amygdale complex is known as a key region being involved in the extinction of fear conditioning and anxiety regulation.^2,3 Based on these findings, we predicted that the effect of anxiety reduction through congruence would be mediated by amygdala activity and employed ROI-based analysis with amygdale ROIs.

Methods

30 pairs (60 participants) of healthy adults took part in the experiment, and recorded brain activation during the task with 3T MRI scanner. In each pair, one person was assigned to the role of client and the other to the role of therapist. The two participants communicated only by voice while they were in the MRI scanners. The client saw anxiety inducing pictures and was asked to tell the therapist “I feel anxious.” The therapist saw either anxiety inducing pictures (AN; anxiety image watching) or neutral pictures (NE; neutral image watching). There were three types of therapist’s response: Saying “I see” (ACC; acceptance response), “Oh really?” (QUE; questioning response), or by giving no response (NO; no response condition). After each trial, the client rated their anxiety on a visual analogue scale (VAS). By the combination of what therapist watched and responded, we defined the congruency: AN-ACC and NE-QUE as congruent condition, and AN-QUE and NE-ACC as incongruent condition. We analyzed client’s brain activation during the verbal interaction and reporting their anxiety with VAS. The amygdala ROI was created based on the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas, and the probabilistic cytoarchitectonic maps implemented in the JuBrain (SPM) Anatomy Toolbox. All analysis was done by in-house scripts with HCP and SPM software.

Results

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that the therapist’s congruent response could significantly reduce client’s anxiety compared with other two conditions. Next, we tested brain activation on amygdale complex, and there was also a significant main effect on amygdala activity, which was higher in the congruent condition. We further subdivided the amygdala into three subregions: the centromedial (CM), laterobasal (LB), and superficial (SF) subdivisions. In the CM and SF subdivisions, activity was significantly higher in the congruent than in the incongruent condition, whereas no such effect was observed in the LB subdivision. The VAS related activation in amygdale complex did not show such congruency related activation. These results suggest that amygdala activity might be associated with client’s anxiety decrease caused by therapists’ congruent words, rather than reporting anxiety itself. 

Conclusion

Our results suggest that not only the therapist’s response itself, but whether the response is congruent with the therapist’s own emotional state, promotes reducing client anxiety. The clients’ amygdale complex, especially CM and SF, mediate the decreased anxiety of clients by therapists’ congruent response. Our finding seems to be consistent with previous report that SF has been suggested to be particularly sensitive to social information processing.^4 It is still ambiguous what verbal information in therapists’ responses have effect to relieve clients’ anxiety. Regarding neural basis of anxiety relieve, we need further analysis to reveal how neural mechanisms and networks are behind the activation associated with amygdala. In addition, it is also important to reveal how therapist’s brain activation, that is behind congruent response, has effect on client’s brain, from two-brain science perspective.
