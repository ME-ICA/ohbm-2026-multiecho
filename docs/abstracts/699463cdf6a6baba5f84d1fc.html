Introduction

Human adults with congenital anosmia have no perception of odor, but whether they respond to social chemosignals was tested in only very few studies. A relatively widely studied human social chemosignal is the body-odor obtained in the state of fear. Here we set out to use functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) to study the brain-response to the smell of fear in isolated congenital anosmic and normosmic controls.  

Methods

We obtained body-odor during first-time parachuting as a smell of fear.  Samples were stored at −80°C and thawed to room temperature prior to use.

Behavioral study: Each participant (17 anosmic and 20 normosmic, all men) completed two sessions, one concurrent with the smell of fear, and the other with blank control (counter-balanced, double-blind). In each session, the participant first watched a 2-minute emotionally neutral nature video, followed by 3 tasks: “Fearfulness rating”, where subjects rated the fearfulness of 27 faces. The images, sourced from the NimStim set, depicted three categories of facial expressions: happy, ambiguous, and fearful [1]; “Empathy rating”, where participants rated their emotional engagement with various scene photos chosen from IAPS database [2]; The “Emotional Stroop”, where participants identified the color of a written word while ignoring its content. During the experiment, Galvanic Skin Response (GSR), and Electrocardiogram (ECG), were recorded.

Imaging study: We scanned 31 participants (17 male normosmics, 14 male congenital anosmics) in a 3-Tesla Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (32-channel head-neck coil). Each participant completed two sessions, with 7 min anatomical scanning in between, one session with fear sweat and the other with blank pad, counterbalanced across participants. In each session, the participant first watched two one-minute videos, followed by 2 tasks: “Fearfulness ratings” and “Empathy ratings”.  We used a computer-controlled olfactometer and “odor canopy” setup as previously described in detail [3].

We used a T2*-weighted gradient-echo EPI sequence with TR = 1450 msec, TE = 30 msec, flip angle= 68°, FOV = 220 × 220 mm2 , matrix = 96 × 96, 2.3×2.3×2.3 mm3 isotropic voxels, no gap, 60 slices, GRAPPA-2 acceleration. Image acquisition was tilted at 15° off AC-PC plane.

Results

Behavior: Normosmics exhibited heightened sympathetic arousal (elevated GSR levels) when exposed to fear sweat, particularly during trials involving happy faces. In contrast, anosmics showed no comparable sympathetic activation. In the empathy task, there was no significant main effects (all F1,33 < 0.02, p >0.88), but significant interaction between the Groups and Condition (blank vs. fear). Post hoc analysis revealed that exposure to fear sweat, led to a significant increase in empathy ratings in the normosmics group (t19 = 2.68, p =0.015, 𝑑̂ = 0.6), and remarkably, to a significant decrease in empathy ratings in the anosmics group (t16 = -2.29, p = 0.04, 𝑑̂ = 0.56).

Brain imaging: Consistent with a behavioral response, we observed modulation of the brain response by social chemosignal in congenital anosmia. The site of clearest homology in response was in the right superior frontal gyrus, where we saw a response in both anosmic and normosmic men, a response that remained convincing in anosmia even following strict thresholding (p < 0.001). In fact, an analysis probing for statistically significant differences between the normosmic and anosmic brain response uncovered only the putamen (F1,56 = 541, p = 0.044). Post hoc analysis revealed decreased activity for normosmic men (Blank=8.4±20.7, Fear=-1.8±19.4, t16=2.6, p=0.02) and no change in anosmic men (Blank=2.1±11, Fear=7.7±18.5, t13=-1.47, p=0.16), with a significant difference between the groups (t29=-2.86 , p=0.008). There were no significant changes in normosmic women (Blank=7.8±15.1, Fear=8.1±15.1, t8=-0.06, p = 0.96) nor in anosmic women (Blank=13.4±12.7, Fear=10.5±17.7, t7=0.4, p = 0.67).

Conclusion

These results imply a behavioral and brain response to a social chemosignal in congenital anosmia. Given the implications, we treat these results with caution and will seek a path to increase our confidence in these findings.
