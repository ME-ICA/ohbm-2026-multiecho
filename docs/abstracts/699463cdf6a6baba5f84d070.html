Introduction

The zona incerta (ZI) is a subcortical region increasingly recognized as an interconnected node modulating cognitive, sensorimotor, and affect-related processes. Recent research reveals a dopaminergic ZI subregion, named the A13, is implicated in emotion processing deficits and chronic pain, whereas its stimulation restores locomotion deficits in mouse models of Parkinson’s Disease. Despite its clinical potential, the A13 has not been identified in humans, preventing translation to clinical applications. Here, we provide evidence for a putative human A13 through a novel multi-modal approach by integrating: (1) post-mortem transcriptomics, (2) ex vivo 15T MRI; (3) ex vivo histology; and (4) in vivo 7T structural and diffusion-weighted MRI. These findings offer a spatial mapping of the A13 homologue in the human brain, and a generalizable cross-modality framework for characterizing the internal organization of other subcortical regions of interest.

Methods

Gene expression in the left ZI was extracted from the AHBA1 using the abagen toolbox2 (n = 36, M = 5, F = 1). PCA on a curated set of ZI-specific markers (CALB2, TH, ACHE, SST, PVALB, NOS2, CALB1; classically used to delineate subregions) was followed by k-means clustering (k=5), differential expression, and over-representation enrichment analyses to delineate a TH-rich subregion. Ordinary kriging was used to generate a voxelwise TH expression map with leave-one-out cross-validation performed to best refine the localization of this region on the AHBA dataset. To further refine its spatial boundaries, we acquired T1-weighted images (3D-FLASH: TR/TE = 30/8 ms, flip angle = 25°, resolution = 0.1 mm3 isotropic) of an ex vivo specimen at 15.2T (Centre for Functional and Metabolic Mapping, CFMM; Western Body Bequeathal Program, Western University, Canada) and registered the volume to an in vivo ZI-specific template3 to guide serial sectioning for immunohistochemistry (IHC). Voxels in the top 15th percentile of TH expression were intersected with a published rZI mask and compared to the TH-stained IHC sections to refine boundaries. Quantitative T1 and T2* maps from in vivo 7T imaging of healthy adults3 (3D MP2RAGE: TE/TR=2.73/6000 ms; flip angle=4°; resolution=0.7 mm3 isotropic; 3D ASPIRE: TE/TR = 4.61/35 ms; flip angle=13°; resolution=0.8 mm3 isotropic; n = 32; M = 20, F = 12) were acquired and evaluated to test for regional differences across ZI subregions (A13; rZI, cZI as defined by a ZI-specific 7T atlas2) and adjacent white matter (H1, H2) in subject native space using paired Wilcoxon tests followed by FDR correction. Structural connectivity was derived from the Human Connectome Project 7T diffusion data4 (n=169, M=68, F=101; TR/TE=7000/71.2 ms, flip angle=90°, resolution=1.05mm3 isotropic; b=1000,2000 s/mm2, directions=64; 15 b=0 s/mm2) using probabilistic tractography from dilated ZI masks to 180 cortical parcels, forming a 1981×180 group-average matrix5. Kriging of 11,314 stable ZI-relevant genes yielded voxelwise maps that were concatenated into a 1981×11314 matrix. The gene expression and connectivity matrices were SVD-normalized, z-scored, concatenated, and embedded through cosine similarity and diffusion maps to identify multimodal gradients and quantify modality contributions.

Results

Our approach revealed a distinct rostromedial ZI cluster ("cluster 0") significantly enriched in TH expression (Kruskal-Wallis, adjusted p < 0.001, Figure 2a). Guided by transcriptomic coordinates and ex vivo 15.2T MRI, TH-immunohistochemistry (IHC) confirmed a tightly organized population of TH-positive neurons in the rostromedial ZI, positioned superior to the H2 field of Forel. This refined parcellation (“putative A13”, MNI centroid: -6.7, -8.6, -4.6; Figure 2b) provides direct histological support for a TH-rich A13 subregion in the human brain. 

Applying the refined parcellation to 7T in vivo quantitative maps revealed significantly elevated T1 values in the putative A13 relative to adjacent H1 (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, adjusted p<0.001, 95% CI=[98.8,150.7]) and H2 (adjusted p<0.001, 95% CI=[69.4,92.1]) white matter tracts, with reduced T2* values with the rZI (adjusted p<0.05, 95% CI=[0.40,1.72]) and cZI (adjusted p<0.01, 95% CI=[0.32,3.46]), indicating microstructurally distinct features within this TH-rich ZI subregion and consistent with a densely cellular subcortical nucleus (Figure 2b).

To contextualize this specialization within the broader ZI organization, PCA of voxelwise expression maps identified a compact rostromedial component overlapping the putative A13 (PC2: 23.4% explained variance), demonstrating that this region carries a molecular signature distinct from the rest of the ZI (Figure 2c).  Predominant spatial patterns as captured by principal components were largely unaffected by testing alternative methods of non-linear dimensionality reduction techniques. Joint embedding of gene expression and cortico-incertal structural connectivity further revealed a conserved pattern of organization along the rostral-caudal axis (G1), yet the rostromedial specialization captured by PC2 was absent (Figure 2c). We further conducted a series of sensitivity analysis to investigate whether alternative joint-decomposition techniques affected the principal axis, all of which characterized a primary axis of rostral-caudal distribution shared between the molecular scale and structural connectivity. Collectively, these results delineate a molecularly distinct, TH-rich rostromedial ZI nucleus consistent with a human A13, embedded within a conserved rostrocaudal organizational scaffold but exhibiting unique molecular properties not reflected in macroscale connectivity.

Conclusion

The A13 nucleus is well characterized in rodents, but no human homologue has been clearly identified.  Here, we characterize a TH-rich, molecularly distinct rostromedial ZI subregion consistent with a human A13 region, and demonstrate its in vivo differentiability using ultra-high field MRI. The putative human A13 subregion embedded within the rostromedial ZI, represents a discrete specialization embedded within a broader rostral-caudal organizational axis, which is conserved across molecular expression and cortico-incertal connectivity. These findings illustrate that deep brain structures appearing homogeneous at mesoscopic scales can contain molecularly distinct subregions.
