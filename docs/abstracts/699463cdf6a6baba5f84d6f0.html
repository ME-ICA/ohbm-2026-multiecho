Introduction

Chronic migraine associated with medication overuse headache (CM-MOH) is a painful and disabling neurological disorder. Neuroimaging studies showed altered functional connectivity (FC) across multiple regions, including the core networks of the “triple network model” (i.e., default mode network, salience network, and central executive network) in these patients [1,2]. Overall, these regions play a key role in pain modulation, reward processing, and the formation of behavioural habits. Several neurotransmitter pathways, including serotonin, dopamine, noradrenaline, and opioid circuits, appear to be critically involved in the pathophysiology of this disorder. Alterations of the serotonin system in CM-MOH could be associated with migraine chronification, and enhanced pain perception. Moreover, disruptions of the dopaminergic system, affecting both mesocorticolimbic (MCL) reward pathways associate to drug abuse and nigrostriatal (NS) circuits involved in habit formation, may reinforce medication overuse behaviours in MOH. Additionally, noradrenergic signals are pivotal for sensory and nociceptive integration, thus fostering central sensitization and pain facilitation in MOH patients. Finally, since the overuse of headache medication leads to both addiction and migraine chronification, it has been suggested that in these patients the opioid system may be dysfunctional, underlying an altered pain modulation mechanism [3,4]. Taken together, these observations suggest that FC alterations may arise from disruptions within these key neurotransmitter systems. 

However, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying CM-MOH remain poorly understood, particularly the contribution of specific neurotransmitter systems to the functional brain alterations associated with migraine chronification and medication overuse. 

Methods

To address this issue, 91 CM-MOH patients and 35 demographically matched healthy individuals (CTRL) underwent an eyes-open resting state fMRI acquisition (T2*-weighted BOLD echo-planar imaging sequence, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FOV = 80×80 mm, voxel size = 3×3×3.2 mm3, 450 volumes, duration = 15 minutes) using a 3T MRI scanner (Philips). The quality of functional and structural MRI images was validated and visually inspected using the software MRIQC. Resting-state fMRI data were pre-processed using the CONN toolbox with the “Default MNI” pipeline, and subsequently denoised. Then, Receptor-Enriched Analysis of Functional Connectivity by Targets (REACT) approach [5] was applied to compare the neurotransmitter-related functional connectivity (nrFC) between groups in serotonin (5-HT1a, 5-HT1b, 5-HT2a, 5-HT4 and 5-HTT distribution), noradrenaline (NET distribution), opioid (μ distribution), mesocorticolimbic and nigrostriatal dopamine (D1, D2 and DAT distribution) systems by using open-source PET-based templates, considering age and sex as nuisance covariates. All data were considered significant using the following thresholds: p-FWE < 0.05 at the cluster level, and p < 0.001 uncorrected at the voxel level. Moreover, the spatial overlap between each resulting functional cluster and networks' regions involved in the triple network model was investigated. 

Results

nrFC patterns associated with serotonin receptors (5-HT1a, 5-HT1b, 5-HT2a) and transporter (5-HTT) revealed enhanced connectivity in the left fusiform gyrus in CM-MOH patients compared with CTRL. On the other hand, decreased serotonin-related connectivity in frontal (frontal pole, medial frontal cortex) and cingulate (anterior and posterior cingulate cortex) regions was observed in these patients. No significant differences between groups were observed when considering 5-HT4 serotonin receptor. Spatial overlap analysis revealed that the serotonin-related clusters located in the anterior cingulate cortex and frontal pole overlapped with the corresponding anterior salience network regions. In the nrFC maps associated with the dopaminergic MCL circuit, CM-MOH patients showed reduced nrFC in the medial superior frontal gyrus and the anterior part of the left hippocampus (D2 receptor). Additionally, these patients reported increased nrFC in the left posterior putamen (D1 and D2 receptors), which is part of the dorsal striatum, when considering the dopaminergic NS pathway. No significant difference was observed in either of the two dopaminergic systems with respect to DAT. In opioid system CM-MOH patients showed decreased nrFC in the bilateral supramarginal gyrus which clusters overlapped with the corresponding posterior salience network regions. Finally, no significant difference in nrFC was found between CM-MOH patients and CTRL when considering noradrenaline transporter (NET). 

Conclusion

CM-MOH patients showed dysfunctional activity in serotonin, dopamine, and opioid neurotransmitter systems. Serotonin-related alterations, partially overlapping with the anterior node of salience network regions, may be linked to pain modulation and headache pathogenesis, promoting MOH. Dopamine-related alterations within the MCL and NS systems could affect reward processing,  similar to mechanisms described in addiction, and maintain the medication-overuse habits, respectively. Opiod-related alterations, which partially overlapped with regions belonging to the posterior node of salience network, may be associated with migraine chronification and medication addiction behavior, thus fostering dysfunctional mechanisms of pain modulation [3,4].  These findings shed light on the functional neural mechanisms underlying CM-MOH, thus providing evidence for serotonin, dopamine, and opioid involvement in its pathophysiology.
