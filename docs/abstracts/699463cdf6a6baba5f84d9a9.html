Introduction

Brain age models are regression methodologies that leverage age-related structural patterns to estimate an individual’s biological brain age from neuroimaging data. Deviations from normative aging are quantified through the brain age gap (BAG), the difference between the brain age predicted and chronological age. Despite promising applications in normal and pathological aging trajectories, the reliability and interpretability of brain age models remain subjects of ongoing debate. One of the widely acknowledged limitations is that brain age estimations are systematically biased: younger individuals tend to have their brain age overestimated, while older adults are typically underestimated1. This bias appears across model types and dataset distributions and has been attributed to several factors, including regression to the mean, non-Gaussian sample distributions, and regression dilution effects. While post-hoc correction strategies have been proposed to mitigate these biases, they introduce significant drawbacks2 which raise concerns about the robustness and generalizability of BAG as a standalone biomarker of brain health. Therefore, in this study, we propose a novel Bayesian prediction framework designed to accurately estimate brain age while directly addressing the issue of systematic age bias, applied to the OASIS-3 dataset. We hypothesized that regression dilution is the primary source of this bias and incorporated the uncertainty in the predictors inside the model by assigning them probability distributions. 

Methods

Neuroimaging data were obtained from the Open Access Series of Imaging Studies (OASIS-3), comprising T1-weighted structural MRI scans from 1,038 participants, all annotated with the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR). To mitigate a potential confounding effect due to scanner field strength, only scans acquired at 3T were retained for analysis. Participants with missing scanner identification, non-AD dementias, or other unrelated neurological diagnoses were excluded. The subset with CDR-3, comprised of 10 scans of four participants, were also excluded due to insufficient sample size. The final dataset was divided into three subsets: (i) a training set used to fit the Bayesian brain age prediction model (N = 331), (ii) a test set to assess BAG bias (N=144), and (iii) a prediction set (total N = 446; CDR-0 = 273; CDR-0.5 = 115; CDR-1 = 46; CDR-2 = 12) to evaluate group differences in BAG.The structural MRI data was processed using Freesurfer v6.0 automated pipeline. The cortical parcellation followed both Desikan-Killiany (DK) and Destrieux  atlases, without manual intervention . Regional estimates of cortical thickness and cortical volume were extracted for each parcellation. Four separate regression models were built, one for each combination of cortical metric (thickness or volume) and atlas (Desikan–Killiany or Destrieux). All regional features were standardized (z-scored) within sex groups and then were used as predictors in a Bayesian linear regression framework. The Bayesian regression approach employed in this study adheres to established principles of Bayesian statistical inference. Specifically, we assumed Gaussian distributions for both predictors and outcome variables and assigned non-informative priors to the regression coefficients to reflect a lack of strong prior beliefs. The predictive relationship was modeled as a linear function. By assuming a statistical distribution to the predictors, the model directly addresses the attenuation bias issue. To provide a benchmark for BAG bias, we additionally implemented a standard linear regression model, i.e, without assigning a probability distribution to the predictors. Model bias was evaluated by computing the Pearson correlation coefficient between the estimated brain age gap (BAG) and chronological age, with significant positive or negative correlations indicating residual age bias. Predictive accuracy was quantified using the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) between predicted and actual age values. To assess the model's discriminative capacity across diagnostic categories, we conducted a one-tailed t-test comparing BAG estimates of different CDR scores against healthy controls. 

Results

The Bayesian model consistently demonstrated effective correction of age-related bias across all four atlas–metric combinations. The BAG values derived from the corrected Bayesian model showed no significant correlation between BAG and chronological age for the test dataset, unlike the traditional linear regression model (Figure 1). The model using cortical thickness following the Destrieux atlas was found to be the most precise, exhibiting the lowest MAE values. For this optimal model (Destrieux/Cortical Thickness), the MAE was 11.0 years for females and 7.7 years for males. Beyond calibration, the Bayesian corrected model showed strong clinical relevance in distinguishing diagnostic categories. The estimated BAG was significantly different for healthy controls (CDR-0) compared to individuals with mild to moderate AD (CDR-1 and CDR-2) across all four brain age models (one-tailed Student’s t test, p < 0.05). Furthermore, the model was sensitive enough to show a significant difference against the CDR-0.5 groups (questionable cognitive impairment) for all models, except for the one utilizing cortical thickness and the Destrieux parcellation. These findings confirm a consistent and significant association between elevated BAG and greater cognitive impairments. 

Conclusion

Our results support the effectiveness of the proposed Bayesian prediction framework in addressing systematic biases by producing BAG estimates with no significant correlation across the age spectrum. This approach successfully overcame regression dilution effects directly inside the model, eliminating the need for complex and potentially problematic post-hoc correction procedures. The observed trade-off, where MAE values are higher than some recent studies, is expected when eliminating attenuation bias, but the key strength lies in the model’s ability to address bias intrinsically. The model demonstrated clinical utility, showing a consistent and significant association between elevated BAG and higher CDR scores, reflecting clinically meaningful deviations from normative aging. The ability to achieve these results using metrics commonly found in clinical fast reconstruction pipelines further reinforces the clinical meaning of this study. Together, these results support the use of Bayesian approaches to enhance the reliability, interpretability, and clinical relevance of brain age modeling in the study of aging and neurodegenerative diseases.
