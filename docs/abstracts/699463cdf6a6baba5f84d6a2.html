Introduction

Paper has been published Kashyap, A., Geenjaar, E., Bey, P. et al. Using an ordinary differential equation model to separate rest and task signals in fMRI. Nat Commun 16, 7128 (2025). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-62491-6

Task-based functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has enabled non-invasive measurement of cortical responses to stimuli and cognitive tasks, with over 100,000 studies performed by 2023 [1]. However, a fundamental challenge persists in characterizing task-induced activity changes relative to the large, non-stationary baseline cortical activity observed during resting state. While resting-state networks have been linked to homeostatic functions such as priming plasticity and maintaining inter-regional interactions, the precise relationship between rest and task processes remains unclear [2,3]. Current understanding suggests task processes may represent weighted activations of resting-state sub-networks, but mechanistic details of how these quasi-rhythmic cortical processes interact during task performance are poorly understood [4]. This study addresses the significant open question of how network-connected regions that integrate information during rest relate to stimulus-driven processes during specific tasks [5].

Methods

We extended generative Ordinary Differential Equations (ODE) models for fMRI activity using Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) to model rest activity during task epochs [6]. Unlike traditional Brain Network Models that focus on steady-state properties, our ODE framework directly fits measured fMRI data to model network components, enabling interpretation of individual fMRI trajectories during both rest and task epochs [7,8]. Each brain region's dynamics were modeled the sum of interactions across network edges fitted using a Taylor Polynomial. Separate network ODEs were trained on Human Connectome Project datasets including Rest, Gambling, Working Memory, Emotion, and Relational tasks [9]. We hypothesized that stimulus-independent processes could be represented by linear combinations of Rest and Task ODE models. To test signal separation efficacy, we evaluated five behavioral measures (reaction time, trajectory classification, accuracy, missing trials, and functional connectivity) across fourteen sub-tasks, comparing brain-to-behavior relationships under different separation frameworks.

Results

Our analysis demonstrated that task-specific network ODEs are subsets of rest ODEs across all fourteen tested subtasks. The optimal signal separation occurred when background, stimulus-independent activity was approximated as the difference between Rest and Task ODE models. This separation significantly enhanced behavioral predictions, yielding a 9% increase in explanatory power (R²) for reaction time predictions across 14 sub-tasks on a trial-by-trial basis. The improved predictions extended to missing trial rates and accuracy measures at the individual level, as well as task trajectory classification and dynamic functional connectivity analysis.

Conclusion

This work contributes to the field by applying an ODE model with biophysical properties to interpret the Task fMRI signal. To gain real insight into brain dynamics, structure, and behavior, it is best to address it by creating a single framework where all these elements can interact in a causal manner. In this manner we are able to establish that the Active Cortex Model, demonstrating that all cortical processes are active during rest and a subset of them are elevated to perform a task, is the most likely explanation of the relationship between rest and task. Thus, this work advances our fundamental understanding of the rest-task relationship while offering improved tools for isolating task-specific signals and strengthening brain-behavior correlations in neuroimaging research.
