Introduction

Although the histopathological hallmark of Parkinson’s disease is the accumulation of abnormally-folded alpha-synuclein in Lewy bodies, increasing evidence highlights the contribution of cerebral small vessel disease (SVD), in worsening disease severity. SVD is an age-related disorder of the arterioles and capillaries. It is characterised by four radiological features: white matter hyperintensities (WMH), cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), lacunes, and enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS). It frequently co-occurs with Parkinson’s, and higher burden of SVD is associated with worse gait, cognition, and mood, and correlations with higher severity on motor scores1.  

White matter structural changes captured by diffusion MRI may occur before conventional SVD markers become visible. Diffusion-based metrics have been shown to outperform traditional MRI features in both explaining clinical deficits and tracking SVD disease progression2. Altered diffusion metrics have also been reported across the Parkinson’s continuum. Using fixel based analysis, a higher order diffusion model, our group has shown extensive white matter macrostructural changes (reductions in fibre cross section) in Parkinson’s patients  who later progress to dementia3, as well as microstructural changes (reductions in fibre density) in Parkinson’s patients with established dementia4. A recent study in Alzheimer's has shown strong associations between SVD burden and reductions in fibre density across several tracts, particularly in relation to WMH5. So far, the relationship between SVD burden and MRI diffusion changes in Parkinson’s has not yet been examined, nor whether SVD burden and MRI diffusion changes relate to cognitive severity in Parkinson’s patients. Here, we examine the relationship between SVD burden and white matter integrity in patients with Parkinson’s and controls.

Methods

Methods 

Participants underwent assessments of general cognition (MoCA) and motor symptom severity (MDS-UPDRS-III).  Data on vascular risk factors including smoking status, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes mellitus, obesity (body mass index [BMI] >30), and history of a vascular event other than stroke was used to calculate a Vascular Risk Compound Score (VRCS).  

MRI imaging 

MRI imaging protocol was acquired on the same 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma scanner and included: 3D FLAIR sequence (TR=4800ms, TE=403ms, voxel size=1m3, FOV=256mm, matrix=256x256x192; acquisition time ~5min; 3D T2 sequence (TR=3200 ms, TE=404 ms, voxel size=1 mm³, FOV=256 mm, matrix=256×256×192; acquisition time ~5 min); DWI with 2×2×2mm isotropic voxels, matrix =220×220×72, TE=3260 ms, TR=58 ms, b=50 s/mm2/17 directions, b=300 s/mm2/8 directions, b=1000 s/mm2/64 directions, b=2000 s/mm2/64 directions, and acceleration factor=2. Multi-echo fast low angle shot (FLASH) multiparameter scans with proton density weighting using the following parameters: TR=25ms, eight equidistant echoes with TE1=2.3ms and TE8=18.4ms, flip angle=6°,bandwidth=488Hz/pixel, matrix size=224x256x180 with 1mm3 isotropic resolution, acquisition time 7m8s with GRAPPA enabled; were used to generate SWI images using the CLEAR-SWI pipeline. 

SVD burden 

Visual rating scales were used to rate WMH on 3D FLAIR, CMB on SWI images, lacunes and enlarged perivascular spaces on T2-weighted imaging. We rated total SVD burden on MRI on an ordinal scale from 0 to 4, by counting presence of each of the 4 MRI features, based on STRIVE2 international criteria.

 

Fixel-based analysis

Diffusion MRI images were pre-processed using an established Mrtrix3 pipeline. Fiber orientation distributions (FODs) were computed using multi-shell three-tissue-constrained spherical deconvolution using the group-average response function for each tissue type (grey matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid). Each participant's FOD image was registered to a study-specific template using affine and nonlinear registration.

Three measures were derived per participant:

  1. Fiber cross-section (FC): a measure of tract macrostructure. 
  2. Fiber density (FD): a measure of microstructural changes within tracts. 
  3. Fiber density and cross-section (FDC): a combined measure of change at both the micro- and macro-structural levels. 

We performed whole-brain fixel based analysis within all white matter fixels (as per the John Hopkins University atlas), with age and sex as covariates and family-wise error correction (FWE) for multiple comparisons. In addition, we performed tract of interest analysis. Mean fibre density and fibre cross section was calculated on 11 tracts (based on previous work): posterior thalamic radiations, splenium, body and genu of the corpus callosum, superior longitudinal fasciculi, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculi (includes inferior longitudinal fasciculus), and superior fronto-occipital fasciculi. 

WMH volumes

For a subset of subjects with available FLAIR imaging (n=37, of which Parkinson’s n=24, and controls n=13), total WMH volumes were derived using semi-automated segmentation method BIANCA. Mean FD/FC/FDC was calculated within hyperintense white matter and neighbouring normal appearing white matter (NAWM). 

Statistical Analyses

Shapiro–Wilk’s test was used to assess normality. To compare between Parkinson’s and controls with respect to demographic characteristics and vascular risk factors we used two-tailed t-test or Mann–Whitney tests for non-normally distributed data. We performed a simple linear regression analysis to explore associations between SVD burden and cognition. For a subset of subjects with available WMH volumes, we compared mean fibre density and mean fibre cross section in WMH regions to neighbouring normal appearing white matter using Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test corrected with FDR, and correlated WMH volumes with clinical measures.

Results

We included 40 patients with Parkinson’s and 18 control participants. 

The groups did not significantly differ in age, sex distribution, cognitive performance (MoCA), VRCS scores or overall SVD burden. WMH volume significantly predicted MoCA score after adjusting for age and sex (F(3,34) = 2.92, p = .04, R² = 0.13). Similarly, simple linear regression showed that SVD burden was a significant predictor of MoCA score after controlling for age and sex (F(3,54) = 2.92, p = .04, R² = 0.09). 

At whole-brain level, in patients with Parkinson’s, higher SVD burden was associated with reduced fibre cross-section within the genu of corpus callosum (10.6% reduction, pFWE = .046).  No associations were observed with fibre density or combined FDC measure. 

There were no significant differences between PD and controls in the selected tracts for FD, FC or FDC. 

Within WMH regions relative to NAWM, fibre density was increased in both Parkinson’s patients and controls (Parkinson’s FD in WMH vs NAWM: W = 237, p =.005; controls W = 85, p =.04), after FDR correction. There were no significant differences between PD and controls for FD in the WMH. 

In WMH compared to NAWM, fibre cross-section was decreased in both Parkinson’s and controls (Parkinson’s W = 8, p<.0001; controls W = 10, p =.01), after FDR correction.  There were no significant differences between PD and controls for FC in the NAWM. 

Conclusion

We examined the relationship between total SVD burden and white matter integrity in a cohort of cognitively normal individuals with Parkinson’s and compared with healthy controls. We find reduced fibre cross-section in the genu of corpus callosum with increased SVD burden in patients with Parkinson’s disease. We also found increased fibre density in WMH, and reduced fibre cross section in NAWM, in both PD and controls. This may reflect WMH preferentially affecting larger, denser fibres. Further, we show higher SVD burden relates to poorer cognitive scores in PD, showing the importance of vascular disease in contributing to cognitive decline in Parkinson’s.
