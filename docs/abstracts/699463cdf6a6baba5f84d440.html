Introduction

Population cohorts such as the UK Biobank (UKB) enable the study of how complex traits relate to brain structure and genetic variation. Integrating questionnaire data, neuroimaging, and genomics provides a multimodal framework for identifying the biological basis of mental health–related traits.

Depressive and hypomanic symptoms are common in the general population and exist along a continuum with clinical mood disorders. Subthreshold mood symptoms may reflect an increased risk for developing major depression (MDD) or bipolar disorder (BD). However, the neural and genetic mechanisms underlying this dimensional variability in mood remain poorly understood, limiting our understanding of how population-level symptom variation maps onto biological substrates.

In this study, we investigated brain functional connectivity associated with dimensional lifetime mood lability traits and its genetic correlates in the general population.

Methods

The UKB is a general population cohort where psychiatric diagnoses are available for only a subset of participants. In contrast, self-reported mental health data are largely available (n=157,315), allowing a dimensional analysis of mood. To capture individual differences across the spectrum of mood disorders, including mood instability and subclinical symptoms, we used responses from the CIDI-SF (Composite International Diagnostic Interview – Short Form), which assesses lifetime depressive symptoms, and the bespoke questionnaire designed to assess lifetime manic symptoms [1]. An overview of the study's workflow is presented in Fig. 1. We applied multiple correspondence analysis (MCA), an extension of principal component analysis (PCA) for categorical data, to identify the main dimensions of mood variation in the population. Resting-state functional connectivities (FC) were computed using the Schaefer 100-region cortical atlas [2] and the Tian subcortical atlas [3]. Mood-associated FC were identified via bootstrapping and permutation tests. We filtered these FC for significant heritability (p_BONF<0.05) using GCTA [4]. To uncover the genetic basis of these heritable mood-associated FC, we conducted a multivariate genome-wide association study (GWAS) using MOSTest (Multivariate Omnibus Statistical Test [5]).

Results

MCA derived two continuous scores (PC1, PC2) representing the principal axes of lifetime mood variation in the population, together explaining 90.79% of the variance (Fig. 2A). We identified significantly heritable FC associated with PC1 (528 FCs) and PC2 (56 FCs), which spanned multiple large-scale networks, including the subcortical, default mode, salience, frontoparietal, dorsal attention networks, previously implicated in mood disorders, as well as somatomotor network (Fig 2B-2D). MOSTest identified 40 lead single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs, p<5e-8) associated with FC patterns linked to PC1 and 16 for PC2. Some of these SNPs are linked to brain-expressed genes and act as expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs), regulating the expression of genes previously implicated in mood disorders (e.g., RTN1, LHPP).

Conclusion

These findings support resting-state connectivity as a biologically meaningful endophenotype for exploring the genomic basis of mood variability. Our results validate the dimensional phenotyping strategy and demonstrate that mood-related variability in the general population engages functional networks involved in mood disorders. 

While some identified genes have been reported in prior neuroimaging-genetics studies (e.g., CADM2, VRK2), others appear novel in this context (e.g., RTN1, LHPP), providing new insights into the biological pathways linking brain function and mood traits.

By embracing a dimensional approach, this study offers insights into the neurobiological substrates of dimensional lifetime mood lability traits that extend beyond standard categorical psychiatric diagnoses.
