Introduction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) offers a unique, non-invasive window into biological tissue, enabling structural characterisation without destruction. For human brain mapping, this capability is essential when linking imaging markers to underlying biology. Diffusion MRI (dMRI), in particular, probes tissue microstructure by measuring water motion and inferring histological features. Organoids are three-dimensional, self-organizing models of the human cerebral cortex that recapitulate key aspects of brain development and could provide a complementary platform for structural validation of MRI markers to current animal and human imaging. We study cortical organoids at early (D69–D76) and mature (D253–D255) stages, spanning diameters of 0.5–2 mm, and address the technical challenge of resolving microstructure at micrometer scales within feasible experimental times. Using ultra-high field MRI (28.2 T), strong gradients (3 T/m), and fast SE-EPI acquisition, we achieve high-resolution dMRI while preserving tissue integrity over long scans. Advanced diffusion contrasts reveal age-dependent heterogeneity within and across organoids. Crucially, three-dimensional lightsheet microscopy provides complementary ground truth of cell and axonal density, enabling direct structural validation of MRI-derived markers. This work illustrates how organoids can bridge microscopic anatomy and macroscopic MRI, advancing quantitative human brain mapping across scales.

Methods

The developed workflow for living brain organoids is depicted in Figure 1. Organoid specimens: 13 cortical brain organoids (DIV55-110 [1])  are assessed, of which 10 are young, ranging in age from D69 to D76, and 3 are more mature, ranging from D253 to D255. Each organoid was transferred via a glass pipette into a 5 or 3 mm MR tube atop an agar substrate. The rest of the tube was filled with neural medium. MRI system: Experiments were performed on a 28.2 T NMR spectrometer with an Avance Neo console was used with an IProbe containing a 5mm volume coil and a gradient insert of 3 T/m with a slew rate of 30k T/m/s (Bruker, with ParaVision 360 3.3). The probe temperature was set to 37 °C. MRI experiments. Longitudinal ADC estimates were obtained for repeated 2D PGSE EPI experiments over 48 hours: voxel size 44.8x44.8x120 µm3, Δ 3 ms, δ 1 ms, repetition time 3.8 s, echo time 8.1 ms, 8 segments, b 0 and 1.0 ms/µm2.  Diffusion kurtosis: PGSE EPI 3D, voxel size 44.8x44.8x44.8 µm3, Δ 4.3 ms, δ 1.2 ms, repetition time 3.8 s, echo time 9 ms, 8 segments, b 0, 0.6, 1.2, 1.8, 2.4 and 3.0 ms/µm2.   ADC diffusion-time dependence: PGSE EPI 3D, voxel size 44.8x44.8x44.8 µm3, Δ 2.73, 5, 10, 15, and 25 ms, δ 0.9 ms, repetition time 3.8 s, echo time 29.5 ms, 8 segments, b 0 and 1.0 ms/µm2. High-resolution diffusion tensor imaging: PGSE EPI 3D, voxel size 19.5x19.5x19.5 µm3, Δ 2.7 ms, δ 0.9 ms, 9 directions, repetition time 3.8 s, echo time 9.4 ms, 8 segments, 2 repetitions, b 0.1 and 1.0 ms/µm2.

 Following MRI acquisition, organoids are fixed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for further immunostaining and analysis by lightsheet microscopy.  For the immunostaining, primary antibodies used, were mouse anti ZO-1 1:250 (Bdbiosciences, Cat.610966) in combination with secondary donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 1:750 (Thermo Scientific, Cat.A21202), SMI312 1:500 (BioLegend, mouse, Cat.837904, ) in combination with secondary donkey   anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 1:750 (Thermo Scientific, Cat.ab175738). TO-PRO3 (Thermo Scientific, Cat.T3605) was added in the mix of secondary antibodies to stain the nucleus of cells in the far red spectrum. Initially, the concentration for TO-PRO3 was 1:5000, and it was later increased to 1:1000. The organoids were imaged with an UltraMicroscope Blaze (Miltenyi Biotec) lightsheet microscope equipped Neo sCMOS camera (2048×2048 pixels. Pixel size: 6.5 x 6.5 µm2) and Imspector software (version 7.7. (Miltenyi Biotec). 

Results

High-resolution diffusion MRI (dMRI) using echo-planar imaging (EPI) for small living organoids (<2 mm) at 28.2T, followed by lightsheet microscopy, is feasible and can be incorporated into a workflow for assessing brain cortical organoids (Figure 1). The longitudinal ADC assessment showed that for the cortical brain organoids, structural changes, inferred by diffusion markers, did not occur until after 20 hours of dMRI measurements. The high-resolution dMRI analysis—through diffusion kurtosis, ADC diffusion-time dependence, high-resolution diffusion tensor imaging — confirms spatial heterogeneity in the microstructure of the brain cortical organoid (Figures 1 and 2). This variability is particularly evident when comparing organoids at early and mature developmental stages.

Diffusion kurtosis. The differences observed in organoid aging reveal that the median ADC is consistently higher (0.66-0.76 vs. 0.59, Δ = 4.3 ms) in younger organoids compared to mature ones, while kurtosis values are lower (0.85-1.09 vs. 1.43, Δ = 4.3 ms). Additionally, the kurtosis distributions are narrower in younger organoids. Similar to diffusion kurtosis, a higher ADC is observed in the younger organoid, and this trend holds for all diffusion times. 

Figure 2 illustrates the results of the ADC diffusion-time dependence experiment for a mature organoid. The dMRI images (Figure 2a) acquired at different b-values and Δ values clearly demonstrate that the organoid exhibits microstructural features that correlate with the lightsheet microscopy image (Figure 2d). We investigated the variance of Δ across different regions within the organoids to characterize the structural heterogeneity (Figure 2b). Mean ADC was calculated for the entire organoid, as well as for two smaller regions (Figure 2c), and the results were plotted against Δ to assess the diffusion-time dependence of ADC.

Across the organoid, signal intensity visibly increases with an increase in Δ, while ADC generally decreases as a function of diffusion time in most regions. In both mature and young (not shown) organoids, ADC in the periphery is higher than in the core, suggesting that mature organoids may exhibit a densely packed core structure, likely related to necrotic tissue.

The diffusion-time dependence is distinctly different between young and mature organoids; the older organoid shows a steep change in ADC between Δ = [2.7, 10] ms both globally and regionally (Figure 2b). In contrast, the younger organoid displays a slight ADC increase followed by a decrease in the periphery (not shown), along with a more gradual linear diffusion-time dependence in core regions across the complete range of measured diffusion times (Δ = [2.7, 25] ms).

Conclusion

The variability in apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) and kurtosis values among organoids of similar ages can be attributed to differences in their developmental stages. In the early stages, brain organoids consist of multiple ventricular-like zones (VLZs) that vary in size and number. Due to their self-organizing nature, each individual organoid exhibits unique cytoarchitecture [2]. As development progresses, the VLZs expand due to cell proliferation and migration, leading to increased complexity. In more mature organoids, these VLZs tend to diminish, accompanied by a greater diversity of neuronal cell types [3]. In terms of structural composition, single-cell profiling of the cortical organoids has reliably identified various cell types, including glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons, intermediate and ventral progenitors, radial glia, and astroglia [4].

As organoid models increasingly exhibit realistic features of brain structure and function, their application in MRI presents a promising complementary platform for the development and validation of MRI biomarkers. Recent studies have reported diffusion MRI experiments on fixed brain organoids at 11.7T, achieving resolutions of 310 x 310 x 800 µm³ [4] and isotropic resolutions of 30 µm using Gadavist [5]. The current study utilizes fast echo-planar imaging (EPI) readout at ultra-high field strength, significantly reducing acquisition times and pushing spatial resolution down to below 20 µm isotropic, thereby enabling high-fidelity MRI of living organoids.

The unique 28.2T system facilitates these high spatial resolutions for dMRI measurements, while its high gradient strength and slew rate support short diffusion times using pulsed field gradients and oscillating gradients in future research. Additionally, a medium exchange setup can be integrated to facilitate longer measurement durations. Future work will also focus on quantitatively validating histological estimates (e.g., cell size and density) using microscopy techniques.
