Introduction

The brain development in mammals is a very complex process starting during the fetal stage and lasting between several months to several years. One of the most striking feature of this stage is the rapid appearance of cortical folding, in a large variety of species. Understanding the origins of this folding has led to several hypotheses that remains debated 1.

The last ten years have seen an increase of biomechanical models of brain development at a macroscopic scale (see Fig 1). The aim of such models consist in reproducing the cortical folding process that occurs during the fetal period in humans and in a large number of mammals as well 2. Whether it concerns the design of the models or their validation, one observe a plurality of hypotheses, features and metrics 3, which may make the comparison between models difficult but can also be seen as a diversity of approaches to objectivize a complex phenomenon. Additionaly, to our knowledge, the energy consumption of computational models has never been used as an objectivation factor.

Our contribution is twofold : based on an attempt to review exhaustively all the computational models of brain development, we propose first a classification of models, second a typology of metrics to validate the models. Third, we introduce a novel metric that quantifies the « energy realism » of a model and eventually discuss some practical and epistemological implications of this approach.

Methods

Typology of models and validation metrics

We review the literature on computational models of brain development through a systematic search in pubmed, web of knowledge and OpenAlex databases. We use the following terms ("cortical folding" OR "brain growth" OR "cortical growth") AND model AND simulation, with a restriction on the title/abstract. After the removal of duplicates, we keep only the articles whose contributions are based on models simulating 2D or 3D geometric shapes from an initial configuration. For instance, biomechanical models with temporal simulations 3 enter in this framework while probabilistic models used to model the cortical variability do not.

We use 4 categories for the different journals : generalist (e.g. Nature Comm.), interdisciplinary (e.g. PloS Comp Biol), theoretic (e.g. Phys Review) and neuroscience (e.g. Cereb Cortex).

Based on a carefull inspection of the articles, we propose to characterize each model according to the following typology : 1) Dimension (2D/3D), 2) Geometry (synthetic/realistic), 3) Mechanisms, 4) Criterion, 5) Metric(s) used to validate, 6) Scale of the metric(s) (Global, regional, local, spectral).

Energy realism

There exist very few articles that quantifies the amount of energy required by a fetal brain to develop. In 4 the author estimates the total energy invested in fetal tissue at 5672 kJ. It is also said that an energy investment of 4.56 kJ/g is required for neonatal development of brain and nervous tissue. Given that a newborn brain is about 400g, one obtains 1824 kJ for the brain only (almost 1/3 of the total) which corresponds to E_real ~ 0.5 kWh.

We compare this approximate value to the amount of energy E_sim used to run a computational model composed of N entities (e.g 3D points). There are two different approaches for the comparison. We can

1) compute an absolute ratio between E_sim and E_real

2) compute a normalized ratio where the mean energy per entity E’_sim is compared with the mean energy per neuron E’_real.

Results

Basic statistics on the dataset

We obtain 82 unique articles on the three databases. We add 4 other articles not present. Based on a careful inspection of the methods we keep only 47 articles (55%) and do not include 33 articles (38%). We find 6 review articles (7%) that are used for the discussion.

The 47 articles range from 1982 to 2025. The distribution by year is plotted on Fig 1A.

An apparent diversity of models

In the 47 articles, we identified several categories of models : 1) 1D (2%), 2D (51 %) or 3D (66%) 2) with synthetic (72 %) or realistic (30 %) geometries, 3) based on hyperelasticity/elastoplaticity (87%). Marginal paradigms based on reaction-diffusion or geometry are present (13%). We observe different refinements with axonal growth (21 %), skull constraints (10%), neural migration (6%).

On Fig 1A the percentage by year of the realistic models shows a decrease since a notable peak in the 2010’s. On Fig 1B the presence of 2D and 3D models (sometimes both are present in an article) tends to stabilize.

A plurality of validation approaches

In the 47 articles, we identify several approaches to validate the models

Visual (58%), GI (28%), Curvatures (23%), Thickness (13%), Hinge or pits (11%), Depth (11%). Very few articles (4%) validate the models based on the average distance with respect to a ground truth and only before 2012. It is also interesting to note the temporal evolution (Fig 1C and 1D) of two other features : 1) validation with a ground truth (45%), visual comparison (21%) or no ground truth (34%), 2) the scale of the metric, Global (64 %), Regional (47%), Local (15%).

Energy realism

In 3 articles, we found informations regarding computation time, the number of elements and/or the underlying hardware (CPU or GPU). For instance in 3 it corresponds to 25-35H, 1.9 millions of tetrahedrons, on an HPC. Without knowledge on the power of the CPU we propose an estimate between 100 and 400W. We proceed similarly for the 2 other studies.

The absolute ratio can be less than 1 for one article (2022). But the normalized ratio is always greater than 1. Fig 1E shows the absolute energy (left axis) and the relative energy per element (right axis) in log scale. We note an increase in terms of energy consumption with a specificity related to the last article, considering the training or the inference phase. In the last case, the energy per element is one order of magnitude larger than the one of a real brain.

Conclusion

In our work, we have extensively reviewed the literature on computationel models of brain development. We can observe several phenomenons when focusing on the nature of the models: 1) a transition after 2009, with an almost constant presence of such models per year, 2) a moderate presence of realistic models 3) a dominance of biomechanical paradigms with limited refinements. Regarding the validation metrics, visual evidence remain massive but we note a large diversity of approaches, coming from geometric or anatomical features. Global metrics are predominant on local and regional ones. In view of all these elements, it appears that no decisive paradigm shift seems to be at stake, both in model design and model validation.

Regarding the energy realism of the models, the literature does not allow to produce a large series of values. We found only 3 articles with some scattered information that allows to estimate the amount of energy used to simulate brain development. Considering the absolute energy is not very relevant and the normalized energy, even if questionable, is closer to the scaling power of a model.

Our proposal to consider this new metric raises several comments. 

First the article that uses a deep learning approach questions the proportion of energy to consider. While the training phase is energy costly, the inference is more comparable to a real brain metabolism. But which phase has to be used in order to compare a model to a real brain ? Second, there is a large gap between the realism of the models and what the real brain metabolism is at fetal stage. In a world with increasingly limited resources, it is paradoxical to build complex and costly models that aim to explain only one aspect of "reality" at the expense of another, the energy aspect.

Our article is not intended to call to a stoppage of research on brain modeling. It is more a reflexive and critic view on the trends of this sub-field. We hope the community will hear it and tend to share more information on less usual metrics, such as the energy cost.
