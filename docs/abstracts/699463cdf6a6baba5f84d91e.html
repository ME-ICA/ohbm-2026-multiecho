Introduction

Dementia rates are rising fastest in regions where access to advanced neuroimaging is limited, creating an urgent need for scalable diagnostic tools. Electroencephalography (EEG) is inexpensive, portable, and sensitive to early neural changes in Alzheimer’s disease (AD)12 and related disorders, yet it remains underused in clinical settings. The central obstacle is not the absence of EEG biomarkers, but the lack of evidence that they remain robust and generalizable across the heterogeneous recording systems, populations, and clinical environments that characterize real-world practice.

Most existing EEG–machine learning studies rely on single-center datasets, yielding optimistic results that do not hold up to external validation. Demonstrating that EEG biomarkers can withstand cross-site variability is essential for their clinical adoption, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where the diagnostic gap is greatest.

To address this need, we conducted a large multicenter study across five countries, designed to evaluate not only the discriminability of established EEG markers for AD, MCI, and FTD, but—critically—the stability, interpretability, and clinical relevance of these predictions under realistic cross-center heterogeneity. Our framework integrates standardized preprocessing, rigorous cross-center validation, probability calibration, and SHAP-based interpretability to directly test the translational readiness of EEG for dementia screening. In particular, we assessed whether model-derived probabilities aligned with the current gold standard neuropsychological test.

Methods

The study included resting-state EEG from 739 individuals across five independent centers in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, and Turkey (294 cognitively normal [CN], 156 MCI, 249 AD, 40 FTD), recorded using heterogeneous EEG systems differing in sensor density (32–132 channels), sampling characteristics (200–2048 Hz), hardware manufacturers, and montage configurations. All recordings underwent a fully automated preprocessing pipeline involving band-pass filtering (0.5–45 Hz), resampling to 250 Hz, epoch segmentation, adaptive artifact rejection, average referencing, and bad-channel interpolation34.

We extracted 23 canonical EEG biomarkers spanning four major feature families—spectral, information‑theoretic, connectivity, and complexity markers, generating a 92-dimensional feature space through spatial and temporal summarization34. Classification relied on Random Forests with Platt-scaled calibration. Robustness was evaluated using a three-tier validation framework: (i) within-center stratified cross-validation, (ii) multi-center pooled validation, and (iii) leave-one-center-out (LOCO) validation to assess generalization to entirely unseen acquisition environments, including unseen EEG systems. All experiments included 500 repeated cross-validations and 500-permutation tests. SHAP values were used to quantify feature relevance and examine neurobiological coherence. Results

Across centers and validation tiers, the CN–AD contrast showed consistently strong discrimination. Within-center AUCs ranged from 0.74 to 0.83, and multi-center training maintained or enhanced these results (AUCs 0.73–0.84). Most importantly, LOCO validation demonstrated robust cross-site generalization with AUCs of 0.69–0.86, all statistically significant (Results figure panel A). Model predictions remained stable across validation strategies, with Spearman correlations up to ρ = 0.92 between within-multi center outputs and similarly high correlations between multi-cross center predictions (ρ ≈ 0.91).

Model-derived AD probabilities showed strong and consistent associations with cognitive impairment across centers and strategies, including negative correlations with MMSE and semantic fluency and positive correlations with Trail Making A/B (Results figure panel B). These stable neuropsychological associations confirm that predictions captured clinically meaningful variance rather than site-specific noise.

Performance for CN–MCI and FTD–AD was more limited, particularly under LOCO conditions, reflecting known physiological subtlety in prodromal states and the small FTD sample (Results figure panel A). Probability correlations between validation strategies decreased accordingly, highlighting reduced stability under domain shift. In the CN–MCI distinction (within–cross ρ ≈ 0.34; multi‑cross ρ ≈ 0.08, n.s.) and in the FTD–AD comparison (within–cross ρ ≈ 0.23; multi‑cross ρ ≈ –0.24). These declines were paralleled by weaker correlations with neuropsychological measures in these contrasts (Results figure panel B)

SHAP analyses revealed that the most influential features—alpha and beta reductions, increased delta/theta activity, lower spectral entropy, and reduced permutation entropy—were highly consistent across validation tiers and aligned with well-established electrophysiological signatures of AD.

Conclusion

This multicenter study demonstrates that EEG markers of spectral slowing, reduced complexity, and connectivity disruption enable robust and reproducible discrimination of Alzheimer’s disease across diverse populations and acquisition systems. High correlations in predicted probabilities across validation tiers, strong associations with cognitive impairment, and coherent SHAP-derived feature relevance highlight the stability, interpretability, and clinical meaningfulness of these biomarkers. In contrast, MCI detection and subtype differentiation remain challenging, emphasizing the need for larger samples and harmonization strategies. Overall, these findings support EEG-based machine learning as a feasible and cost-effective approach for dementia screening, particularly in resource-limited regions.
