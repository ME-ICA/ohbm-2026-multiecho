Introduction

Cerebral Small Vessel Disease (CSVD) is a chronic, progressive condition affecting the brain's small blood vessels.1 White Matter Hyperintensities (WMH) are the imaging biomarker of CSVD and critical predictors of cognitive decline, dementia, and stroke.2,3 However, WMH exhibit significant morphological heterogeneity, ranging from large confluent regions to small, discrete punctate lesions.Accurate segmentation remains challenging due to high inter-rater variability in manual assessment and incomplete detection by intensity-based segmentation methods.4 Therefore, automated quantification of WMH is crucial for assessing CSVD severity and monitoring disease progression.4 Deep Learning (DL) has achieved state-of-the-art performance in medical image segmentation.4

However, translating this to routine WMH quantification presents three challenges.First, clinical practice often lacks complete multi-modal data, with patients having only T1-weighted or T2-FLAIR scans available.Second, the impact of preprocessing across different imaging modalities on DL training performance requires systematic evaluation.Third, deploying 3D MRI models in resource-constrained settings presents computational barriers.Thus, developing a framework that addresses modality variability, preprocessing optimization, and computational efficiency is essential for clinical deployment.This study aims to establish an automated WMH segmentation framework for clinical CSVD assessment.We systematically evaluated segmentation performance across three modality configurations (T1-weighted alone, T2-FLAIR alone, and T1–FLAIR fusion) and two preprocessing strategies, using a patch-based sliding window approach to enable high-resolution processing while maintaining computational feasibility.This systematic evaluation provides practical guidance on modality and preprocessing choices that balance segmentation accuracy with routine clinical implementation.

Methods

This study included 1,240 participants (age range: 49.6–92.7; males: 584; females: 656) with available T1-weighted and FLAIR-weighted MRI scans. The cohort was stratified into two main groups: a training dataset (n=100) and a large-scale test dataset (n=1,140). The training dataset was further partitioned into a training set (n=80) and a validation set (n=20), both with ground truth labels manually delineated by an experienced neurologist. The test dataset was utilized to assess model scalability, where reference labels were automatically generated using the Lesion Segmentation Tool (LST) based on SPM, a widely established method for WMH segmentation.

All MRI images underwent a standardized preprocessing pipeline. First, skull stripping of T1-weighted images was performed using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs), producing both skull-stripped images and corresponding brain masks. FLAIR-weighted images were registered to the T1-weighted space and subsequently skull-stripped by applying the T1-derived brain mask through binary multiplication. Second, all skull-stripped images underwent Z-score intensity normalization to standardize image intensities across the cohort. To systematically evaluate the impact of preprocessing, we designed a factorial experiment involving two processing levels: (1) Raw images, which included only registration without skull stripping or intensity normalization, and (2) Preprocessed images, which included skull stripping, registration, and Z-score normalization. Each image modality set was then combined into three input configurations: T1-weighted alone, FLAIR-weighted alone, and combined T1-FLAIR fusion. This resulted in six distinct model training scenarios: three with raw images and three with preprocessed images.

We employed the 3D SegResNet architecture implemented in MONAI (Medical Open Network for AI)5, an open-source PyTorch-based framework. To address GPU memory limitations while preserving high spatial resolution for detecting small WMH lesions, we utilized a patch-based training strategy with patch size of 96×96×96 voxels. Six independent models were trained corresponding to the six image configurations described above. Model training utilized the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 10-4 without any learning rate scheduler. The hybrid loss function combined Dice Loss and Focal Loss, minimizing over 180 epochs with a batch size of 1. Training was performed on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU 12 GB VRAM, CUDA 12.8.

Model performance was evaluated using three complementary metrics: (1) Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) for voxel-level overlap; (2) 95th Percentile Hausdorff Distance (95HD) for boundary precision; and (3) a Lesion-wise F1-Score for detection-level accuracy. Performance was primarily assessed on the validation set against manual gold-standard labels to determine segmentation accuracy. Subsequently, the final models were deployed on the test dataset using LST-generated labels as the reference standard to evaluate large-scale clinical feasibility.

Results

The results from the validation set of the T1-only, FLAIR-only, and T1-FLAIR fusion models on raw and preprocessed image data are summarized here. As illustrated across all configurations in Figure 2A, the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) value ranged from 0.3 to 0.9, 95th Percentile Hausdorff Distance (95HD) values from 25 to 1 mm, and Lesion-based F1-Scores from 0.2 to 0.85. Overall, models trained on preprocessed data consistently outperformed their raw counterparts across all metrics. Among the preprocessed configurations, the T1-FLAIR fusion model demonstrated superior segmentation accuracy, achieving a DSC of 0.86, 95HD of 1.5 mm, and F1-Score of 0.70. This performance was comparable to the FLAIR-only model (DSC: 0.83; 95HD: 3.0 mm; F1: 0.65), whereas the T1-only model yielded significantly lower results (DSC: 0.75; 95HD: 12.0 mm; F1: 0.5). Visual examples of the segmentation quality on the validation set are provided in Figure 2B.

In the large-scale test dataset, DSC values ranged from 0.50 to 0.80, 95HD values between 2 and 20 mm, and Lesion-based F1-Scores from 0.50 to 0.75 across all configurations. Models utilizing preprocessed inputs consistently outperformed those using raw images, showing clear improvements in both volumetric overlap (DSC: 0.60 vs. 0.50) and boundary precision (95HD: 5.0 vs. 7.8 mm). Among the preprocessed configurations, the T1-FLAIR fusion model maintained the highest relative performance with a DSC of 0.70, 95HD of 3.5, and F1-Score of 0.68, surpassing both the FLAIR-only (DSC: 0.65; 95HD: 4.5 mm; F1: 0.62) and T1-only (DSC: 0.50; 95HD: 7.5 mm; F1: 0.48) models. As can be observed in Figure 2D, mislabeled non-WMH hyperintensities, such as meningeal artifacts, which our model correctly excluded.

Conclusion

By systematically evaluating a 3D SegResNet framework integrated with a patch-based sliding window strategy, our study overcame memory constraints to preserve the detection of small, discrete lesions. Our results demonstrated that while the T1-FLAIR fusion strategy achieved optimal segmentation precision, the model exhibited remarkable robustness against label inconsistencies. Furthermore, our findings indicated that optimized single-modality models, even those utilizing T1-weighted images alone, demonstrated potential utility as surrogates for estimating WMH burden. These findings highlight the versatility of the proposed method, offering high-precision tools for individual clinical assessment and scalable solutions for CSVD burden quantification.
