Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease that poses a significant challenge to global health, profoundly impacting individuals, families, and healthcare systems. Early detection of AD is crucial, as it allows for timely interventions that could slow disease progression and improve patient outcomes. The US Food & Drug Administration recently granted traditional approval for lecanemab and donanemab for treating mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and mild AD in patients with amyloid-beta (Aβ) proteinopathy or pathologic tau deposition. The advent of these novel immunotherapies highlights the need for cost-effective and time-efficient biomarkers for early diagnosis to enhance treatment effectiveness. Traditionally, AD diagnosis has relied on observing cognitive decline, which typically occurs in later stages, often missing the critical window for effective intervention. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) could provide a non-invasive and more accessible substitute (compared to PET and CSF/plasma tests). In addition, the advent of deep learning methods has revolutionized MRI’s potential by enabling the detection and use of subtle features previously unrecognizable by human experts, thus advancing the field towards progression trajectory prediction and facilitating timely medical treatment. Besides, interpretable models have provided initial insights into the “black box” of deep learning, potentially enhancing the confidence of physicians and patients in medical imaging models applied to clinical practice.

In this study, we employ an interpretability approach based on a deep learning model to compute individualized AD risk maps, which will be used to investigate divergent trajectories of Alzheimer’s disease progression.

Methods

We included participants from the Sino Longitudinal Study on Cognitive Decline (SILCODE)1, a registered ongoing longitudinal clinical trial focused on AD in the elderly Han population in mainland China. 1105 scans from 722 participants (60.8% female; mean age = 66.1 ± 7.7 years at baseline) passed quality control. Diagnostic distribution at baseline was: 211 normal cognition (NC), 235 subjective cognitive decline (SCD), 193 MCI, and 83 AD. Grey matter density (GMD) and grey matter volume (GMV) maps derived from T1-weighted MRI were generated using the voxel-based morphometry (VBM) module in DPABI2. 

We developed an interpretable approach that enables individualized identification of brain regions at risk for AD3. Overall, we constructed a neutral reference brain template and applied regional perturbation to estimate each region’s contribution to AD risk for an individual patient. 

First, we constructed a reference brain template whose predicted AD probability was approximately 0.5, representing an intermediate state between typical AD (risk score = 1) and NC (risk score = 0). To obtain this template, we performed 10,000 random samplings from AD and NC participants in the ADNI dataset. In each sampling, 100 participants were randomly selected, and their GMD and GMV maps were averaged to form candidate templates. The pre-trained MRI-based AD classifier was then applied to estimate the AD risk score for each candidate, and the template whose predicted score was closest to 0.5 (0.499928 in the current study) was chosen as the final reference.

Next, we performed a regional perturbation analysis to estimate the contribution of each brain region to the AD risk of an individual participant. Starting from the reference template, we sequentially replaced one region at a time with the participant’s own GMD and GMV values. Each region was defined as a cube with an edge length of 18 mm, moved across the brain with a step size of 12 mm. For every replacement, the modified image was input into the classifier to obtain a new AD risk score. Repeating this procedure across all regions and reprojecting the resulting scores back onto the brain yielded an individualized AD brain risk map for each subject.

Subsequently, we used the individualized AD brain risk maps as features and applied the SuStaIn algorithm to derive subgroups characterized by distinct disease-progression trajectories4. We first extracted 13 cortical/subcortical regions of interest (ROIs) from the risk maps using the Harvard–Oxford atlases. For each subject, mean risk values within each ROI were computed, and baseline scans were identified by selecting the earliest available session per individual. We restricted the dataset to high-quality AD, MCI, and NC baseline scans and computed ROI-wise z-scores relative to the NC distribution.

Following the SuStaIn framework (Young et al., 2018), we modeled disease progression as a sequence of discrete z-score events. For each biomarker, the number of z-score levels (2 or 3) and the corresponding maximum attainable z-score (3 or 5) were determined empirically based on the distribution of extreme values across subjects, consistent with the original SuStaIn recommendations. These biomarker event sequences were used as input to the SuStaIn model. We ran SuStaIn using 25 random start points, a maximum of four candidate subtypes, and 100,000 MCMC iterations under the exact-likelihood formulation. Model selection was based on the maximum log-likelihood and stability across MCMC samples, yielding a three-subtype solution. For each individual, subtype and disease stage were assigned using maximum-likelihood estimates computed across the posterior subtype–stage distributions.

Finally, the learned subtype-specific progression sequences were mapped onto the Desikan–Killiany template for visualization using BrainPainter, enabling anatomical illustration of biomarker evolution across SuStaIn stages.

Results

Using individualized brain risk maps as inputs, SuStaIn identified three distinct Alzheimer’s disease progression subtypes—a Typical subtype (f = 0.50), a Frontal subtype (f = 0.26), and a Subcortical subtype (f = 0.24). The three subtypes share a common feature: elevations in hippocampal and amygdalar risk scores consistently precede abnormalities in nearly all other brain regions. However, each subtype also exhibits distinct progression characteristics, as described below.

The Typical subtype exhibited the canonical AD-like progression pattern. Abnormality first emerged in the medial temporal structures (hippocampus, amygdala). Later stages showed widespread neocortical abnormalities. This subtype represents the majority of participants.

The Frontal subtype showed an early and predominant involvement of the frontal lobe, with extensive and rapidly progressing frontal cortical abnormalities appearing from the earliest stages. Subcortical structures became abnormal only at mid-to-later stages. Compared with the Typical subtype, this pattern highlights a clear frontal-dominant pathway, suggesting an executive-related variant of AD progression.

The Subcortical subtype was characterized by early subcortical involvement, with the accumbens, pallidum, and putamen reaching their highest levels of abnormality earlier. Abnormalities in the frontal lobe emerged substantially later than in the other subtypes.

Conclusion

The subtypes and progression trajectories derived from individualized AD brain risk maps demonstrate preliminary biological plausibility. However, substantial validation and exploratory analyses remain necessary. 

First, an optimal feature-selection strategy should be established, particularly to identify brain regions most strongly associated with AD progression. Second, longitudinal data should be used to verify whether follow-up MRI sessions align with and support the predicted trajectories. Third, differences in clinical profiles and cognitive measures across subtypes need to be systematically examined. Finally, the prevalence of APOE-ε4 carriers should be compared among subtypes to explore genetic distinctions underlying these progression patterns.
