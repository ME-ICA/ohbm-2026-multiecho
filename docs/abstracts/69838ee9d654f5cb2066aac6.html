Introduction

Hallucination mechanisms in schizophrenia are difficult to isolate due to disease

heterogeneity and compensatory factors. Several network hypotheses implicate:

(1) disrupted default mode (DMN), executive (EN), and salience network (SN)

interactions; (2) aberrant hippocampal-cortical connectivity driving memory in-

trusions; and (3) altered thalamocortical gating. The hippocampus serves as a

central hub processing sensory input and projecting to prefrontal regions, with

evidence suggesting it precedes hallucinatory experiences. The dorsolateral pre-

frontal cortex (dlPFC), part of the EN, filters irrelevant information and shows

hypoconnectivity with DMN regions in schizophrenia. Thalamic gating dysfunc-

tion is thought to drive altered perception, with hyperconnectivity to sensory

regions and hypoconnectivity with prefrontal cortex. Psilocybin has the poten-

tial to offer a controlled pharmacological model, producing visual and auditory

hallucinations with mechanistic similarities to psychosis.1 Here, we test these

network hypotheses using spectral dynamic causal modeling (spDCM) to esti-

mate effective connectivity changes during psilocybin administration across con-

ditions emphasizing different modalities.2 We assess how connectivity changes

relate to subjective hallucinatory experiences, aiming to identify effective con-

nectivity differences underlying psilocybin-induced hallucinations

Methods

Sixty healthy, psychedelic-naive participants (aged 18-55) completed an open-

label, baseline-controlled study.3 Baseline and psilocybin (19mg oral) sessions

occurred two weeks apart. Approximately 80 minutes post-dose, participants

underwent fMRI during four conditions: eyes-closed rest, guided meditation,

ethereal music listening, and passive movie watching (silent moving clouds).

Subjective experiences were assessed using the 11-Dimensional Altered States

of Consciousness (11D-ASC) questionnaire 360 minutes post-dose. A composite

of audiovisual, complex, and perceptual subscales indexed overall hallucinatory

experience.

Bilateral regions of interest were specified using AAL atlas coordinates: dor-

solateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC / frontal mid), hippocampus (Hipp), superior

occipital gyrus (SOG / occipital sup), medial temporal gyrus (MTG / tempo-

ral mid), and thalamus (Thal). A fully-connected spDCM model without ex-

ogenous inputs was specified for each subject and session. SpDCM estimates

effective connectivity (cross-spectral density) capturing the causal influence one

region exerts over another.

Second-level analyses used Parametric Empirical Bayes (PEB) to test group-

level effects. For each condition, we assessed: (1) pre- vs. post-psilocybin

connectivity changes; (2) behavioral associations between post-dose connec-

tivity and 11D-ASC hallucinatory subscales. Behavioral association models

were constrained to connections showing significant psilocybin-induced changes.

Bayesian model averaging identified parameters with strong evidence (posterior

probability > 0.99). Connectivity is reported in Hz; behavioral associations as

normalized beta coefficients.

Results

Psilocybin induced widespread connectivity changes with a consistent lateral-

ized hippocampal pattern across conditions. During rest, the left hippocam-

pus broadly inhibited contralateral sensory regions (lHipp→rSOG: −0.15 Hz;

lHipp→rMTG: −0.16 Hz) and thalamus (lHipp→rThal: −0.13 Hz). In contrast,

the right hippocampus shifted toward excitation, with rHipp→lMTG inhibition

weakening (from −0.21 to −0.09 Hz) and rHipp→rdlPFC becoming excitatory

(+0.10 Hz). This asymmetry—left hippocampal inhibition, right hippocampal

excitation—persisted across rest and meditation conditions.



Behavioral associations revealed left hippocampal output as the primary cor-

relate of hallucination severity. During rest, lHipp inhibition of rSOG (β=0.12),

rMTG (β=0.08), and rThal (β=0.16) each positively correlated with 11D-ASC

hallucinatory subscales. The strongest association occurred during movie view-

ing, where lHipp→lThal inhibition (−0.13 Hz) predicted symptoms (β=0.20).



Thalamic contributions showed an unexpected inverse pattern. Sensory-

driven thalamic inhibition was inversely related to hallucinations: lSOG→lThal

(β=−0.08) and lMTG→lThal (β=−0.08) during movie. Thalamic excitation

also inversely correlated with symptoms (β=−0.11). However, thalamic out-

puts to dlPFC showed positive associations—rThal→ldlPFC excitation (+0.08

Hz) correlated with symptoms (β=0.09) during movie, and lThal→rdlPFC in-

hibition (−0.08 Hz) correlated during rest (β=0.10).



The dlPFC displayed asymmetric top-down modulation. During rest, ldlPFC

inhibited contralateral hippocampus (−0.12 Hz), SOG (−0.07 Hz), and MTG

(−0.13 Hz), while rdlPFC excited these same regions (+0.12, +0.11, +0.15 Hz

respectively). Critically, ldlPFC→rHipp inhibition inversely correlated with hal-

lucinations (β=−0.08), suggesting intact prefrontal control reduces symptoms.

During movie, dlPFC cross-excitation increased (R→L: +0.17 Hz change; L→R:

+0.12 Hz from n.s.)



Condition-specific patterns emerged in sensory regions. During music, tem-

poral cortex became broadly inhibitory, with lHipp→contralateral MTG inhi-

bition correlating with symptoms. Auditory cortex excitation of right dlPFC

(rMTG→rdlPFC: +0.08 Hz) correlated with hallucinations during rest (β=0.10),

potentially indicating auditory-prefrontal coupling in the absence of external

input. During meditation, thalamic excitation of left hippocampus—unique to

this condition—correlated with symptoms, suggesting internally-directed states

engage distinct thalamo-hippocampal dynamics.

Conclusion

This study reveals condition-dependent effective connectivity patterns under-

lying psilocybin-induced hallucinations in regions implicated in schizophrenia.

The hippocampus emerges as a central hub, with lateralized effects: left hip-

pocampal inhibition of sensory cortices and thalamus consistently predicted

hallucination severity, supporting the ”memory-driven effect” hypothesis. Right

hippocampal excitation of dlPFC during auditory stimulation may indicate con-

nections relevant to delusional interpretation of perceptual experiences.4



Contrary to predictions, thalamic influence on frontal regions was inversely

related to symptoms, questioning the broad applicability of thalamic gating

models to psilocybin. Sensory-driven bottom-up inhibition of thalamus showed

inverse correlations with hallucinations, suggesting hippocampal and top-down

modulation better explain symptom severity than disrupted sensory filtering.



The dlPFC showed asymmetric modulation of sensory and memory regions,

with condition-dependent patterns suggesting complex executive network in-

volvement. These findings provide mechanistic insights into hallucination net-

works using a controlled pharmacological model, highlighting the importance

of hippocampal-cortical interactions and challenging purely thalamocortical ac-

counts of altered perception.
