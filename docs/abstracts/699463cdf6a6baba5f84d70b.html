Introduction

We aim to investigate the change of the causal brain network during resting state after exposure to humorous films. We have previously shown that exposure to humorous films as positive emotions stimuli (humorous-neutral film paradigm, HNM), induces overall increased brain activity and enhanced functional connectivity within the emotion-reward network during the exposure phase (HNM) 1.

Here we use the same data set including a) resting state condition prior to visual-emotional exposure (RS1), b) visual-emotional exposure (HNM), and c) post-exposure resting state (RS2) to address two follow-up questions: 1) do functional changes induced by the visual-emotional stimuli modulate the post-exposure resting state (RS2) causal brain network; 2) are changes in the RS2 causal network limited to the functional network specifically engaged by the stimuli exposure (emotion-reward network), or does the post-exposure causal modulation extend to additional RS2 networks.

By implementing a causal discovery approach, we explore the changes in the brain’s causal graph induced by the visual-emotional exposure, both at the level of the brain network selectively active during the processing of emotional stimuli (amygdala-reward network), and the global causal network. 

Methods

The functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) single-session protocol comprised three sequential acquisitions: firstly a basal brain activity measure (resting state condition RS1), followed by the emotional stimuli composed by standardized Stanford Affective Library short humorous films – 10- to 40-seconds each, for a total of 12 minutes (HNM), and finally a post-exposure brain activity measure (resting state condition RS2). The fMRI protocol was applied to 40 participants (37 females, age 27.7 +/− 8.6 years) (Sanz-Arigita et al., 2021 for additional details). All MRI data were acquired by a MAGNETOM Prisma 3T MR scanner equipped with a 64-channel head-neck coil. The MRI scanning session included a routine sagittal T2-weighted FLAIR to exclude participants with brain structural abnormalities and a sagittal high-resolution T1-weighted magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) for co-registration to the functional images. Three consecutive T2*-weighted echo planar images (EPI) were acquired, corresponding to the conditions of basal resting state (RS1), humorous-neutral film paradigm (HNM), and post-film resting state (RS2). All acquisition parameters, tested to minimize the susceptibility artifact, are common to the three EPI sequences: TR  =  2700 ms; TE  =  27 ms; flip angle  =  82°; voxel size 2.5 mm isotropic, matrix size = 88 × 88 × 42; 282 volumes were acquired in every EPI sequence. In order to correct for magnetic distortion effects, we also acquired field map images using dual echo 2D gradient-echo sequences. The following pre-statistics processing was applied: gradient distortion correction, motion correction, correction of susceptibility-induced distortions, removal of non-brain structures (FSL), regression of the heartbeat and breathing physiological signals, high-pass temporal filtering (Gaussian-weighted least-squares straight line fitting, with sigma  =  90.0 seconds), pre-whitening to remove temporal autocorrelations, mean-based intensity normalization of all volumes by the same factor, and spatial smoothing (4 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel). 

Following the preprocessing, the functional scans were first aligned to the average EPI scan, then to the high-resolution T1-weighted image, which was subsequently registered to the MNI152 standard space using nonlinear registration (FSL). Time courses of the brain activity recorded in RS1 and RS2 were extracted for brain regions defined by the Automated Anatomic Labelling template (AAL; 90 x 11280 time course matrix per subject). We employed the Temporal Causal Discovery Framework (TCDF; PyTorch implementation) to compute subject-wise whole-brain causal graphs, defined by causal directionality and time-lag information, corresponding to the pre-intervention resting state condition RS1 and post-intervention resting state condition RS2. TCDF uses Attention-based Convolutional Neural Networks combined with a causal validation step and outputs a causal graph, including the time delay between cause and effect 2, 3. Finally, we computed graph analysis metrics to describe and compare the RS1 and RS2 causal graphs.

Results

We found differences between RS1 and RS2 both at the level of changes in time-lag between nodes, and the nature of the main hubs composing the causal graphs. While we found no differences between the time-lag in the global causal graph (two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p>0.05), local differences both decrease and increase of causal time-lag, concentrate within Frontal, Precentral and Temporal brain regions, as well as between Frontal-Precentral and Frontal-Temporal causal links (Figure 1a).

Similarly we found no differences in graph analysis descriptors between RS1 and RS2 global causal graphs (small increase in cluster coefficient and decreased path length in RS2 compared to RS1). However, we found a shift in the graphs’ hub composition: While RS1 main connected nodes are mostly located in temporal and parietal lobe regions of the left hemisphere (temporal middle and inferior, parietal superior and inferior parietal regions), RS2 hubs are predominantly frontal on both hemispheres (frontal inferior, middle and orbital regions) (Figure 1b).

Conclusion

We have employed a causal discovery approach to explore causal changes in the brain induced by the visual-emotional exposure, namely watching short humorous films. Preliminary results indicate that, in absence of changes in global graph analysis metrics, the passive stimuli modulate the causal brain graph in resting state condition by changing both its time-dependent configuration and the brain regions most connected within the causal graph. Furthermore, these changes are not exclusive to the brain network primarily modulated during the exposure to the visual-emotional stimuli (amygdala, putamen caudate and insula), rather affecting regions involving frontal lobe regions and cognitive functions other than emotion-reward control, like working memory.
