Introduction

Epilepsy affects nearly 1.0% of the population with over 30% experiencing drug-resistant epilepsy.1 Drug-resistant epilepsy, or failure to achieve sustained seizure freedom after trials of two adequate and well tolerated antiseizure medications, has a significant morbidity and mortality profile. While surgical approaches including resection and ablation provide benefit to some patients, neuromodulation has emerged as an alternative for patients seeking less invasive treatments or have contraindications to surgical intervention. One promising non-invasive approach is transcranial ultrasound (TUS), which uses acoustic sonication to target deep brain structures. For example, our prior work has demonstrated an average seizure reduction ~50% in six patients with mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (mTLE) after TUS.2 However, the network level mechanism underlying this improvement in seizure burden has not been well characterized. Therefore, in this study, our goal is to utilize functional MRI to understand network changes in epilepsy following TUS.

Methods

Five patients (1 female, mean age: 41 years) with drug-resistant mTLE underwent serial TUS treatments targeting the hippocampus. Preprocedural planning included structural imaging using a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (Erlangen, Germany) with a 20-channel head coil: T1w and T2w to exclude structural lesions in the ultrasound path, UTE to exclude bone abnormalities in the temporal acoustic window, and SWI to exclude blood products near the hippocampus. Additionally, a seven-minute resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) sequence (TR = 2000.0 ms, TE = 30.00 ms, 85° flip angle, voxel size: 3.4375 x 3.4375 x 4.0000 mm3, 32 axial slices) was obtained prior to sonication. Four targets in the anterior hippocampus were selected in 4 mm increments from anterior to posterior. Patients underwent six sessions (two per week for three weeks) with sonication targeted through the ipsilateral acoustic temporal window (TUS device specifications3). The ultrasound was delivered perpendicular to the long axis of the ipsilateral hippocampus. For each target, 140 s sonications were performed with the following parameters: f0 = 548 kHz, PRF = 500 Hz, Duty Cycle = 18–50 %, BRP = 7 s, ISPTA = 0.50–1.1 W/cm2, PNP = 0.14–0.42 MPa. All reported intensities are free-field values based on measurements in degassed deionized water at ambient atmospheric pressure and room temperature. An additional seven-minute rs-fMRI was obtained one month following treatment. fMRI was preprocessed using HALFpipe4 (v. 1.3.1) including realignment, slice timing correction, co-registration, skull stripping, normalization to MNI space, spatial smoothing (6.0 mm FWHM), grand mean scaling (10,000 mean), and gaussian filtering (125 s High-Pass). For confound regression, a six-pipeline multiverse analysis was utilized to determine optimal preprocessing (Figure. 1) using MRIQC for quality metrics. Ultimately, parallel pipelines with and without global signal regression were utilized, with both pipelines regressing out motion parameters and signals in the white matter and cerebrospinal fluid. Analyses  and additional preprocessing of fMRI data were performed using CONN5 (RRID:SCR_009550) release 22.v2407 and SPM (RRID:SCR_007037) release 12.7771, including the regression of potential confounding effects characterized by linear trends (2 factors) within each functional run. Group-level independent component analyses (group-ICA) were performed to estimate 40 temporally coherent networks from the fMRI data combined across all subjects and conditions. The blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal from every timepoint and voxel in the brain was concatenated across subjects and conditions along the temporal dimension. A singular value decomposition of the z-score normalized BOLD signal (subject-level SVD) with 64 components separately for each subject and condition was used as a subject-specific dimensionality reduction step. The dimensionality of the concatenated data was further reduced using a singular value decomposition (group-level SVD) with 40 components, and a fast-ICA fixed-point algorithm with hyperbolic tangent (G1) contrast function was used to identify spatially independent group-level networks from the resulting components. Last, GICA3 back-projection was used to compute ICA maps associated with these same networks separately for each individual subject and condition with the default mode network (DMN) component identified based on visual inspection. Given the limited sample size in the preliminary cohort, DMN were analyzed on the level of individual patients.

Results

There were no adverse events, and no side effects reported during or after treatment. No seizures occurred with any participants during treatments. DMN were reliably generated for all five patients both pre- and post-treatment. DMN showed notable changes following TUS, with decreased organization in the bilateral frontal lobe, regardless of seizure lateralization or treatment location (Figure. 2).

Conclusion

We demonstrate patient-level network changes in the default mode network following TUS treatment for drug-resistant epilepsy. These preliminary findings indicate TUS modulation can alter brain network characteristics in resting-state brain activity, indicating a potential mechanism underlying the treatment, as well as evidence of target engagement.
