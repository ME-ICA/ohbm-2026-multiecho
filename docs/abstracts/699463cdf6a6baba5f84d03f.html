Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a global health challenge. By the end of 2024, an estimated 40 million individuals globally were living with HIV, with 1.3 million new diagnoses that year.¹ While combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) has significantly improved outcomes, managing chronic symptoms remains burdensome for many people living with HIV, including pain, anxiety, depression, appetite loss, nausea, and insomnia.² To mitigate these symptoms, people living with HIV frequently use cannabis as self-medication.³ However, the neural and cognitive implications of comorbid HIV and cannabis use remain poorly understood. HIV infection is associated with neurocognitive impacts including executive functioning deficits such as impaired decision-making, increased impulsivity, and reduced cognitive flexibility, which persist even with viral suppression through cART.⁴ Similarly, chronic cannabis use has been linked to alterations in the striatum, a key component of the brain's reward network crucial for reinforcement learning and decision-making.⁵ The striatum's role in both HIV-related neurocognitive impairment and cannabis-related functional changes makes it critical for understanding how these conditions may interact. While both HIV and cannabis use independently affect striatal function, whether their co-occurrence produces distinct connectivity patterns or whether striatal connectivity relates to neurobehavioral performance across these populations remains unclear.

Methods

A total of 73 participants were stratified into four groups based on HIV serostatus and chronic cannabis use (HIV+/CB+, HIV+/CB-, HIV-/CB+, HIV-/CB-). Participants completed neuropsychological testing and MRI scanning. The Iowa Gambling Task (IGT) assessed decision-making through selecting from four decks to maximize gains, and performance was quantified as net score: advantageous minus disadvantageous deck selections. The Grooved Pegboard Test (GP) assessed fine motor dexterity with both hands. Resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) imaging was conducted in one 8-minute scan with eyes closed (245 volumes, TR=2000ms, TE=30ms, 2.3mm isotropic voxels), with denoising using anatomical CompCor to regress physiological signals, motion parameters, and framewise displacement (0.35mm threshold). Bilateral putamen and nucleus accumbens (NAcc) regions from the Oxford Structural Atlas were used as seeds to examine dorsal and ventral striatal connectivity, respectively. Whole-brain connectivity maps were generated using AFNI, producing Fisher z-transformed correlation maps. One-sample t-tests identified brain regions showing significant positive rsFC with each striatal seed region, with results corrected using cluster-based thresholding (voxel-wise p<0.001, cluster-corrected p<0.05; Figure 1). To examine brain-behavior relationships and test for group differences, we used linear regression models in R: Outcome = β₀ + β₁(Striatal rsFC) + β₂(Group) + β₃(Striatal rsFC × Group) + ε. Significant interactions were followed by group-specific analyses. 

Results

To characterize baseline striatal rsFC, we conducted seed-based analyses using bilateral putamen and NAcc from the Oxford Structural Atlas. One-sample t-tests revealed each seed showed significant positive rsFC with distributed networks (voxel-wise p<0.001, cluster-corrected p<0.05). Putamen (dorsal striatal) rsFC encompassed motor/premotor cortex, somatosensory cortex, insula, and cingulate, consistent with motor control functions. NAcc (ventral striatal) rsFC included prefrontal and limbic structures, aligning with reward processing. To examine brain-behavior relationships, we assessed associations between striatal rsFC strength and neurobehavioral performance (Figure 2). Ventral striatal (NAcc) rsFC showed a marginal positive association with IGT performance (β=6.78, p=0.098; Figure 2a), suggesting stronger ventral striatal rsFC relates to better decision-making, though this did not reach statistical significance. Ventral striatal rsFC was not significantly associated with GPD performance (β=3.97, p=0.49; Figure 2b). Dorsal striatal (putamen) rsFC showed no significant association with either IGT (β=7.17, p=0.19, Figure 2c) or GPD performance (β=1.96, p=0.80, Figure 2d). Contrary to hypotheses, no significant Group×rsFC interactions emerged for any striatal seed (all p>0.05), indicating associations between striatal rsFC and neurobehavioral performance did not differ across groups.

Conclusion

Baseline rsFC patterns for putamen and NAcc were established, with each seed demonstrating connectivity to functionally specialized networks consistent with their established roles in motor function and reward processing, respectively. The marginal positive association between ventral striatal (NAcc) rsFC and IGT performance across all participants aligns with the ventral striatum's known role in value-based decision-making. Importantly, contrary to hypotheses, no significant group differences or Group×rsFC interactions emerged, suggesting that in virally suppressed individuals, HIV serostatus and cannabis use may not substantially alter striatal rsFC patterns or their relationships with neurobehavioral performance. These findings provide insight into potential protective effects of viral suppression on subcortical network integrity and suggest that striatal connectivity remains relatively preserved across these populations when HIV is well-controlled. Future work will examine whether symptom severity, immune markers, and duration of HIV infection or cannabis use relate to individual differences in striatal rsFC and neurobehavioral performance.
