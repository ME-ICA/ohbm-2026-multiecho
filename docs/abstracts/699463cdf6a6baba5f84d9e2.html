Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a heterogeneous psychiatric condition associated with increased morbidity and mortality with respect to the general population, particularly due to concurrent cardiometabolic diseases1. This elevated risk and its pathophysiology are difficult to disentangle, as MDD encompasses substantial variability in its clinical presentations and neurobiological correlates, spanning brain structure, white-matter microstructure, functional activation, and inherited genetic liability2. A data-driven stratification may therefore uncover biologically grounded MDD subtypes that differ in vulnerability to medical complications and, ultimately, may inform tailored interventions3. In this study, we combined multimodal neuroimaging and polygenic scores (PGSs) with unsupervised and supervised machine-learning methods to (i) identify neurobiologically-informed subgroups of individuals with lifetime MDD and (ii) determine which features could mainly contribute to cardiometabolic comorbidities within each subtype. By integrating structural, microstructural, and functional brain data with genetic information, we aimed to characterize distinct biological profiles that may confer differential risk for cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cognitive dysfunction.

Methods

The sample comprised 3,887 individuals with lifetime MDD from the UK Biobank. All participants underwent multimodal neuroimaging acquisition and blood sampling, and data were pre-processed using standard UK Biobank pipelines4. Extracted neuroimaging features included regional cortical thickness, surface area, grey matter volumes, fractional anisotropy (FA), axial diffusivity (AD), radial diffusivity (RD), positive (Spos) and negative (Sneg) strengths of functional connectivity between resting-state networks, and task-based brain activation during emotional and neutral stimuli. Genotype data were used to derive PGSs for psychiatric, medical, and inflammatory traits using LDpred2-auto. A consensus clustering framework5 was applied to identify homogeneous patient subgroups. K-means clustering was iterated 1,000 times, with each iteration sampling 80% of participants, generating consensus matrices for cluster solutions ranging from k=2 to k=5. The optimal solution was defined by maximal internal validity (i.e., based on Silhouette and Davies-Bouldin scores). Cluster significance was assessed using 10,000 Monte-Carlo simulations with the sigclust R package. Between-cluster differences in input features and clinical variables of interest, including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cognitive performance, were tested using Wilcoxon rank-sum and χ² tests, adjusting comparisons for False Discovery Rate. Within each cluster, nested cross-validated elastic-net models were trained to predict cardiovascular diseases and diabetes, applying a sub-sampling procedure to account for class imbalance. Statistical significance of these models was evaluated via 1,000 permutations, and mean weights of features with confidence intervals were estimated through 5,000 non-parametric bootstrap iterations.

Results

The optimal and significant clustering solution consisted of two clusters (Cluster0: N=2,030; Cluster1: N=1,857; p<0.001). Cluster1 was characterized by larger surface areas (q<0.05), widespread reductions in cortical thickness (q<0.001), cortical and subcortical volumes (q<0.001), FA (q<0.001), and higher AD (q<0.001) and RD (q<0.001) in all white matter tracts (Figure 1A) with respect to Cluster0. Additionally, it showed reduced emotional-task activation (q<0.05), decreased Spos and Sneg connectivity strength across most networks (q<0.05), and higher PGSs for MIF (q=0.047) and RANTES (q=0.046). From a clinical perspective, Cluster1 exhibited higher rates of cardiovascular diseases (q=0.013) and diabetes (q=0.021), as well as lower motor (q=0.015) and processing (q=0.030) speed performance as compared to Cluster0. Significant elastic-net models for both cardiovascular disease and diabetes in each cluster (q=0.009-0.047) highlighted distinct significant feature contributions across clusters. In predicting diabetes (Figure 2B), the PGS for type 2 diabetes (T2D) was a significant positive contributor in both clusters; however, diffusivity measures (i.e., AD and FA) showed the strongest negative associations in Cluster0, whereas grey-matter metrics (including lateral orbitofrontal volume and area, and thickness of pars opercularis, inferior parietal, superior frontal, and caudal middle frontal cortices) were most influential in Cluster1. For cardiovascular disease prediction (Figure 2C), multimodal features contributed significantly across both clusters, though Cluster1 displayed stronger genetic influences and a broader set of implicated brain regions. For instance, the T2D PGS was positively associated with cardiovascular diseases in both clusters, while PGSs for interleukin-16 and triglycerides were significant only in Cluster1. AD in the external capsule contributed positively in both clusters, whereas AD and RD in the corpus callosum and superior corona radiata were influential solely in Cluster1. Finally, resting-state connectivity most contributed negatively across clusters, with more networks being involved in Cluster1.

Conclusion

Our data-driven framework demonstrates that MDD can be stratified into two neurobiologically distinct subtypes that differ in cardiometabolic vulnerability. One subtype showed convergent cortical thinning, diffuse white-matter alterations, reduced resting-state and affective activation, and elevated polygenic liability for metabolic-inflammatory traits, paralleled by increased risk for cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and poorer cognitive performance. This profile is consistent with immuno-metabolic models of depression, positing an inflammatory and metabolic dysregulated subtype with heightened somatic burden and atypical neurovegetative symptoms. Importantly, cardiometabolic risk pathways differed across clusters: diabetes was primarily associated with grey-matter integrity in the more compromised cluster, whereas diffusivity measures predominated in the other. These findings refine our understanding of MDD neurobiology, highlight subtype-specific mechanisms linking depression to adverse medical outcomes, and support the development of precision psychiatry approaches aimed at identifying individuals most likely to benefit from targeted preventive or therapeutic strategies.
