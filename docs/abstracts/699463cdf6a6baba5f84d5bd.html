Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder marked by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra. The primary treatment for PD is levodopa (L-Dopa) administration; however, long-term use often leads to motor complications, prompting the exploration of alternative therapeutic approaches. One such approach is Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS), a neuromodulatory intervention that delivers targeted electrical stimulation—typically to the subthalamic nucleus (STN). However, both approaches have a margin of error, for example, determining the correct dosage of the drug, which may cause side effects, or choosing the stimulation parameters (e.g. amplitude, frequency, pulse width, relative distribution of currents across contacts, timing of stimulation concerning ongoing activity). The risk of not selecting the optimal stimulation parameters is high. Current tuning practices rely on a trial-and-error process, which is time-consuming, physically demanding for the patient, requires a specialized clinical team, and often fails to achieve the optimal configuration.

Methods

To predict the effects of DBS on large-scale brain dynamics, we employed a mean-field neural mass model based on the adaptive quadratic integrate-and-fire (aQIF) framework [1], explicitly incorporating dopamine modulation. The model was extended to include an external current that simulates biphasic electrical stimulation, mimicking DBS effects. Each brain region was represented as a neural mass, interconnected according to individual structural connectomes, and included excitatory, inhibitory, and neuromodulatory couplings. Model predictions were validated using EEG and deep electrode recordings from the STN. Furthermore, a Bayesian inversion framework utilizing a deep neural network (DNN) was implemented to infer neural states under ON/OFF stimulation conditions, while quantifying parameter uncertainty.

Results

We first examined simulations of L-Dopa administration, followed by systematic variation of DBS parameters, to assess their impact on large-scale brain dynamics and the topological properties of neural avalanches (spontaneous bursts of neuronal activation). Across all patients, the inferred dopaminergic tone was consistently higher following L-Dopa administration and lower before treatment [2]. The same modeling framework successfully distinguished pre- and post-DBS states in multiple patients, quantifying how stimulation influences large-scale brain activity.

Conclusion

This work presents a computational framework for understanding how L-Dopa and DBS modulate large-scale neural dynamics in Parkinson’s disease, providing mechanistic insights and supporting optimization of individualized therapies. Our work is complementary to most modelling literature in PD [3], which primarily focuses on the simulation of activities in the beta range. We focus on aperiodic activities instead, which have received only limited attention in Parkinson’s disease thus far. Furthermore, we focus on large-scale dynamics and efficient parameter estimation with the associated uncertainty, rather than directly utilizing a cost function to fit the model. Other modeling works in PD, for example in the case of the Virtual Deep Brain Stimulation, took a multi-scale simulation approach to simulate the effects of deep brain stimulation [4]. However, the scope of our model is different, as we provide an explicit account of the concentrations of Dopamine and the amplitude of stimulus, which brings us closer to the pathophysiological mechanisms occurring in PD. In other words, our model explicitly encapsulates physiological knowledge about the role of Dopamine and its effects on neural activities. Finally, our model aims to describe the whole brain, unlike previous works which represented the cortex as a single node.
