Introduction

Functional connectivity (FC) is a core tool for probing interactions among brain regions and how those interactions evolve over time1. While widely used correlation-based FC measures1 summarize synchrony, they provide limited access to directed influences2 and can overlook subtle localized-time changes2,3. As time-varying FC analyses become increasingly common, there is a need for approaches that simultaneously quantify when directed interactions occur, how long they persist, and how strongly information is conveyed.

We introduce a time-resolved framework that combines sliding-window estimation with a linear time-invariant causal model and an information-theoretic characterization. Building on the Sliding-Window Prediction Correlation (SWpC) formulation recently developed4,5, here we introduce channel capacity as a novel biomarker of directed communication—an information-theoretic metric that quantifies the maximum reliable rate of information transfer. We evaluate reliability using resting-state calcium/LFP-fMRI in rodents and assess task sensitivity in human motor-task fMRI by characterizing directed interactions and their time-varying capacity.

Methods

Data. Three datasets are used to validate our proposed methodology. The first dataset is 22 scans of concurrent local field potential (LFP) and fMRI recordings in 10 resting-state rats. The second dataset is 86 scans of concurrent calcium imaging and fMRI recordings in 9 resting-state mice. The third dataset from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) is the fMRI recordings of 45 subjects either in a resting state or conducting a movement with either hand or either foot.  

The resting-state signals are divided into overlapping 50-second windows. Connection duration5, p-corr strength5, and channel capacity are calculated as on each window for each directed ROI pair applicable to the dataset. Then the results are averaged across all the windows and all the scans.

Channel model. We model the communication channel as a finite impulse response (FIR) filter with a stationary additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) independent to the input. The mathematical relation between the input and output signals is yt = b0 xt + ... + bk-1 xt-k+1 + wt (1). This is the simplest model that is both dynamic and stochastic. The model is applied to any directed pair of signals in a window of fixed length as illustrated in Fig 1.

Parameter identification. This channel model has (k + 2) parameters, consisting of k coefficients of the impulse response b, the noise mean μ, and the noise variance σ2. Given a pair of observed input and output timeseries x and y, Eq (1) can be written as a linear regression model y = X b + w where X is the convolution matrix of x. The vector y and each column of X are z-scored so there is no need to include an intercept term and μ becomes zero. In functional connectivity analysis, we typically focus only on positive influence between different ROIs, so we impose the constraint b ≥ 0. The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of b is the solution to the non-negative least squares (NNLS) b* ∈ arg max b ≥ 0 ||X b − y||2, and can be obtained through a standard solver (such as lsqnonneg in MATLAB). The MLE of σ2 is the residual sum of squares (RSS) per observation s2 = ||X b* − y||2 / n. P-corr strength is defined as the correlation between the observed and predicted output, and can be calculated as √ (1 − s2).

Model selection. We use the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) to identify the model order k and prevent overfitting. In our linear Gaussian model, the BIC can be calculated as (n ln s2 + k ln n). It represents a balance between the fit (s2) and number of parameters (k). The value of k that minimizes the BIC is chosen as the model order and considered as the connection duration. In the HCP data, the available time sequences are relatively short, so instead of the BIC, we use corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc).  

Channel capacity. Thanks to the simplicity of the proposed channel model, the channel capacity can be easily estimated using the water filling algorithm. Let S(ω) = s2 / |H(ω)|2 be the power spectrum density (PSD) of the equivalent input noise (EIN) where H(ω) = Σn=0k−1 b*n exp(− j n ω) is the frequency response of the FIR filter. Then the channel capacity is given by C = ∫0π (log(a / S(ω)))+ dω where (z)+ denotes the rectifier (z)+ = max {0, z}, and a is a constant (“water level”) determined by the power constraint of the input signal: ∫0π (a − S(ω))+ dω = ½. Numerically, H(ω) is calculated on equally spaced values between 0 and π using fast Fourier transform (FFT) with zero-padding, and the integrals are replaced by sums over these values.  

Results

BOLD and LFP. The LFP data are recorded using two electrodes symmetrically placed on the two hemispheres, which allows us to verify the connectivity symmetry between two hemispheres. The results show that, in BOLD and each frequency band of the LFP, the median directional asymmetry is significantly (p<0.005) smaller than the scan variability, in all three measures (duration, p-corr strength, and channel capacity). Fig 2A plots the bootstrap correlations between BOLD and each frequency band of the LFP using these three measures respectively. The bootstrap correlations calculated using channel capacity have similar mean as the bootstrap correlations calculated using p-corr strength but have slightly smaller variances. These two tests show that channel capacity provides results consistent with expectations.

BOLD and calcium imaging. This dataset has good spatial resolution which allows us to map the data into 60 ROIs and demonstrate the scalability of the channel capacity with data size. Fig 2B compares the connectivity maps between BOLD and calcium imaging using three measures respectively. The histogram of connectivity values in each connectivity map is also plotted next to it. We can see that channel capacity follows the same pattern as the p-corr strength. In addition, channel capacity always gives a positive value even when p-corr is clipped to zero.

BOLD under motor tasks. The final validation test of channel capacity we propose in this abstract is to analyze the effective connectivity in human fMRI data during motor tasks. The asymmetry in the connectivity maps calculated with channel capacity is summarized as directed circular graphs in Fig 2C. The results demonstrate that channel capacity effectively captures the directionality of the information flows in the brain during foot or hand movement. 

Conclusion

We proposed channel capacity as an information-theoretical model-based dynamic effective connectivity measure, integrated with the SWpC framework. It captures the directed flow of information, accounts for the time-varying dynamics, and scales well with the data size. We used multimodal rodent data and task-related human data to confirm that the results are consistent and robust. 

Channel capacity is a well-established concept in engineering with concrete physical implications. It is also closely related to Granger causality. The approach proposed in this abstract can be extended to more complex channel models of brain connectivity and applied to clinical datasets in future studies.
