Introduction

Epilepsy imaging traditionally focuses on detecting static epileptogenic lesions, but interest is growing in visualizing dynamic post-seizure changes. These include markers of peri-ictal blood–brain barrier dysfunction and transient perfusion alterations, detectable with gadolinium enhancement, susceptibility imaging, arterial spin labelling, or ictal SPECT; the latter being the only method partly integrated into clinical care. Clinical translation of postictal imaging is limited by logistical barriers, as capturing early post-seizure effects requires immediate scanner access, rapid patient handling, and long exams. Portable ultra-low-field MRI (ULF-MRI), operating below 0.1 T and usable at the bedside without shielding or helium cooling, offers a potential solution.1 ULF-MRI has shown promising diagnostic performance across multiple neurological conditions and has proven feasible in intensive care settings.2–4 This study uses ULF-MRI to advance seizure imaging with diffusion-weighted MRI, enabling scans immediately after seizures and at short intervals. We aim to map near-continuous postictal diffusion dynamics, hypothesizing that apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) changes fluctuate throughout the postictal period and may colocalize with the seizure onset zone.

Methods

After seizures recorded at the EEG monitoring unit, the ULF-MRI was brought to the patient’s room, and a diffusion-weighted scan was started immediately after EEG electrodes were removed. Diffusion-weighted scans were repeated at variable intervals. A total of 69 scans were acquired in 11 individuals with epilepsy, of which 12/69 scans hat to be discarded. For one subject, all 4 scans had to be discarded. Ten individuals remained (median age 37 years, range 24-70; female:male 5:5) and 57 postictal ULF-MRI scans were included, 38/57 were performed within 12 hours (720 minutes) after seizure termination and 15/57 later than 24 hours after seizure termination (interictally). In 9/10 cases, a seizure onset zone could be delineated based all clinical evidence from presurgical evaluation. In addition, one individual (81 years, female) was scanned 72 hours after status epilepticus at the neurological intensive care unit. For reference, 20 healthy individuals were included (median age 32.5, range 29-54, female:male 10:10). ULF-MRI scans were acquired using a 0.064 T Swoop Portable MR Imaging System (Hyperfine, Inc., Guilford, CT, USA). Postictally, axial diffusion-weighted sequences (b = 0 and b = 900 s/mm2) were acquired (in-plane resolution 2.4 mm, slice thickness 5.9 mm, scanning time 10:21 min:s) and ADC maps were computed. At least one scan included an additional axial FLAIR, 3D T1- and T2-weighted sequence (total scanning time 39:10 min:s). Postictal ADC alterations in were assessed longitudinally using generalized additive models with region-wise z-scores (relative to the healthy control sample) as dependent variable. This was computed for seven regions of interest (20/30/40 mm spheres centered around the presumed seizure onset zone, ipsi-/contralesional white matter, ipsi-/contralesional cortex).  Whole-brain ADC alterations in different postictal phases were assessed cross-sectionally using voxel-based morphometry. To evaluate localization performance, parcel-wise ADC z-scores (AAL atlas) were computed for the scan closest to the peak of focal diffusion restriction, and the parcel corresponding to the presumed seizure onset zone was ranked.

Results

After focal seizures, postictal trajectories in a 20 mm sphere around the seizure onset zone showed a three-phase pattern: (1) <130 min, increased diffusivity; (2) 130-350 min, restricted diffusion; (3) >350 min, increased diffusivity (Fig. 1). This pattern was not observed for focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. White matter diffusivity was consistently elevated in the postictal phase and was more pronounced after focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizures than after focal seizures, particularly during phase (2). The most extreme increase in white matter diffusivity was observed after status epilepticus. In 7/9 cases with known seizure onset, the corresponding parcel was ranked in the lower quartile (median rank 14/116, range 3-80). This distribution differed significantly from uniformity (Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests D = 0.57, p = 0.003). One individual underwent 11 postictal scans, and their individual trajectory closely mirrored the group-level pattern (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

The three-phase pattern observed around the presumed seizure onset zone parallels a proposed postictal sequence: initial hyperperfusion without clear ADC change (stage 1), vasogenic edema with increased ADC (stage 2), cytotoxic edema with reduced ADC (stage 3), and later neuronal loss with gliosis and increased ADC (stage 4).5 Our phase 1 likely reflects a combination of stages 1/2, and phase 2 aligns with stage 3. This study demonstrates the feasibility of postictal diffusion-weighted imaging with ULF-MRI and identifies distinct temporal diffusivity patterns. The three-phase trajectory near the onset zone and widespread white matter involvement underscore the potential of ULF-MRI to capture transient postictal changes not easily accessible with conventional scanners.
