Introduction

Facial expressions are rapidly prioritised by the visual system and shape how we act toward others. Pain expressions are theoretically ambiguous: they can signal threat and motivate withdrawal, or signal vulnerability and motivate care [1]. This makes pain a strong test case for motivational accounts, which propose that expressions are organised by action tendency (approach vs avoidance) more than by valence [2]. A key neural candidate is the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), which supports decoding dynamic and socially salient facial cues; disrupting right pSTS impairs expression recognition [3]. Despite this, pain has been largely excluded from classic emotion models and from many posed face sets. New naturalistic databases that include spontaneous pain now allow ecologically valid tests [4]. This study asks two linked questions. Behaviourally, are pain faces perceived as closer to withdrawal-related expressions (fear, disgust) or to affiliative approach signals (happy, victory)? Neurally, do pain faces elicit stronger right than left pSTS responses? In Experiment 1, participants complete a triplet odd-one-out task to derive representational dissimilarity matrices from similarity judgements without imposing category labels, analysed using representational similarity analysis. Experiment 2 uses 7T fMRI to compare hemisphere-specific contrasts within functionally defined left and right pSTS ROIs. We predict that pain will cluster with fear and disgust and show a right-leaning pSTS bias, supporting an avoidance-leaning interpretation when contextual cues are minimal.

Methods

All participants were UQ undergraduates (Australia). Experiment 1 recruited 176 first-year psychology students via SONA; 10 did not complete all 300 trials and were excluded (N = 166). Eligibility required normal or corrected-to-normal vision and a desktop/laptop (mobile devices incompatible). The task was completed online via Pavlovia (approximately 25–30 min) for 0.5 course credit. Experiment 2 recruited 43 fourth-year psychology students with no MRI contraindications; 15 datasets were excluded for excessive head motion (N = 28). ROI analyses reported here include participants meeting all motion and ROI inclusion criteria (final analysed N reported in Results). The 7T session lasted approximately 60min and participants received course credit. All participants provided written informed consent.

Experiment 1 used 120 naturalistic faces (20 per category: Happiness, Disgust, Fear, Neutral, Pain, Victory) drawn from the Wild Faces Database (WFD) and WFD-HAS. Images were selected via (i) between-category discriminability checks on published ratings, (ii) within-category composite ranking (agreement, intensity, genuineness; Neutral excluded intensity), and (iii) demographic balancing. Experiment 2 used 45 faces (15 Pain, 15 Happy, 15 Neutral) drawn from the same validated sources, centrally cropped and scaled to 400 × 400 px on a mid-grey background; non-face trials acquired during the scan were not analysed.

Experiment 1 was a jsPsych triplet odd-one-out task hosted on Pavlovia. On each of 300 self-paced trials, three faces appeared at the vertices of an equilateral triangle and participants selected the face “most different” from the other two. No category labels or feedback were provided. Triplets were sampled without within-trial repeats and positions were randomised, with rest screens every 100 trials. Experiment 2 comprised six rapid event-related runs collected at 7T (Siemens MAGNETOM Plus; 32-channel head coil). Each run began with 2s fixation, followed by 540 trials in random order (200ms image; 300ms ISI; TR = 500ms; 544 volumes/run; 3,264 volumes total). Stimuli were centrally presented while participants maintained fixation on a bullseye and performed a vigilance task (button press to bullseye contrast inversion; 24 reversals/run; 500ms response window).

Acquisition and preprocessing. Functional data were acquired with ultra-fast 2D gradient-echo EPI optimised for ventral temporal and pSTS coverage (1.8 mm isotropic voxels; 32 slices; TR = 500ms; TE = 25.2ms; flip angle = 40°; FOV = 209mm). Structural data used T1-weighted MP2RAGE (0.75 mm isotropic). Preprocessing in AFNI included slice timing correction, despiking, motion correction, EPI-anatomical alignment, nonlinear normalisation, 3mm smoothing, high-pass filtering, percent-signal-change scaling, and censoring of high-motion frames (FD > 1 mm) with motion regressors.

Analyses. Experiment 1 used representational similarity analysis to construct a 120 × 120 behavioural RDM. Within each trial, the two non-selected stimuli were coded as similar and the selected odd-one-out as dissimilar, then aggregated across participants to estimate pairwise dissimilarity. The behavioural RDM was compared with model RDMs (categorical, Pain-as-avoidance, Pain-as-approach) using Spearman correlations and visualised with multidimensional scaling [5]. Experiment 2 used first-level GLMs (3dDeconvolve) modelling Pain, Happy, Neutral (plus Objects) and planned contrasts Pain > Neutral, Pain > Happy, and Happy > Neutral. ROI analyses extracted beta estimates from Glasser atlas pSTS parcels (dorsal and ventral; left and right) and tested within-hemisphere effects and hemispheric asymmetry (paired tests) in SPSS.

Results

Experiment 1: Behavioural representational geometry of pain

After excluding incomplete datasets, 166 participants (300 trials each) contributed 49,800 valid odd-one-out judgements across 120 faces. The resulting behavioural RDM had no missing values and showed the expected category structure: when sorted by expression, within-category similarity exceeded between-category similarity, and the RDM correlated with the categorical model (Spearman ρ = .47, p < .001). Trial coverage across stimulus pairs was sufficiently broad to avoid sparse-cell bias.

To adjudicate how pain is organised in perceptual space, we compared the behavioural RDM to two competing models: Pain-as-Negative (pain grouped with fear and disgust) and Pain-as-Positive (pain grouped with happiness and victory). The behavioural RDM correlated strongly with the Pain-as-Negative model (ρ = .71, p < .001) and more weakly with the Pain-as-Positive model (ρ = .40, p < .001). A 2D MDS embedding mirrored this outcome: emotion categories formed distinct clusters, with pain positioned near fear and disgust and clearly separated from happiness and victory, emerging directly from participants’ similarity judgements.

A 10,000-iteration permutation test confirmed that Pain-as-Negative outperformed Pain-as-Positive: the observed correlation difference was Δρ = .32, p < .001, far outside the null distribution. A Fisher r-to-z comparison converged (z = 28.49, p < .001). In a multiple regression including the categorical, Pain-as-Negative, and Pain-as-Positive models simultaneously, the model explained 54% of variance in the behavioural RDM (R² = .54, p < .001). The Pain-as-Negative predictor contributed uniquely and strongly (β = .65), the categorical model contributed modestly (β = .13), and Pain-as-Positive added no unique explanatory power (β ≈ 0). Overall, across ~50,000 judgements, pain was consistently represented as more similar to avoidance-related expressions (fear, disgust) than to approach-related expressions (happiness, victory).

Experiment 2: Hemispheric lateralisation of pain in pSTS

Following fMRI quality control and exclusions for motion and usable ROI data, 28 participants were retained for ROI analyses (df = 27). Beta estimates were extracted from anatomically defined bilateral pSTS for Pain, Happy, and Neutral, and contrasts were computed within each hemisphere (Pain-Neutral, Pain-Happy, Happy-Neutral). One-sample t-tests showed no significant within-hemisphere biases for any contrast in left or right pSTS (all ps > .090), with contrast means near zero.

However, hemispheric asymmetry emerged when directly comparing right versus left pSTS. Paired t-tests showed a significant rightward bias for Pain-Neutral (Right minus Left): M = 0.04, SD = 0.09, t(27) = 2.19, p = .037, d = 0.41, 95% CI [0.002, 0.07]. No reliable lateralisation was observed for Pain-Happy (t(27) = 0.13, p = .899) or Happy-Neutral (t(27) = 0.97, p = .340).

Subregion analyses indicated that this effect was specific to dorsal pSTS. Ventral pSTS showed no within-hemisphere bias and no hemispheric lateralisation for Pain-Neutral (all ps ≥ .150). In dorsal pSTS, the Pain-Neutral hemispheric comparison was significant: t(27) = 2.58, p = .016, d = 0.49, 95% CI [0.01, 0.07], whereas Pain-Happy and Happy-Neutral remained non-significant. Descriptive group-averaged pSTS contrast maps for Pain-Neutral visually reflected this rightward asymmetry.

Conclusion

This study combined a behavioural odd-one-out task with 7T fMRI to test whether pain expressions are read as approach- or avoidance-relevant cues. Across measures, pain showed an avoidance-weighted signature. Behaviourally, raw similarity judgements produced a perceptual geometry in which pain clustered most closely with fear and disgust. A right-leaning asymmetry emerged selectively in dorsal pSTS for Pain-Neutral, with no reliable lateralisation for Pain-Happy or Happy-Neutral and no effect in ventral pSTS. Together, these results indicate that, under a neutral comparison frame, pain is treated as an avoidance-relevant signal and is preferentially carried by right dorsal pSTS. This places pain in motivational space rather than discrete emotion categories, and specifies and subregion.

Behavioural analyses showed robust category structure, but model tests consistently favoured a motivational organisation for pain. The Pain-as-Negative (avoidance-linked) model outperformed the Pain-as-Positive (approach-linked) model, and pain’s nearest neighbours were fear and disgust. Because participants received no labels or feedback, this structure likely reflects inferred meaning and action affordances rather than category names. Dense trial coverage and permutation tests supported the stability of pain’s position across the full stimulus space, making a purely low-level feature account less parsimonious given the heterogeneity of naturalistic exemplars.

Neural results allow for further interpretation. The lateralisation pattern did not support a global right hemisphere for emotion rule. Instead, hemispheric allocation depended on anatomical location and comparison frame: rightward weighting appeared in dorsal pSTS for Pain-Neutral, but not in ventral pSTS and not for contrasts involving Happy. Within-hemisphere univariate effects were near zero, indicating that relative allocation (right > left) carried the critical information rather than large absolute activation in one hemisphere. This pattern is consistent with dorsal pSTS functioning as a social-salience and distress-decoding hub whose contribution increases when pain is judged against neutral, a frame that leaves the threat versus need interpretation less constrained.

Convergence across levels links an abstract geometry of perception (pain near fear/disgust) to a plausible allocation mechanism (rightward dorsal pSTS weighting) and clarifies when and where avoidance coding emerges. Future work can test the proposed mechanism by manipulating context and comparators. Embedding pain faces in scenes that bias helping versus threat interpretations, varying the comparator (neutral vs positive vs negative), and using dynamic pain expressions should clarify when avoidance signatures strengthen or recede. Acquiring behavioural and neural measures within the same individuals, and extending analyses beyond pSTS while incorporating individual differences (pain history, empathy, sex), would further specify moderators.

Overall, these results indicate that under neutral comparison, pain expressions are read and routed like avoidance-relevant signals. Behaviourally, pain sits near fear and disgust; neurally, right dorsal pSTS shows selective rightward weighting for Pain-Neutral, with no parallel effects for other contrasts or ventral pSTS.
