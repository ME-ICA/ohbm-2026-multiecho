Introduction

Altered psychomotor behavior, as observed in catatonia, is poorly understood1. Recent advances in understanding the physiology of the motor cortex have prompted a revision of the classical Penfield homunculus2. The body's representation in the primary motor cortex (effector regions) is interspersed with integrative regions (intereffector regions that orchestrate movement planning and coordination, as well as signals from the body suggesting a role in higher-order psychomotor control. In the current study, we investigated whether patterns of motor (effector and intereffector) connectivity differ between patients with and without catatonia.
 

Methods

This resting-state functional MRI study included 72 patients with psychosis and catatonia, 88 patients with psychosis without catatonia, and 63 matched healthy controls3,4. Using seed-based analyses with motor cortex effector and intereffector regions as seeds5, we quantified whole-brain functional connectivity differences between patients with and without catatonia. We used age, sex, medication as olanzapine equivalent and framewise displacement as controlling variables. We additionally examined the association between fine motor behavior (manual dexterity task) and M1 connectivity. 

Results

We found different functional connectivity patterns in patients with catatonia than patients without catatonia or healthy controls. Patients with catatonia had lower connectivity between the inferior intereffector area and temporoparietal cortex as well as the supplementary motor area than controls or patients without catatonia. Moreover, in patients, better motor performance in the manual dexterity task was associated with higher connectivity between the inferior intereffector and the parietal and motor cortices as well as the supplementary motor area. 

Conclusion

The findings indicate that intereffector regions play a key role in the neural basis of catatonia’s distinctive psychomotor disturbances.
