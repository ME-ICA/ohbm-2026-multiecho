Introduction

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) is a highly heterogeneous condition that affects brain structure and function. TBI is increasingly described as a “disconnection syndrome”, in which symptoms are highly associated with disrupted structural connectivity (SC) and functional connectivity (FC)1. A pathological hallmark of TBI is injury to white matter tracts due to diffuse axonal injury, contusions and secondary injury cascades which leads to damage/disconnection between different brain regions and networks. Damage to these networks impairs communication between brain regions, producing persistent motor, cognitive, and behavioral deficits. Many patients experience long-lasting and sometimes permanent functional impairments—including problems with memory and attention, executive dysfunction, dysphoria, anxiety, fatigue, and sleep disturbancs2. Understanding how brain structure and function change after injury, and how these changes relate to outcomes, is critical for developing interventions that can reduce these long-term consequences.

Traditional neuroimaging studies have often examined either structural or functional connectivity in isolation. However, such single-modality approaches may not fully capture the complexity of brain changes post-TBI. Multimodal neuroimaging—particularly combining functional (e.g., fMRI, EEG, fNIRS) and structural (e.g., sMRI, DTI) techniques—offers a more comprehensive perspective3. These methods allow for integrated insights by leveraging both shared and complementary data across imaging modalities.

This study aims to systematically review the application of multimodal neuroimaging (MN) techniques in traumatic brain injury (TBI), with a focus on methodological approaches, cross-modal integration strategies, and associated structure–function–behaviour relationships and clinical implications.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for systematic reviews and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025626997). To identify potentially relevant literature, the following databases were searched from 2014 to 2024: PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Web of Science. Eligible studies met the following criteria: (1) Participants diagnosed with TBI; (2) Studies using two or more neuroimaging techniques, specifically dMRI, fMRI, EEG, or fNIRS, to examine structural and functional brain changes; (3) Studies using genuine data fusion approaches, including asymmetric and symmetric fusion methods; (4) Peer-reviewed, Methodology and results described in detail, English language. Studies using only visual inspection or simple data overlay methods without statistical integration will be excluded.

After the search, references from different databases were imported into Covidence and deduplicated. Title and abstract screening, as well as full-text screening, were conducted by N.S. and R.S. independently, with any disagreements regarding inclusion resolved through discussion and consensus in a joint meeting. 

The risk of bias and the methodological quality of studies was assessed independently by N.S. and J.H. using the Joanna Biggs Institutes(JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. In total, 26 cross-sectional studies, 7 cohort studies, and 1 case-control study were appraised using the corresponding JBI checklists according to study design. Among the included studies, 34 presented a low risk of bias and 1 presented a moderate risk of bias.

Relevant information was extracted from the included studies by N.S. using an adapted version of the Covidence Extraction 2.0 template. The extracted data encompassed demographic characteristics, neuroimaging modalities and parameters, fusion methodologies (including analytical frameworks and technical details), and key findings stratified by research purposes. Notably, data specific to fusion methods were independently extracted by N.S. and J.H. to ensure reliability.

Because of the considerable heterogeneity in study designs, imaging modalities, and analytic methods, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, data were synthesized qualitatively following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guidelines.

Studies were first stratified by (1) study characteristics, including study design, participant population, injury severity, and outcome measures (2) neuroimaging characteristics, distinguishing between multi-sequence MRI and cross-modal combinations (e.g., MRI with EEG or fNIRS). For each included study, key information was extracted and tabulated, including imaging parameters, fusion level (e.g., feature-level), analytical methods (e.g., univariate correlation, multivariate pattern analysis, machine learning), and primary findings (e.g., SC–FC correlation, brain–behavior associations, classification or prediction outcomes).

Results

We identified 6,153 records, of which 34 studies met the inclusion criteria. Most studies employed cross-sectional designs and focused on adults with mild TBI. Multi-sequence MRI approaches predominated (76.5%), particularly combinations of dMRI and fMRI, while only 23.5% implemented true cross-modal integration (e.g., MRI with EEG or fNIRS).

All included studies applied feature-level fusion, whereby modality-specific features were extracted and subsequently integrated prior to or during statistical modelling. Fusion strategies varied, with 29% adopting asymmetric, modality-constrained approaches and 71% employing symmetric strategies in which modalities were treated jointly and equally. Data-driven analyses dominated (91%), most commonly using univariate statistical methods (e.g., Pearson or Spearman correlation; 65%), while advanced multivariate or machine learning techniques (e.g., support vector machines, partial least squares, joint ICA) were used less frequently.

Structural–functional and brain–behaviour associations were primary analytic targets in 85% of studies; however, findings were highly heterogeneous due to variability in participant characteristics, injury severity, outcome domains, imaging modalities, and fusion approaches. Despite this heterogeneity, convergent evidence suggested that white-matter alterations were frequently associated with disruptions in functional network organisation, although the direction and strength of SC–FC correspondence varied across modalities and post-injury stages.

Machine learning models were primarily developed to differentiate TBI subgroups from healthy controls, including acute, chronic, and paediatric mild TBI. Reported classification accuracies ranged from 78.4% to 97.6%, with sensitivities of 76.6–94% and specificities up to 100% under internal validation (predominantly 10-fold or leave-one-out cross-validation). Multimodal frameworks combining dMRI and fMRI consistently outperformed unimodal models. Outcome-focused applications included classification of persistent post-concussive symptoms (AUC = 0.86), identification of symptom-based PCS subtypes, and prediction of post-TBI attention deficits (>82% accuracy), highlighting the growing potential of multimodal and hybrid machine learning approaches for precision outcome modelling。

Conclusion

Multimodal neuroimaging research in TBI is currently dominated by multi-sequence MRI combinations and simple correlation-based analyses. Despite persistent methodological and logistical challenges, emerging cross-modal fusion approaches are critical for capturing the complex and dynamic neural alterations following TBI that are often overlooked by unimodal methods. These approaches also enhance understanding of structure–function–behaviour relationships and support more individualised diagnosis and outcome prediction.
