Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) frequently coexist. Cross-sectional studies have consistently demonstrated a markedly higher prevalence of MDD in individuals with MASLD compared with those without, however, the evidence from prospective studies is limited and no study has simultaneously assessed the directionality of their association within a unified analytical framework. Moreover, as most existing evidence is observational and subject to residual confounding, determining whether this association reflects causation remains a central challenge.

Both MASLD and MDD exhibit a prolonged preclinical phase, with physiological disturbances accumulating years before clinical onset1. MASLD, in particular, often remains asymptomatic until substantial metabolic or hepatic injury has already developed. Consequently, studies relying solely on using clinical diagnoses to characterize MASLD or MDD tend to capture advanced diseases2, but whether either condition, even at subclinical stages, predisposes to the later onset of the other, is unknown.

The mechanisms by which MASLD might predispose an individual to depression or vice versa are also not fully understood. There is accumulating evidence that plasma metabolites and brain structure are disturbed in people with chronic liver diseases and constitute key elements in the pathophysiology of depression. Yet no study has systematically examined whether MASLD and MDD share similar metabolomic or brain signatures or whether specific metabolites/brain regions mediate their association.

Methods

Leveraging data from the UK Biobank, we systematically investigated the directionality and causality of associations between MASLD and MDD and explored the potential mechanism driven by plasma metabolites and brain structure (Figure 1). The behavioral analysis included up to 329,165 participants aged 40–73 years from UK Biobank, of whom 183,159 had plasma metabolites (n=249) measured using a nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) platform and 33,308 had neuroimaging data (grey matter volume, GMVs, n=110). 

In accordance with the 2023 international consensus3, MASLD at baseline was defined as fatty liver index >60 (FLI, calculated from body mass index, waist circumference, triglycerides, and γ-glutamyltransferase using a previously validated formula), absence of excessive alcohol consumption (>30 g/d for males or 20 g/d for females) or other liver diseases, and the presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk factor (e.g., overweight/obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or insulin resistance). Because individual FLI components were unavailable during follow-up, incidence of severe MASLD was ascertained via linkage to hospital inpatient records and death registries using ICD-10 codes K76.0 and K75.8. Depression was also ascertained from hospital admission and death records using ICD-10 codes F32 or F33. Depressive symptom severity at baseline was quantified using the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4). All participants were followed from the date of recruitment until the first diagnosis of MASLD or MDD, death, loss to follow-up, or the censoring date, whichever occurred first.

We applied Cox proportional hazards models to assess the bidirectional associations between MASLD and MDD using follow-up time as the time scale. Models were adjusted for covariates (i.e., age, sex, race, material deprivation, educational attainment, physical activity, smoking status, alcohol intake status, and metabolic syndrome) and results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals. We also evaluated additive interactions between either condition (MDD or MASLD) and established risk factors on the other condition to identify potential synergistic effects by calculating the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and using 5,000 bootstrap samples to determine statistical significance. In parallel, we examined whether the coexistence of MASLD and MDD confers a synergistic increase in all-cause and cause-specific premature mortality by grouping causes of death into 16 major categories based on ICD-10 chapters. We conducted bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses using the ‘TwoSampleMR’ package in R to investigate potential causal relationships between MASLD and MDD.

Finally, by capitalizing on high-throughput plasma metabolomics and neuroimaging data, we characterized shared metabolomic/brain signatures and potential mediating pathways that may underlie the MASLD-MDD relationship. We performed metabolome-wide association analyses to examine the relationships between 249 NMR-based plasma metabolites or 110 regional GMVs and (i) clinical indices of hepatic steatosis (FLI) and depressive symptoms (PHQ-4 scores), and (ii) the risk of incident MASLD and MDD. Further, we used the ’mediation’ package in R to quantify the extent to which circulating metabolites or brain structure explained the associations between MASLD and MDD4. All models adjusted for the same covariates as in the primary analyses. The significance of indirect effects was assessed using 5,000 bootstrap iterations.

Results

Over more than 14 years of follow-up, the prospective analyses showed significant bidirectional associations between MDD and MASLD after adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle and clinical factors. Specifically, individuals with MASLD had a 1.35-fold (HR=1.35, 95% CI=[1.28, 1.41], P= 2.61×10-35) higher risk of developing MDD than those without; while individuals with MDD had a 1.76-fold higher risk of developing MASLD compared with those without (HR=1.76, [1.50, 2.08], P=1.09×10-11). Dose-response analyses indicated that depressive symptoms increased MASLD risk across the full severity spectrum, whereas MASLD predicted depression only at higher levels of steatotic burden (Figure 2A). Additive interaction analyses identified significant synergistic effects, where concurrent high deprivation and MASLD conferred a 30% higher MDD risk than the sum of their individual risks (RERI=0.30, 95% CI=[0.12, 0.46], Figure 2B, left panel), and concurrent metabolic syndrome and MDD conferred a 1.10-fold higher MASLD risk than the sum of their excess risks (RERI=1.10, [0.22, 2.02], Figure 2B, right panel). Mendelian randomization analyses provided evidence for a deleterious effect of genetic liability to depression on MASLD risk, but nonsignificant effects in the reverse direction.

Figure 2C illustrates the joint effect of MASLD and MDD comorbidity on premature mortality. A significant synergistic interaction was observed, with the combined effect of MASLD and MDD on premature mortality risk being 16% higher than the sum of their individual effects (RERI=0.16, 95% CI=[0.02, 0.30], P=0.0106). Cause-specific analyses further showed elevated mortality risks across endocrine, circulatory, respiratory, digestive, genitourinary, and COVID-19–related causes.

The metabolome-wide association analyses identified 196 metabolites significantly associated with baseline PHQ-4 scores (covariate-adjusted β = -0.070 to 0.064, P<0.05/249, Figure 2D, left panel), with fatty acid-related measures, such as docosahexaenoic acid, degree of unsaturation, and omega-3 fatty acids showing the strongest associations. During a median 13.72-year follow-up, 103 of 249 metabolites were significantly associated with incident MASLD (covariate-adjusted HRs =0.73 to 1.38, P<0.05/249, Figure 2D, middle panel). The most significant metabolites were primarily related to fatty acid metabolism and HDL lipid composition, including phospholipids, cholesterol, cholesteryl esters, and total lipid content. Notably, the metabolomic association pattern of PHQ-4 scores was strongly correlated with that of MASLD incidence (r=0.81, P=6.59×10-59), with 100 metabolites consistently identified across both traits (Figure 2D, right panel). Mediation analyses were conducted on 62 metabolites exhibiting consistent associations with FLI, PHQ-4, and incident MDD/MASLD, and only for the association from depression to MASLD due to its significant causal estimates. Among these, 58 metabolites significantly mediated the association from PHQ-4 scores to MASLD incidence, explaining 1.94% to 8.86% of the association (P<0.05/62, Figure 2E). Lipid composition and particle measures related to large and very large HDL subclasses showed the most pronounced mediation effects.

The neuroimaging analyses revealed 5 and 49 brain regions significantly associated with baseline depression symptoms and incident MASLD, respectively (Figure 2F). Four brain regions were consistently identified by both conditions, including frontal medial cortex, central opercular cortex, accumbens, and caudate. Further analysis indicated that the mean GMV of these shared brain regions significantly mediated the association between depressive symptoms and MASLD (P=0.001).

Conclusion

These findings demonstrated a bidirectional association between MDD and MASLD, with a causal effect only from MDD to MASLD. Given that effective disease-modifying treatments for MASLD remain limited2, early identification and targeted management of emerging depressive symptoms may offer an actionable opportunity to reduce subsequent MASLD burden, a possibility that future interventional studies should evaluate. Further, we observed a significant synergistic interaction between metabolic syndrome and depression, producing a combined MASLD risk that exceeded the sum of their individual effects. The synergy indicates that metabolic syndrome and depression should not be evaluated independently when assessing MASLD risk; analytic frameworks that incorporate cross-system interactions may better capture true susceptibility.

We also found that depression and MASLD acted synergistically in increasing all-cause premature mortality beyond additive expectations. Clinical encounters often prioritize the physical condition, leading to under-recognition or undertreatment of depression and delayed management. Recognizing and addressing this joint clinical burden, rather than managing each condition separately, may be crucial for improving long-term outcomes in this high-risk group. Moreover, MASLD and MDD showed strikingly similar metabolomic association patterns and shared brain structural alterations, which significantly explained their association. These findings lend support to the hypothesis that MASLD and depression share an overlapping disruption in core metabolic processes and neurobiological basis, providing insight into their biological linkage.

Overall, this study identifies depression as a potential early risk factor and support the importance of integrating mental–physical care to mitigate MASLD incidence and the excess mortality associated with their coexistence5.
