Introduction

Humans exhibit a remarkable capacity to extract abstract structure from limited experience, enabling knowledge to be flexibly transferred and generalized across contexts while continuously incorporating new information. This capacity to acquire new knowledge without disrupting existing representations—a defining feature of advanced intelligence—is referred to as continual learning. Despite its importance, the biological basis of continual learning remains poorly understood. In particular, how structural maturation and functional reorganization coordinate to support this learning capacity across development and aging has yet to be systematically characterized. Insights into these mechanisms in the human  brain can also inform artificial intelligence, where achieving lifelong learning across changing tasks without catastrophic interference remains a key challenge. Here, we examine lifespan trajectories of four neural substrates hypothesized to underlie continual learning: (i) neurogenesis-related learning capacity indexed by hippocampal subfields and the striatum; (ii) functional modularity; (iii) cortical hierarchy; and (iv) memory reactivation. By jointly mapping these features from gestational week 26 through 87 years of age, we characterize how the brain reorganizes the mechanisms underlying continual learning across the lifespan (Fig. 1A).

Methods

We analyzed a harmonized multi-cohort lifespan dataset comprising 3,850 participants from 26 gestational weeks to 100+ years, spanning the dHCP, HCP-Development[1], HCP-Young Adult[1], HCP-Aging[1], and Cam-CAN[2]cohorts (Fig. 1B). Structural MRI was available for all participants; resting-state fMRI for 3,679 individuals; and movie-watching fMRI for 652 individuals from the Cam-CAN cohort. Lifespan trajectories were modeled using Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS) to capture nonlinear development and age-dependent variability (Fig. 1C).

Our framework quantified four dimensions. i) Neurogenesis-related learning capacity was indexed by intracranial-volume–corrected volumes of the hippocampus, striatum, and dentate gyrus/CA3 subfields. ii) Functional modularity  was assessed using a network segregation metric derived from a task-based 12-network Yeo parcellation,defined as the normalized within–between connectivity contrast[3].iii) Cortical hierarchy was characterized using the principal functional gradient spanning unimodal to transmodal cortex[4]. Finally, iv) memory reactivation was assessed during naturalistic movie viewing by quantifying coupling between cortical event segmentation and hippocampal activity at event boundaries identified using Greedy State Boundary Search (GSBS)[5] (Fig. 1D–G).

Results

To understand how the human brain sustains continual learning across the lifespan, we examined how neural resources are dynamically reweighted to support knowledge accumulation and generalization. As will be specified below, we found that the continual learning of the human brain relies on, rather than a single uniform mechanism, an orchestrated dynamical process across  different ages, reflecting its sophisticated design in effectively utilizing the functional resources.

We observed that in early life, there is a rapid growth of hippocampal subfields, including the dentate gyrus and CA regions, and the striatum, which may provide representational capacity for sequential learning and reward/motor processing (Fig. 2A). Indeed, volumes peak in adolescence (17-18 years), indicating its early structural expansion before a gradual pruning process. As the growth stabilizes, however, the focus seems to be shifted towards the topological organization of neural systems. For instance, network modularity rises throughout childhood and peaks in early adolescence (~12–15 years), with the sensory systems showing a prolonged period of its maturation in terms of functional segregation (Fig. 2B), which may decouple themselves from other cognitive processes, one of the critical mechanisms for stable learning. As entering the age of 18, the brain seems to prepare a more advanced strategy of continual learning, namely abstraction and generalization, by forming an integrative hierarchical organization as a central architecture. Indeed, the functional gradient from the sensory to association cortex strengthens across development and peaks in early adulthood (~20–30 years) (Fig. 2C), which may suggest that the hierarchical scaffolding builds upon early sensory specialization. Notably, this hierarchy declines with aging in an asymmetrical fashion: separation among sensory systems compresses more rapidly, whereas higher-order association structure is relatively preserved, indicating a reduced influence of sensory inputs on abstract representation.Finally, one of the most critical components for continual learning in the human brain is how continuous experiences are segmented and contextualized. While the temporal hierarchy of cortical event segmentation is preserved across the lifespan, hippocampal encoding at event boundaries shifts with age (Fig. 2D). In youth, hippocampal activity aligns preferentially with mPFC-defined boundaries (20~31 years), consistent with schema-based encoding. In older adults, this coupling shifts toward precuneus-defined boundaries, reflecting increased reliance on spatiotemporal context as higher-order schematic support wanes.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that continual learning is supported by a constellation of structural and functional neural substrates, each contributing distinct capacities yet operating in a highly coordinated manner. For instance, representational capacity, functional segregation, hierarchical abstraction, and episodic encoding do not operate in an isolated manner but, as an integrated and progressively emerging mechanism, collectively enable the brain to balance the incorporation of new information with the preservation of prior knowledge. This interplay allows continual learning to remain robust across the lifespan, adapting to developmental expansions, maturational stabilization, and aging-related constraints. These insights highlight that human continual learning is not merely the sum of its parts, but a synergistic system, finely tuned to optimize knowledge acquisition and generalization throughout life.
