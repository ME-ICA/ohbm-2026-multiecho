Introduction

Adversity effects on brain structure and function have been extensively described. Such alterations can be particularly detrimental during sensitive developmental windows, e.g., adolescence, when the brain shows heightened plasticity.^1 Previous studies found that childhood maltreatment and low socioeconomic status were associated with decreased hippocampal volume.^2 However, evidence of childhood adversity effects on hippocampal microstructure is scarce and limited to single-event trauma or adult samples.^3,4 Therefore, in this study, we aim to investigate the prospective effects of adversity in development on hippocampal T1w/T2w ratio, an in vivo proxy for microstructure, primarily sensitive to myelin-related tissue properties. Besides T1w/T2w ratio, we intend to reproduce prior findings on volume and examine possible effects on hippocampal thickness, an intermediate phenotype. We assess these biomarkers as global measures and along the anterior-posterior and proximal-distal axes to capture heterogeneity across hippocampal subregions, with the proximal-distal axis reflecting subfield organization. Previous reports of subfield-specific volume reductions associated with childhood maltreatment raise the possibility that thickness and T1w/T2w ratio of hippocampal subregions may not be homogeneously affected.^5 

Methods

Behavioral and neuroimaging data of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study at baseline were used (N = 5297, agemean = 119 ± 7.54 months, 48% females). To parse family-related adversity, we performed an Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) on all available respondents (N = 11505) using items from the youth- and caregiver-reported Family Environment Scale, as well as youth-reported Parental Monitoring Questionnaire and Children’s Report of Parental Behavior. The EFA yielded three factors: two representing Adverse Family Environment from the youth and caregiver perspectives, and one describing Neglectful Parenting (Fig. 1A). This factor structure was reproduced in the MRI subsample using Confirmatory Factor Analysis. Additionally, neighborhood disadvantage and income-to-needs ratio were included as socioeconomic adversity indicators. Automatic segmentation of the hippocampus was performed with the HippUnfold toolbox (https://hippunfold.khanlab.ca/en/latest/) on bias-corrected and co-aligned T1- and T2-weighted images. These images were used to compute T1w/T2w ratio, which was then projected onto the hippocampal surfaces. Using the multi-hist7 atlas implemented in HippUnfold, subfields boundaries were represented along the proximal-distal axis relative to the neocortex for volume, thickness, and T1w/T2w ratio. Additionally, thickness and T1w/T2w ratio were mapped along the anterior-posterior axis (Fig. 1B). The dentate gyrus was included in the volumetric analysis only, as its generated surface is less reliable given the small size and high folding of the structure. Partial Least Squares Regression (PLS-R) was used to investigate adversity-specific effects on the three hippocampal biomarkers – volume, thickness, and T1w/T2w ratio – at the global and axis-level. To train and evaluate PLS-R models, the data were split into 70/30 training and test sets while accounting for family structure. Site-related variability was mitigated with ComBat harmonization estimated on the training data and applied to the test set. In a complementary analysis, the effect of overall adversity exposure was assessed using Linear Mixed Models. A cumulative adversity score was computed by summing individual adversity measures and used as a predictor of each biomarker at both global and axis levels. All analyses controlled for BMI, age, sex, pubertal stage, and intracranial volume.

Results

PLS-R models of the three biomarkers at the global and axis level described a general socioeconomic-health latent dimension (component explained variancemean = 17%, R2 range = 0.03-0.29, ppermutation < 0.001), with socioeconomic adversity measures contributing more than family-related adversity indicators. Our results indicated that socioeconomic adversity was more consistent across different hippocampal biomarkers. Higher neighborhood disadvantage was associated with reduced hippocampal volume, as well as with decreased thickness and T1w/T2w ratio, both at global and axis levels (βrange = -0.13 to -0.04, all pFDR < 0.001; Fig. 2A). In contrast, higher income-to-needs ratio was associated with increased values of these metrics (βrange = 0.03 to 0.12, all pFDR < 0.001). On the other hand, family-related adversity showed biomarker-specific effects. The EFA-derived youth-reported adverse family environment was associated with reduced volumes globally and along the subfields of the proximal-distal axis (βrange = -0.02 to -0.01, all pFDR < 0.05), whereas the neglectful parenting factor was associated with increased hippocampal thickness along the same axis (βrange = 0.01 to 0.02, all pFDR < 0.05). In complementary analysis, cumulative adversity exposure predicted reduced volume globally and across multiple subfields of the proximal-distal axis (subiculum, CA1, CA2, and dentate gyrus), as well as decreased thickness and T1w/T2w ratio in the subiculum and CA1 (βrange = -5.80 to -0.0013, all pFDR < 0.05; Fig. 2B). Along the subregions of the anterior-posterior axis, cumulative adversity effects were strongest in anterior and posterior regions (βrange = -0.0018 to -0.0011, all pFDR < 0.05). 

Conclusion

Our results replicate prior reports of reduced hippocampal volume in children exposed to lower socioeconomic status and childhood maltreatment. By extending these associations to hippocampal thickness and T1w/T2w ratio in early adolescence, our findings indicate that adversity affects different markers of the hippocampal morphology at both global and axis-specific levels in this developmental period. Adversity-specific effects were widely distributed along the hippocampal axes, particularly for indicators representing socioeconomic disadvantage. In contrast, cumulative adversity effects appeared more localized, with stronger involvement of most proximal subfields, and anterior and posterior extremes. The regions affected by adversity along both axes correspond to segments with distinct functional specialization and structural plasticity. At the multivariate level, our results yielded a dominant component capturing intertwined social and biological factors as strong correlates of hippocampal volume, thickness, and T1w/T2w ratio in early adolescence. These findings highlight that understanding the effects of adversity on hippocampal maturation requires the consideration of multifactorial aspects of a child’s environment in relation to the intrinsic organization of the hippocampus.
