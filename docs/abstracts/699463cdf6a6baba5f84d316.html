Introduction

Semantic processing requires the integration of perceptual and motor inputs with abstract meaning, raising the question of which brain regions serve as interfaces between these domains. While regions such as the anterior temporal lobe and fusiform gyrus are established as key semantic hubs, the cortical mechanisms functioning as a specific sensorimotor–semantic interface—linking high-level meaning to motor execution—remain poorly understood^1,2. Area 55b, located in the middle precentral gyrus, has recently emerged as a candidate for this integrative role due to its unique anatomical positioning between phonological, semantic, and motor territories^3. However, direct empirical evidence characterizing 55b across the full spectrum of communicative modalities (listening, reading, speaking, and writing) is limited. Furthermore, it remains unknown whether 55b operates as a uniform functional unit or acts as a complex interface supported by fine-scale internal organization and biological hierarchies.

To address this gap, we combined high-resolution fMRI data from participants performing four lexical tasks with a multi-modal analytical framework integrating activation mapping, multivariate decoding modeling, and cortical gradient characterization. We tested the overarching hypothesis that Area 55b serves as a critical sensorimotor–semantic interface coordinating lexical production and comprehension.

Methods

To address this gap, we conducted an fMRI study in which 30 native Chinese speakers performed four block-designed lexical tasks spanning comprehension and production: listening to spoken words, reading written words, naming pictures, and writing words from pictures (Fig. 1A). Stimuli were drawn from six semantic categories. Each task included a modality-matched control condition—listening to scrambled speech, reading unfamiliar Korean characters, speaking digits, and drawing geometric figures—allowing semantic processing to be isolated from low-level perceptual and motor demands.

This design enabled us to test five key hypotheses. First, we asked whether area 55b is engaged in word processing across modalities and whether it shows preferential involvement in production (naming, writing) relative to comprehension (listening, reading). We used univariate activation analyses and conjunction analyses to identify modality-general word responses in 55b. Production–comprehension differences were assessed using paired t-tests, and task class (production vs. comprehension) was decoded from multivoxel activation patterns in 55b using multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) with linear support vector machines (Fig 1B F1).

Second, we tested whether semantic information is more strongly represented during production than comprehension in area 55b. To this end, we performed semantic category decoding separately for each task using MVPA and compared decoding accuracy between production and comprehension conditions (Fig 1B F2).

Third, we examined whether area 55b exhibits functional heterogeneity organized along intrinsic connectivity gradients, and whether this heterogeneity aligns with its information-coding properties. We hypothesized that posterior 55b is more strongly connected with the sensorimotor cognitive action network (SCAN) and preferentially supports phonological representations, whereas anterior 55b is more strongly connected with the language network and preferentially supports semantic representations^4,5. To test this, we estimated local functional connectivity gradients within area 55b using resting-state fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project. Functional connectivity matrices were computed and subjected to dimensionality reduction to derive intrinsic gradients. The first gradient was retained as the principal axis of functional variation, and vertices within 55b were divided into high- and low-gradient subregions. We then conducted neural encoding analyses using phoneme embeddings (derived from 28 articulatory features , e.g., place and manner of articulation) and semantic embeddings (derived from an open large language model GLM-4), and tested for double dissociations in encoding strength between the two subregions (Fig 1B F3).

Fourth, we examined whether area 55b functions as an information-flow hub, based on the hypothesis that an interface region both integrates inputs from distributed cortical areas and broadcasts information to multiple downstream targets. We tested this by estimating effective connectivity using Neural Perturbational Inference, which quantifies directed information flow to and from 55b (Fig 1B F4).

Fifth, we tested whether area 55b exhibits a steeper transition along the sensory-to-association axis across multiple cortical hierarchies—myelination, gene expression, cortical expansion, receptor density, and the principal connectivity gradient—compared with other regions that also show modality-general activation relative to control tasks. Monotonic sensory-to-association trends were quantified using the Jonckheere–Terpstra test, and the strength of these hierarchical transitions was compared across regions (Fig 1B F5). 

Results

Our analyses revealed that area 55b fulfilled multiple predictions for a sensorimotor–semantic interface.

First, we identified regions showing modality-general engagement during word processing. Area 55b, together with three additional regions—the inferior frontal junction (IFJ), anterior fusiform gyrus, and superior frontal language area (SFL; defined using the Glasser 360 atlas)—showed significantly greater activation for lexical tasks than for their corresponding control conditions across all modalities (p < 0.001, FDR-corrected). 

Second, area 55b and SFL showed preferential involvement in production (naming, writing) relative to comprehension (listening, reading). Specifically, area 55b (t(29) = 9.23, p < 0.001, Bonferroni-corrected, d = 1.68) and SFL (t(29) = 7.01, p < 0.001, Bonferroni-corrected, d = 1.28) exhibited greater activation during production than comprehension, whereas this distinction was not observed in the anterior fusiform gyrus or IFJ (p > 0.05). Consistent with these univariate results, task class (production vs. comprehension) could be decoded above chance (chance = 0.50) in area 55b (accuracy = 0.75, p < 0.001) and SFL (accuracy = 0.64, p < 0.001), but not in the fusiform gyrus or IFJ (p > 0.05).

Third, semantic category decoding analyses revealed a selective enhancement of semantic representations during production in area 55b. Semantic category information was more robustly decoded during production than comprehension in 55b (t(27) = −2.74, p = 0.045, FDR-corrected), with above-chance (chance = 0.5) decoding during production (accuracy = 0.54, p = 0.023, FDR-corrected) but not comprehension (p > 0.05). This production-specific enhancement was not observed in IFJ (t(27) = 0.16, p = 0.87), SFL (t(27) = 0.28, p = 0.87) nor the fusiform gyrus  (t(27) = −0.62, p = 0.87).

Fourth, intrinsic connectivity gradients revealed a posterior–anterior functional specialization within area 55b, with posterior portions aligned to the SCAN ( Dice = 0.746) and anterior portions aligned to the language network (Dice = 0.422). Functional measures systematically followed this intrinsic axis. The posterior portion showed the strongest production bias (t(29) = 10.24, p < 0.001) and robust decoding of task class (production vs. comprehension; accuracy = 0.72, chance = 0.50, p < 0.001) than anterior portion, consistent with sensorimotor-linked articulatory processing. In line with this, phonological encoding was stronger posteriorly, whereas semantic category decoding was stronger anteriorly (both p < 0.001), revealing a double dissociation. 

Fifth, we tested whether area 55b functions as an information-flow hub within the lexical network. Effective connectivity analyses, conducted within a network spanning sensory, motor, and multimodal regions, revealed that area 55b exhibited the highest in-degree and out-degree among all conjunction-defined regions (all comparisons p < 0.001). This pattern indicates that 55b both integrates directed inputs from distributed cortical sources and exerts strong causal influence on downstream targets, consistent with its proposed role as an information-flow hub.

Finally, we showed area 55b exhibits a uniquely steep transition along the sensory-to-association axis across multiple cortical hierarchies. Across five independent hierarchy maps—myelination, gene expression, cortical expansion, receptor density, and the principal connectivity gradient—area 55b showed consistent and monotonic sensory-to-association transitions (Jonckheere–Terpstra tests: all p < 0.001). In contrast, other regions that also exhibited modality-general activation relative to control tasks showed weaker, inconsistent, or non-monotonic trends. Together, these results indicate that area 55b uniquely shows a sharp architectural transition across cortical hierarchies.

Conclusion

In summary, our results converge on Area 55b as a sensorimotor–semantic interface that helps coordinate lexical comprehension and production. Although we found a small set of regions showed reliable word-related engagement across listening, reading, naming, and writing, 55b stood out by combining modality-general recruitment with a clear production-weighted profile, indicating that it is not merely a sensory- or effector-specific node. Across complementary analyses, 55b consistently differentiated production from comprehension and showed stronger expression of meaning-related information when an explicit output plan had to be generated, aligning with the idea that semantic constraints are upregulated at the point where they must be converted into executable actions. Importantly, 55b also exhibited structured internal organization: intrinsic connectivity gradients indicated a coherent axis of specialization within the region, consistent with a transition from more sensorimotor-anchored processing to more language-network–aligned semantic integration, rather than a functionally uniform “premotor language area.” 

Network dynamics further supported an interface role, as effective connectivity placed 55b in a central position for information exchange within the word-processing circuitry, consistent with integrating distributed inputs and influencing downstream targets during lexical operations. Finally, the same functional profile was mirrored in cortical architecture, with 55b uniquely occupying a sharp boundary along sensory-to-association hierarchies, suggesting that its interface function is grounded in underlying biological gradients. Together, these findings identify Area 55b as a key cortical locus for linking semantic representations to sensorimotor implementations, enabling flexible word use across modalities.
