Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases represent a significant burden of illness for the elderly. It manifests as various cognitive and motor impairments affecting quality of life. These diseases are accompanied by alterations in neural processing and connectivity that affect cognitive processes. Cardiovascular diseases have been linked to neurodegeneration with altered blood flow being frequently observed.^1 Given the key role of the vascular system in providing nutrients, it is plausible that these deleterious alterations in cardiovascular disease lead to metabolic dysregulation. How such dysregulation occurs mechanistically is not yet well understood. However, neurons use nutrients such as glucose and oxygen to synthesize adenosine triphosphate (ATP) molecules, the primary source of energy for spiking. There are primarily two methods for ATP synthesis in the human body: oxidative metabolism and glycolysis. Most ATP synthesis occurs through oxidative metabolism, a more efficient process albeit slower. Glycolysis, on the other hand, is a fast process that initiates at times of lower oxygen availability or rapid increase in energetic requirements, due to spiking which is then compensated by increased oxidation through hemodynamic response-induced blood flow increases. Higher glycolysis has been shown to correlate with higher accumulation of amyloid-𝛽, a precursor to neuron cell death in neurodegeneration. We hypothesize that lower oxygen supply leads to higher glycolysis levels which releases lactate and hydrogen ions in the process. This triggers acidification activating amyloid precursor protein increasing the rate of accumulation of amyloid-𝛽. To be able to verify this hypothesis, we need to build a mechanistic level of understanding that connects neuronal activity with metabolism of oxygen and glucose.

Methods

In this study, we use a computational modelling approach to connect neuronal firing and metabolic processes. With this, we explore various experimental findings of age and cardiovascular biomarkers effects on experimentally-measured brain activity. We built a spiking neural network model in a shared pool of lactate, glucose, and oxygen (Figure 1). Neurons synthesized ATP molecules from the existing glucose and oxygen through a combination of oxidative metabolism and glycolysis. The proportion of each process depended on oxygen glucose index (OGI) driven by the available concentrations of oxygen and glucose and two spike-driven mechanisms: a rapid increase in glycolysis, and a delayed hemodynamic response to restore the OGI balance. We used conductance-based models with excitatory, inhibitory, and leak currents, modelled in addition to metabolic currents. We added a metabolic inhibitory current driven by ATP-sensitive potassium channels KATP. At low ATP, these channels would open, driving neurons into hyperpolarization. We also modelled hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN) channels to activate the cells when quiet. These currents helped drive spontaneous neuronal firing in the absence of an input. We modelled the facilitation of amyloid-𝛽 accumulation by the acidity of the interstitial space using pH. The model architecture included 10,000 neurons with 4:1 excitatory to inhibitory ratio. We added random connectivity with 3% connectivity probability following previous work.^2 We modelled the concentration of glucose, oxygen, and lactate in the interstitial space that were governed by use rates to generate ATP molecules, compensatory mechanisms, and spike-driven demand processes. A spike by a neuron would initiate two processes: a rapid one that increases the use of glucose and glycolysis rates, and a slower one consisting of a hemodynamic response that increases oxygen supply to rebalance the OGI. The hemodynamic response was included through integration of the balloon model^3 into the oxygen concentration terms. This inclusion allowed us to also generate predictions that can be compared to experimental results from functional magnetic resonance imaging.

Using this model, we tested first a healthy range based on parameters of interest taken  from the literature, with other parameters being optimized to balance the network. We then tested an aging scenario based on reduced cerebrovascular reactivity, where the time constants of oxygen replenishment were reduced by a factor of 20% from the baseline. We also tested multiple scenarios of hypoxia to simulate different cardiovascular scenarios. We tested a severe but short-lived oxygen restriction and milder but chronic restriction, and examined their effects on lactate and pH levels in the interstitial space.

Results

We first ran simulations testing the network model’s operation over healthy ranges of metabolites. With these, we were able to reproduce similar patterns to those in Chintaluri and Vogels^2 (Figure 1A) where inhibitory KATP channels were more active when there was an input current activating the network. These channels dominated leading to a net inhibitory current to reduce the rate of spiking and maintain healthy ATP levels. When input was turned off, neurons were driven into hyperpolarization and the HCN channels became dominant, firing again after a brief period of silence with coordinated bursts first, followed by more consistent and random firing. We then simulated the hemodynamic response to a 2 seconds-on 2 seconds-off stimulation patterns in the young and old scenarios, and were able to reproduce experimental hemodynamic responses with the older showing a lower peak (Figure 2B).^4 We also tested the resting state differences between young and old hemodynamic responses and found increased relative power of the 0.1-0.3 Hz frequency bands in the old group (Figure 2C; t=-4.077; p=9.6×10-5), despite these having lower overall power (standard deviation) than the young group (Figure 2D; t=6.754; p=1.27×10-9) consistent with experimental findings.^5 We then ran experiments simulating chronic and transient oxygen drop as a proxy for cardiovascular disease. In the chronic case, the drops caused gradual decrease in pH, pushing it into acidic regions more favorable to amyloid precursor protein (BACE-1) activity, though it did not reach its peak activity pH levels (Figure 2E). In the transient conditions, mild reduction in oxygen led to mild acidification that was recoverable in small drop percentages (Figure 2F), but larger drops led to rapid acidification and increases in lactate concentration (Figure 2G-H). 

Conclusion

We built a spiking neural network model that was able to reproduce results from multiple modelling and experimental studies. The advantage of this model is that it combines the neuronal and metabolic effects and in particular cardiovascular effects. This combination makes it a powerful tool to predict the effects of cardiovascular disorders in imaging studies since it accounts for both the effects on the neuronal activity and the measurement of BOLD signals. We were able to reproduce experimental fMRI results of aging using oxygen parameters. We have yet to explore the effects of aging on glucose and lactate uptake.

We also simulated the effects of brief oxygen restriction, which could emulate the effect of a stroke as well as more chronic but milder effects that could emulate milder forms of atherosclerosis. Our results suggest that mild cardiovascular symptoms can be recovered, while stronger ones can lead to more drastic effects such as neuronal death. However, these symptoms drive the neuronal population to pH levels that are more preferential to amyloid precursor protein (BACE-1), which could in turn result in a higher amyloid-𝛽 accumulation. 

Limitations of this work include that our models do not account for interventions to restore blood, and operate on the scale of seconds, meanwhile neurodegeneration develops at much larger time scales. Future work includes confirming these findings through experimental results from human fMRI and biomarkers of oxygen and glucose. Our results provide testable predictions, and suggest that maintenance of a healthy metabolic balance is essential for slowing neurodegeneration and can suggest potential early interventions.
