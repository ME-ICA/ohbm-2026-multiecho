Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly heterogeneous syndrome. Under the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) framework, more than a hundred symptom constellations can meet diagnostic criteria, and patients further differ in treatment response, prognosis, and demographic and genetic factors [1]. The pronounced heterogeneity suggests that a single diagnostic label may mask meaningful biological variability. Rather than treating depression as a single, unified entity, identifying its subtypes and corresponding biomarkers may provide new insights into its underlying mechanisms and contribute to more effective diagnosis and treatment. At the same time, the long-established symptom-based clinical framework remains valuable, and research on biotypes grounded in these theoretical and clinical foundations could be benefit to ensure interpretability and translational relevance.

In this study, we identify the heterogeneity and biotypes of depression with approach that integrates both the clinical symptomatic foundations and the brain functional framework of neuroscience. We employed individualized fMRI measures that were simultaneously associated neurobiological functions and depressive symptoms, and adopt the clustering process for high-dimensional brain data. This approach identifies five depression biotypes with distinct clinical and neurofunctional profiles. Moreover, using accessible resting-state measures, our model shows cross-site consistency in distinguishing MDD from healthy controls, supporting the robustness and potential clinical utility of these features. By aligning imaging-derived markers with symptom theory and clinical diagnostics, the study enhances the interpretability of neuroimaging measures and advances their applicability to clinically meaningful subtyping.

Methods

Participants and data. We used multi-site data from the Depression Imaging REsearch ConsorTium (DIRECT), comprising 1,660 patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) and 1,341 healthy controls (HC). Clinical assessment included clinician-rated Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) item scores and standard demographic/clinical information (age, sex, education, episode status, medication).

Imaging quality control and preprocessing. Structural and resting-state BOLD fMRI were checked for head motion, image/registration quality and brain coverage; scans with mean framewise displacement > 0.2 mm or insufficient coverage/quality were excluded. After quality control and removal of participants with incomplete data, preprocessing followed the DIRECT protocol using DPABISurf and fMRIPrep procedures [2, 3]: discard initial volumes, realignment and slice-timing correction, tissue segmentation, spatial normalization to MNI space, nuisance regression (CSF, WM), band-pass filtering (0.01-0.1 Hz) and smoothing.

Term-based meta-analysis and seed derivation. We extended publicly available Neurosynth [4] term-based meta-analytic database with depressive symptom-relevant corpora. Using NLP embeddings (BERT), we matched MDD symptom descriptions and RDoC human functioning constructs [5] to meta-analytic terms by cosine similarity. For each symptom/function item, the top candidate terms were screened by an expert panel; 1-5 representative terms per item were selected and used to generate symptom/function-specific association maps (FDR q = 0.01). The mean association map per item served as its theoretical seed map. See Figure 1a and b.

Multiview clustering (biotype identification). For each participant, we applied dual regression using the symptom/function-specific seed maps to obtain 42 individualized whole-brain maps and converted to z-score maps relative to healthy references. For 42 maps (views) from all participants, we applied simplified multi-view kernel spectral clustering to the fused kernel and set k = 5 (search range k = 2-10). See Figure 1c and d. For comparison, alternative model configurations were evaluated combining several input metrics, including ALFF, fALFF, ReHo, binary and weighted degree centrality, single-view dual regression maps and multi-view dual regression maps (each was computed with/without global signal regression or normalization), with six clustering methods (k-means, k-medoids, hierarchical, spectral, Gaussian mixture, multi-view kernel spectral). All metric maps were harmonized for site effects using ComBat before clustering.

Clinical and performance validation. Symptom profiles across clusters were tested for HAMD total and item scores, followed by Tukey-Kramer post hoc tests (q < 0.05). Imaging profiles were compared with ANCOVAs on symptom/function-specific DR maps controlling for age, sex, education and head motion; post hoc Tukey-Kramer and GRF correction (voxel-wise p < 0.001, cluster p < 0.05) controlled family-wise error. Clustering performance was assessed by internal (silhouette) and external (clinical accuracy: agreement with diagnostic labels MDD vs HC) indices. We performed leave-one-site-out and ten-fold cross-validation. In each fold multi-view kernel spectral clustering was trained on the training set and held-out participants were directly assigned to nearest training centroids via the k-nearest-neighbor (KNN) search, to achieve individual-level biotype identification; clinical accuracy and silhouette metrics were examined for training and test folds.

Results

Individualized multidimensional symptom/function-specific brain maps. Based on expert evaluations, several closely related items were merged (e.g., early, middle, and late insomnia in HAMD), whereas selected items were excluded from subsequent analyses because no terms in the term corpus met expert consensus. Ultimately, 42 symptom or functional items were retained, and prior seed maps were derived to generated individualized whole-brain symptom/function-specific measures (see Figure 1b,c). These measures formed a theoretically guided, multidimensionally scalable and individualized feature representation for subsequent biotype identification.

Five biotypes with distinct clinical phenotypes of MDD patients. Applying multi-view kernel spectral clustering (k = 5) to the normalized 42-dimensional brain maps from all samples identified five biotypes. Among MDD patients, total HAMD-17 scores did not differ significantly across biotypes (n = 1,314), indicating that the biotypes did not reflect overall depression severity. Significant differences were observed across multiple HAMD item scores (n = 968), revealing dissociable clinical symptom profiles (Figure 2a). Accordingly, the biotypes were characterized as a hypochondriasis-dominant subtype (MDD-HC, see Figure 2a in red), a typical depression with low insight subtype (MDD-Type1, see Figure 2a in blue), a dysregulation subtype (MDD-Type2, see Figure 2a in yellow), an anxiety-insomnia subtype (MDD-Type3, see Figure 2a in pink), and a typical depression with high insight subtype (MDD-Type4, see Figure 2a in green). Notably, MDD patients in the hypochondriasis-dominant subtype showed high hypochondriasis scores with broadly distributed symptom scores and were predominantly mapped into a healthy cluster.

Convergence of brain and symptom profiles across biotypes. Although symptom differences and brain functional differences did not exhibit a strict one-to-one correspondence, and brain functional profiles related to RDoC constructs still require further refinement, the identified biotypes demonstrated a meaningful degree of convergence between clinical symptom profiles and symptom-specific brain functional patterns (Figure 2a,b,c). Importantly, for depressed mood, the core symptom of MDD, biotypes exhibited distinct and representative brain functional patterns (Figure 2b). These patterns were consistent with the biotype-specific functional alterations observed across other symptom dimensions (Figure 2c), i.e., across multiple symptom-specific contrasts, each biotype showed relatively consistent and characteristic brain functional profiles when compared with other biotypes, indicating potentially stable and differentiable symptom-brain profiles and internally coherent symptom-brain associations within biotype.

A diagnostically meaningful and cross-site reproducible classification. Across 144 combinations of fMRI feature representations and clustering methods (k = 2–10), multi-view kernel spectral clustering using normalized multiview brain maps consistently achieved the highest diagnostic alignment with clinical labels. Averaged across k values, this configuration yielded the highest mean clinical accuracy (0.621; Figure 2e), with optimal performance at k = 5 (mean clinical accuracy = 0.661; mean silhouette coefficient = 0.687): one cluster predominantly included healthy controls (clinical accuracy within cluster = 0.73), while four clusters represented distinct biotypes predominantly included MDD patients (clinical accuracy within cluster = 0.56, 0.74, 0.64, and 0.70). Cross-validation analyses further supported the robustness of the classification. In ten-fold cross-validation, mean clinical accuracy was 0.64 in training sets and 0.62 in test sets (Figure 2g). In leave-one-site-out validation, mean clinical accuracy reached 0.64 in training sets and 0.66 in held-out sites (Figure 2f), indicating a diagnostically meaningful and cross-site reproducible biotype classification.

Conclusion

In this study, by integrating brain imaging with clinical symptom and neuroscientific functioning foundations, we propose a theoretically guided approach to characterize the heterogeneity and identify clinically meaningful biotypes of depression. Using individualized resting-state fMRI measures that are explicitly linked to depressive symptoms and neurobiological functions, and applying multiview clustering methods for high-dimensional fMRI measures, we identified five MDD biotypes with distinct clinical and neurofunctional profiles. Moreover, based on accessible resting-state measures, the derived biotypes showed consistent alignment with clinicians’ diagnostic classifications when distinguishing individuals with depression from healthy controls across sites, supporting the robustness and potential translational relevance of the approach.

Compared with alternative fMRI metrics and clustering strategies, the multiview kernel spectral clustering applied to multidimensional dual-regression brain maps yielded superior internal coherence and external clinical validity. Importantly, the identified biotypes were not only separable in clustering space but also interpretable in terms of symptom phenotypes and symptom-relevant brain functional patterns, addressing a key limitation of data-driven subtyping approaches that rely on algorithmic solutions with limited clinical grounding.

This work advances previous neuroimaging-based subtyping studies by explicitly integrating the symptom-based diagnostic criteria with the neuroscience-derived functional framework. While earlier efforts have demonstrated that functional brain patterns can stratify patients into subgroups, such approaches often lack clear correspondence with established clinical constructs. By aligning imaging-derived features with diagnostic manuals and symptom scales, the present study enhances interpretability, facilitates communication between neuroscience and clinical practice, and supports potential generalizability across sites and populations.

More broadly, the present results highlight the value of theory-constrained, multidimensional approaches for addressing the challenges of high-dimensional neuroimaging data and disease heterogeneity. By combining symptom-/function-specific priors with multiview modeling of brain measures, this framework provides a principled and scalable strategy for identifying clinically relevant biotypes. While further longitudinal and predictive validation is warranted, the present findings suggest a promising step toward precision-oriented characterization of depression that is both neuroscientifically grounded and clinically meaningful.
