Introduction

Neural oscillations are fundamental to cortical processing, reflecting neuronal population and network dynamics. Alpha (≈8–12 Hz) and beta (≈13–30 Hz) rhythms correlate with a range of perceptual, sensorimotor, and cognitive processes. Peak oscillatory frequency also varies across individuals, which may be related to neuroanatomical variation. More recently, peak alpha oscillatory frequencies have been found to be associated with better cognitive performance, suggesting that differences in peak frequencies may reflect meaningful variation in function.

Myelination plays a critical role in signal propagation by regulating axonal conduction velocity and temporal synchrony among neuronal populations. This extends to the cortex, where intracortical myelin is emerging as a key factor in modulating communication, with recent work implicating it in neural oscillations. Accumulating evidence has shown relationships between cortical myeloarchitecture and magnetoencephalography (MEG) electrophysiological networks, with brain regions structurally similar in myelin content exhibiting functional connectivity via MEG-derived oscillatory networks1.

Intracortical myelin can be noninvasively estimated using T1-weighted/T2-weighted (T1w/T2w) ratio mapping regionally and at a high resolution. Previous work has linked T1w/T2w myelin estimates to performance and electrophysiological activity during a cognitive task2. This could support the notion that differences in oscillatory dynamics may partially reflect variations in intracortical myelin, and that these relationships may be meaningful in the context of cognition.

In the present study, we explore the relationship between peak alpha and beta oscillatory frequencies and intracortical myelin. Using data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP)3, we investigated whether inter-individual differences in peak alpha and beta frequencies are associated with regional and network-level intracortical myelin content, using T1w/T2w data as a proxy.

Methods

Data were obtained from participants with both MEG and cortical T1w/T2w data (used as a proxy for intracortical myelin) from the Human Connectome Project (HCP). The sample included 89 healthy participants (44 females, 45 males; age range 22–35 years) drawn from multiple HCP releases (Q1, Q2, Q3, S500, S900, MEG2). All MRI and MEG data were acquired and preprocessed as described in HCP protocols3–5. In brief, structural images were collected on the HCP’s custom 3T Siemens Skyra scanner at 0.7 mm isotropic resolution, using a 3D MPRAGE sequence for T1w images and a variable flip angle turbo spin-echo (Siemens SPACE) sequence for T2w images. Cortical myelin-sensitive maps were generated from the T1w/T2w ratio using the HCP processing pipeline providing standardized estimates of intracortical myelin. Peak alpha and beta frequencies were derived from both resting-state and task-related (motor, working memory and language processing) MEG recordings for each participant.

Cortical T1w/T2w data were parcellated into 360 regions according to the HCP Multimodal Parcellation atlas, as well as into 7 functional networks based on Yeo’s atlas. To visualize estimated myelination patterns, average T1w/T2w values across participants were mapped onto the cortical surface in Connectome Workbench for the 360-region parcellation and summarized in bar graphs for the 7 Yeo networks. Using the ppcor library in R, Spearman partial correlations were computed between T1w/T2w values in each region or network and peak alpha and beta frequencies, controlling for age group and gender. Multiple comparisons were addressed using false discovery rate (FDR) correction when testing for statistical significance. Regional correlation patterns were visualized on the cortical surface, while network-level correlations were displayed in bar graphs.

Results

In terms of the 360-region parcellation, while correlations were heterogenous across the cortical surface, T1w/T2w values showed weak-to-moderate positive correlations with peak alpha frequencies in several regions of the cortex (mean rs: 0.06, range: -0.15, 0.32), with the strongest correlations in occipital and frontal regions. On the other hand, T1w/T2w values tended to correlate more negatively, albeit weakly, with peak beta frequencies over the cortex (mean rs: -0.02, range: -0.21-0.20). However, none of these correlations were significant after FDR correction.

At a network level, T1w/T2w values in the 7 networks showed weak but positive correlations with peak alpha frequencies (mean rs = 0.07, range: 0.02-0.13), whereas T1w/T2w values tended to correlate negatively with peak beta frequencies in 5 out of the 7 networks (mean rs = -0.02, range: -0.04, 0.02). None of these correlations were statistically significant.

Conclusion

Although non-significant, these trends are consistent with theoretical predictions linking myelin to oscillatory frequency and previous multimodal evidence showing structural–MEG relationships. Regionally, the T1w/T2w ratio, a proxy of intracortical myelin, correlated most strongly with peak alpha frequencies near occipital and frontal areas, which are regions that typically show high activity in the alpha band in the literature. Additionally, both intracortical myelin measures and peak alpha frequency have been recently found to be related to better cognitive performance, potentially suggesting an advantageous mediating or additive effect on information processing. On the other hand, the correlations between T1w/T2w values and peak beta frequencies tended towards a negative in most regions and networks. Since beta frequencies are thought to reflect more local communication among areas, a negative relationship could suggest that well-myelinated circuits may need less oscillatory beta activity to coordinate activity. However, given the weakness of the correlations, the absence of statistically significant effects and the exploratory nature of the analysis, such interpretations remain speculative.

Importantly, the regional analyses appeared to capture more nuanced spatial patterns than the network-level analyses, suggesting that higher-resolution parcellations may be more sensitive to subtle structure–oscillation relationships. Future studies using larger samples, more granular cortical parcellations, or more targeted regional hypotheses may be better positioned to detect and characterize these effects.
