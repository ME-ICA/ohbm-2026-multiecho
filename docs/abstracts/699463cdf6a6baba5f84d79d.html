Introduction

A long-debated question in the field of cognitive neuroscience is whether or what kind of visual information in the external environment can be attended and detected by the human brain in an automatic and unconscious way. It is well known that physically or biologically salient visual stimuli such as simple features and emotional information attract attention efficiently. Attentional capture by salient features (e.g., abrupt onset, motion, and color) and emotional stimuli (e.g., fearful faces) facilitates us to detect potentially important events (e.g., dangers) quickly and is considered to be essential for human adaptive behavior and survival. It remains unclear, however, whether such attentional capture is a purely bottom-up process or depends on top-down attention and conscious awareness [1-2]. 

By using a perceptual learning (PL) paradigm, our recent studies indicated that well-trained non-salient shapes may capture attention in a distinct way from the salient features do [3, 4, 5]: this attentional capture that followed PL appears when the trained shape is task-irrelevant and is presented outside the focus of top-down spatial attention. In the present study, we aim to investigate whether conscious awareness is necessary for the automatic attentional capture by the learned non-salient shapes.

Methods

The training procedure was similar to that used in our previous studies [3, 4, 5]. Healthy young adults received training of a visual search (VS) task, in which subjects were required to find a target shape within an array of distractors. The target consisted of a triangle having one of four possible orientations (pointing left, right, up or down), surrounded by distractor triangles of two other orientations (Fig. 1A). Each subject was given 8 training sessions within 12 days, with at least one night between consecutive sessions. Each training session consisted of 1,800 trials, of which a random 80% contained a target and 20% contained only distractors. Target detection accuracy was measured according to the formula p’ = (p-fp) / (1-fp), where p and fp refer to the proportions of positive and false-positive responses respectively.

One or two days after the last training session, each subject participated in an EEG test session in which the Trained triangle (i.e., target during the VS training) and Untrained triangle (a similar triangle never presented during the training) pairs were presented peripherally and were irrelevant to the central RSVP task (Fig. 1D). The triangle pairs (Fig. 1B) were presented either with backward masking (Fig. 1C) or not, with the Trained triangle in either the left visual field (LVF) or right visual field (RVF). In order to examine the visibility of the peripheral Trained triangle in the post-training EEG test, a dual-task behavior session was performed right after the EEG session, which used the same stimulus sets as the EEG session. In the dual-task session, besides the central RSVP task, subjects were also required to perform an additional peripheral task of detecting the position (left or right) of the Trained triangle.

Results

Similar to our previous study [3], subjects showed very poor performance at the beginning of training. Their performance improved significantly during the course of training. The final performance level, however, was still considerably lower than is typically observed in feature search tasks with salient targets. In the dual task session after training, subjects can well detect the unmasked Trained triangle when they were required to do that; however, they were lack of reportable awareness of the peripheral Trained triangle (i.e., the accuracy was around chance level) when it was masked immediately after presentation.

The N2pc component of event-related potentials (ERPs) served as the brain marker of attentional capture (Fig. 2). Consistent with our previous findings, the well-trained non-salient shape elicited a significant involuntary N2pc when it was task-irrelevant and outside the focus of top-down spatial attention . Strikingly, this involuntary N2pc appeared even when the non-salient shapes were briefly presented with backward masks and invisible to the participants. This PL-induced N2pc was localized to inferior occipital cortex while emerging at around 170-230 ms post shape onset. 

Figure 2. In the EEG session after training, the peripheral irrelevant Trained triangle elicited a significant N2pc when compared with the Untrained triangle, regardless of whether they were masked or not. At the initial phase (170-220ms), the N2pc showed very similar waveforms and scalp distributions for the Mask and Nomask conditions (A). This initial part of N2pc, which may reflect unconscious and involuntary attentional capture, was localized to inferior occipital cortex (B).

Conclusion

The present findings provide novel and strong neural evidence supporting that, after PL, nonsalient shapes can be automatically detected and attended by the human brain even without awareness. Based on the present and previous studies, we propose that unconscious attentional capture may be mediated by intermediate stages of visual cortical processing involving ventral extrastriate areas.
