Introduction

The dopamine system is central to learning, motivation, and self-regulation. During adolescence, this system undergoes marked reorganization as cognitive control networks mature and vulnerability to psychiatric disorders increases. Central to these changes are the dopaminergic midbrain nuclei, including the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and substantia nigra (SN), which play a critical role in reward learning and adaptive behaviour1.

Reward learning depends on reward prediction errors (RPEs), defined as the discrepancy between expected and actual outcomes2. In humans, striatal BOLD activity reliably tracks RPEs, increasing for unexpected rewards and decreasing for omissions, consistent with reinforcement-learning models. These signals also evolve over time with early learning recruiting subcortical regions when outcomes are surprising and later learning engaging cortical regions as value estimations stabilize3.

Despite the midbrain’s central role in reward processing and learning, the midbrain itself has been largely absent from human studies, especially across development. The VTA and SN are small, deeply situated, and lie adjacent to major blood vessels, making them highly susceptible to noise4. Traditional methods like histology are limited to rare post-mortem samples, and PET imaging is unsuitable for youth. As a result, most neuroimaging work has inferred dopaminergic learning signals indirectly from striatal activity, leaving fundamental questions about midbrain RPE encoding and how these evolve across learning unresolved.

To overcome these methodological barriers, the present study used high-resolution 7-Tesla MRI, which provides the spatial precision and signal sensitivity required to investigate small dopaminergic nuclei. Using a passive, event-related reward-learning task, we tested whether VTA BOLD responses scale with computationally modeled trial-by-trial RPEs and whether these midbrain learning signals change across early versus later phases of learning.

Methods

One hundred and twenty right-handed participants (aged 10–30) from an ongoing developmental study completed a reward-learning experiment. The sample included both adolescents and adults, allowing future developmental comparisons. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. During a single MRI session, high-resolution structural MRI, functional MRI, and physiological data (cardiac and respiratory traces) were acquired.

MRI data were collected using a Siemens MAGNETOM 7T scanner. Anatomical images were acquired using a magnetization-prepared 2 rapid acquisition gradient echo sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 6000 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.13 ms, voxel size = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm, field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm, matrix size = 320 × 320, and 208 sagittal slices. Functional images were acquired using a multiband echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence sensitive to BOLD contrast with the following parameters: TR = 1000 ms, TE = 20 ms, voxel size = 2.3 × 2.3 × 2.3 mm, FOV = 208 × 208 mm, matrix size = 90 × 90, with 60 axial/oblique slices and 600 volumes. 

Participants completed a passive, event-related reward-learning task (10 minutes) in which they viewed three novel fractal cues paired with probabilistic point outcomes. After scanning, participants completed a behavioural assessment consisting of two parts: (1) pairwise comparisons indicating which of two stimuli was associated with higher reward value, and (2) estimating the average points earned for each stimulus.

Preprocessing of the fMRI data and statistical analyses were conducted in SPM12. Rigorous quality control was applied to ensure the quality of the imaging data, including physiological noise modeling with the TAPAS PhysIO toolbox and motion assessment using framewise displacement (FD). Nine participants were excluded due to excessive motion (>5% of volumes exceeding 0.5 mm FD), missing behavioural data, or onset timing drift. The final sample included 111 participants (68 females).

First-level general linear models included trial-wise parametric modulators reflecting computationally estimated RPE magnitude according to the updating average of points following each stimulus during the task. Separate positive and negative RPE modulators were modelled for each stimulus type to capture learning-related changes in BOLD signal over time. Positive RPEs captured trials in which outcomes exceeded participants’ expectations, whereas negative RPEs reflected outcome below-expectation.

In addition to the RPE parametric modulators, each first-level GLM included a full set of nuisance regressors to account for non-neural sources of BOLD variance. Six motion parameters (three translations, three rotations) from SPM’s realignment step were included to capture residual movement-related fluctuations. Physiological noise was modelled using 14 RETROICOR-style cardiac and respiratory regressors generated through the TAPAS PhysIO toolbox, including harmonics, respiratory volume per time, and heart rate variability. Together, these nuisance predictors minimized contamination of midbrain BOLD activity from motion, cardiac pulsatility, and breathing-related artifacts, ensuring that observed effects reflected neural responses rather than physiological confounds.

At the second level, whole-task RPE effects were examined using a contrast comparing positive vs. negative RPEs. This contrast isolated neural activity that scaled with the direction and magnitude of prediction error across the full paradigm. As an exploratory analysis, learning-related changes in RPE signaling over time was addressed by dividing the task into first- and second-half segments. For this analysis, the same contrast used in the whole-task analysis was computed separately for each half of the task, followed by a direct comparison (First Half > Second Half) to identify regions showing greater early-task sensitivity to RPE magnitude. 

Results

To evaluate learning of stimulus–reward contingencies, we analyzed responses from the post-task assessment completed immediately after scanning. Behavioural data from the pairwise comparison questions showed that more than 95% of participants accurately identified the higher-valued stimulus in each pairing, indicating robust internalization of relative reward values. As a further measure of learning, participants estimated the average number of points associated with each stimulus. These estimates closely approximated the true average outcomes, with the modal response matching the actual average for all stimuli. Together, these results demonstrate that participants successfully internalized the probabilistic reward structure, providing strong behavioural evidence that learning occurred over the course of the passive task.

Whole-brain analysis of the contrast between positive and negative prediction errors (FDR-corrected p < .05) revealed robust activation in regions classically implicated in reward processing, including the VTA, caudate, and medial and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex2. Significant engagement was also observed in salience network regions such as the cingulate cortex and anterior insula5, as well as nodes of the frontoparietal control network5. The recruitment of these distributed networks indicates sustained attentional engagement.

During the first half of the task, the contrast between positive and negative RPEs (FWE-corrected p < .05) revealed significant activation in core reward-related regions, including the VTA, caudate also thalamus, and bilateral anterior insula. In contrast, the second half of the task (FWE-corrected p < .05) showed absence of midbrain activity but sustained engagement of the bilateral anterior insula and cingulate cortex, along with emerging activation across frontal and parietal cortices. Additional recruitment was observed in the medial prefrontal cortex, middle cingulate gyrus, and hippocampus, suggesting a shift toward more cortical, evaluative, and memory-related processes as learning stabilized.

To assess whether activation patterns differed meaningfully across the course of learning, we computed a contrast directly comparing early and late task activity for the positive versus negative RPEs. This contrast assigned positive weights to regressors from the first half of the task and negative weights to their matched regressors in the second half (First Half – Second Half).

Results revealed greater activation during early learning in the dopaminergic midbrain, bilateral caudate, thalamus, and cingulate cortex. This pattern suggests that in subcortical regions implicated in reward learning, i.e., midbrain and striatum, prediction error signalling were more strongly engaged during the initial phase of learning, when outcomes are most informative and surprising, and expectations are still being updated3.

Conclusion

This study provides direct evidence that RPEs can be reliably measured in human dopaminergic midbrain nuclei using high-field 7T MRI and a passive reward-learning paradigm. By integrating computational modelling with rigorous physiological and motion noise correction, we showed that BOLD activity in the VTA and striatum scales with RPE magnitude, demonstrating robust sensitivity to learning-related variability.

Our exploratory temporal analyses further reveal distinct learning dynamics across neural systems. In dopaminergic nuclei and striatum, RPE-related responses were strongest early in learning and declined as expectations stabilized, consistent with a transition from surprise-driven updating to stable value representations3. In contrast, salience and frontoparietal networks remained consistently active across the task, suggesting sustained engagement of attention, monitoring, and cognitive control processes even after reward contingencies become predictable5. These dissociable temporal profiles highlight complementary roles of subcortical and cortical systems in reward learning in which midbrain and striatum encode dynamic prediction errors, whereas cortical networks support ongoing task-state representation3.

Methodologically, this work establishes a validated pipeline for isolating midbrain BOLD signals by combining ultra-high-field acquisition, TAPAS PhysIO physiological modelling, framewise-displacement motion control, and computational parametric modulation. This approach mitigates vascular and respiratory contamination, enabling precise measurement of learning signals in small subcortical nuclei. The findings also support the feasibility of using passive learning paradigms in developmental and clinical populations for whom active decision-making tasks might not be feasible.

These results lay the foundation for our future research examining how midbrain learning signals evolve across adolescence and early adulthood. Given adolescence is a period of heightened reward sensitivity and increased psychiatric vulnerability1, characterizing these developmental trajectories has significant implications for understanding normative learning mechanisms as well as risk for disorders involving dopaminergic dysfunction.

Together, this work demonstrates that human midbrain RPE signals are measurable, interpretable, and dynamically organized across learning, providing a methodological and conceptual framework for future developmental and clinical investigations.
