Introduction

For two decades, neuroscience researchers have investigated the potential of fMRI functional connectivity (FC: interregional correlation of low frequency activity) to characterize the functional organization of the human brain. Several studies have related FC metrics to network organization and to cognition and behavior.1 Most studies derive FC from resting-state fMRI (RS) due to its ease of acquisition and generalizability to patients with neurological conditions, to children and to unconscious individuals. In RS, participants are scanned without any cognitive or sensory tasks assigned to them. However, this approach lacks control over the cognitive state of the participants, potentially introducing variability. Minimizing intersubject variability in FC is essential for clinical biomarker development, since variability in normative data will be minimized, maximizing sensitivity to abnormality. An alternative to RS that is just as easy to acquire in patients is fMRI during movie watching (MW). The typically rich content of movies also recruits multiple cognitive and sensory systems, synchronizing activity across participants (particularly in the sensory cortices).2 The driven nature of MW may reduce variability in FC at the group level, enhancing sensitivity to abnormality. In this study, we compared FC from MW and RS (4 sessions each) acquired at 7T in 79 healthy participants (HCP’s1200 release3 ). Data were parcellated4 to reduce computational complexity. We hypothesized that 1) Connectivity estimates are more reliable and less variable across the sample for MW compared to RS, and 2) that individual differences (deviations from mean connectivity for individuals) are different in MW and RS. We tested these hypotheses with FC fingerprinting and variance-decomposition methods.

Methods

Data and Preprocessing

79 healthy, young (22-36), unrelated, adult (n = 41F/38M) participants from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) S1200 release were selected3. All participants were scanned in both MW and RS using a Siemens Magnetom 7T MR scanner (SC72 gradient coil 70–100 mT/m, multiband factor of 5, echo time = 22 ms, repetition time = 1 s, 1.6 mm isotropic voxel size). Four 15-min sessions of RS and MW were each acquired over two days. The data were preprocessed and cleaned using HCP minimal preprocessing, surface alignment, and ICA-FIX cleaning pipelines5. We analyzed the data by surface-based cortical regions of interest (ROIs), using the 360 ROIs of the multimodal Glasser parcellation.

Task Description

In the 4 15-min RS sessions, participants were instructed to lie in the scanner and fixate on a cross on the display. During each 15-min MW session, participants were presented with 4 or 5 video clips (64-259 sec in duration). These were clips from Hollywood movies and independent films and varied in their content; most had some plot features. Clips were separated by 20-sec resting periods; 20-sec rest periods also began and ended each MW session. All movie clips differed, except the same clip, 84 sec long, was the last clip presented in all 4 sessions.

Functional Connectivity Analysis

The activity timeseries of each ROI was indexed as the average activity across vertices in that ROI at each timepoint. Pearson correlation between timeseries from each pair of ROIs indexed their functional connectivity, resulting in a 360 by 360 correlation matrix for each task, session, and participant. Each column of these matrices is a 360*1 vector that represents the connectivity pattern of a particular ROI which we refer to as that ROI’s fingerprint. All analyses were performed on Fisher’s z-transformed fingerprints, separately for each ROI.

Analysis 1. Our connectivity model assumes each ROI has a fingerprint with 3 mutually independent components: 1) a group component, g, reflecting the common fingerprint across participants and sessions, 2) a subject-specific component, s, unique to each individual, and consistent across sessions after taking g into account. This represents reliable (within task), idiosyncratic connectivity. Finally, 3) the measurement noise, ε, is the unexplained fingerprint variance (Fig 1a).

We calculated the total variance for each ROI fingerprint, across subjects and sessions, separately for RS and MW (νtotal = νg + νs + νε). We then decomposed this into the 3 components (νg, νs, and νε). The group component is implicit in the reliability of the ROI fingerprint across sessions, within each subject. Accordingly, for each subject, we calculated the covariance of the ROI fingerprint across the four sessions, yielding explained variance (νexplained =νg + νs). Subtraction of this value from νtotal yields νε. Finally, the covariance across subjects and sessions for each ROI fingerprint yielded the common group variance (νg). By subtracting this from νexplained we estimate νs. The test-retest reliability of fingerprint estimates was defined as the ratio of νexplained over νtotal for each ROI, separately for RS and MW.  The ratio of νg over νexplained , rgroup, indexed the reliable group component (Fig 1a). We then compared these values to evaluate hypothesis 1.  

Analysis 2. To investigate whether idiosyncratic connectivity is captured similarly by RS and MW we subtracted the group average fingerprint of each session and task from the fingerprint for each participant. These residual fingerprints reflect each individual’s deviation from the group mean. The average cosine similarity between the residual fingerprints of RS and MW across all sessions were estimated to index the deviation similarity for each ROI. (Figure 1b) The within-condition average cosine similarities of residual fingerprints were estimated for both tasks, and their geometric mean was statistically compared with the cross-task values for each ROI.

Results

Analysis 1 revealed that the test-retest reliability in FC fingerprints (νexplained / νtotal) is much higher in auditory and visual areas of the temporal and occipital regions when calculated from MW compared to RS. However, FC fingerprints of dorsal medial prefrontal, posterior cingulate, and insular regions were more reliable in RS, compared to MW. The reliability of connectivity fingerprint estimates did not differ between MW and RS in the rest of the brain (Fig 2a – top). The proportion of variance explained by the group component (νg / νexplained) was higher in MW than in RS in the auditory cortex, visual cortex, and regions of the frontal lobes. The group component was higher in RS than in MW In a few scattered ROIs across the frontal and parietal cortex. Variance explained by the group component did not differ between RS and MW in the rest of the cerebral cortex. (Fig 2a – bottom).

Analysis 2 revealed that idiosyncratic fingerprints deviate from the group average in different directions in MW and RS, suggesting that fingerprints are qualitatively distinct between the two paradigms. This was true across the brain except for inferior frontal regions, and in one ROI in the right temporal lobe. These differences in direction are stronger in auditory, visual, and somatosensory cortices (Fig 2b).

Conclusion

In this study we investigated the FC fingerprints of 79 healthy individuals in MW and RS conditions. We aimed to compare the two paradigms in terms of the reliability, group-level similarity, and individual variability of the fingerprint estimates.

In our first analysis, we broke down the variance of fingerprints into 3 components reflecting the component attributable to the group,  a component reliable across sessions but attributabel to the individual, and the measurement noise. MW yielded more reliable estimates of FC in auditory and visual cortices. This is consistent with the greater demand for audiovisual processing in the MW condition. The observed reliability in these regions in MW that are absent in RS reflect the robust functional organization that is evoked during audiovisual stimulation consistently regardless of the content of the movie.

RS evoked more reliable connectivity patterns in prefrontal, posterior cingulate, and insular regions. These regions are part of the default-mode network (DMN) which is associated with mind-wandering – also known as task-negative network. Greater activation of the DMN during RS may be why organization appears more reliable in these regions. We did not observe any differences between the two tasks in the rest of the cortex. These results together imply that depending on the ROI, MW or RS could yield more replicable and reliable fingerprints within an individual, and that neither of these paradigms is superior at a whole-brain level; rather, it may be best to combine MW and RS to benefit from the strengths of both.

The second analysis revealed that idiosyncratic variations from the group mean fingerprints are not the same in MW and RS. This dissimilarity is more salient in the auditory, visual, and somatosensory networks. These regions show reliable fingerprint estimates in both MW and RS and therefore the differences are not due to noise. As they are also associated with early processes and auditory and visual networks are engaged with the audiovisual content in MW, we conclude our observation shows distinct qualitative fingerprints for these regions, suggesting that each paradigm recruits distinct modes of functional organization. 

In summary, we conclude that MW and RS each capture different aspects of the functional organization of the human brain. In an individual level, depending on which region of the brain is of interest of a study, one of the paradigms could yield more reliable FC estimates. The combination of RS and MW may yield ROI-based norms that are most sensitive to abnormality in patients with neurological disorders such as epilepsy.
