Introduction

The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) has traditionally been divided into dorsal, perigenual, and subgenual sectors, with distinct functions ascribed to each. However, this anatomical taxonomy does not align cleanly with the functional organization of the ACC. Functional activations and connectivity patterns frequently traverse cytoarchitectonic borders. Moreover, depending on the measurement and clustering methods used, parcellations defined by functional connectivity (FC) can vary across studies^1,2, implying that apparent functional “modules” may depend strongly on the scale and perspective of the analysis.

Recent work on connectivity gradients offers a complementary view by suggesting that finer-grained gradients may underlie coarser, patchy boundaries inferred from clustering^3. Yet, extant gradient studies have been almost entirely data driven. The relationship between the gradient-indicated functional topography and that based on traditional demarcations remains unclear.

In this study, we address this question by examining spatial gradients of ACC connectivity with the prefrontal cortex (PFC). Our rationale is to define gradients that are anatomically grounded – anchored to axes that respect ACC morphology and known functional organization of the PFC – rather than abstract dimensions derived from manifold learning alone. Based on recent findings of anatomical connectivity gradients across ACC subregions^4, we hypothesize that such gradients will be mirrored in the resting-state FC patterns. We tested this hypothesis by dividing the ACC and the PFC into evenly sized parcels that follow the longitudinal axis of the ACC and the anterior-posterior (A-P) axis of the PFC. Topographically organized, gradually changing FC strength along these axes would suggest functional gradients supported by the underlying anatomical connectivity strength. By relating structure and function, we aim to refine current models of ACC contributions to cognition and affect.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing. Seven-Tesla (7T) resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were freely obtained from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) database. A total of 175 individuals (age 22-36 years, 107 females) who had also completed diffusion and structural scans were included. The details of participants, procedure, and MRI acquisition have been documented in corresponding publications^5. All HCP subject recruitment procedures and informed consent forms, including consent to share de-identified data, were approved by the Washington University Institutional Review Board. All data were minimally preprocessed following the HCP pipeline. 

Definition of ACC and PFC subregions. The ACC subregions were defined by subdividing area 24 and area 32 of each hemisphere into 10 equal-distanced labels along the longitudinal axis in the fsaverage template brain (Fig. 1a).  Starting from the most caudal-dorsal position, the subregions were ordered from 1 to 10 towards the rostral-ventral end of the axis. The PFC subregions were based on the Schaefer 500 atlas in the same fsaverage template. Labels from the Desikan-Killiany atlas were used to define the spatial extent of the PFC, such that labels from the Schaefer atlas intersecting with any of these Desikan-Killiany labels were marked as within-PFC: medial orbitofrontal, frontal pole, superior frontal, rostral middle frontal, caudal middle frontal, lateral orbitofrontal, pars opercularis, pars triangularis, parsorbitalis, and insula. To create subregions along the A-P axis, the MNI coordinates of each PFC label were extracted from the volumetric template of the same Schaefer 500 atlas. The PFC labels were then grouped by average y-coordinate into 5 subregions that evenly divided the A-P range covered by all the PFC labels (Fig. 1b).

FC analysis.  Two types of FC measures were used for gradient analysis. For each pair of ACC subregion and Schaefer label, mean FC was measured by Pearson’s correlation between the 900-second BOLD time series from each subregion (Fig. 1c). Rate FC was measured by first dividing the BOLD signals into 15 60-second windows and computing Pearson’s correlation within each window. Then, for each ACC subregion, a threshold was applied to the window-wise FC, and the fraction of above-threshold windows was defined as the rate-FC (Fig. 1d). The threshold was determined by the 70th percentile of window-wise FC values between the ACC subregion and all of the 250 Schaefer labels in the same hemisphere. The rationale of using all 250 rather than the PFC labels was to identify transiently strong ACC-PFC connections relative to the average FC level across the brain, so that the mean FC and rate FC provide complementary measures of FC strength and do not necessarily implicate each other. Different percentile cutoff values in the range of 10 to 90 were tested, and no qualitative changes in the rate FC map were observed between 70 and 90. Thus, the 70th percentile was chosen as a conservative cutoff value without qualitatively affecting the result. Finally, the mean FC and rate FC values for each PFC subregion were calculated by averaging the connectivity values within each subregion. 

Gradient analysis.  A spatial gradient was defined as a pattern of gradual change following the relative positions on the anatomical axis being examined without interruption. Gradients were evaluated from two perspectives: First, the change in FC value for each PFC subregion was assessed relative to the ACC subregion's position. Second, the change in FC value for each ACC subregion was evaluated according to the PFC subregion position. To test the statistical significance of observed gradients, a two-way ANOVA was conducted, with the ACC and PFC subregions, classified by relative position along their respective anatomical axes, as factors.

Results

The mean FC and rate FC maps both captured classical resting-state network patterns with the ACC subregions: The dorsal ACC subregion in area 32 showed strong connectivity with the anterior insula (Fig. 1c & 1d, middle panel), indicating the cingulo-opercular network; the ventral ACC subregions in area 32 and area 24 both showed strong connectivity with the posterior cingulate cortex, the angular gyrus and the dorsolateral PFC, indicating the default-mode network (Fig. 1c & 1d, right panel). These network patterns were more prominent in the rate FC than in the mean FC measurement. 

Beyond the classical network patterns, we observed gradient patterns in the ACC-PFC connectivity evaluated along the longitudinal axis for the ACC subregions and the A-P axis for the PFC subregions (Fig. 2), and separately for areas 24 and 32. The gradient patterns were consistent between the mean and rate FC measures (Fig. 2, top vs. bottom), but qualitatively differed between areas 24 and 32 (Fig. 2, left vs. right).

From the PFC perspective, the gradient manifests as a sequential rearrangement of the FC pattern across ACC subregions. The most posterior PFC subregion showed the strongest FC with the ACC subregion 1 (consistently for areas 24 and 32), with the FC strength decreasing gradually towards subregion 10 (Fig. 2, A-P range -30 – -10). Conversely, the most anterior PFC subregion showed the lowest connectivity with ACC subregion 1, with the FC strength increasing gradually towards ACC subregions 7 & 8 (Fig. 2, A-P range 50–70). In between the two patterns, PFC subregions located in the intermediate A-P ranges showed gradual shifts from one pattern to the other, with the amount of shift following the relative A-P position of each region. 

From the ACC perspective, the gradient manifests as a sequential rearrangement of the FC pattern across the PFC subregions. Moreover, such a rearrangement was observed for area 32 only. Subregion 1 of area 32 showed the strongest FC with the most posterior PFC subregion, the second strongest FC with the second most posterior PFC region, and so on. In other words, the FC strength of the PFC subregions followed the same order of their relative A-P positions (Fig. 2, area 32, subregion 1, comparing points vertically). Conversely, for subregion 10 of area 32, the FC strength followed the opposite order of the PFC subregions’ relative A-P positions (Fig. 2, area 32, subregion 10, comparing points vertically). The area 32 subregions located in the intermediate range showed a gradual rearrangement of the FC strength, with the strongest FC found with the PFC subregion, whose relative position on the A-P axis matched the relative position of the ACC subregion. For most subregions of area 24, the FC strength with PFC subregions followed the same order, with stronger FC found with more anterior PFC subregions (Fig. 2, area 24, comparing points vertically).

To test the statistical significance of the observed patterns, for each FC measure, we first conducted a four-way ANOVA with ACC area, hemisphere, PFC subregion and ACC subregion as factors and found a significant four-way interaction (mean FC: F(76, 34800) = 4.13, p < 0.001; rate FC: F(76, 34800) = 11.281, p <  0.001). Then, averaging across hemispheres, we conducted a two-way ANOVA for each ACC area separately, with PFC subregion and ACC subregion as factors, and found a two-way interaction in each test (mean FC, area 24: F(36, 8700) = 9.599, p < 0.001; mean FC, area 32: F(36, 8700) = 26.438, p < 0.001; rate FC, area 24: F(36, 8700) = 76.697, p < 0.001; rate FC, area 32: F(36, 8700) = 287.766, p < 0.001). The F-values suggest a greater effect size for rate FC than for mean FC, and for area 32 than for area 24. 

Conclusion

Together, our findings indicate that the FC strength between the ACC and PFC follows a highly organized spatial gradient along the longitudinal axis of the ACC and the A-P axis of the PFC. These gradients are anatomically grounded in the sense that they follow native anatomical coordinates rather than abstract dimensions defined by manifold embedding techniques. This organization suggests that ACC-PFC coupling is constrained by underlying spatial structure and is therefore amenable to systematic description and cross-study comparison.

A key observation concerns area 32, for which the gradients derived from the ACC perspective and the PFC perspective converge. The ACC and PFC subregions that show the strongest FC were located at corresponding positions along their longitudinal and A-P axes, respectively. This topographic correspondence is consistent with findings from nonhuman primate tract-tracing studies, where anatomical connectivity strength between the ACC and PFC showed similar correspondence among their subregions^4. The close agreement strongly supports an anatomical foundation of the FC gradients observed in the current study.

The spatial pattern of the gradients further aligns with current views of the ACC and PFC’s functional topography, indicating potential functional significance of these gradients. Along the longitudinal axis of the ACC, dorsal, perigenual, and subgenual sectors have been broadly associated with cognitive control, choice evaluation & flexible switching, and affective processing, respectively. Along the A-P axis of the PFC, more posterior regions are thought to support lower-level, more concrete aspects of working memory and control, whereas more anterior regions support higher-level, more abstract representations. The gradients we identify provide a possible bridge between these two schemes: by smoothly linking specific ACC subregions to progressively more anterior or posterior PFC subregions, they suggest a continuous mapping between the ACC functional roles and the hierarchical organization of PFC.

From the ACC perspective, we also identified a systematic difference between areas 32 and 24. Whereas the FC gradient of area 32 traverses the PFC hierarchy in a more graded fashion, the FC gradient for area 24 is dominated by coupling with anterior PFC: nearly all area 24 subregions show their strongest connectivity with the most anterior PFC subregions, with a gradual decline in strength toward more posterior subregions. This pattern is compatible with the view that area 32 is more strongly associated with cognitive and evaluative processes, whereas area 24 is more affective-limbic in nature and preferentially linked to systems involved in pain and affective salience.

Finally, our approach has limitations that suggest avenues for future work. We used MNI coordinates to subdivide the PFC along its a-P axis, which, although practical and reproducible, remains somewhat arbitrary relative to fine-grained anatomical landmarks. Future studies could refine the definition of PFC subregions using more detailed cytoarchitectonic maps, subject-specific anatomical features, or multimodal parcellations that integrate structural and functional information. Extending this anatomically grounded gradient framework to other large-scale networks, and to individual-level analyses, may further clarify how continuous connectivity motifs in the ACC and PFC support flexible cognition, affect, and behavior.
