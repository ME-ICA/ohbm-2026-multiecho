Introduction

Cognitive flexibility refers to the ability to switch between task rules, which entails a ‘switch-cost’ manifesting as slower and less accurate responses. Prior literature have shown distinct causal roles of left-prefrontal and left-parietal cortices during task-preparation, where prefrontal regions including inferior frontal gyrus and preSMA have been linked to updating task goals1 and switching response sets2 respectively. The intraparietal sulcus in the parietal cortex has been shown to be associated with updating response-sets1. However, it remains unclear how these region-specific neural processes evolve during task-switching and when within a trial they become causally critical for switching. Identifying this temporal characterization is crucial for understanding how cognitive control operates and guiding interventions in pathological conditions characterized by cognitive rigidity. To address this, we used a multimodal, data-driven approach combining EEG and TMS where participants performed a number-letter switching task, allowing us to investigate motor switching, cognitive switching, or both. We hypothesized that the cognitive switching would have the highest switch cost and that, although the pre-frontal (preSMA) and parietal cortex (left inferior parietal cortex) may play a causal role in task-switching, their contributions to cognitive switching would be distinct. We expected the preSMA will be critical during the late response-preparation phase, whereas IPL will be critical during earlier stimulus-response integration.

Methods

We investigated neural mechanisms underlying cognitive flexibility using a multimodal approach combining EEG, TMS and TMS-EEG across three experimental sessions (Figure 1). Twenty-five healthy right-handed adults (13 F, mean age = 26.4 ± 4 years) completed Experiment 1, 16 of them completed Experiment 2, and 13 completed an additional control session (Experiment 3). In experiment 1, EEG was recorded while participants performed a bimanual number-letter switching task that yielded four trial-types in a 2x2 design defined by whether the task rule and/or response hand changed relative to the previous trial, thereby yielding four trial-types: no switch (NS: same rule and same response), motor-switch (MS: same rule, different response), cognitive switch (CS: different rule, same response), and cognitive-motor switch (CMS: different rule, different response). In experiment 2, single monophasic TMS pulses were delivered over specific locations and at time points identified as behaviorally relevant by experiment 1, and EEG was recorded concurrently at 10 kHz during task performance. In experiment 3, TMS alone was delivered over the right occipital cortex and at different time points as control conditions during performance of the same task.

EEG was recorded with 64 channels at 1 kHz, preprocessed and analyzed at both sensor- and source-levels. ERPs were decomposed into stimulus-, response-, and central-components using residue iteration decomposition (RIDE). Time-frequency analyses used Morlet wavelets (1-40 Hz). EEG source localization identified left preSMA and left IPL as task relevant. Using these EEG substrates, we delivered single-pulse monophasic TMS to each region in separate stimulation blocks during task performance with concurrent EEG. PreSMA was stimulated in an individually determined 400-600 ms response-preparation window derived from participant’s no-switch RTs, to perturb the frontal negativity and mid-frontal theta band (preSMALATE), whereas IPL was stimulated to disrupt the parietal P300 component (IPLEARLY). The right occipital cortex (OCCLATE) site served as a control stimulation site. To control for pulse timing, we additionally perturbed preSMA at 200 ms (preSMAEARLY) and IPL at the individually estimated no-switch RT (IPLLATE).

Behavioral data in the Experiment 1 were analyzed for differences in RT, accuracy and normalized-RT (RT/accuracy) scores with non-parametric repeated measures ANOVA. For Experiment 2 and 3, we collated all stimulation conditions in a single linear mixed-effects model to test for stimulation-conditions X trial-types interactions.

Results

In experiment 1, behavioral analysis showed significant main effect of trial-types on reaction time, accuracy, and normalized reaction time (all, p < 0.001), with CS condition producing the largest switch cost (Figure 2A). EEG data revealed distinct neural signatures for motor and cognitive switch behavior. The ERP of CMS vs MS contrast revealed larger P300 for CMS condition (200-400 ms, p < 0.01) localized to left IPL. The CS vs NS contrast showed a sustained frontocentral negativity (400-600 ms, p < 0.01) and localized to the left preSMA. Time-frequency analysis using the Morlet wavelet method revealed increased mid-frontal theta power for the CS vs NS contrast (400-600ms). Guided by these results and supported by prior fMRI/TMS literature, preSMALATE (400-600 ms) and IPLEARLY (200 ms) were selected for testing causality, empirically using concurrent TMS-EEG in experiment 2. 

In experiment 2 and 3, we found that stimulation of left preSMA at the timing of the frontocentral negativity (preSMALATE) selectively disrupted cognitive switching. Linear mixed-effects analysis of z-score normalized reaction times (RTs on TMS trials expressed as z-score relative to no-TMS trials RTs) showed a significant stimulation-condition X trial-type interaction (p = 0.001). Post-hoc analysis revealed that preSMALATE condition slowed only cognitive switch trials (CMS/CS, p < 0.001) but not repeat trials (NS/MS, p > 0.05). Crucially, this effect was specific only to the preSMALATE condition, there were no effects of trial-type for other stimulation conditions (Figure 2B). 

Preliminary TMS-EEG analysis showed no clear changes in TMS-evoked potentials, yet preSMALATE stimulation reduced mid-frontal theta power that was elevated for CS trials at baseline and bringing it closer to NS levels, suggesting disruption of theta-mediated cognitive control.

Conclusion

In this study, we used a data-driven approach to investigate the causal role of frontoparietal cortices in task-switching and to determine when during a trial these regions become critical for successful switching. Consistent with task-switching literature, behavioral results from experiment 1 confirmed that cognitive switch trials produced the largest switch-cost, thus validating the sensitivity of our paradigm. Furthermore, ERP results revealed distinct spatiotemporal signatures – a P300 in the inferior parietal cortex and late frontal negativity in preSMA which served as precise targets for the subsequent TMS-EEG experiment. In accordance with our hypothesis, experiments 2 and 3 demonstrated that late phase preSMA activity, specifically during the late response-preparation phase (400-600 ms) of the trial, is causal for task switching. The preSMALATE condition selectively slowed cognitive switch (CS/CMS) but not repeat (NS/MS) trials. In contrast, IPL and occipital stimulation had no effect on switch costs despite robust parietal activation. This dissociation suggests that while IPL may index task-set representations3, active reconfiguration required to overcome cognitive switch-cost is uniquely driven by preSMA4. This effect coincided with elevated mid-frontal theta, consistent with accounts linking mid-frontal theta to cognitive control at response selection5. By identifying this critical time window for preSMA, the study provides a precise target for temporally specific neuromodulation aimed at enhancing task-switching performance and reducing cognitive rigidity in clinical populations.
