Introduction

“The order in which the fingers of the musician fall onto the keys or finger-board is determined by the signature of the composition.” Back in 1951, Karl Lashley1 used this example to illustrate the hierarchical nature of serial behaviour, highlighting that the selection and ordering of serial motor commands is governed by higher-level, abstract action plans. Today, 75 years on, the hierarchical nature of motor behaviour is firmly established and is frequently associated with prefrontal gradients of cognitive control2. 

In the domain of music, recent work on piano performance has demonstrated a similar prefrontal gradient3, in line with Lashley’s idea: Pianists imitating unfamiliar chord sequences without auditory feedback were shown to prioritize predictions of what chord to play next (e.g., which keys to press according music-structural rules) over the selection of how to play those chords (e.g., which fingers to move). These two levels of action plans were reflected in distinct fronto-temporal (what) and fronto-parietal (how) neural networks. Both networks converged in the lateral prefrontal cortex (LPFC), with anterior regions contributing to musical structure-building and posterior regions to movement planning. 

However, this study left unclear how these two levels of action representation interact. How do temporally extended, music-structural plans govern local low-level motor plans?

Methods

To answer this question, we reanalysed the fMRI data of Bianco et al. (2022)3. In this study, 26 professional pianists (8 females, 17.1 ± 5.5 years of training, 25.6 ± 4.1 years old; mean ± SD) had played novel musical chord sequences on a muted MR-compatible piano by imitating a model hand on screen (Fig. 1a). A Prediction by Partial Matching (PPM) model4 was applied to the stimuli to estimate the predictability (information content, IC) of each chord, in terms of musical structure (what, structure IC) and finger selection (how, motor IC; Fig. 1b). We hypothesised that higher structural predictability of chords should result in stronger facilitation of motor plans, and may be indicated by a modulation of activity in hand motor regions and faster response times.

Functional images were acquired in a 3 Tesla Siemens PRISMA whole-body MR scanner using a 32-radio frequency-channel head coil (EPI with TE = 30 ms, TR = 2000 ms, 37 interleaved slices, 3×3×3.68 mm3 voxel size, 210×210 FOV, 77° flip angle).

fMRI data were analysed with SPM12 using standard preprocessing procedures. A linear model was first fitted on the subject level with one boxcar function modelling the onset of each chord, standardized parametric modulators coding chord-position (chord 1, 2, 3, or 4 in the sequence to remove effects of motor effort at sequence onset or monitoring demands at sequence end), motor IC, and structure IC, as well as 6 motion parameters. These regressors were convolved with the canonical haemodynamic response function. Parametric modulators were orthogonalized such that each modulator accounted only for variance not explained by preceding modulators (Fig. 1c). Two first-level t-contrasts were computed in each participant, estimating the positive and the negative relationship between brain activity and structure IC (while controlling for chord-position in the sequence and motor IC). These first-level contrast images were fed into the second-level group analysis using one-sample t-tests against zero. Results were thresholded at a cluster-forming threshold of p < .001 at the voxel level and p < .05 FWE-corrected at the cluster level.

Results

A parametric modulation of brain activity with higher structural predictability (Fig. 1d) was found in left M1 (BA4/6) controlling pianists’ right hand (used for performance), as well as bilateral lateral occipital cortex. In line with our hypothesis, this suggests that structurally predictable chords indeed facilitated motor plans for executing the chord, along with visual expectations (which chord will be displayed next on screen).

The reverse contrast for structurally less predictable chords indicated strong involvement of bilateral LPFC (BA44/45/47), along with insula and SMG (BA39/40), amongst others, replicating our previous findings in LPFC. Given that predictability was particularly low at the beginning of the sequences (Fig. 1b), and the ordinal position of chords was controlled for in the model (Fig. 1c), we attribute this finding to LPFC’s established role in musical structure building5. 

Conclusion

Lashley was right: Higher-level, abstract action plans—here operationalized as the rule-based structure of chord sequences—can govern the selection of local motor plans, as shown by increasingly strong activity in left primary hand motor cortex when abstract plans became more predictable, ready, and set for execution. Moreover, LPFC was confirmed to play a key role in music-structural processing beyond perception5, including music production3.
