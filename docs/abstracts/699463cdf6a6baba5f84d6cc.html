Introduction

Predictive processing (PP) posits that the brain generates internal predictions to optimise behaviour, based on prior beliefs and incoming sensory information 1. Within PP, expectation suppression is the attenuation of neural responses to expected stimuli 2. In light of competing ‘dampening’ and ‘sharpening’ accounts of ES (which suggest that ES is underpinned by activity reduction of neurons tuned towards, or away from the expected stimuli, respectively), the Opposing Process Theory 3 seeks to reconcile these accounts and theorises that both processes may occur at different time points in the predictive process. The (OPT) has suggested that initial sharpening is followed by later dampening of the neural representations of the expected stimulus. Our recent EEG study 5 yielded direct evidence that ES is expressed as two opposing mechanisms, sharpening followed by dampening within trials, but dampening followed by sharpening across trials as learning takes place. We hypothesise that within-trial latency differences in valid vs. invalid trials might be mediated by relatively lower- vs. higher-level regions, while across-trial timing differences suggest that higher-order regions may learn statistics faster than lower-order regions. This is supported by earlier research which proposes a distinct mechanism, i.e., top-down pattern-separability, through which predictions exert their effects 5. In this 7T fMRI study, we test if these dissociable dynamics of expectation effects can be explained in the context of hierarchical learning mechanisms.

Methods

Experimental paradigm

Approximately 650 previously generated images, from four categories, were taken from the stimuli pool in McDermott et al. (2024). Approximately 700 sound files of monosyllabic words were also generated using artificial intelligence software Voicebooking (Voicebooking.com BV, 2021-2025). Sound files were pitch-controlled to be easily  distinguishable from scanner noise. The experimental paradigm was also adapted from McDermott et al. (2024), modified to include auditory stimuli. Participants were exposed to pairs of stimuli, comprising artificially generated natural scenes drawn from different scene categories, followed by artificially generated monosyllabic words split into male and female voices. In each trial, participants were presented with a Leading stimulus ie. an image from one category for 1000 ms, followed by a Trailing stimulus, ie. a spoken monosyllabic word for 1000 ms, with 1000 ms interstimulus interval and 4-6 TRs intertrial interval (Fig. 1A). A fixation cross was presented throughout the experiment. Each image belonged to one of four categories (‘forest’, ‘beach’, ‘church’, ‘library’), while each word belonged to one of two categories (‘male’, ‘female’). To control for repetition suppression (RS), categorised images and individual words were used to avoid the repetition of stimuli. As such, each individual image and word was only presented once. Category pairs and the transitional probabilities between them were determined by the transitional probability matrix depicted in Fig. 1B. Specifically, categories were paired in a 2:1 manner, where two different ‘Leading’ categories could result in one ‘Trailing’ category with 75% validity. For example, both ‘beach’ and ‘forest’ as ‘Leading’ categories would result in ‘male’ as a ‘Trailing’ category with 75% validity. Participants were not informed of the associations between categories, and were instead instructed to count the number of times a word was spoken backwards, which occurred in ~5% of trials. This acted as catch trials to ensure participants maintained attention to the stimuli. The occurrence of these catch trials was randomised, as was trial order. Participants did not undergo a training session, such that the neural signatures of early learning processes may be investigated using laminar fMRI. Each participant completed eight blocks consisting of 80 trials per block, totalling ~ 90 mins of testing. 

Data Analysis

Data were collected using a 7T Siemens Magneton System with a single-channel transmit and 32-channel receive NOVA head coil (Nova Medical Inc., USA). To define anatomical structures and the gray matter ribbon we collected T1 weighted images using a Magnetization Prepared 2 Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echo (MP2RAGE) sequence (0.7 mm isotropic, 240 slices, TR = 5000 ms, TE = 2.39 ms, TI1/TI2 = 800/2750 ms, FA1/FA2 = 4°/5°, GRAPPA Factor 3). Functional (T2* weighted) data were collected using 2D gradient-echo (GE) multi-band (MB) echo planar imaging (EPI) at 0.8 mm isotropic resolution (58 slices, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 26 ms, MB Factor 2, GRAPPA Factor = 3, phase encoding direction set to posterior to anterior).

Anatomical and functional data was analysed using BrainVoyager (BV, version 23.2, Brain Innovation, Maastricht, the Netherlands) and custom Python scripts. Preprocessing included slice scan time correction using sinc interpolation, 3D motion correction using trilinear interpolation and high-pass filtering (GLM Fourier) with a cut-off of 7 cycles per run. We conducted a  whole-brain voxel-wise GLM contrast to compare valid versus invalid conditions averaged across all 8 runs. Cluster thresholding was applied at p < 0.001 and results were visualised in coronal, sagittal, and transverse views (Fig. 2). Data were analysed using a general linear model and support vector classification.

Results

We acquired high-resolution functional images at 0.65 isotropic resolution at 7T covering the primary and higher-order auditory cortex (Heschl’s Gyrus, Planum Polare and Temporale, Superior Temporal Gyrus), prefrontal cortex (Inferior Frontal Gyrus), and medial temporal lobe (Hippocampus). Whole-brain voxel-wise analyses revealed significant activation differences between valid and invalid trials The resulting activation map identified clusters of significant activation primarily in the auditory cortex and occipital cortex. Significant clusters were observed with peak activation at t = 10.889 in Heschl’s gyrus.

Conclusion

We used the increased SNR of 7T to collect 0.65 mm isotropic functional data covering auditory and frontal regions. Using a visual-auditory statistical learning paradigm we aimed at distinguishing the dissociable dynamics of expectation effects in the context of hierarchical learning mechanisms. Our initial results demonstrate sufficient data quality to reliably detect cortical activation in response to predictable auditory stimuli. This leads the way for more extensive analyses, including decoding analyses to parse out the opposing effects of sharpening and dampening. Based on the Opposing Process Theory 3 and our previous EEG study 4, we expect to find sharpening and dampening effects at different time points throughout the experiment, corresponding to either decreased variance (sharpening) or amplitude (dampening) for responses to valid stimuli.
