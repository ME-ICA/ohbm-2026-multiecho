Introduction

Childhood trauma (CT) (physical or emotional abuse, neglect and sexual violence) is a well-known vulnerability factor for various psychiatric disorders.1 It is also associated with earlier onset and more severe clinical expression of these disorders. However, its underlying cognitive and neurofunctional mechanisms remain largely unexplored from a transdiagnostic perspective. Among the brain structures involved, the amygdala plays a central role, particularly in the emotion regulation (ER) difficulties frequently observed following adversity.2 Yet, studies on its functional connectivity remain inconsistent, mainly due to an approach considering amygdala as a unitary structure.3 This study aims to investigate, from a transdiagnostic perspective, the links between exposure to early adverse experiences and alterations in emotion regulation, using a cognitive and a neurofunctional approach. Specifically, we will examine how these difficulties relate to altered resting-state functional connectivity of the medial and lateral amygdala nuclei with prefrontal regions and the hippocampus.

Methods

136 participants (M=34.6 years) from the Transdiagnostic Connectome Project were included : 62 patients with mood disorder, post-traumatic stress or anxiety disorders, and 74 healthy controls. CT were assessed with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form (CTQ-SF); ER strategies with the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (CERQ-Short) and diagnoses with the SCID-5.

Spearman correlations were used to examine the relationship between CT and ER strategies, using the Bonferroni method for multiple comparisons corrections. We performed multivariate regressions to compare patients and controls’ scores while adjusting for age and sex. 

Resting-state fMRI data were acquired on two harmonized Siemens 3 T Prisma scanners (TR= 800 ms, TE=37 ms, 488 volumes, voxel size= 2mm3). T1-weighted image was acquired using an MPRAGE sequence (total of 144 slices,TR= 2200 ms, TI= 1100 ms; slice thickness= 1.2 mm and image resolution= 1.2 mm3). Resting-state data  were preprocessed with MRIQC and fMRIPrep, and denoised using 24 motion parameters (including rotation and translation, their first derivatives and the squares of both), white matter and CSF signals. Mean BOLD ROI time series were extracted using a custom Brainnetome-based atlas including bilateral amygdala (medial and lateral), hippocampus (caudal and rostral) and PFC subregions (22 ROI in total). Pearson correlations between ROI time series were computed and Fisher z-transformed to derive subject-level connectivity matrices. 

ROI-to-ROI analyses based on edge-wise linear models were applied to investigate connectivity alterations involving amygdala subregions. Resting state z-transformed functional connectivity values (zFC) were modeled independently for each edge involving amygdala ROIs, using two separate interaction models : Model 1 (Childhood trauma) : zFC ~ Group (patients vs controls) × CTQ + age + sex and Model 2 (Emotional regulation) : zFC ~ Group (patients versus control) × ER_non_adaptive + age + sex. Multiple comparisons corrections were performed separately for each amygdala node (FDR correction).

Results

Preliminary analyses suggest an association between CT and maladaptive ER strategies, supporting the idea that early-life stress may be related to difficulties in managing emotions. In the full sample, including both patients and healthy controls, Spearman’s correlations adjusted with Bonferroni correction showed significant associations between CTQ total score and self-blame (ρ = 0.28, pcor < 0.01), catastrophizing (ρ = 0.46, pcor < 0.001), and rumination (ρ = 0.34, pcor < 0.01). This suggests that individuals with higher exposure to childhood adversity tend to rely more on negative and self-critical thinking when regulating emotions.

Emotional maltreatment was particularly associated with maladaptive ER strategies : emotional abuse was significantly related to self-blame (ρ = 0.32, pcor < 0.01), catastrophizing (ρ= 0.43, pcor  < 0.001), and rumination (ρ = 0.33, pcor  < 0.01) and emotional neglect to catastrophizing (ρ = 0.41, pcor < 0.001) and rumination (ρ = 0.32, pcor < 0.05). In contrast, sexual and physical abuses did not show significant correlations with ER strategies after Bonferroni correction. 

Preliminary (non corrected) fMRI results on 90 participants suggest that in patients, higher CT was associated with stronger connectivity between left medial amygdala and left rostral hippocampus in comparison to control (β = 0.106, p < 0.05). Regarding emotional regulation, higher use of non-adaptive ER strategies was associated with increased left vlPFC-left lateral amygdala connectivity (β = 0.060, p < 0.05) for both patients and controls. Although patients show overall reduced connectivity between those two regions compared to controls (β = -0.081, p < 0.05), we did not observe a significant interaction Group x ER_non_adaptive on this edge (β = - 0.03, p > 0.05). 

Conclusion

These preliminary findings suggest that childhood trauma is associated with an increased use of maladaptive emotion regulation strategies, particularly catastrophizing, rumination, and self-blame. Emotional abuse and neglect seem to have the strongest associations with these cognitive difficulties, suggesting that these types of maltreatment, often overlooked compared to other forms of abuse, may have significant consequences on emotional regulation. Consistent with current litterature, these results also highlight the importance of considering the specific nature of childhood trauma, rather than solely focusing on cumulative adversity, when investigating its impact on emotional regulation. 

MRI preliminary findings show that examining the distinct subregions of the amygdala is particularly relevant for understanding emotion regulation difficulties in individuals with a history of trauma, in a transdiagnostic framework. Differences between patients and controls in medial amygdala-hippocampus connectivity, associated with CTQ scores, may reflect amygdala involvement in memory-related networks, although this link is more consistently reported for lateral regions.4 In terms of emotional regulation, reduced connectivity between left vlPFC and left lateral amygdala in patients compared to controls may indicate a transdiagnostic disruption of the amygdala-vlPFC pathway involved in top-down emotional regulation.5 These findings point to distinct functional contributions of medial and lateral amygdala subregions, which future consolidation of these results will help to clarify. 

A key aspect of this study is its transdiagnostic approach, which combines self-reported emotion regulation strategies and neurofunctional data. By linking cognitive and neural mechanisms, this study could help to identify specific brain circuits altered in individuals with psychiatric disorders struggling with emotion regulation difficulties and childhood trauma history. Understanding these disruptions could lead to new therapeutic targets (such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation - rTMS, neurofeedback, psychotherapies) and more personalized treatments for childhood trauma-exposed individuals, beyond diagnosis-based approaches.
