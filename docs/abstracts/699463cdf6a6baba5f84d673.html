Introduction

While Alzheimer's Disease (AD) has traditionally been conceptualized as a disease of gray matter pathology, with amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles driving downstream neurodegeneration, this paradigm has been challenged by recent experimental evidence. An increasing number of studies suggests that white matter (WM) alterations might represent an upstream vulnerability factor and play a central pathophysiological role in promoting the onset and progression of AD. In line with this hypothesis, evidence from animal studies has demonstrated that myelin dysfunction acts as a primary driver of amyloid deposition through multiple converging mechanisms. For example, age-related myelin deterioration promotes the accumulation of cortical amyloid-beta both directly, through the formation of axonal swellings that contain the amyloid precursor protein, and indirectly, by impairing normal microglial function involved in Aβ clearance.

Collectively, these studies suggest that measures of WM health could provide sensitive biomarkers for the characterization of early AD stages, with potential applications in early diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression. Although several diffusion imaging studies have demonstrated widespread WM alterations in preclinical AD, particularly microstructural damage in late-myelinating fibers of amyloid-positive cognitively normal individuals, critical questions remain unanswered before these biomarkers can be validated and considered for clinical implementation. Specifically, it is unclear which micro- and macro-structural changes occur in tracts directly connected to early amyloid-accumulating regions (epicenters), whether these alterations are confined to particular classes of WM fibers, and to what extent they precede or promote subsequent amyloid deposition.

Methods

Participants. Data were drawn from the AMYPAD-PNHS cohort. AMYPAD-PNHS general inclusion criteria were age>50 and no diagnosis of dementia (CDR score <1). For this study, we selected individuals that had an amyloid-PET and a diffusion MRI scan acquired within the same study visit. 

Amyloid-PET processing and Epicenter Identification. Details of amyloid-PET imaging, processing, and harmonisation within the AMYPAD study have been reported previously. Standardised uptake value ratio (SUVr) images were generated using the whole cerebellum as the reference region in native space. Global Aβ burden was quantified by converting SUVr values to Centiloid units. Reference regions from the Desikan-Killiany (DK) atlas were warped into native space, and amyloid-PET SUVR values were computed for each region. To identify group-level amyloid epicenters, we first determined the patient's specific epicenters as the 5% of the 68 cortical DK regions with the highest amyloid-PET SUVRs, and subsequently counted how many times each region appeared as an epicenter over the whole group. The medial-orbitofrontal cortex and the precuneus were selected as group epicenters. 

Epicenter-connected WM pathway reconstruction. White-matter bundles originating from the two  epicenter regions (orbitofrontal cortex and precuneus) were reconstructed using subject-specific diffusion MRI processed with QSIPrep/qsirecon (v0.19.0). Left and right epicenter masks in T1 space were extracted and multiplied with subject-specific FreeSurfer-derived gray-matter–white-matter interface (GMWMI). Streamlines were then reconstructed using MRtrix3 tckgen with anatomically constrained tractography, seeding from the ROI-specific GMWMI. For each epicenter, only intrahemispheric streamlines were reconstructed. 

Epicenter-connected WM bundle subdivisions. To further characterize the connectivity profile of each epicenter-connected WM pathway, we subdivided the intrahemispheric tractograms into anatomically defined bundles according to their cortical endpoints, following established neuroanatomical literature. For the precuneus, we determined three bilateral sub-bundles with distinct endpoints: (1) the local connections (endpoints: precuneus, superior parietal), (2) the connections to the medial temporal lobe (endpoints: isthmus cingulate, parahippocampal), and (3) connections to the frontal lobe (endpoints: caudal/rostral anterior cingulate, medial orbitofrontal, superior frontal). For the orbitofrontal cortex, we determined two bilateral sub-bundles with distinct endpoints: (1) the local connections (endpoints: frontal and anterior cingulate cortex), (2) the connections to the medial temporal lobe (endpoints: superior temporal, middle temporal and inferior temporal). 

Sub-bundle tractograms were then generated using tckedit with ordered inclusion criteria, requiring streamlines to pass from the epicenter ROI to one of the predefined endpoint groups. 

Micro- and Macro-structural WM properties. For each reconstructed bundle, we computed tract-specific microstructural and macrostructural properties. For macrostructure, we computed streamline length mean and standard deviation, streamline endpoint density mean and standard deviation, bundle volume and endpoints volume. To quantify microstructural integrity, we sampled diffusion tensor–derived maps: fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), and axial diffusivity (AD), along each streamline using tcksample, and computed global tract averages from the resulting values. Only for epicenter-connected WM pathways, we also investigated gradients of microstructural properties based on their distance from the epicenter. To do so, microstructural values were averaged across streamlines within fixed distance bins (5mm) from the epicenters.

Statistical Analysis. For each of the metrics computed, we built linear models with the SUVR in the corresponding epicenter as predictor, and age and sex as covariates. 

Results

504 participants met the inclusion criteria for this study. Mean age was 65.9 (± 7.5), and 209 (39.5%) were males. Overall, participants were mostly cognitively unimpaired, where 8.5% (N=45) had a CDR=0.5 (very mild impairment), and the overall average MMSE was 29 (± 1.16). The majority of participants (N=302) had a negative amyloid PET (Centiloid <10), while 124 were considered ‘gray-zone’ (with a Centiloid between 10 and 24) , and 40 were labelled as amyloid-positive (Centiloid >24). 

The 5 regions that were more often identified as amyloid epicenters at the subject level were the medial orbitofrontal cortex, the superior frontal gyrus, the caudal and rostral middle frontal gyrus, and the precuneus. The medial orbitofrontal cortex and the precuneus were selected as group-level amyloid epicenters. 

Full intrahemispheric WM pathways connected to the two amyloid epicenters are shown in Figure 1A and 1D. Medial orbitofrontal cortex SUVR was associated with both micro and macrostructural changes of the connected WM pathways. Specifically, we observed higher MD and AD, and lower streamline length and volume of endpoints in association to higher amyloid SUVRs (Figure 1B). When looking at gradients of microstructural alterations along the orbitofrontal-connected WM pathway, we observed that higher MD, AD and also RD were observable in proximity to the amyloid epicenter (within 15mm; Figure 1C). 

Precuneus SUVr was associated with macrostructural changes of the connected WM pathway. Specifically, we observed higher streamline length mean and standard deviation, lower endpoints density mean, and higher bundle volume in association to higher amyloid SUVRs (Figure 1E). We did not observe gradients of amyloid-related microstructural alteration along the tract (Figure 1F). 

When looking at the two sub-bundle partitions originating from the medial orbitofrontal cortex,  we observed amyloid-related changes in micro and macro-structural properties of both the local connections and the connections to the temporal lobe (Figure 2B). Specifically, higher MD, RD, and AD and lower streamline length mean and standard deviation were observed in association to higher amyloid SUVRs in the epicenter in both sub-bundles, and most strongly in local connections. 

We then looked at the three bundle subdivision originating from the precuneus. In this case, precuneus SUVR was associated with microstructural changes in local connections, i.e. higher FA and lower RD, as well as lower endpoints density standard deviation. Precuneus SUVR was also associated with macrostructural changes in endpoints density standard deviation and mean in connections to the frontal lobe. No association was found with properties of connections to the temporal lobe. 

Conclusion

Our findings show that in a largely cognitively unimpaired population, amyloid accumulation in early epicenter regions is associated with distinct micro- and macrostructural alterations in their connected white-matter pathways. Orbitofrontal amyloid was linked to widespread microstructural injury and proximal gradients of tissue disruption, whereas precuneus amyloid was associated primarily with macrostructural reorganization of connected tracts. Sub-bundle analyses revealed that these effects are anatomically specific and differ across major fascicles, with local pathways showing the strongest associations. Together, these results support a model in which early amyloid deposition is accompanied by selective and region-dependent white-matter alterations, highlighting epicenter-connected tracts as promising biomarkers for detecting very early disease-related changes in Alzheimer’s disease.
