Introduction

Humans spend about one-third of their lives sleeping, yet the neural dynamics underlying sleep and its disorders remain only partly understood.  

A prominent example is Central Disorders of Hypersomnolence (CDH), characterized by excessive daytime sleepiness. While some conditions, such as narcolepsy, are well-understood and supported by established biomarkers, others lack reliable markers and share overlapping clinical features. 

This diagnostic uncertainty motivates approaches that do not rely on predefined clinical labels. Accordingly, we aim to learn data-driven representations from overnight sleep recordings to better characterize sleep dynamics in CDH and assess their diagnostic relevance using self-supervised learning.

It has already been demonstrated that contrastive learning, a self-supervised learning approach that trains models to distinguish similar from dissimilar data points, applied to EEG data, can extract representations that distinguish different sleep stages.1 

In self-supervised learning, the choice of pretext tasks determines which structures are captured in the data, yet its impact on EEG representations remains poorly understood. Moreover, although contrastive learning has been utilized with electroencephalography (EEG) data, it has yet to be applied to EEG recordings from individuals with CDH. By applying contrastive learning to raw EEG data, we aim to uncover underlying representations that may indicate sleep instability and offer diagnostic insights.

We therefore investigate how different pretext tasks influence contrastive learning representations obtained from overnight recordings of patients with CDH. We hypothesize that training models on specific pretext tasks that exploit temporal and patient-specific features in overnight EEG recordings will produce latent representations that reflect sleep architecture. Additionally, we hypothesize that these representations can help distinguish between patient subgroups.  

Methods

Methods 

Datasets and Preprocessing

We evaluated our pretext tasks on two different datasets: a subset of the database from the international Swiss Primary Hypersomnolence and Narcolepsy Cohort Study (iSPHYNCS)2, which is a multicenter research initiative to identify new biomarkers in central disorders of Hypersomnolence (n = 180); the open source Sleep-EDF Database Expanded3 (Sleep-EDF) with n = 76 healthy subjects not taking any sleep-related medication. The iSPHYNCS dataset comprises overnight recordings of electroencephalography (EEG), electrooculography (EOG), and electromyography (EMG) signals, segmented into 30-second epochs and downsampled to 200 Hz. The Sleep-EDF recordings also consist of overnight recordings, but they only include EEG channels and were retained at 100 Hz. 

Semi-automatic artifact rejection was conducted by identifying epochs with abnormally high power compared to neighbouring epochs. EEG power was calculated using Welch’s method in the slow band (0.5-4.5 Hz) and a fast band (20-40 Hz). For each epoch, power was compared to the mean power within a 15-epoch window centred around it. Epochs were marked as artefacts if the power ratio exceeded 2.5 in the slow band or 2 in the fast band. After automatic rejection, all recordings were also visually inspected, and additional noisy epochs were discarded.  

Model Architecture and Inputs

Models were based primarily on a convolutional neural network architecture adapted from Chambon et al. (2018)4, with additional experiments using EEGNet5 and a pretrained ResNet for comparison. Model inputs consisted of multichannel EEG, EMG, and EOG signals. We finally set on EEGNet for the Sleep-EDF dataset, whereas for the iSPHYNCS dataset, we used the Chambon architecture, after visually inspecting the resulting representations. 

Contrastive Learning Tasks

Our models were trained on two main contrastive learning tasks: (1) a temporal proximity task, and (2) a patient-level task. 

The temporal proximity task encourages the model to learn temporal relations in sleep by bringing together sleep epochs (30 second recording data) that are close in time and separating those that are temporally distant. Positive pairs consisted of epochs sampled within ±5 minutes of each other, whereas the negative pairs were sampled from epochs separated by 15-40 minutes. We evaluated two variants of this task: one in which negative pairs were restricted to epochs from the same individual (shuffling not allowed), and another allowing negative pairs to originate from different individuals (shuffling allowed).  

The patient-level task encourages the model to learn individual-specific differences. Positive pairs consisted of epochs sampled from the same patient, and negative pairs consisted of epochs from different patients.  

Training Procedure 

Signals were z-score normalized per recording, and missing values were encoded using a sentinel value and excluded from sampling. After conducting an extensive hyperparameter search, we selected the following training configurations: Models were trained using stochastic gradient descent (learning rate 0.0055, weight decay 0.0002) with a batch size of 128 for up to 100 epochs. Early stopping was applied based on validation loss with a patience of 10 epochs. Training was performed with five-fold cross-validation. 

To support contrastive learning, we applied a set of stochastic data augmentations to each epoch, including additive Gaussian noise, amplitude scaling, DC shifts, temporal shifts, temporal reversal, and zero masking. Augmentations were applied independently to each view with a probability of 0.5. 

Representation Extraction and Visualization

Latent representations were obtained from the embedding layer after a linear projection head with an output dimension of 100. To qualitatively examine the learned representations, embeddings were projected into two-dimensional space using Unform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).  

Results

Results

The temporal proximity task achieved an average validation accuracy of approximately 70% across both datasets, indicating successful discrimination between temporally close and distant epochs. The patient-level task achieved a validation accuracy of roughly 60% on the iSPHYNCS dataset. As both tasks serve only as auxiliary objectives, performance is reported for completeness and not as a primary evaluation metric. 

Proximity Task 

In the proximity task, the model effectively learned sleep stage patterns without labels in both datasets (Fig. 1). When negative pairs were limited to the same individual, the latent space showed a well-organized distribution across sleep stages. In the Sleep-EDF dataset, sleep stages formed distinct, well-separated clusters, with apparent differences among Wake, REM, and NREM. In contrast, representations from patients with CDH showed increased overlap between REM and Wake stages, while NREM stages remained relatively structured. Allowing negative pairs from different individuals decreased sleep-stage separation but enhanced patient-specific clustering. Notably, within each patient cluster, sleep stages stayed localized, indicating that stage-related information was still preserved. 

Patient-level Task

In the patient-level task, the model successfully distinguished recordings from different individuals. Furthermore, the representations reflected individual differences and recording sites. Within patient clusters, sleep stages remained partially separable.

Conclusion

Conclusion

This work demonstrates the potential of self-supervised contrastive learning to generate structured, interpretable representations from unlabeled sleep recordings in both healthy individuals and patients with Central Disorders of Hypersomnolence. The learned latent features across various tasks reflect meaningful organization related to sleep stages, patient identity, and recording site, highlighting the sensitivity of representation learning to task selection and data characteristics.

In patients with CDH, these latent representations show greater overlap between REM and Wake stages compared to healthy controls. Since this population is known to experience fragmented and unstable sleep, the physiological significance of this overlap needs further validation through downstream analyses. However, it suggests that self-supervised models may capture altered sleep dynamics beyond traditional labels. Permitting negative pairs from different patients changed the representation space from being dominated by sleep stages to being structured more around individual patients, while still preserving stage-related patterns within each person. The patient-level task effectively distinguished individuals and demonstrated a significant effect of the recording site. This suggests that self-supervised learning can simultaneously encode multiple variability present in the data.

As with contrastive learning methods in general, downstream tasks are essential to evaluate the significance of the learned representations. Since our current results are mainly qualitative, we suggest, as an initial step toward quantitative assessment, evaluating the learned representations on an automatic sleep-stage classification task, which provides a well-established benchmark. 

A limitation of the current approach is that is doesn’t include temporal information from overnight recordings, despite sleep being a dynamic process that evolves over the night. The static approach likely restricts the range of features captured. Moving forward, it will be important to integrate temporal information into the model to better characterize sleep dynamics at both individual and group levels. Additionally, including patient metadata within the framework may help differentiate shared and disorder-specific patterns within the iSPHYNCS cohort, revealing subtle sleep differences across this diverse group of sleep disorders.

In summary, this study demonstrates that self-supervised contrastive learning can be utilized to learn structured representations from unlabeled sleep EEG data in both healthy individuals and patients with Central Disorders of Hypersomnolence. Importantly, our results show that the choice of the pretext task has a clear impact on the learned representations, emphasizing the need for careful task selection based on the specific objective. Further research is required to systematically evaluate which pretext and downstream tasks are most suitable for capturing sleep dynamics and inter-individual differences with potential diagnostic relevance.
