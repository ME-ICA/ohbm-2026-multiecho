Introduction

From answering questions in class to formal exams, performance feedback is a ubiquitous feature of academic learning. Yet, the majority of neuroimaging studies of feedback processing focus on learning arbitrary associations, typically in probabilistic contexts (e.g., Poldrack et al., 1999). Less studied are deterministic tasks with real-world consequences, like mathematics. Typically, feedback produces a characteristic pattern in the ventral striatum: increased activity for positive feedback and decreased activity for negative feedback, mirroring responses to monetary rewards and punishment (Tricomi et al., 2006). In real-world domains, feedback provides information about performance, but also carries an evaluative component, potentially exaggerating the response to negative feedback. In this study, we assess processing of feedback from performance in a math task, relative to an arbitrary paired-associates memory task. Further, we examine whether math anxiety may exaggerate the ventral striatum response to negative feedback specifically to a math task. Math anxiety is marked by feelings of tension or apprehension when an individual approaches mathematics tasks and situations. Neuroimaging studies report heightened posterior insula activity during the anticipation of a math task in math anxious young adults (Lyons & Beilock, 2012) and in the amygdala during a math task in anxious children (Young et al., 2012). Yet no imaging studies to date have considered feedback processing in this population.

Methods

Potential participants completed the Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale, and those scoring in the top 20% formed the High Math Anxiety group (HMA; n = 7; M age =  21.2, 2 male) while the bottom 20% formed the Low Math Anxiety group (LMA, n = 12, M age 22.0; 2 male). Before entering the scanner, participants completed the study phase of a paired associates Memory task where they learned 56 pairs of words in a multiple-choice format (e.g., LANE is paired with JOKE and not BATH). In the scanner, participants completed a functional imaging run with 28 trials per condition, in three interspersed conditions: Simple Math (single-digit multiplication problem, e.g., 7 x 3); Complex Math (teens times single-digit multiplication problems, e.g., 3 x 17); Memory (previously studied items). Each trial began with a cue, followed by the task, and then performance feedback, each separated by jitter (Figure 1). Outside the scanner, participants completed a post-test of the same conditions, without feedback. Finally, they completed a battery of questionnaires, including the Approach and Avoidance Achievement Motivation, Math Anxiety Rating Scale, Theory of Intelligence Scale, Trait Anxiety Inventory, and the Calculation and Math Fluency subtest of the Woodcock-Johnson III Tests of Achievement. Functional MRI data were preprocessed and analyzed in FSL (version 6.0.7), and 6 mm spherical regions of interest (ROIs) were defined from peaks identified from relevant terms in NeuroSynth: posterior insula for cue processing (search term: disgust), the amygdala for task performance (search term: anxiety), and the ventral striatum for feedback processing (search term: reward).

Results

Complex Math was most challenging (67% correct), while Simple Math was the easiest (94%), and Memory performance was intermediate (76%). There were no significant performance differences between the groups on any tasks. Our primary contrast of interest is brain responses to negative feedback (following errors) in the arbitrary Memory task vs. the real-world relevant Complex Math tasks. (There were too few negative feedback trials to examine Simple math on this contrast.) In the ventral striatum, negative feedback following a Complex Math error produced a larger deactivation relative to baseline than negative feedback following Memory errors (p = .004). This pattern was consistent over both HMA and LMA groups, with no difference between them (p =  .498) Further, in the current sample, there were no differences between Complex Math and Memory during the cue in the posterior insula activity in either group or between groups (all ps > .1). In the amygdala, during task performance there was greater activity for the Memory task than the Complex Math task (p = .004) but no effect of group or interaction (all ps > .6).

Conclusion

We found that negative feedback on the Complex Math task produced a larger decrease from baseline in the ventral striatum than negative feedback on the Memory task. Interestingly, in this preliminary sample, this exaggerated striatal response to negative feedback was apparent both in individuals with math anxiety and without it. These results suggest that  feedback indicating incorrect performance on a deterministic math task, with years of practice and an expectation of success,  is a more evaluative response than feedback on a just learnt, arbitrary memory task. Larger samples will help reveal the role of math anxiety in the ventral striatum and in the insula and amygdala.
