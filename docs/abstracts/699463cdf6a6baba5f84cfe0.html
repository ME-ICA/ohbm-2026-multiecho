Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is frequently comorbid with depression1, and multisite chronic pain (MCP) is a common accompanying manifestation2. Our previous work has suggested a potential causal influence of multisite chronic pain (MCP) on the development of depression3, and other studies have also reported genetic correlations and shared overlapping biological pathways between RA and MCP4. Existing work has largely focused on isolated molecular markers, obscuring the complex neuro–immune–metabolic interactions that may jointly underlie RA, depression, and chronic pain. To address this gap, we applied a supervised multi-omic integration framework to identify peripheral molecular signatures associated with MCP5, evaluate their mechanistic roles across RA and depression, and map the multidimensional biological landscape linking peripheral molecular variation to brain structure and broader systemic phenotypes (Fig. 1).

Methods

We analyzed peripheral multi-omic profiles and structural MRI data from UK Biobank participants, encompassing 59 biochemical measures, 168 metabolites, and 2,920 proteins. Neuroimaging features comprised cortical thickness and volume in 148 cortical regions and tract-averaged fractional anisotropy (FA) in 48 white matter tracts. Using peripheral data from UK Biobank participants (19,484 at baseline; 32,870–399,476 participants with up to 15.9 years of follow-up), we first applied a supervised multi-omic integration framework (FIT toolbox; Fig. 1a), using the MCP score—defined as the number of body sites with pain persisting for more than three months—as a continuous supervision signal. The identified multi-omic signatures were then projected onto five ICD-defined patient groups (prevalent depression, prevalent RA, RA–depression comorbidity, incident depression, and incident RA) to test whether the signatures exhibited graded dysregulation with increasing disease burden. We then further characterized the identified multi-omic signatures through prospective prediction of depression and RA risk (Fig. 1b), biological enrichment analyses (Fig. 1c), and association analyses linking peripheral molecular variation to brain structure (Fig. 1d), polygenic liability, and broader clinical and behavioral phenotypes (Fig. 1e).

Results

Using peripheral multi-omic data from 19,484 UK Biobank participants, we identified MCP-associated signatures across biochemical, metabolomic, and proteomic layers (r = 0.12-0.35, P <0.001; Fig. 2a). These signatures displayed a linear gradient across disease burden, from depression to RA to their comorbidity (F (3, 14,315) = 76.84, P < 2 × 10⁻16; Fig. 2b), predicted incident RA (HR = 1.10–1.22) and depression (HR = 1.09–1.10), and partially mediated the bidirectional association between RA and depression, with mediation proportions ranging from 1.67% to 9.64%. Mendelian randomization further implicated HNMT and FGF21 as putative causal contributors, while MME emerged as a protective causal factor. Across neuroimaging phenotypes, multi-omic layers exhibited modality-specific associations: biochemical markers showed the broadest spatial associations with cortical thickness (P < 0.01, Cohen’s d = -0.06 – -0.05; Fig. 2c), metabolomic signatures were most broadly linked to white-matter microstructure (P < 0.001, d = -0.07 – -0.05), and proteomic signatures were associated with reduced cortical volume exclusively in the left short insular gyrus (P < 0.001, d = -0.12). These peripheral signatures also correlated with lifestyle-related health phenotypes, including smoking, fish intake, and physical activity (Fig. 2e), and with polygenic risk scores for immune, metabolic, and psychiatric traits (Fig. 2d).

Conclusion

We identify coordinated peripheral multi-omic signatures that capture shared immune, metabolic, and neural processes underlying MCP-linked comorbidity between RA and depression. These signatures form a graded axis of dysregulation across increasing disease burden and predict the risk of incident RA and depression. They also partially mediate the bidirectional association between the two conditions and converge with structural brain alterations, genetic susceptibility, and health-related behaviors. Continued integration of multi-omic and multidimensional data may inform more targeted strategies to reduce the shared burden of RA–depression comorbidity and refine the biological architecture of immunopsychiatry.
