Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is phenotypically and neurobiologically heterogeneous, yet much of the ASD research has been focused on how autistic individuals differ from their non-autistic counterparts. This is a vastly important research domain given the pathologizing attitude many societies take toward ASD, especially as it pertains to receiving treatment and intervention. However, in part due to criticism from autistic self-advocates in recent years, there is growing interest in within-ASD heterogeneity and data-driven subtyping.

Autistic subtype identification represents a critical research direction for two reasons. First, diagnostic efforts would be bolstered significantly by a broader and more nuanced understanding of the heterogeneity of the autistic phenotype. Clinicians could better identify cases of ASD if the diagnostic criteria were applicable across autistic subtypes. Second, personalized intervention plans would become more effective and common if each autistic individual could be characterized with more detail. 

Sensory differences are a core feature of ASD, but prior literature is divided as to whether they reflect distinct subtypes or a more subtle gradient of atypical sensory processing1. Concurrently, task-based functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and functional connectivity (FC) enable a way to explore the neurobiologically-grounded underpinnings of ASD.

In this work, we implement unsupervised clustering machine learning methods on self-reported Sensory Profile data from autistic participants to extract low-dimensional latent sensory structure. We then characterize these factors demographically and then validate this latent structure in insula-seeded FC during both resting state as well as during a biological motion (biopoint) task by including the latent sensory structure as continuous predictors in voxel-wise linear mixed-effect models.

Methods

Sensory Profile

Sensory Profile self-report data were available from 226 autistic participants (ages 8-24 years). We used both raw item-level responses (60 items) and four quadrant-level scores (low registration, sensory seeking, sensory sensitivity, sensory avoiding). Any missing data items were imputed using multivariate iterative imputation. Data were z-scored prior to analysis. 

Initially both the item-level and quadrant-level data were subjected to two methods of clustering: k-means and spectral clustering (Ng-Jordan-Weiss algorithm2). The number of clusters k was tuned from two to five, and clustering solutions were evaluated for both silhouette score and cross-validated adjusted Rand index (ARI). Spectral embeddings were additionally evaluated using scree plots of eigenvalues to assess intrinsic dimensionality of the sensory space (Figure 1a and 1b). The extracted spectral embeddings were then used as continuous sensory scores for downstream neuroimaging analyses. Item-level data were evaluated for feature importance to establish which areas of sensory experience most contributed to differentiation along the sensory dimension.

Functional Imaging

fMRI data were available from 113 autistic participants (ages 8-17 years) from four data collection sites. either Siemens 3T TrioTim scanners or Siemens Prisma scanners equipped with 32-channel head-coils. Whole-brain T1-weighted MPRAGE images (FOV 256 mm³, 1x1x1 mm voxels, TE=3.3 ms, TR=2000 ms) and whole-brain gradient-echo EPI images (FOV 220 mm³, 3.4x3.4x4 mm voxels, TE=25 ms, TR=2000 ms) were collected. Preprocessing was conducted using fMRIPrep and included motion correction, slice-timing correction, skull-stripping, spatial smoothing, spatial normalization, and high-pass temporal filtering.

To reduce the dimensionality of the fMRI data while focusing on a sensory-relevant region, only connections within and originating from the insula were considered (insula-seeded FC). The insula was divided into three constituents: the dorsal-anterior insula (dAI), the ventral-anterior insula (vAI), and the posterior insula (PI). For each seed, seed-to-voxel FC matrices were computed by correlating the seed time series with every brain voxel and applying Fisher z-transformation to correlation coefficients. These voxel-wise FC matrices were the inputs to the statistical models. Insula-seeded FC matrices were generated for both resting-state and biopoint tasks. 

Statistical Method

Voxel-wise connectivity was modeled as the outcome of interest using mass-univariate linear mixed effects model implemented in AFNI’s 3dLMEr package. For each seed and fMRI task, the primary fixed effect predictors were the continuous sensory spectral score and biological sex, plus their interaction. Covariates included scan age, an IQ proxy (measured using Differential Abilities Scale – Greater Conceptual Ability score), Pubertal Development Scale total score, mean framewise displacement, and data collection site. Site was modeled as a random effect; two sites upgraded scanners halfway through the study, leading them to be considered as separate sites pre- and post-upgrade. Due missing data, the final number of autistic participants evaluated was 68.

Results

Sensory Clustering

For each clustering analysis, the best cross-validated k value was 3; however, none of the methods produced well-separated or stable clusters. For the k-means clustering analysis, item-level features showed low cohesion and moderate instability (silhouette=0.062, ARI=0.371±0.135); with slightly better results but an overall similar pattern at the quadrant level (silhouette=0.245, ARI=0.469±0.196). Spectral clustering yielded similar results (item-level silhouette=0.057; ARI=0.263±0.138; quadrant-level silhouette=0.253; ARI=0.371±0.172).

The principal component analysis and spectral scree plots in Figures 1b and 1c reveal the Sensory Profile data follows a pattern of single-dimension dominance, likely representing a single ‘severity’ metric. This interpretation is illustrated in Figures 2a and 2b where the same data that produce unstable clusters form a continuous manifold when visualized with a derived ‘total sensory score’ overlaid. 

Feature-importance analysis showed that the strongest contributors to the sensory spectral score came primarily from the Sensory Sensitivity and Sensory Avoiding quadrants. Some top-contributing items included “I stay away from crowds,” “I stay away from noisy settings,” and “I become bothered when I see lots of movement around me,” representative of sensory hyperreactivity and avoidance.

Functional Imaging

The results of the mass univariate linear mixed-effect modeling revealed significant associations between the sensory spectral score and the dAI seed (q=0.01) during the biopoint task, with no such effects observed in the resting state task (q≈1.0). In the vAI seed, biological sex emerged as the only significant predictor in both scan conditions (biopointq=0.005; resting state q=0.002). There was a significant interaction between sensory spectral score and biological sex in the PI seed in the biopoint condition (q=0.05) again with no effect in the resting state counterpart (q≈1.0). The two most significant voxel clusters are shown in Figure 2c and 2d; the blue coloration indicates that higher sensory spectral scores, and thus more severe sensory sensitivity, results in statistically significantly lower connectivity values. The top row reveals hypo-connectivity between the dorsal anterior insula and the precentral gyrus (primary motor cortex), and the bottom row shows hypo-connectivity between the dorsal anterior insula and lateral occipital cortex. 

Conclusion

The present findings indicate sensory sensitivity in ASD follows a continuous latent dimension and does not separate into distinct sensory subtypes. The sensory spectral score captures a coherent pattern of over-reactivity and avoidance that aligns with classic autistic behavioral traits like sensory overload and inability to handle unpredictable environments. The one-dimensional sensory structure indicates that the sensory-based heterogeneity in ASD is best characterized as a sensory severity gradient rather than different subtypes or categories.

Most importantly, this severity gradient revealed specific neurobiological relevance during the perception of biological motion. Autistic participants that scored higher along this severity gradient exhibited lowered connectivity between the dorsal anterior insula and the primary motor cortex during the biological motion task, with no corresponding effect during resting state. The task-specificity of this finding suggests sensory severity modulates action-relevant salience processing rather than a stable intrinsic connectivity profile. This effect aligns with the item-level feature importance analysis, which revealed the sensory sensitivity scores are most impacted by items involving crowded, unpredictable, or socially dynamic environments. 

One interpretation is that autistic individuals who fall on the high end of the sensory severity gradient may show altered integration between interoceptive processing or salience detection (dorsal anterior insula responsibilities)3 and motor simulation systems (primary motor cortex)4 when viewing biological motion. Such hypoconnectivity could influence the ability to evaluate and integrate sensory cues into motor preparatory states. Alternative explanations are indeed possible, and further work is needed to confirm these findings. Additionally, the interaction between sensory severity and sex in the posterior insula is not explored in this analysis but could provide further nuance to the sex-modulated effects of biological motion processing.

Some future directions include explorations of sensory-based functional connectivity differences in other task-based scans; in this project, autistic participants completed five functional tasks, and the other three (face identification, language, and social reward) could interact with sensory severity in other ways. Further, the sensory severity gradient should be further quantified and validated through association with other behavioral outcome measures, including the CBCL or BRIEF subscales. More confirmation of this effect and its isolation to ASD should be explored as well through analysis of non-autistic participant data – the expected results would be a distinct sensory severity distribution as well as no significant relationship between this and the primary motor cortex.
