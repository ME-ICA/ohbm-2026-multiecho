Introduction

The human brain, like the body, develops nonlinearly over the first two decades of life. Although often studied apart from its embodied context, the brain does not develop nor operate in isolation. Rather, it is embedded in a network of blood vessels that transports both nutrients and waste throughout the body, as well as a network of nerves that traffics electrical signals to and from internal and external organs. As a result, brain structure and function are influenced by the structure and function of the body at large. Indeed, recent work has shown that the brain and body influence each others' structural integrity,1 that the brain and body share electrophysiological rhythms,2 and that body health is a predictor of brain health.3 However, due to challenges in acquiring large-scale brain and body assays, there remains no comprehensive assessment of brain-body relationships across multiple brain features and organ systems. How brain development is coupled with body development therefore remains unknown.

Methods

Here we comprehensively quantify brain-body coupling in the first two decades of life using electronic health records from > 100,000 children who have visited the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. Brain features (192 whole-brain and regional morphometric measurements; Figure 1a) are derived from clinically acquired MRI scans with limited imaging pathology (N=7628),4 and include whole-brain, cortical, and subcortical grey matter volume, white matter volume, and cortical surface area. Body features (76 distinct components mapped using standardized LOINC codes; Figure 1b) are derived from multiple organ systems, including cardiovascular (e.g. pulse rate, blood pressure), pulmonary (e.g. forced vital capacity, peak expiratory flow), musculoskeletal (e.g. height, weight), immune (e.g. leukocytes, platelets), renal (e.g. creatinine), hepatic (e.g. bilirubin, albumin), and metabolic (e.g. lipid and glucose levels). Nonlinear normative growth charts for all brain and body features are modelled with Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale and Shape (GAMLSS) to convert raw measurements to age-, and sex-adjusted centile scores. To relate brain and body features across individuals, we correlate (Spearman’s r) centile scores for brain and body measurements that are collected within 90 days of one another. All p-values are Bonferroni-corrected for multiple comparisons.

Results

We find that multi-organ body features are associated with global brain volume, regional brain volume, and cortical surface area (289 statistically significant associations at the study-wide Bonferroni significance level of p < 1.8x10-5; Figure 1c-d). Whole brain volume, cortical grey matter volume, subcortical grey matter volume, and cortical surface area are correlated with multiple hematology-derived measurements including erythrocyte distribution width (r = -0.20, p = 10-14 with total grey matter volume), hemoglobin concentration in red blood cells (r = 0.13, p = 10-9 with right amygdala grey matter volume), albumin protein in blood plasma (r = 0.22, p = 10-6 with left fusiform surface area), and immunoglobulin A (r = -0.23, p = 10-7 with right pars opercularis surface area). Regional brain-body associations remain statistically significant after regressing total intracranial volume from all brain features. Additional regional associations emerge after removing effects of total intracranial volume, including between grey matter volume of amygdala, inferior parietal cortex, superior parietal cortex, and middle temporal cortex, and immune system markers such as lymphocyte count (e.g. r = 0.17 , p = 10-6 with right superior parietal grey matter volume).

Conclusion

By curating clinically acquired measurements of the brain and body in thousands of children with minimal or no imaging pathology, we find widespread associations between brain structure and body composition. Ultimately, this work places the brain back in its whole-body context to understand how brain and body systems are intertwined across human development.
