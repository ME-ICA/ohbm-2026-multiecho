Introduction

Sleep-wake disturbances (SWD) are highly prevalent in Parkinson’s disease (PD), affecting up to 90% of patients and contributing substantially to reduced quality of life, and accelerated disease progression. Insomnia, sleep fragmentation, excessive daytime sleepiness, and REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) are among the most common manifestations, appearing early in the prodromal phase of PD. Growing evidence suggests that SWD are not merely symptomatic consequences of neurodegeneration but may actively contribute to its progression through mechanisms such as reduced glymphatic clearance of neurotoxic proteins and inflammatory stress. Despite being clinically relevant, effective treatment options for SWD in PD remain limited. Pharmacological therapies can be constrained by side effects or interactions with dopaminergic medication, underscoring the need for targeted nonpharmacological approaches.

Deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) is well established for motor symptoms, but its effects on sleep are modest and inconsistent, reflecting the fact that the STN is not a core node of sleep wake regulation. In contrast, the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) is anatomically positioned to influence sleep-wake circuitry through GABAergic projections to the thalamus, superior colliculus, and pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN). Recent clinical and preclinical work1 indicates that modulation of SNr output can bias transitions between wakefulness and sleep, and that co-stimulation of the STN and SNr is feasible and may benefit gait, mood, and SWD. Importantly, low-frequency SNr stimulation preserves synaptic fidelity and induces periodic inhibitory “silent periods,” whereas higher frequencies suppress neuronal output more broadly suggesting the presence of functionally distinct sub-regions within the SNr. Identifying the optimal SNr sub-region for DBS may therefore offer a novel, circuit-specific therapeutic strategy to improve SWD in PD.

Methods

The data for the study was obtained from the ICEBERG dataset acquired at the Paris Brain Institute (ICEBERG study, NCT02305147) and includes multimodal imaging and clinical assessments from healthy individuals and patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) across three different time points. The imaging data included T1-w sagittal MP2RAGE 1-mm isovoxel; 2D TSE axial neuromelanin (NM)-sensitive sequence, voxel size: 0.4×0.4×3 mm3; T2*-w multi-echo transverse 3D FLASH sequence for Quantitative Susceptibility Mapping (QSM) reconstructed as 1-mm iso-voxel; Diffusion-weighted images (DWI) were acquired at b=0, 2000 s/mm2 at 64 directions with voxel size 1.7x1.7x1.7 mm. The hyperintense substantia nigra pars compacts (SNc) on NM-MRI was automatically segmented in individual space using Nigranet.2 The whole SN was automatically segmented based on the hyperintensity in QSM images.3 A study specific template was prepared from 35 healthy subjects of the ICEBERG cohort using antsMultivariateTemplateConstruction2 from the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) package to generate multimodal templates as shown in Figure 1. A probabilistic atlas of SNr ROI was also developed from the above healthy cohort. The clinical assessments performed on the ICEBERG cohort include the UPDRSIII and NMSS. Thirty-five healthy subjects and 45 age and sex matched PD subjects at visit 3 in the ICEBERG cohort were selected for the voxel-wise correlation analysis. The voxel-wise correlation analysis was performed using SPM for the SNr ROI on the QSM and FA maps with the clinical scores to identify subregions within SNr associated with motor and sleep alterations in PD. 

Results

The substantia nigra reticulata (SNr) was obtained by taking the difference between the SN and SNc as in Figure 1, demonstrated on study specific templates on different modalities. The voxel wise correlation analysis revealed that the posterior ventral region of SNr on the QSM map, was correlated with the UPDRS-III score and the same territory was also correlated on the FA map with the NMSS sub-score for sleep/fatigue domain at cluster level FWE correction of p<0.05. Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the functional relevance of microstructural and susceptibility-based changes within the SNr in PD using QSM and FA within a study-specific SNr region of interest derived from the ICEBERG cohort. The voxel-wise correlation analyses revealed that the posterior-ventral portion of the SNr showed a shared pattern of association with both motor impairment, indexed by UPDRS III scores on QSM, and sleep/fatigue disturbances, indexed by NMSS sub-scores on FA maps. This spatial convergence suggests that this subregion may represent a functional hub where pathological processes contribute to both motor and non-motor symptoms especially sleep, intersect.

These findings are consistent with prior work showing that the SNr is not simply an output nucleus but participates in integrative motor and arousal circuits.1 Our observation that both QSM-based (reflecting increased iron deposition) and FA-based (reflecting microstructural changes) correlations localize to an overlapping SNr territory aligns with evidence that motor and non-motor symptoms in PD share partially convergent pathophysiological substrates rather than representing entirely distinct processes. Importantly, identifying a subregion of the SNr jointly associated with both motor dysfunction and sleep/fatigue symptoms provides insight into the neural basis of symptom clustering, which has been widely recognized in PD. The posterior-ventral SNr, strongly connected with thalamic and brainstem arousal nuclei, may therefore represent a bottleneck region whose degeneration contributes to multidomain impairment.

In conclusion, this study highlights the value of combining high-resolution neuromelanin-sensitive MRI, QSM, and diffusion-based measures within a well-defined SNr atlas to delineate symptom-relevant sub-regions in PD. The identification of a shared vulnerable zone associated with both motor and sleep/fatigue alterations underscores the need to consider the SNr as a clinically relevant target when interpreting PD progression or planning neuromodulatory interventions.
