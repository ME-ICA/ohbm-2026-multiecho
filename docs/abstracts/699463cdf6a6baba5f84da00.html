Introduction

Music is a powerful auditory stimulus that engages widespread perceptual, cognitive, and motor brain systems, including networks involved in movement, language, memory, attention, and emotion.1 In patients with Disorders of Consciousness (DoC), music therapy has been proposed to have positive implications for the recovery process 2, while offering a nonverbal, emotionally salient stimulus that can be individualized to the patient. Despite these promising benefits, the neural dynamics underlying the effects of music in DoC remain poorly understood.

A prominent hypothesis regarding DoC posits that impaired consciousness arises from altered brain network dynamics, characterized by a reduced exploration of the brain’s dynamical repertoire. Resting-state fMRI studies suggest a “monopolization” of neural trajectories by a limited number of network states.3 EEG microstates are short, quasi-stable spatiotemporal patterns of global brain activity, and are considered a close electrophysiological proxy of large-scale brain networks identified with fMRI.4 

In this study, we examined EEG microstate dynamics in DoC patients during the resting state and while passively listening to their preferred music. Our research addressed two main questions: (1) To what extent are brain dynamics in DoC constrained to a limited subset of EEG microstates versus flexibly exploring a broader repertoire? (2) How does preferred music modulate EEG microstates’ dynamics? We hypothesized that resting-state activity would exhibit preferred microstate “hubs,” reflecting reduced exploration of different microstates, whereas music listening would promote a more flexible exploration of the microstate repertoire.

Methods

Four participants suffering from DoC with different levels of consciousness (MCS: Minimally conscious state, and UWS: Unresponsive wakefulness syndrome) were included in this study. Subjects are referenced as: Sub1 (UWS), Sub2 (MCS+), Sub3 (MCS-), and Sub4 (MCS-). We recorded high-density EEG (128 electrodes, Biosemi)  activity during resting-state and passive music listening conditions. Eight minutes of EEG activity was recorded in both conditions. Passive music listening involved listening to preferred songs, proposed by the participant’s primary caretakers, in a random order. 

The EEG data were preprocessed using MNE-Python. We applied a zero-phase, two-pass, 4th-order Butterworth IIR filter with a passband from 1 to 40 Hz, which was followed by detrending the signal and a notch filter to remove the line noise at 50 Hz. Followed common average referencing. Noisy segments of the data were rejected by visual inspection and using quality metrics (e.g., db amplitude, variance, 1/variance, and z-score), and noisy channels were interpolated using spherical splines. A minimum length of 3 minutes of EEG was ensured after the noisy segment removal. At last, ICA was applied to remove eye, muscle, and other types of artifacts. We used the +microstate  toolbox 5 for calculating the EEG microstates, which uses a modified k-means clustering method, based on topographical dissimilarity. First, the standard deviation of the voltages across all channels at each time point, known as the Global Field Power (GFP), was extracted from the preprocessed EEG data as follows: GFP = sqrt( (1/K) * sum_i ( V_i(t) - V_mean(t) )^2 )

Let k be the number of EEG electrodes, Vi(t) the potential of the i-th electrode at time t, and Vmean(t) the average potential across electrodes at time t. Once the GFP was obtained for the entire EEG, by concatenating both resting state and passive listening data, recording, and topographic maps at the local maxima of the GFP in time were clustered via a modified k-means algorithm. We determined the optimal number of clusters to be six using the global explained variance method. Real-time EEG microstates were then derived post hoc by fitting these cluster maps back to each time point in the data. Because we hypothesize that microstate parameters correlate with network dynamics and due to between-subject aetiological differences, microstates were computed individually for each subject. 

Two measures were derived from the microstates: (1) the average microstate duration, (2) the state transition probability matrix. Only transitions with probabilities exceeding those of a baseline distribution are displayed in Figure 1. This baseline distribution was generated by performing 1000 random permutations of the microstates order for each subject and condition, creating a null distribution to assess statistical significance. That is, microstate labels were permuted randomly 1000 times, and the associated transition probability matrix of microstates was calculated for each permutation and averaged for the 1000 permutations at the end. This gives us the baseline distribution, that is, the transition probability matrix that we can expect to have by chance. The significant (compared to the baseline distribution) transition probabilities can be observed in Figure 1.

Results

Concerning our first hypothesis regarding the appearance of some hub microstates during the resting-state, we can observe that there are all-to-all significant transitions between the microstates in Sub1 and Sub2. Although there are a few fewer transitions for a single microstate in Sub3 and Sub4; there are no dominant (hub) microstates in any subject. Regarding our second hypothesis concerning the effects of music on the dynamics of the microstates, we can observe from Figure 1 that there is no significant difference in the number of transitions between rest and passive conditions. However, we can observe in Figure 2 that the mean duration of microstates significantly increases in Sub2 for all microstates, while the mean duration of microstate B in Sub1 (rest: M=34.36ms, SD=± 18.14, passive: M=32.34ms, SD=± 15.42) and Sub3 (rest: M=38.95, SD± 23.84, passive: M=27.83, SD=± 11.84)  decreases during the passive condition, the difference in mean in smaller. A bigger difference in Sub2 is expected as this patient is MCS+, which is clinically diagnosed as having a higher level of consciousness compared to the rest of the subjects in this study.

Conclusion

Our results show that the mean duration of the microstates, specifically in the MCS+ (Sub2) subject, is increased during music listening. This can indicate that the brain dynamics in this state are becoming more stable as the microstates persist for longer durations before transitioning to other microstates. Additionally, greater changes for the MCS+ subject specifically can indicate that the perception of music is preserved better in higher levels of consciousness. Moreover, this effect can also be due to the damage in different neural pathways caused by different aetologies in each patient.
