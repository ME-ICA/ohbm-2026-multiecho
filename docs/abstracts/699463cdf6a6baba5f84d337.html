Introduction

Navigated repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (nrTMS) is applied worldwide in presurgical evaluation to map the cortical areas that are responsible for speech processing and production (speech cortical mapping, SCM).^1 In SCM TMS the patient’s cortex is noninvasively stimulated while they perform a language task, such as object naming.^2 A cortical area is defined as speech related, if repeated speech errors are observed when stimulating that area. SCM TMS is performed as a priori for patients preparing for a neurosurgical operation, with the objective of locating and avoiding the resection of cortical areas involved in the processing and production of speech. Thus, SCM TMS is considered valuable in designing awake craniotomy and refining the language tests conducted during the operation. Since object naming is a high-level cognitive task with large individual variance in the exact organization of the underlying cortical network, the most accurate localization could be achieved if the speech mapping procedure parameters, e.g., pulse timing and location, were optimized for each patient. We studied here whether spatial and temporal cortical activation patterns obtained with magnetoencephalography (MEG) could guide speech TMS mapping, by giving the TMS operator prior information about the patient’s individual speech-related brain activations.

Methods

We recruited 13 healthy native Finnish speakers for an object naming task which was ran both during MEG and SCM with nrTMS (see Fig. 1 A-B). During both measurements, the subjects were instructed to name aloud in Finnish the same set of color images optimized for Finnish speakers (high familiarity, high name agreement, both living and non-living objects). In MEG, the subjects also performed two control tasks: silent picture naming and picture observation. In SCM TMS, subjects were stimulated over a large area in the left hemisphere, extending from the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) to the angular gyrus, and from the superior and middle temporal cortices to the sensorimotor cortex. We stimulated the subjects with default timings of the TMS pulse delivery (picture-to-TMS-intervals (PTIs) of 0, 200, 300, and 400 ms), as well as with 3-4 individualized custom times (PTIs of e.g., 180, 230, 340, and 460 ms) that were different for each participant. We chose the custom times based on each subject’s evoked MEG responses during object naming, by selecting the time instances where new cortical areas started to activate or where the left-hemispheric evoked activity peaked (see Fig. 1 A-B). The TMS-evoked speech errors were subsequently analyzed based on video recordings of the experiment, by comparing each speech error to a baseline recording.^1 Errors were classified based on the work by Corina et al, to performance errors, no response errors, semantic errors, and other errors.^3 We calculated the error rates for each subject for each PTI. We tested with one-tailed binomial tests whether stimulating over any cortical area (frontal, sensorimotor, parietal, and temporal) at any PTI yielded significantly (p<0.05) more speech errors compared to the average errors rate of that subject.

Results

Group-level MEG results (see Fig. 1 C) demonstrated bilateral activation of the visual cortex, followed by activation in the parietal lobe and middle-posterior temporal lobe ~150 ms after the picture onset. Frontal lobe activation was observed after ~300 ms in the left hemisphere, with the temporal lobe activation shifting towards the anterior middle temporal lobe.  

In nrTMS SCM, we found the highest error rate by using a PTI timing that was customized based on the MEG results for 8 of 13 subjects (see Fig. 2 C). The highest overall error rates were obtained with PTIs at 301-399 ms and 201-299 ms (4.17% and 4.08%, respectively). The 201-299 ms range had more significant PTI and stimulation location combinations (5) compared to 301-399 ms range (2). At the group level, speech errors were found everywhere in the stimulated area, with the largest number of errors detected after stimulation of the frontal and parietal lobes (see Fig. 2 A), including the IFG, inferior-middle sensorimotor areas, supramarginal gyrus, and angular gyrus. Speech error incidence (the number of subjects who made at least one speech error after stimulation of a specific cortical area) (Fig. 2 B), revealed that speech errors, regardless of subject, were most reliably elicited in  the IFG, inferior motor cortex, supramarginal gyrus, and the anterior and middle sections of the temporal gyrus. Error rates varied across subjects from over 7% (two subjects) to less than 1% (one subject). Binomial testing of error rates revealed 16 significant stimulation area and PTI combinations in 9 subjects. Eight of the significant PTIs were observed in the time window of 200-275 ms. Eight significant stimulation areas were in the frontal lobe, with 68% of their stimuli in the IFG.

Conclusion

Speech production errors were observed in all subjects during speech cortical mapping (SCM), although individual variability in the error rates was large. Spatially, the most reliable stimulation area to evoke speech errors was the IFG, which was highlighted by the large overall number of errors, number of subjects with errors after stimulation, and the number of significant stimulation time point and location combinations. Spatially, these results are in agreement with previous SCM TMS findings.^4 Temporally, the PTI ranges with most evoked errors were 201-299 ms and 301-399 ms. Our results, regarding the best PTI, differ from previous work from, e.g., Sollmann et al^5, who reported a clear decrease in group level error rate when increasing the PTI from 0 ms to 500 ms in 100 ms increments. Our results suggest that without a priori information about optimal PTI for each individual, mapping of fairly large cortical areas with different PTIs is recommended, to maximize and increase reliability of the induced speech-related errors, starting with PTI 200 ms or 201-299 ms.
