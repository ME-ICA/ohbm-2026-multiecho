Introduction

Repeatedly rehearsing information has long been known to facilitate long-term memory retention. This phenomenon, also known as the “testing effect”, has recently been implicated in accelerating systems consolidation by shifting memory systems contributions towards the neocortex. Previous fMRI studies employing encoding and recall repetitions found an increased involvement of cortical regions, particularly the medial posterior parietal cortex (mPPC), with a concurrent decrease of hippocampal engagement over learning repetitions1,2. Moreover, these rehearsal-related activation changes were accompanied by learning-specific changes in mPPC microstructure, suggesting the rapid emergence of an independent neocortical memory engram shortly after learning3. Rehearsal may speed up neocortical learning by repeatedly reactivating mnemonic information during retrieval, leading to the fast integration of freshly encoded memories into existing cortical networks4. However, the precise neural mechanisms underlying rehearsal’s beneficial effects on memory remain poorly understood. In addition, it is unclear how rehearsal influences the quality of these fast-emerging neocortical memory traces. While the hippocampus has traditionally been linked to storing highly detailed episodic memories, the neocortex has been associated with hosting more generalized semantic information. It is currently unknown whether rehearsal differentially affects memory formation for specific aspects of memory, and where resulting memory traces are represented in the cortex. In this study, we address these questions by combining functional MRI with a behavioral paradigm that allows us to separate the effects of rehearsal vs. single-opportunity learning on memory, as well as to differentiate semantic (category-level) vs. detail-rich (exemplar-level) memory aspects.

Methods

Healthy, young adults (planned N = 60) perform a complex association task designed to reveal the specific contributions of rehearsal to memory. Throughout the task, participants encode and recall unique associations between a cue and a target stimulus on a distinct background scene either once (SIN items) or four times (REH items). During recall, we test memory along three dimensions: Participants are presented with the cue and are asked to recall the category of the target, choose the correct exemplar out of four options (i.e., the target image and three lures), and indicate the location of the target on the scene. To assess memory stability, participants perform additional recall tests at 60 min and at 72 h after learning. 

We examine memory systems contributions during learning and delayed recall using fMRI at 3T. All MRI data were obtained on a 3T Siemens PRISMA Magnetom scanner with a 64-channel head-neck coil at the Max Planck Institute for Biological Cybernetics in Tübingen, Germany. Functional imaging was conducted with a multiband EPI sequence (2000 ms TR; 30 ms TE; 72° FA; 208×208 mm² FOV; 2 mm isotropic voxel size; 2 mm slice thickness, 60 transversal slices with interleaved slice acquisition, GRAPPA 2, MB 2). Additionally, a magnetic B0 field map was acquired for distortion correction of the functional images. For anatomical reference, a high-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE (2300 ms TR, 4.18 ms TE, 900ms TI, 9° FA, 256×256×176 mm³ FOV, 1 mm isotropic voxel size, GRAPPA 2x1) was obtained.

Preprocessing of the imaging data was performed using a custom pipeline. Anatomical images were ACPC-aligned, bias field corrected, brain extracted, and registered to the MNI152NLin6Asym template. Functional data were motion-corrected and then registered to the 2 mm template space in a single resampling step. The resampled functional images were intensity normalized, smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm FWHM, and high-pass filtered at 0.01 Hz. 

Functional data were analyzed in a whole-brain two-level model using SPM12. On the first level, we used fixed effects GLMs to model activity that is specific to REH vs. SIN items during the complex association task and the delayed recall tests. Additional nuisance regressors included 6 head motion parameters and framewise displacement. First-level contrasts were smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 8 mm FWHM and entered into second-level random effects GLMs. Resulting statistical maps were corrected for multiple comparisons and labeled using the Harvard-Oxford Structural Atlas. All reported contrasts are full-volume family-wise error corrected (pFWE < .05), if not indicated otherwise. Additionally, we performed a conjunction analysis on the statistical maps derived from the delayed recall tests to identify brain regions that show stable differential activity for REH vs. SIN items over time.

Results

Rehearsal improves memory performance

Preliminary analyses (n = 33) revealed that rehearsal improved recall performance from the first (i.e., REH1) to the fourth (i.e., REH4) learning repetition for category (t32 = 4.83, p < .001) and exemplar (t32 = 11.11, p < .001) information (see Fig. 1A). Performances for REH items remained stable at 60 min after learning for category (t32 = -0.39, p = .700) and exemplar (t32 = 0.40, p = .691) information, whereas performance for SIN items decreased for both memory dimensions (category: t32 = 2.29, p = .029, exemplar: t32 = 4.03, p < .001). There were no differences between REH and SIN items in memory loss from the 60 min to the 72 h delayed recall (category: t32 = 0.34, p = .735, exemplar: t32 = 0.59, p = .559). 

Rehearsal-related shift towards the neocortex remains stable for at least 72 h and is related to memory performance 

To investigate how memory systems contributions change with rehearsal, we assessed differences in functional activity for REH4 and SIN items during encoding and recall. During encoding, REH4 items showed higher functional activation in large clusters covering the posterior parietal cortex, including the precuneus, posterior cingulate cortex, and the supramarginal gyrus compared to SIN items (see Fig. 1B). We found functional activity in these areas to be linearly increasing over REH encoding repetitions. By contrast, REH4 items exhibited widespread lower activation in the areas of the dorsal and ventral visual stream, extending into the hippocampus, and in the inferior frontal gyrus compared to SIN items (see Fig. 1D). Notably, we found a similar activation pattern for linearly decreasing activity for REH items over encoding repetitions. 

A subset of regions differentially activated during encoding of REH vs. SIN items also displayed similar differences in recall. The areas of ventral visual stream and the inferior frontal gyrus also showed lower activation for REH4 vs. SIN items, specifically during recall of exemplar information. This pattern overlapped with areas exhibiting decreasing activity over recall repetitions for REH exemplar information. Lower activity for REH4 vs. SIN items also persisted across both delayed recall tests at 60 min and 72 h after learning (pFDR < .05). On the other hand, an involvement of the precuneus during recall of REH vs. SIN items emerged only across delays, and was specifically observed for the recall of category information (pFDR < .05). 

We also assessed whether functional activation changes over REH encoding repetitions were related to memory performance. In the precuneus, activity changes across repeated encoding of REH items were positively correlated with changes in recall accuracy from REH1 to REH4 for exemplar information (r = .35, p = .047), indicating that linearly increasing precuneal activity was linked to a stronger learning increase for item details over repetitions (see Fig. 1C). Moreover, changes in encoding-related activity over REH repetitions in the cluster spanning the left ventral visual stream were related to recall performance for exemplar information of REH items at 72 h after learning (r = -.43, p = .015), i.e. a linear decrease in ventral visual stream activity was associated with better long-term retention of detail-rich rehearsed information (see Fig. 1E). 

Conclusion

Our preliminary findings indicate that rehearsal creates precise and stable memories. Rehearsal drastically improves recall performances within only four learning repetitions and promotes long-term retention by preventing short-term accuracy loss. On a network level, rehearsal rapidly changes memory processing in the neocortex. Over rehearsal, activation during encoding increased in the precuneus, while the hippocampus and areas of the ventral visual stream showed the opposite pattern. This encoding-related decrease in the ventral visual stream is linked to better long-term retention of item details, indicating a role of this region specifically in fine-grained object recognition. This finding aligns well with prior work implicating the ventral visual stream in supporting object recognition by providing and integrating increasingly complex individual object features along its processing hierarchy5. The role of the precuneus, however, is not as clear. In line with previous evidence, we found precuneal activity to increase over repeated encoding. This increase in activation seems to not be specifically connected to either category or exemplar components. Recall activity specific to rehearsal in the precuneus only emerges for category information during delayed recall periods. In contrast, the encoding-related increase in precuneal activation is associated with performance improvements for detailed memory over repetitions. This finding is in line with previous studies linking the precuneus to representations of conceptual and semantic information on the one hand, and detailed episodic representations on the other hand. Together, our findings point to rehearsal as a driver of fast-emerging, stable, and behaviorally relevant neocortical memory traces.
