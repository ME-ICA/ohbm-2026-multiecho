Introduction

Understanding how brain structure shapes neural dynamics is fundamental to explaining cognition, development, and disease. The brain's computational abilities emerge from precisely organized networks where information flows through specialized pathways that conform to systematic organizational principles. However, standard artificial neural networks (ANNs) often overlook these biological constraints, thereby limiting their value in understanding brain function and developing effective treatments.

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), a specialized type of ANN, are powerful tools for modeling neural dynamics because they can process time series inputs and incorporate information about the brain’s spatial organization1,2. RNNs have been used to model sensory and cognitive processes in humans and other animals. However, typical RNN architectures exhibit two critical departures from biology (Figure 1A). Firstly, they employ all-to-all connectivity where every node processes task inputs and contributes directly to outputs; secondly, their internal connections (i.e., the hidden layer) are not spatially constrained. This allows networks trained on identical tasks to converge on vastly different architectures that depart from known biology, where specialized regions handle distinct functions. For example, visual information enters the brain through the visual cortex, while decision-making primarily engages the association cortex. In this work, we developed biophysical recurrent neural networks (bioRNNs) that address these limitations by incorporating two constraints that capture fundamental principles of brain organization (Figure 1B).

Methods

Our networks consisted of 100 nodes representing cortical regions from the Schaefer brain atlas3, and included two biophysical constraints (Figure 1B). First, we implemented input/output (I/O) masking that restricts task inputs to the 14 nodes designated as "visual cortex" and constrains outputs to the 27 non-overlapping nodes representing "association cortex," mirroring sequential information flow in the brain. Next, we introduced spatial embedding that constrains the hidden layer’s connectivity based on the brain's sensorimotor-association (S-A) axis4, a fundamental organizational gradient spanning from primary sensory areas to higher-order regions involved in complex cognition. This biophysical embedding uses inter-regional distances along the S-A axis to shape connection strengths. 

We trained our networks on established cognitive tasks, including Go-NoGo (GNG) and Delayed Match to Sample (DMS), which are part of the NeuroGym library5. We tested three RNN configurations: (1) standard (‘vanilla’) RNNs, which did not employ I/O masking or spatial embedding; (2) masked RNNs without spatial embedding; (3) masked and spatially embedded RNNs. For each configuration, we trained 100 randomly initialized networks, resulting in a total of 300 trained networks. Training was performed in PyTorch using the Adam optimizer. 

We performed two main comparisons of our networks. First, we assessed the effect of the two biophysical constraints on training speed and task performance (accuracy). Then, we compared simulated dynamics to empirical brain dynamics based on regional intrinsic neural timescales (INTs). INTs measure how long neural activity persists in a region, and are known to vary systematically in the brain, with the shortest INTs in sensory regions and the longest in association regions. Empirical INTs were based on resting-state fMRI data from 1,086 subjects from the Human Connectome Project. 

Results

Biophysical constraints proved to be crucial for both training success and emergent dynamics, especially when task demands were high (Figure 2A). For the simple GNG task, all RNNs achieved 100% accuracy, but introducing biophysical constraints increased the training burden, resulting in a longer delay to peak performance. However, for the highly demanding DMS task, introducing I/O masking resulted in a severe learning deficit (a drop from 95% accuracy to 38% at full training), which recovered to 81% accuracy through spatial embedding of the hidden layer connections. 

Critically, bioRNNs exhibited neural dynamics that correlated with those observed in human brains (Figure 2B). We assessed INTs by comparing autocorrelation decay in node-level time series from trained networks and from resting-state fMRI data. BioRNN INTs correlated significantly with empirical INTs (rho = 0.46, p < 0.001), while those of vanilla RNNs did not (rho = -0.21, p = 0.041).

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that incorporating simple biological constraints into RNNs allows them to capture fundamental aspects of neural computation. Our framework additionally opens up the possibility of incorporating diverse neurobiological features, including gene expression patterns and cytoarchitectural properties, into neural network models. This positions bioRNNs as versatile experimental platforms bridging neuroscience discovery and AI advances. 

Our findings have implications across multiple neuroscientific domains. BioRNNs provide tools for (i) testing how brain structure constrains function in systems neuroscience, (ii) modeling the emergence of the cortical hierarchy and neurodevelopmental disruptions in developmental neuroscience, and (iii) creating disease modeling platforms in clinical applications by perturbing biological constraints to illuminate structure-to-symptom pathways. Our future work will expand the framework to incorporate other biological constraints (e.g., myelin) and interrogate the brain structure-function relationship underlying symptoms in various psychiatric conditions.
