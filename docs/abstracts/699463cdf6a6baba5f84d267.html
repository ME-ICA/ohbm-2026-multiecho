Introduction

Sleep is a complex physiological function that is tightly associated with the brain [1]. Sleep is also associated with the cardiovascular and thermoregulatory systems [2,3]. Around sleep onset, the distal skin temperature (DST) increases before reaching a plateau during the night [4]. In late life, both heart rate variability (HRV) during sleep and DST increase before sleep onset may index the integrity of autonomic–thermal control. We hypothesized that higher sleep HRV and stronger HRV - Temperature coupling may be related to structural differences in regions supporting interoception and autonomic regulation, reflecting preserved brain–body integration.

Methods

522 adults aged 65–80 years (mean ± SD: 73.8 ± 2.6 years old) with 290 women (55%) wore an Oura Ring for up to a month, to record DST and heart rate, and participated in an MRI study. For each participant, HRV was averaged across at least seven sleep episodes. Temperature was defined as the final DST of the pre-sleep increase from the same episodes. Vertex-wise cortical thickness was modeled from T1 images processed with FreeSurfer using a general linear model with intercept, HRV, Temperature, their interaction (HRV - Temperature), and total intracranial volume (TIV) as a covariate. Multiple comparisons were controlled with FreeSurfer’s cluster-wise correction; cluster-wise p-values (CWP) are reported.

Results

On average, sleeping heart rate was 60.8 bpm (± 7.2) and HRV was 36.1 ms (± 21.8). The Temperature at the end of the DST increase was 35.5°C (± 0.4). HRV and Temperature were significantly correlated (p < .001) with a negative relationship (coefficient = -0.15). Higher sleep HRV was positively associated with cortical thickness in the left inferior temporal cortex (cluster size = 1568.83 mm²; peak MNI: −45.2, −55.4, −8.0; CWP = 0.012 and the right rostral middle frontal cortex (cluster size = 1604.30 mm²; peak MNI: 42.2, 29.2, 20.9; CWP = 0.010). The HRV - Temperature interaction was positively associated with cortical thickness in bilateral anterior temporal–insular regions (left cluster size = 3291.93 mm²; peak MNI: −28.2, 5.8, −34.2; CWP < .001; right cluster size = 2484.33 mm²; peak MNI: 35.1, −1.4, 10.2; CWP < .001).

Conclusion

Findings link sleep-related physiology to cortical morphology in older adults. Mainly, stronger HRV - Temperature coupling—reflecting coordinated parasympathetic dominance with distal vasodilation at sleep onset—maps to bilateral insula–temporal pole, centers for interoception and autonomic–limbic integration [5]. These region-specific associations support a brain–body mechanism whereby preserved cortical substrates integrating cardiovascular and thermoregulatory signals may be associated with healthier sleep physiology in older adults. Future work should relate these physiological markers to sleep quality and clinical outcomes.
