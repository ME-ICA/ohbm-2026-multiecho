Introduction

Neuronal integrity and function play an important role in the maturation of large-scale cortical networks. However, it is not well understood if cortical neurometabolism is organized along macroscale hierarchical gradients and how such organization evolves during the development from childhood to adulthood. N-acetylaspartate (NAA), a marker of neuronal density and mitochondrial function, provides a unique window into cortical maturation. However, due to the lack of high-resolution whole-brain metabolic imaging capability, cortex-wide NAA connectivity mapping has not been well studied. In this study, we leveraged a high-resolution magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging technology to map cortex-wide NAA connectivity gradients. We also investigated age-related differences in global gradient architecture and network-specific positioning across canonical functional systems, which yielded useful insights.

Methods

This cross-sectional study included a total of 166 healthy participants: 78 children (mean age = 10.35 ± 0.25 years) and 88 adults (mean age = 31.3 ± 8.8 years). MRI data were acquired on Siemens 3T scanners at Shanghai Mental Health Center and Shanghai Children's Medical Center (Shanghai, China). Whole-brain proton MR spectroscopic imaging (¹H-MRSI) was collected using the SPICE sequence (TR/TE = 160/1.6 ms; spatial resolution = 2.0 × 3.0 × 3.3 mm³; FOV = 240 × 240 × 120 mm³). High-resolution structural images were acquired using a 3D MPRAGE sequence (isotropic resolution = 1.0 mm³; FOV = 240 × 240 × 192 mm³).

Metabolic maps were water-referenced and normalized to MNI space using a two-step registration procedure anchored by the MRSI water signal and the T1-weighted image. Voxel-wise NAA maps were parcellated using the Schaefer-100 atlas. For each region, NAA distributions were extracted, and participant-specific metabolic similarity matrices were constructed by computing Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) between all regional pairs.

To emphasize the strongest metabolic similarities, matrices were row-wise thresholded by retaining the top 20% positive connections prior to gradient estimation. Cortical gradients were derived using diffusion maps embedding, and participant-specific gradients were aligned to a group template using iterative Procrustes rotation. For cytoarchitectural visualization, Economo–Koskinas classes were collapsed by merging primary and secondary sensory cortices. To assess group differences in metabolic connectivity while controlling family-wise error at the subnetwork level, we applied the network-based statistic with an edge-wise t-threshold and 5,000 permutations.

Results

Children exhibited a more compressed NAA gradient architecture compared with adults. In PC1–PC2 space, adult cortical parcels were more widely dispersed along the principal gradient axis, whereas parcels in children showed tighter clustering, indicating reduced metabolic gradient segregation (Figure 2A, left). Group comparisons further demonstrated widespread age-group differences in both PC1 and PC2 (PFDR < 0.05), consistent with developmental strengthening of hierarchical neurometabolic organization (Figure 2A, right).

Network-level analyses demonstrated that developmental effects were not uniform across cortical systems. PC1 distributions differed most prominently in higher-order association networks, with the default-mode and frontoparietal control networks showing the largest age-related increases in dispersion, consistent with progressive metabolic specialization of transmodal cortex. In contrast, visual and sensorimotor networks showed relatively smaller differences, suggesting earlier stabilization of metabolic organization in primary systems, while the salience network showed intermediate patterns (Figure 2B, left). Cytoarchitectural stratification further supported this hierarchy. When parcels were grouped by Economo–Koskinas classes, PC1 values showed systematic ordering across classes in both age groups, indicating preserved alignment between metabolic gradients and cytoarchitecture throughout development (Figure 2B, right). At the connectivity level, group-mean metabolic networks revealed age-related reconfiguration, with network-based statistics indicating that developmental effects were concentrated in interactions involving higher-order association systems rather than primary sensory–motor networks.

Conclusion

This study provides the first population-level, in vivo characterization of cortical neurometabolic gradients in the human brain. Our experimental results showed that regions located higher along the principal gradient had higher neurometabolite concentrations, lower intracortical myelination, reduced mitochondrial density and oxidative energy capacity, and preferential involvement in internally oriented cognitive functions. These findings may establish neurometabolic gradients as a fundamental macroscale organizational dimension of the human cortex, linking cellular energetic architecture to large-scale brain organization and providing a framework for investigating metabolic alterations in health and disease.
