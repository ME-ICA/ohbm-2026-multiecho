Introduction

Deception is a cognitively demanding process that imposes a behavioral "lie cost," characterized by longer response times and reduced accuracy due to increased demands on executive control. Neuroimaging meta-analyses indicate that this process consistently recruits a frontoparietal control network, including the middle and superior frontal gyri, inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), anterior cingulate cortex, and inferior parietal lobule (Christ et al. 2009). Beyond general executive demands, the neural correlates of deception appear to be content-specific; for instance, lying about personal beliefs versus past events recruits distinct neural substrates associated with self-referential and episodic processes, respectively (Ofen et al., 2017). While the cognitive costs of deception are well-documented, individual differences in personality—specifically psychopathic traits—may fundamentally alter these dynamics. Psychopathy is associated with atypical neural organization, particularly deficits in executive control and socio-affective processing. However, neuroimaging studies directly examining the interaction between psychopathy and deceptive behavior remain scarce, and it is unknown how psychopathic traits modulate the neural implementation of lying across different types of content. To address this gap, this study combined large-scale trait screening with an fMRI paradigm to investigate how psychopathy influences the behavioral and neural costs of lying about beliefs versus episodic memories.

Methods

We collected online questionnaire data from 291 participants (M = 23, SD = 3.35; 91 females and 199 males) who completed the Self-Report Psychopathy Scale–III (SRP-III). Individuals in the top and bottom 30% of the SRP total-score distribution were invited to participate in the fMRI experiment. The fMRI sample comprised 60 participants (M = 23, SD = 2.83), divided into a high-psychopathy group (HighP; 14 men, 16 women) and a low-psychopathy group (LowP; 9 men, 21 women).

Using a paradigm adapted from Ofen et al. (2017), participants performed a mixed 2 (Question Type: Belief vs Episodic) × 2 (Cue: Lie vs Truth) × 2 (Group: HighP vs LowP) design. Belief questions probed personal opinions or social beliefs; episodic questions probed personally experienced events. On each trial, a cue instructed participants to respond either honestly (“truth”) or deceptively (“lie”) to a yes/no question. For each participant, lie and truth cues were approximately balanced within each question type (belief, episodic), such that the number of lie and truth trials was matched. Each unique question appeared only once per participant with a single assigned cue, and the mapping of questions to lie versus truth was counterbalanced across participants. Accuracy and response times (RT) were recorded for all trials.

Functional images were acquired on a 3T scanner and preprocessed in SPM12 (slice timing correction, realignment, coregistration, normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing). First-level general linear models contained separate regressors for Belief-Lie, Belief-Truth, Episodic-Lie, and Episodic-Truth. At the group level, whole-brain analyses tested the main effects of Cue (Lie − Truth), Question Type (Belief − Episodic), their interaction, and group differences in lie–truth contrasts (LowP − HighP, HighP − LowP).

Results

Behaviorally, deceptive responses were characterized by reduced accuracy and longer response times relative to truthful trials. Within the deceptive conditions, belief-related lies yielded lower accuracy and longer response times than episodic lies, indicating that belief-based deception was both less accurate and more time-consuming than episodic deception. There were no significant main or interaction effects involving Group in RT, accuracy, or IES, indicating that high- and low-psychopathy groups showed comparable behavioral performance.

Neural analyses of the Lie > Truth contrast across all participants replicated activation in the bilateral middle frontal gyrus (MFG), superior frontal gyrus (SFG), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), and inferior parietal lobule (IPL). We observed a content-based dissociation: Episodic questions elicited greater activity in the PCC and angular gyrus, whereas Belief questions engaged the left IFG and MFG.

Despite similar behavior, group comparisons revealed distinct neural strategies. In the Lie > Truth contrast, LowP participants (LowP > HighP) showed greater recruitment of frontal control regions (bilateral MFG, right IFG, ACC). Specifically for belief-based deception, the LowP group showed enhanced engagement of the right IFG and insula. Conversely, in the left caudate, while both groups showed deactivation during lying, HighP participants exhibited significantly less suppression than the LowP group.

Conclusion

Lying imposed a clear cognitive cost, particularly for belief-based questions, yet this cost did not differ between the high- and low-psychopathy groups at the behavioral level. Instead, were associated with distinct neural implementations of deception: low-psychopathy individuals showed stronger Lie − Truth effects in frontal control and conflict-monitoring regions, whereas high-psychopathy individuals exhibited attenuated frontal Lie–Truth differences and altered caudate engagement, especially for belief- and episodic-based deception. By replicating core deception-related regions from prior meta-analytic work and extending content-specific effects reported for episodic versus belief lies, these findings suggest that individual traits and lie content jointly shape the large-scale brain systems that support dishonest behavior.
