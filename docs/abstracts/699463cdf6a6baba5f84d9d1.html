Introduction

The cingulum is a white matter (WM) structure implicated in many neurological conditions including Alzheimer's Disease (AD). This complex tract, part of which forms part of the Papez Circuit,1 plays a critical role in higher-order cognitive functions, including attention and memory formation and retrieval. Most research to date has  modeled the cingulum as a single, continuous bundle, yet it has been demonstrated that it may be subdivided into  subgenual (SGC),  parahippocampal (PHC), retrosplenial (RSC), parahippocampal (PHC) components, each with distinct anatomical, microstructural, and functional properties.2

Individuals with Subjective Cognitive Decline (SCD) and Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) are at increased risk of progressing to AD dementia. Characterizing microstructural alterations within individual cingulum subdivisions using diffusion MRI (dMRI) may provide greater anatomical specificity for detecting early neurobiological changes and improving diagnostic sensitivity. 

Accordingly, we used dMRI data from the Dementia Prevention Research Clinic (DPRC) in New Zealand,3 to assess whether anatomically defined PHC, RSC, and SGC subdivisions derived from tractography in native space using HARDI (High Angular Resolution Diffusion Imaging) tractography. We examined three complementary mulit-shell dMRI metrics: Fractional Anisotropy (FA), an indirect measure of tract integrity; Mean Diffusivity (MD), sensitive to demyelination and axonal loss; and Mean Kurtosis (MK), capturing diffusion restriction associated with microstructural complexity and inflammation.

Methods

dMRI data were analyzed from 420 participants enrolled across three DPRC sites in New Zealand:3 controls (n = 66), SCD (n = 92), amnestic MCI (n = 151), and mild AD (n = 40). Group classification was determined by a multidisciplinary team using standard diagnostic criteria. Images were acquired on 3T Siemens systems with a harmonised multi-shell protocol (2 × 2 × 2 mm³, b = 1000 and 2000 s/mm², 100 directions). Preprocessing followed a DESIGNER-aligned pipeline including MP-PCA denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, susceptibility, motion and eddy-current correction, and N4 bias-field correction.

Whole-brain spherical deconvolution tractography (damped Richardson–Lucy) was performed in StarTrack.4 Using a novel tool named Megatrack ,5 individual tractograms are transformed to MNI space. Then a single manual dissection of left and right PHC, RSC, and SGC cingulum subdivisions was performed using inclusion (“AND”, “EITHER END”) and exclusion (“NOT”) ROIs derived from FreeSurfer parcellations, and prior anatomical work on this cingulum subdivision.2 The resulting tract dissections were then mapped back to native space for each participant with Megatrack.5 FA, MD and MK maps were calculated using DESIGNER2-TMI and mean values extracted for each cingulum subdivision in each hemisphere in native space. Group differences in FA, MD and MK were evaluated using a mixed ANCOVA with subdivision and hemisphere as within-subject factors and diagnostic group as a between-subject factor, controlling for age and sex, with FDR correction applied to post hoc comparisons.

Results

Figure 1 shows dissections of the SGC. RSC, PHC, cingulum subdivisions in template space (Figure 1.A), alongside age and gender adjusted diffusion-metric residuals (FA, MD, and MK) plotted for each diagnostic group within each subdivision (Figure 1.B), with significant pairwise differences indicated. A mixed ANCOVA revealed a significant effect of group on MD (p_FDR < 0.001) and MK (p_FDR < 0.001), but not on FA (p_FDR = 0.063). In addition, a significant Group × Subdivision interaction was observed for MD (p_FDR < 0.001) and MK (p = 0.027), indicating that group-related microstructural differences varied across cingulum subdivisions. Hemispheric effects were not present for FA and MD but were pronounced for MK (F = 80.82, pFDR < 0.001). As expected, strong main effects of subdivision were observed across all metrics (FA: F = 2106.5; MD: F = 1259.7; MK: F = 920.49; all pFDR < 0.001), confirming distinct microstructural profiles for PHC, RSC, and SGC. 

In the SGC tract, pairwise group comparisons revealed significantly lower FA in MCI compared with both Controls and SCD. In addition, MCI showed significantly higher MD relative to Controls. No significant differences were observed in MK between any pair of groups within the SGC subdivision.

No pairwise group differences in FA, MD, or MK within the RSC subdivision survived FDR correction.

The PHC subdivision showed the most pronounced group effects. Both MCI and AD demonstrated significantly higher MD compared with Controls and SCD (all pFDR < 0.05), consistent with a progressive increase in diffusivity with advancing cellular atrophy and degeneration. MK exhibited higher values in SCD and MCI followed by a reduction for AD , suggesting early microstructural complexity changes that are not maintained at later stages. Interestingly, no significant group differences were observed in FA within the PHC subdivision.

Conclusion

Our results highlight several key findings. First, they indicate that microstructural metrics (FA, MD, and MK) exhibit robust subdivision-specific differences across the PHC, RSC, and SGC, and that this anatomical differentiation is maintained across the AD continuum. This highlights the importance of treating the cingulum as a heterogeneous tract and considering its subdivision when examining neurobiological changes.

Secondly, the patterns of microstructural change across AD risk groups differed between cingulum subdivisions. MD was higher in MCI and AD relative to controls in the PHC and SGC, consistent with microstructural degeneration at later stages of the AD continuum. In the SGC, FA showed a reduction in MCI compared to SCD and C groups, which is common in other dMRI studies and potentially indicating decreased fiber integrity. Interestingly only in the PHC, MK displayed a non-monotonic pattern, with SCD and MCI showing increased restriction, potentially reflecting early neuroinflammatory restriction from reactive astrocytes and activated microglia present in early AD pathology followed by a reduction in MK later stages (AD), where degeneration and atrophy lessen diffusion restriction.

Importantly, across all metrics, the PHC emerged as the most sensitive subdivision, showing the clearest gradient of change along the AD risk continuum for MD and MK. This aligns with prior research identifying the hippocampal and posterior cingulum regions as early and strongly affected in AD dementia due to their central role in memory processes1.

Together, these results suggest that cingulum subdivisions exhibit distinct microstructural profiles and that their differences in diffusion metrics across AD risk stages should be investigated further. Of note is the PHC, showing the largest between group differences. Considering these subdivisions independently may improve biomarker specificity and enhance our anatomical understanding of the neurobiological changes associated with AD pathology.
