Introduction

Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias (AD/ADRD) involve complex biological changes that initiate decades before clinical symptoms manifest. Current models of AD progression posit that the extracellular accumulation of beta-amyloid (Aβ) plaques occurs early in the disease continuum, serving as a critical upstream trigger.1  As the disease advances, this is followed by the spread of intracellular tau tangles, which is strongly linked to neuronal damage and worsening cognitive decline.2  While Aβ PET is an excellent early predictor and tau PET better tracks disease severity3, analyzing these images separately often misses the critical interaction between them. Emerging evidence suggests that the synergistic relationship between amyloid and tau may be the primary driver of neurodegeneration.4 Consequently, there is a critical need for advanced analytical frameworks capable of integrating these complementary signals. Our project addresses this gap by implementing a data-driven multimodal fusion framework to identify joint biomarkers of disease pathology. By integrating 18F-Florbetapir (FBP) and 18F-Flortaucipir (tau) PET using Joint Independent Component Analysis (jICA), we aim to isolate linked spatial sources of amyloid-tau co-modulation and validate their superior utility in predicting cognitive severity compared to standard global and unimodal biomarkers.

Methods

Study Design and Data Acquisition: Longitudinal multimodal PET data were obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database. To ensure temporal correspondence between modalities, we included only subject visits where FBP and tau PET scans were acquired within a ±180-day window. The final cohort consisted of 378 matched scan pairs across three diagnostic groups: Cognitively Normal (CN, n=215), Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI, n=110), and Dementia (n=53).

Preprocessing and Feature Extraction: Both PET modalities underwent the same preprocessing pipeline to ensure precise voxel-wise correspondence. Images were spatially normalized to the MNI standard template using SPM, smoothed with a 10-mm Gaussian kernel, and spatially downsampled to 3×3×3 mm³ resolution using AFNI. We then constructed a multimodal feature space by flattening the preprocessed intensity maps into 1D vectors. Prior to concatenation, each modality vector was Z-scored to normalize intensity ranges and ensure equal contribution to the joint model. These were then merged, resulting in a final data matrix of 378 samples × 136,470 features (voxels).

Multimodal Decomposition and Statistical Identification: To fully characterize the fine-grained spatial heterogeneity of AD pathology, we applied Joint Independent Component Analysis (jICA) with a high model order of 100 to decompose the fused data into maximally independent multimodal signatures. To benchmark the specific advantages of this fusion framework, we conducted unimodal ICAs on the individual amyloid and tau datasets derived from the same subject cohort. Subsequent statistical identification utilized linear mixed models (LMM) to detect significant group differences in loading coefficients across diagnostic stages (CN, MCI, Dementia), controlling for age, sex, site, race, and longitudinal dependencies (false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected q < 0.05).

Predictive Modeling and Clinical Validation: To evaluate the clinical utility of the identified signatures, we performed a Dominance Analysis (DA) to rank their individual ability to explain variance in cognitive severity (measured by the mini-mental state examination, MMSE). All continuous predictors were Z-score standardized prior to analysis to ensure direct comparability of effect sizes. To validate the advantage of our spatial approach over standard global biomarkers, we analyzed a subset of sessions (n = 42) that had concurrent CSF pTau data available. Using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression, we established a clinical baseline model (MMSE ~ Global_CSF_pTau + Age + Sex + Diagnosis) and systematically compared with models that replaced the global CSF marker with the top 5 DA-identified signatures to assess the added value of spatial topology and specificity. Finally, we assessed a combined model incorporating all five signatures. Demographic and diagnostic covariates were retained throughout to ensure the reported values reflected the unique predictive power of the biomarkers.

Results

Multimodal Component Identification: The jICA decomposition stratified the fused data into three distinct classes of joint sources. The majority (60 components) were identified as co-localized signatures, characterized by coherent, high-intensity modular patterns in both modalities. For signatures lacking this mutual overlap, we designated them as "dominant" based on which modality primarily drove the joint signal topology while the other exhibited only scattered or low-intensity patterns. Using this convention, 11 components were classified as amyloid-dominant and 15 as tau-dominant (Fig 1(a)). The remaining 14 components were identified as noise or artifacts and excluded. Notably, we also identified distinct joint components localized to subcortical, cerebellar, and white matter regions that exhibited robust group differences. These regions, often overlooked in standard analyses, showed significant sensitivity to pathological changes across both early and late disease stages. For instance, specific networks co-localized to white matter showed significant differences in the early transition (CN vs. MCI) but no significant differences in later stages (MCI vs. Dementia), marking them as crucial early biomarkers of disease onset.

Diagnostic Sensitivity and Group Differences: When evaluating the 100 components derived from jICA (for Multimodal Fusion) versus those from unimodal  ICA (i.e. separate ICA for FBP and tau), LMM highlighted the synergistic advantage of fusion. In separating the early disease stages (CN vs. MCI), the multimodal framework identified 33 significant signatures, effectively retaining the high sensitivity of FBP alone (32 significant components) while outperforming tau alone (23 significant components). Crucially, in tracking later-stage progression (Dementia vs. MCI), the multimodal approach significantly outperformed single modalities by capturing the synergistic interaction of accumulated pathology. It identified 46 significant signatures, representing a 21% increase over unimodal tau (38 significant components) and a 44% increase over Unimodal FBP (32 significant components).

Functional Relevance and Predictive Utility: Dominance analysis identified the top five multimodal joint signatures driving cognitive decline. Four of these components were tau-dominant joint signatures mapping to the Triple Network (Central Executive subdomain), Higher Cognition (Temporoparietal), and Visual (Occipital and Occipitotemporal) systems, while one was a co-localized joint signature localized to the Salience network. 

When benchmarked against a clinical baseline derived from global CSF pTau, these spatially resolved biomarkers demonstrated vastly superior predictive utility. While the baseline model explained only 5.4% of the variance in MMSE scores, substituting the global marker with just the single best spatial predictor, the tau-dominant Central Executive joint signature, boosted the explained variance to 46.1%. Furthermore, the comprehensive model combining these top five selected signatures captured 63.9% of the variance (Fig 1 (b)). This corresponds to a 11-fold increase in predictive power, proving that spatially resolved multimodal signatures capture critical severity information missed by global concentration metrics.

Conclusion

This study establishes that the synergistic integration of amyloid and tau PET through data-driven fusion uncovers spatially specific interaction patterns that offer significantly superior prognostic value compared to unimodal imaging or standard fluid biomarkers. By isolating spatially independent sources of co-varying pathology, our framework successfully combined the early detection sensitivity of amyloid with the disease-tracking specificity of Tau. 

Although our direct comparison with CSF pTau was restricted to a subset of sessions (n = 42) due to CSF data availability, the observation of significant increase in predictive power within this cohort underscores the robustness of component-level analysis over global concentration metrics. This finding suggests that clinical variability is driven more by the specific topological distribution of pathology than by its global aggregate burden. Future work will aim to validate these spatial signatures in larger cohorts; however, these results already provide compelling evidence that multimodal fusion can define the granular, precision biomarkers necessary for improved patient stratification and therapeutic monitoring in Alzheimer’s disease.
