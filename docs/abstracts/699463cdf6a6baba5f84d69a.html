Introduction

The large-scale functional organization of the human cortex is hierarchically arranged along connectivity gradients, which undergo significant refinement from childhood to adolescence, marked by a major spatial reconfiguration or “flip” (Dong et al., 2021). However, conventional neurodevelopmental studies rely on broadband fMRI signals, potentially obscuring the contributions of distinct neural oscillatory timescales. Spontaneous brain activity unfolds across multiple frequency bands, each associated with specific neurophysiological processes—faster oscillations with local sensory integration and slower oscillations with long-range, associative functions (Gong & Zuo, 2024; Gong & Zuo, 2025). The multi-band architecture of functional gradients in adults is established (Gong & Zuo, 2023), yet its developmental trajectories remain uncharted. Critically, it is unknown whether the maturation of cortical hierarchy follows a single broadband timeline or multiple frequency-specific schedules, and how individual differences in cognition and social affect modulate these schedules. Addressing these gaps is essential for a mechanistic understanding of brain development and its individual variability.

Methods

We employed a multi-band frequency-resolved approach to map the development of cortical gradients. Resting-state fMRI data from 179 typically developing participants aged 6-19 years (381 scans from the Chinese Color Nest Project) were preprocessed using the Connectome Computation System pipeline, including ICA-AROMA denoising. Preprocessed BOLD time series were decomposed into three distinct slow-frequency bands—slow-3 (0.0822–0.2000 Hz), slow-4 (0.0311–0.0822 Hz), and slow-5 (0.0133–0.0311 Hz)—using the DREAM toolkit, which applies the Natural Logarithmic Linear Law framework to partition signals based on acquisition parameters (Gong et al., 2021). For each frequency band and for twelve age groups, we computed group-averaged vertex-wise functional connectivity matrices. Cortical gradients were extracted via diffusion map embedding using the BrainSpace toolbox. We focused on the first two gradients, which capture the primary unimodal-transmodal and sensory-motor segregation axes. To quantify developmental trajectories, we derived subject-level metrics from the first gradient map: an Integration-Segegration Score (FG-ISS), where positive values indicate integration-dominant (adult-like), negative values segregation-dominant (child-like), and values near zero a transitional configuration. Age-related trajectories of FG-ISS were modeled using generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs). Cross-band developmental coupling was assessed using latent change score (LCS) models to test whether baseline organization in one band predicted subsequent change in another. To investigate individual differences, participants were stratified by social sensitivity (using the Social Anxiety Scale for Children, high-sensitivity defined as score ≥7 within age group) and by general intelligence (Wechsler IQ: high-IQ ≥120, mid-IQ 100-120, low-IQ ≤100). Developmental stage timelines were compared between these subgroups and the full cohort.

Results

Our frequency-resolved analysis revealed a fundamental three-stage developmental progression (Stage I: child-like, II: transitional, III: adult-like) conserved across all bands, refining the conventional two-stage “flip” model. Crucially, the timing of these stages was highly frequency-dependent (Fig. 1). Fast oscillations (slow-3) matured earliest, with Gradient 1 reaching adult-like Stage III by ~12 years. Intermediate frequencies (slow-4) exhibited the earliest onset of Stage II (~6-7 years) but the most protracted course, with Gradient 1 stabilizing only at ~14 years and Gradient 2 not until ~18-19 years. In stark contrast, slower oscillations (slow-5) showed a compressed, peri-pubertal shift, with both gradients rapidly transitioning through Stage II and consolidating into Stage III between ~11-13 years. This slow-5 timeline aligns with the broadband “flip”, indicating prior single-band observations were dominated by this band’s dynamics. Network-level analysis confirmed band-specific reordering sequences along the gradient axis. Cross-band coupling dynamics evolved with age: in childhood (≤12 yrs), higher organization in one band predicted slower growth in another (negative coupling, e.g., slow-5→slow-3); in adolescence (≥13 yrs), this shifted to a positive bidirectional interplay between slow-3 and slow-5. Individual differences significantly modulated these frequency-specific timelines (Fig. 2). Children with high social sensitivity showed dramatically accelerated maturation across all bands, compressing the three-stage trajectory, with slow-4 reaching Stage III as early as 6-7 years—an ~8-year acceleration. High-IQ children exhibited accelerated maturation selectively in the integration-heavy slow-3 and slow-5 bands, reaching Stage III by ~8 and ~6-7 years respectively, but not in the modular-processing slow-4 band. Conversely, children with lower IQ showed generalized developmental delays across all three bands, failing to attain Stage III by mid-adolescence. The mid-IQ, average-sensitivity group converged on Stage III around age 12 across bands, establishing this as a normative multi-band milestone.

Conclusion

This study introduces a novel, frequency-resolved framework for understanding cortical development, demonstrating that the maturation of large-scale organization unfolds along multiple, distinct oscillatory timescales rather than a single broadband schedule. The discovery of a reproducible three-stage, frequency-dependent progression fundamentally advances models of neurodevelopment beyond the established “flip”. Our findings bridge oscillatory dynamics, large-scale network hierarchy, and behavior by revealing that individual differences in core cognitive and social-affective phenotypes are associated with specific accelerations or delays in these frequency-specific timelines. High social sensitivity is linked to a broad, precocious shift across bands, particularly in the intermediate “computational” slow-4 band, potentially reflecting adaptive tuning of socio-emotional circuits. Superior intelligence, however, is selectively associated with earlier maturation of integrative processing in fast (slow-3) and slow (slow-5) bands, underscoring the role of efficient cross-system integration in cognitive ability. This multi-band approach provides a more granular, functionally-informed map of normative brain maturation and generates frequency-specific developmental norms. It establishes a powerful new paradigm for future research, with direct implications for identifying sensitive biomarkers of atypical neurodevelopment, where deviations from these refined timelines may signal specific alterations in hierarchical processing or network integration across distinct neural oscillatory regimes.
