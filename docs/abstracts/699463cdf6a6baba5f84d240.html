Introduction

The human brain is an energetically demanding organ that depends primarily on glucose to sustain neuronal signalling and the maintenance of the resting membrane potential 1. Approximately 75% of the cerebral energy consumption can be attributed to neuronal processes. Since mitochondria generate cellular energy via oxidative phosphorylation, they play a central role in supporting glucose metabolism. Prior work has shown that regional differences in mitochondrial density correspond to varying energetic demands across the human brain 2. Resting cerebral metabolic rate of glucose (CMRGlu) is typically quantified using positron emission tomography (PET) with 2-[18F]fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose ([18F]FDG). However, the extent to which neuronal distribution and mitochondrial abundance jointly shape resting CMRGlu remains unclear. Accordingly, we investigate how the spatial organisation of neuronal distribution and mitochondrial density relates to baseline CMRGlu across the human brain.

Methods

To this end, we integrated three independent datasets. CMRGlu was estimated from previously published data 3 from forty-one healthy participants (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03485066, approved by the ethics committee of the Medical University of Vienna). Participants underwent a 52-minute bolus-plus-constant-infusion [18F]FDG PET/MR scan (Siemens mMR Biograph). During the measurement, arterial samples were drawn to obtain the parent input function. PET data were reconstructed into 30-second frames, corrected for scatter and attenuation, head movement, and normalised to MNI space, and used to compute CMRGlu via Patlak plot. 

Regional neuronal compositions were derived from single-nucleus RNA sequencing data4 obtained from forty-one cortical and subcortical regions defined in the Allen Human Reference atlas. For each region, the average number of neuronal and non-neuronal cells per sample was calculated. The relative neuronal proportion (NEUR) was defined as the ratio of neuronal cells to all cells within each region. 

Regional mitochondrial densities (MitoD) were obtained from publicly available post-mortem datasets 2. To harmonise the datasets, average regional values from CMRGlu, NEUR and MitoD were extracted. Associations were assessed using a weighted linear regression model with CMRGlu as the dependent variable, NEUR, MitoD as the covariates, and region size as the weighting factor. Directionality of associations was assessed in post-hoc weighted Spearman’s rank correlation, with CMRGlu was residualised for each variable accordingly. Bonferroni correction was applied to all post-hoc analyses.

Results

The weighted linear regression revealed significant associations between baseline CMRGlu and both relative NEUR (F2,38 = 29.35, p < 0.001) and MitoD (F2,38 = 21.51, p < 0.001). Post hoc weighted Spearman's rank correlation indicated a moderate positive association (rs = 0.44, pcorr = 0.009) between CMRGlu and NEUR (see Figure 1a). Likewise, the correlation between the CMRGlu and MitoD revealed a strong positive link (rs = 0.70, pcorr < 0.001) between the mitochondrial density and the glucose metabolism rate at rest (see Figure 1b). In contrast, there was no association (rs = -0.05, pcorr = 1.00) between the MitoD and NEUR.

Conclusion

Our study shows that both NEUR and MitoD independently contribute to regional variation in resting-state CMRGlu. The spatial dissociation between mitochondria and relative neuronal proportions suggests that diverse mechanisms underlie their respective relationships with CMRGlu. Higher neuronal density likely reflects increased synaptic density and, thus, elevated glucose demand required to sustain resting-state activity/equilibrium, consistent with prior evidence linking synaptic density and glucose metabolic rate 5. In contrast, MitoD indexes oxidative capacity and cellular composition rather than metabolic demand. For example, fast-spiking interneurons and oligodendrocytes display higher mitochondrial enrichment, whereas neurons with lower energetic demands, e.g., corticothalamic projection neurons, contain fewer mitochondria. The strong association between resting-state CMRGlu and mitochondrial distribution suggests that intrinsic energy requirements and metabolic specialisation shape both MitoD and glucose uptake at rest. Together, these results support a direct link between cellular energy requirements and the glucose supply in the human brain.
