Introduction

　Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly prevalent and disabling condition in which rumination—particularly brooding, a maladaptive form of repetitive negative thinking—plays a key role in symptom persistence and relapse.^1 Rumination is strongly linked to dysfunction of the default mode network (DMN), which supports self-referential processing.^2 Although static connectivity studies demonstrate DMN hyperactivity in MDD, emerging work using time-varying functional connectivity (TVFC) indicates that altered transitions between large-scale networks, such as the DMN and central executive network (CEN), may better capture the cognitive inflexibility underlying rumination. However, the dynamic neural mechanisms associated with rumination remain insufficiently understood. 

　Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and pharmacotherapy are the primary treatments for MDD. CBT includes cognitive restructuring and behavioral activation, which may indirectly influence ruminative thought, whereas pharmacotherapy modulates neurotransmitter systems and may alter neural networks through distinct biological pathways.^3 Yet, it is unclear how these treatments differentially modulate dynamic brain networks relevant to rumination.

　To address this gap, we applied a hidden Markov model (HMM) to resting-state fMRI acquired at baseline and after 16 weeks from patients with MDD undergoing CBT or pharmacotherapy, as well as healthy controls. The HMM framework identifies discrete whole-brain states and quantifies their temporal properties, including fractional occupancy, occurrence frequency, and transition probabilities. This approach enables detailed assessment of network flexibility and stability, which are theoretically linked to ruminative processes. Our aim was to clarify the neural dynamics associated with rumination severity and to determine whether CBT and pharmacotherapy exert distinct modulatory effects on DMN–CEN state dynamics in depression.

Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted within a multicenter project investigating neural mechanisms of major depressive disorder (MDD). Participants were adults aged 20–69 years who met DSM-IV criteria for MDD with a GRID-HAMD17 score ≥8. Exclusion criteria included MRI contraindications, neurological disorders, or poor image quality. Healthy controls (HCs) were matched by age, sex, and education. A total of 135 participants (79 MDD, 56 HC) completed baseline and 16-week assessments. All participants provided written informed consent, and institutional ethical approval was obtained.

Treatment conditions

Patients received either cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) or pharmacotherapy. Twenty-eight patients underwent 16 weekly 50-minute CBT sessions delivered by trained therapists using the standard CBT Manual for Depression. Although therapists were familiar with rumination-focused CBT, treatment followed the general CBT protocol. Thirty-two patients received guideline-based pharmacotherapy, including SSRIs, SNRIs, or NaSSAs. Individuals receiving ECT or rTMS were excluded from treatment analyses. To minimize selection bias due to non-random treatment assignment, propensity score matching was performed using baseline demographic and clinical variables. 

Clinical measures

Depressive symptoms were evaluated with the GRID-HAMD17 by trained raters blinded to treatment allocation. Rumination was assessed using the Rumination Response Scale (RRS), yielding total, brooding, and reflection scores. Assessments were conducted at baseline and 16 weeks. Interrater reliability for GRID-HAMD17 exceeded 0.99.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

Resting-state fMRI and T1-weighted structural images were acquired using a 3.0-T GE Discovery MR750 scanner with a 32-channel coil. During the 10-minute resting scan, participants maintained eyes open and fixated. Preprocessing used the fMRIPrep pipeline, including motion correction and spatial normalization. Regional time series were extracted from 116 AAL3 atlas regions. Principal component analysis was applied to reduce dimensionality before hidden Markov model (HMM) estimation.

Hidden Markov model (HMM)

To characterize time-varying functional connectivity (TVFC), we applied an HMM with multivariate Gaussian emissions to concatenated fMRI time series. A 12-state solution was selected based on free-energy comparison and prior studies. HMM estimation used variational Bayesian inference.^4,5 State activation maps were labeled post hoc by correspondence to canonical networks such as the default mode network (DMN), central executive network (CEN), and somatosensory networks (SMN).

Dynamic metrics

For each participant, we quantified: Fractional occupancy (proportion of time in each state), Occurrence frequency (activation rate per TR), Lifetime (stability within a state), Switching rate (overall rate of state transitions), Transition probabilities between states.
These metrics index neural flexibility and stability relevant to rumination.

Statistical analysis

Group differences (MDD vs HC) were examined using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with age and sex as covariates. Associations between HMM metrics and rumination severity were tested using partial correlations. Treatment effects were assessed using GLMMs including treatment group, time, and their interaction, followed by within-group post hoc analyses. Statistical significance for HMM-derived measures was evaluated using nonparametric permutation testing (1,000 iterations) with FDR correction (p < 0.05). Propensity score–matched analyses further validated treatment-specific effects.

Results

A total of 135 participants (79 MDD, 56 healthy controls) were included in the final analysis. Hidden Markov modeling revealed 12 discrete whole-brain states representing major functional networks, including the default mode network (DMN), central executive network (CEN), and somatosensory networks. Clear group differences emerged between MDD patients and healthy controls. 

MDD vs. Healthy Controls

MDD patients exhibited significantly increased fractional occupancy and occurrence frequency in DMN-related states, particularly state 12 (p < 0.001), as well as increased engagement of a left-lateralized DMN state (state 4) and an insular/salience-related state (state 9). Conversely, they showed reduced occupancy and shorter lifetime in CEN-related states such as state 11 (p < 0.001). Somatosensory network states (states 10 and 11) also occurred less frequently in MDD than in controls. (Figure1A)

Rumination Severity and Brain Dynamics

Across all participants, brooding rumination was positively correlated with the occurrence frequency of state 12 (DMN; r = 0.25, p = 0.005). (Figure2B) Higher brooding scores were also associated with reduced transition probabilities from somatosensory states (state 10 → state 4; state 7 → state 6), indicating reduced neural flexibility and impaired ability to shift between externally oriented and self-referential networks. These associations highlight a dynamic neural signature of rumination centered on heightened DMN dominance and restricted cross-network transitions. 

Treatment Effects: CBT vs. Pharmacotherapy

A significant treatment × time interaction emerged for state 12 occurrence frequency (p = 0.001). Post hoc analysis showed that CBT significantly reduced DMN activation frequency (p = 0.047), while pharmacotherapy showed no comparable change. CBT also reduced the overall switching rate (p = 0.008), suggesting increased network stability after therapy. Moreover, CBT altered transition dynamics: transitions from state 12 to CEN/language regions (state 2) increased, whereas transitions from state 12 to posterior DMN (state 5), and from visual attention networks (state 1) to state 12 decreased. These patterns reflect enhanced executive engagement and reduced involuntary drift into DMN states. Pharmacotherapy showed a trend-level decrease in transitions from state 12 to state 5 (p = 0.059) but produced a distinct pattern, shifting DMN activity toward posterior regions rather than reducing overall DMN dominance.(Figure2)

Clinical Outcomes

Both treatments significantly improved depressive symptoms (HAMD, p < 0.001). However, only the CBT group showed significant reductions in total RRS and brooding scores (p = 0.02), consistent with the observed neural changes in DMN dynamics. Pharmacotherapy improved several somatic and sleep-related HAMD items but did not reduce rumination.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that major depressive disorder (MDD) is characterized by distinct alterations in dynamic large-scale brain network activity, particularly involving the default mode network (DMN) and central executive network (CEN). Using a hidden Markov model (HMM) approach, we identified a specific neural signature of brooding rumination marked by increased frequency of a DMN-dominant state and reduced flexibility in transitioning between self-referential and externally oriented networks. These findings underscore the importance of examining time-varying functional connectivity rather than relying solely on static neural measures when investigating the mechanisms of rumination and depressive pathology.

A major contribution of this work is the demonstration that cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and pharmacotherapy modulate dynamic brain networks through distinct pathways. CBT significantly reduced the occurrence of DMN-dominant states, decreased overall switching rates, and enhanced transitions from DMN activity toward CEN-related states. These alterations suggest improved executive control over internally focused thought and greater neural stability—mechanisms that may underpin CBT’s therapeutic impact on ruminative thinking. In contrast, pharmacotherapy primarily shifted DMN activity toward posterior regions without reducing overall DMN dominance, indicating a different pattern of neural reorganization.

The divergence in neural effects paralleled clinical outcomes: both treatments alleviated depressive symptoms, but only CBT reduced brooding rumination. This alignment between symptom change and dynamic network modulation highlights the potential value of TVFC metrics as mechanistic biomarkers for treatment response, particularly for psychological interventions. Pharmacotherapy improved somatic and sleep-related symptoms without corresponding reductions in rumination, further emphasizing that different treatments engage different neurocognitive systems.

Collectively, these findings suggest that maladaptive rumination in MDD is maintained by rigid and persistent DMN activation patterns and reduced cross-network flexibility. CBT appears to counteract these mechanisms by enhancing adaptive transitions toward executive control networks, whereas pharmacotherapy exerts more region-specific, biologically driven effects. By identifying treatment-specific neural signatures, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how distinct therapeutic modalities engage the brain and points toward the future development of personalized interventions based on dynamic neural markers.
