Introduction

Large multi-site resting-state (rs) fMRI datasets now make it possible to characterize functional brain connectivity (FC) with a level of scale, diversity and statistical power that single-site studies cannot achieve. However, integrating rs-fMRI across heterogenous cohorts presents major challenges: 1) large and diverse samples are required to obtain reliable, generalizable connectivity estimates; 2) scanner and acquisition variability introduce substantial noise; 3) data quality differences and motion artifacts can obscure meaningful FC patterns. To support robust and reproducible functional imaging across diverse datasets, we developed the fMRI module within the NiChart software: a functional framework designed to generate harmonized rs-fMRI derived features of the adult lifespan suitable for large-scale comparative analyses.

Methods

7417 rs-fMRI scans from 14 cohorts (age range: 19-95 years, mean age=52.4) were preprocessed with fMRIPrep1 and postprocessed with XCP-D2 to derive denoised, confound-regressed timeseries. Group-level sparsity-regularized non-negative matrix factorization (SR-NMF)3 was used to generate a consensus group atlas which was then used to initialize a second round of SR-NMF applied to subject-level data to ensure the spatial correspondence of the personalized networks across all subjects. This produced 17 personalized functional networks (pFNs) per subject. We assessed the spatial overlap between these data-driven networks with the Yeo atlas. Quality control (QC) required a mean framewise displacement (FD) threshold of 0.3mm, at least 20% of acquired frames falling below the FD criterion and node-wise non-zero coverage. Following QC, 17 network-level connectivity matrices were generated using pairwise Pearson’s correlation of each network’s time course resulting in 136 features (upper triangle). Two harmonization methods were used to remove site variability while preserving biologically meaningful signals: 1) tangent-space ComBat-GAM4, which projects correlation matrices onto Riemannian tangent space to allow batch effect correction and 2) CovBat-GAM5 to adjust the mean/variance and covariance structure of correlation matrices. Efficacy of the harmonization methods was tested using multivariate distance matrix regression (MDMR), Kruskal Wallis and a variance homogeneity test (Levene). 

Results

Following QC, 6649 participants were retained for analysis. Prior to harmonization, functional connectivity features showed strong site effects across all statistical tests, including MDMR and variance homogeneity assessments (Fig 2). Both harmonization methods substantially removed site effects in feature space. CovBat-GAM effectively removed additive site effects and reduced differences in covariance structure while tangent-space ComBatGAM reduced multiplicative site effects. Post-harmonization features showed improved overlap across cohorts in the MDS plots. Age-prediction using nested cross-validation showed preservation of age-related signal following harmonization. Site-specific age trajectories of between network connectivity converged across studies after harmonization, indicating reduced site-driven bias while retaining age trends.

Conclusion

We integrated rs-fMRI data from 14 cohorts spanning multiple scanners and demographic groups and demonstrated the ability to generate site-invariant functional phenotypes suitable for large-scale comparative analyses and machine learning applications. Although both harmonization methods mitigated scanner/site-related variability, the results show the importance of removing site effects from the covariance structure of functional connectivity matrices in addition to mean and variance corrections, as obtained with CovBat-GAM. Therefore, the processed data presented here, harmonized using CovBat-GAM, will be incorporated as a new fMRI module in NiChart, allowing users to upload their own pre- and postprocessed timeseries data to NiChart to obtain harmonized pFNs for use in further analyses.
