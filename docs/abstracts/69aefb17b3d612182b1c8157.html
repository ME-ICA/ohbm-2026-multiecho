Introduction

One of the gold standard treatments for obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is cognitive behavioral therapy with exposure and response prevention (CBT-ERP). The goal of CBT-ERP is to address automatic negative evaluation of intrusions and break the cycle of avoidance and reinforcement by letting individuals with OCD confront items or situations that give them anxiety without performing their alleviating compulsive behaviors. Although CBT-ERP is an effective treatment, up to 50% of individuals do not show sufficient benefit. This has prompted new treatments to have been developed such as Inference Based Cognitive behavioral therapy (I-CBT). I-CBT targets the dysfunctional reasoning and mistrust of the senses that individuals with OCD experience but crucially does not contain an exposure component. Using a randomized controlled non-inferiority trial in N=197 individuals with OCD, the arrIBA study (Alternative treatment to Reduce chronicity in OCD: Research Into Brain response and Adequacy of treatment; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03929081) showed that both CBT-ERP and I-CBT significantly reduced OCD symptom severity, improved insight, reduced comorbid depression and anxiety up to one year after treatment and there were no significant differences between the groups in clinical improvement, response, remission or drop-out rate.1 While multiple previous studies have investigated the neural correlates of CBT-ERP, no neuroimaging study has yet investigated the mechanisms of action of I-CBT. In the current study we used thata from the arrIBA trial to compare the effects of these two forms of psychotherapy on emotional processing during fMRI to better understand their working mechanisms.

Methods

before and after 20 weekly sessions of psychotherapy, individuals with OCD (N=55) underwent an emotional processing task during functional MRI while general fear and OCD related visual stimuli were presented. Scrambled pictures were used as a neutral baseline condition. In addition, 42 healthy controls underwent the same task once. Using Bayesian region-of-interest (ROI) analyses of prefrontal, limbic, sensory, subcortical and visual brain areas, we assessed (differences in) pre-to-post treatment changes in task-related brain activation and associations with symptom improvement. Furthermore, we associated baseline task-related activation with treatment-induced symptom change.  The study was approved by the ethics committee of VU Medical Center. Study procedures were in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

after treatment, the CBT-ERP group (N=28) showed strong credible evidence for decreased activation across all ROIs during the fear but not OCD (versus neutral) contrast. This reduction was most pronounced in treatment responders. Conversely, the I-CBT group (N=27) showed credible evidence for increased activation during the fear versus neutral contrast in the precentral gyrus and lateral occipital cortex (LOC) that was related to symptom improvement. A similar but less pronounced pattern was seen in the OCD contrast. Furthermore, higher and lower baseline fear (and to a lesser extent OCD)-related activity was associated with symptom improvement in the CBT-ERP and I-CBT group, respectively. Lower baseline activation of the LOC during processing of OCD-related stimuli was associated with symptom improvement across both treatments.

Conclusion

These finding provide support for the proposed working mechanisms of CBT-ERP via fear reduction and I-CBT via sensory engagement. Task-related activation of visual brain areas might be a potential treatment predictor across psychotherapies.
