Introduction

Given the significance of neuroimaging in radiology, biomedical sciences, and neuroscience, there has been an exponential growth of available data. Many large-scale studies such as HCP, ABCD, ADNI, and UK Biobank include diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI). These studies are increasing the size of dMRI datasets from the dozens to the hundreds and thousands. This large-scale adoption poses great promise to study the microstructure and connectivity of the brain at the population-level.

The analysis of such data, including normative and predictive modeling, requires tailored processing workflows to maximize their sensitivity. First, advanced preprocessing pipelines must be applied to improve the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of dMRI-derived biomarkers. Second, data harmonization tools provide solutions to further mitigate the impact of study- or site-specific effects. Despite such best practices, the SNR of structural connectivity data is low, largely due to the probabilistic nature of streamline tracking and the low connectivity strength in certain networks. Here, we propose an innovative method to denoise population-level data for more sensitive brain connectivity analysis by leveraging redundancies across subjects within large cohorts. Our approach aims to improve the SNR of connectivity data and downstream analyses, while also transforming the count data such that it is Gaussian-distributed and thus amenable to common modeling and harmonization approaches.

The denoising of redundant subject-level MRI data series using principal component analysis (PCA) has gained popularity in recent years. The identification of the Marchenko-Pastur (MP) distribution in the eigenspectrum of the data permits the objective differentiation between signal and noise, thereby improving the SNR1. In this work, we extend the MP-PCA approach2 to the denoising of structural connectivity matrices in large-scale cohort studies to improve the sensitivity of brain network analyses.

Methods

Noise distribution - We established the noise distribution of streamline counts using five sessions from a single subject from the MICRA dataset. We generated a set of 2500 bootstrapped diffusion images by randomly sampling with replacement from the set of five sessions. Anatomically-constrained tractography (ACT) was performed using the MRtrix3 toolbox. We evaluated three distributions (Poisson, negative binomial, and Gaussian) with quantile-quantile (QQ) plots and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). 

Denoising approach - To denoise connectivity data, we first created a matrix of streamline counts combined across the population of subjects. We adopted a two-step pipeline for denoising the count data3. For specific noise distributions, e.g. negative binomial, a custom rescaling (biwhitening) of the rows and columns of the matrix was performed to make the noise homoscedastic. Such scaling was accomplished through determining row and column normalization factors estimated through the Sinkhorn-Knopp matrix scaling algorithm. Thereafter, the MP distribution was accurately identified in the eigenspectrum of the scaled matrix and nullified. 

Sensitivity analysis - We compared the sensitivity to age of both streamline counts and network properties from raw, spherical-deconvolution informed filtering of tractograms (SIFT), and denoised streamline counts using data from the HCP-aging cohort (n=506, aged 36 to 100). Using a simple linear model of the form: y = β0 + β1age with y being the varying count types, we calculated the R2 at each of 142 cortical and subcortical regions to quantify the sensitivity of cortical streamline counts to age-related changes. From each individual connectome of raw, SIFT, and denoised counts, we derived graph-theoretical measures of degree, clustering coefficient, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality. To capture the main axes of network variation, for each of the 142 cortical and subcortical regions we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) using the four network features from each count type. Then for each region and principal component, we built the same linear model of the form: y = β0 + β1age with y being the varying principal components and again calculated the R2. We then took the mean R2 across all four principal components to summarize the sensitivity to age-related connectome changes derived from raw, SIFT, and denoised count types. An ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc was used to assess if the distribution of R2 values was significantly different between the varying count types.

Results

Noise distribution - Figure 1A shows the distribution of raw streamline counts derived from the bootstrapped dataset between regions with low and high connectivity. The empirical distribution of streamline counts is best described by a negative binomial distribution, confirmed with a lower AIC and visually with QQ plots.

Denoising approach - Figure 1B depicts the matrix generation and eigenspectrum of the streamline counts from the HCP-aging data. The eigenspectrum of the biwhitened counts closely follows the theoretical MP distribution, suggesting that the streamline count distribution can be effectively denoised. 

Sensitivity analysis - The R2 at each cortical and subcortical region of the raw, SIFT, and denoised counts with age is shown in figure 2A. The denoised counts have a significant increase in R2 as compared to raw (p<0.017) or SIFT counts (p<0.017). Figure 2B shows the mean R2 across the 4 principal components derived using varying graph-theoretical measures. The graph-theory measures derived using the denoised counts have a significantly higher R2 as compared to measures derived using the raw (p<0.017) or SIFT (p<0.017) counts. Taken together, these results suggest that the denoising approach improves sensitivity to age-related connectome changes.

Conclusion

Random matrix theory has been widely adopted for the denoising of MRI series2. However, its use in other applications was challenged by variance in the underlying noise distribution. After establishing the actual distribution, we propose a solution that extends the use of random matrix theory for denoising of connectivity matrices. Unlike previous approaches, we explore redundancy across the population instead of within a single subject. Population-level approaches are increasingly impactful given the interest in normative and predictive modeling4. We observe both an improved reproducibility across sessions and scanners, and an increased precision to aging trajectories after denoising. Furthermore, our approach transforms the count data such that it is Gaussian-distributed, allowing for the application of common modeling tools which often assume this to be the underlying distribution of the data. 

Challenges regarding spurious connections will not be mitigated using these approaches, given that such spurious connections are oftentimes robust and systematic5. Dependency on experimental variables such as cortical parcellation and compatibility with streamline filtering approaches is ongoing. We observe that the efficacy of denoising improves in sub-matrices with sufficiently dense sampling of connections. Therefore, a large number of streamlines is favorable.

Overall, we provide a strategy to denoise connectivity matrices across subjects to improve network analyses in large-scale cohort studies.
