Introduction

Neuromodulation manipulates neuronal activity in targeted brain areas through invasive or non-invasive techniques, e.g. deep brain stimulation (DBS), transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), transcranial focused/unfocused ultrasound (tFUS/tUS) or transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS). These approaches have shown efficacy across a variety of conditions such as depression, anxiety, chronic pain, obsessive-compulsive disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Treatments aim to enhance or disrupt brain activation/connectivity to reduce symptoms or boost recovery. The amygdala is a popular target, given its critical role in the generation and experience of emotions central to depression and PTSD1. However, invasive techniques like DBS require surgery, TMS/tDCS are limited to shallow cortical regions, and real-time neurofeedback requires the patient to be in the scanner during the neuromodulation. tFUS/tUS is a promising noninvasive technique that can reach deep structures, but it requires expensive equipment and does involve patient discomfort.

This work builds upon our previously created tool NeuroGen that uses an AI image generator linked to an encoding model that predicts resulting brain responses to these images3. A user provides a target map of desired brain activity and the tool creates an image to induce that pattern. Previous work showed that the NeuroGen’s AI generated images achieved desired patterns of high activity in face, body and word areas4. Here, we update NeuroGen by combining a state of the art diffusion-based AI image generator and a state of the art brain encoding model into a new tool called NeuroVolve that can functionally target and manipulate brain regions’ activity via visual imagery. In theory it will work for any region in the brain that responds to visual stimuli, and here we focus on the amygdala. We validate our method in silico in the Natural Scenes Dataset2, as well as in in vivo, prospective human fMRI experiments.

Methods

The NSD dataset, which consists of several thousands of images and observed brain responses for 8 individuals, was used to create a population-level encoding model for the amygdala. This encoding model goes from image to BLIP Q-Former latent space to predicted brain responses. Average prediction accuracy is quite good for a non-visual brain region with relatively lower fMRI SNR, r = 0.212, which is also higher than test-retest correlations of brain activity in response to the same repeated images.

  

Figure 1 illustrates the NeuroVolve workflow. Given a specified neural objective (e.g., maximizing fusiform face area activity), NeuroVolve performs iterative optimization to find the Q-former embedding that satisfies the objective. The embedding at each optimization step is used to condition the BLIP-Diffusion5 generator, producing a sequence of 500 images over 500 optimization steps. The final image is fed back into the image-to-brain encoding model, and optimal stimuli are identified based on the resulting optimization curve.

We used the procedure above with two neural objectives: maximizing amygdala activity and minimizing amygdala activity. Both neural objective optimizations started with natural images from the NSD dataset that had average predicted amygdala activity (=0). We also selected natural images from the NSD dataset (the shared 1000 images) that had the highest/lowest predicted activity in the amygdala. In total, we had 5 conditions: natural high, natural low, natural zero, synthetic high (from optimizing the natural zero), and synthetic low (from optimizing the natural zero). 

We collected prospective data from six individuals (3 female, age 20–50) using a GE 3T MRI scanner, including anatomy and whole-brain fMRI. fMRI data consisted of 60 axial slices, 2.03 x 2.03 x 2.4 mm voxels, TR/TE=1950/32ms, multiband 2, with P>>A phase encoding. Subjects viewed a series of 8.4° square images, each presented for 3 seconds (2s on, 1s off), organized into blocks of 14 images within a condition. Subjects fixated on a central dot, and performed a 1-back task. A single 254 second scan consisted of 5 42 second stimulus blocks with 6 seconds of rest between blocks. Each session consisted of 10 image viewing fMRI scans. Data from each scan were preprocessed and coregistered to anatomy, and the response to each individual image was estimated using the GLMsingle package. Voxelwise GLM beta coefficients were averaged across voxels within a given region of interest, and then z-scored across all images within each subject.

Results

Figure 2a shows 5 example images from the 5 categories, including natural (real) images that maximized amygdala (top row) and minimized amygdala (second row).  The natural zero images that induce average amygdala activity (row 3) were used to initialize the optimization to generate synthetic images that maximize (fourth row) and minimize (fifth row) amygdala activity. Figure 2b shows the brain responses in amygdala for each of the images averaged across the 6 participants, for each of the 5 conditions. An ANOVA found that the natural high and synthetic high images had significantly higher responses than the natural low, natural zero and synthetic low (corrected p≪0.01). The synthetic low and natural low had significantly lower activity than the natural zero activity (corrected p≪0.01). Figure 2c shows the t-statistic for differences in regional group-averaged cortical brain responses between pairs of conditions, thresholded to show p<0.01 (corrected). Synthetic high-low shows greater separation than Natural high-low in face and place-specialized regions. 

Conclusion

Here we introduce and validate a tool called NeuroVolve that can generate images designed to achieve user-provided, targeted patterns of brain activity. We showcase its ability to generate images designed to maximally activate and suppress even regions that are not thought of as primarily visual regions, namely the amygdala, and validate that these images elicit desired brain activity patterns in prospective fMRI experiments. While overall amygdala activation levels were not different for optimized natural and synthetic images, there was more separation in activity (high vs low) for synthetic versus natural images in certain face and place regions. These results provide the first evidence that carefully designed AI generated images can modulate amygdala activity and take the field one step closer to a novel neuromodulatory technique.
