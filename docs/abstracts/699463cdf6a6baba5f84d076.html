Introduction

Theory of mind (ToM) is fundamental to social cognition, enabling the understanding of others’ mental states. Extensive research has mapped ToM to the Default Network (DN), particularly the dorsomedial subsystem (DN-B), alongside the Limbic network (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2014). Recently, emerging evidence in non-human animals suggests that social behaviors extend beyond the cortex, involving dynamic interactions across distributed thalamocortical circuits (e.g., Chen et al., 2024). However, the specific contribution of the human thalamus to higher-order social cognition (e.g., ToM) remains largely unknown. Specifically, it is unclear whether distinct thalamic nuclei, such as the mediodorsal (MD) and anterior (ANT) nuclei, differentially contribute to ToM via distinct spatiotemporal dynamics and functional integration patterns. To this end, the present study leverages intracranial EEG (iEEG) to dissect the spatiotemporal and oscillatory dynamics of these thalamocortical loops during naturalistic ToM.

Methods

We recorded stereotactic EEG (sEEG) data from 8 patients with epilepsy while they watched the 5.6-minute animated film 'Partly Cloudy' (Figure 1A). As a control condition, participants also viewed a temporally scrambled version of the same movie (shuffled every 1s) to disrupt narrative coherence. To overcome the inherent spatial sparsity of clinical sEEG coverage, we aggregated data across the cohort, pooling 264 active sites into a common stereotactic space. These sites covered the DN (119 sites: DN-A=36, DN-B=75, DN-C=8), Limbic network (58 sites), and Thalamus (87 sites: MD=4, Anterior=22, Ventral=25, Pulvinar=36) (Figure 1B). ToM events were defined based on time-locked annotations from Richardson et al. (2018), which utilized reverse correlation analysis on fMRI data to identify consensus ToM processing. Data was preprocessed with bipolar re-referencing and notch filtering (60 Hz, 120 Hz, and 180 Hz). We extracted high-frequency activity (HFA, 70-170 Hz) as a proxy for local population firing. To increase the signal-to-noise ratio and capture slow, narrative-driven neural dynamics, the HFA envelope was smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of 1s. For functional connectivity (FC), the smoothed HFA was downsampled to 10 Hz. We calculated Pearson’s r across all electrode pairs, normalized via Fisher’s Z-transform. ToM-specific FC was derived by concatenating the seven ToM events (68s), while non-ToM FC was estimated using a length-matched bootstrapping strategy (described below). Cross-region cross-frequency phase-amplitude coupling (PAC) was assessed using the modulation index (Tort et al., 2010), with unfiltered, unsmoothed data downsampled to 500 Hz to preserve low-frequency phase and high-frequency activity. To control for 1/f noise, we employed a ratio-difference metric contrasting PAC values during ToM events against the non-ToM baseline. To establish a robust baseline for both FC and PAC analyses, we employed a length-matched bootstrapping strategy. Specifically, all non-ToM epochs were concatenated to form a control pool, from which we randomly sampled continuous segments matching the exact total duration of the ToM events. This sampling process was repeated 100 times, computing FC and PAC for each iteration before averaging these metrics to generate the baseline. Two-tailed non-parametric statistics were used throughout the analyses (5000 iterations for permutation tests). To control multiple comparisons, FDR correction was applied for HFA and FC analysis, and cluster-based correction was used for PAC analysis.

Results

HFA analysis revealed significantly stronger activity in DN-A, DN-B, and Limbic networks during ToM events compared to non-ToM baseline (Figure 1C). Notably, the thalamus itself did not show significant HFA increases, suggesting its role may be regulatory rather than involving local computation of ToM content. This activation pattern was absent in the scrambled control condition, where we analyzed the concatenated frames corresponding to the original ToM events (Figure 1D). During ToM periods, FC analysis (Figure 1E) revealed universally enhanced within-network connectivity across MD, ANT, Limbic, and DN-B networks, indicating heightened internal processing coherence. Crucially, inter-regional connectivity exhibited a potential functional dissociation: the MD nucleus exhibited strengthened connectivity with the DN (A/B/C) but reduced connectivity with the Limbic network. This suggests that the MD may facilitate internal mentalizing within the DN by functionally isolating it from immediate affective inputs. In contrast, the ANT showed increased coupling with both DN-B and Limbic networks, potentially routing affective context to the DN. Additionally, the Limbic network displayed stronger FC with DN-B, reflecting active cortico-cortical integration of cognitive and emotional information. Crucially, when compared to non-ToM events, PAC analysis revealed a robust mechanism for fast oscillatory control during ToM events (Figure 2). Significant unidirectional theta/alpha-gamma coupling was observed from the thalamus (both MD and ANT) to cortical networks (Limbic and DN-B), suggesting that the thalamus acts as a temporal pacemaker gating cortical processing windows. No significant bottom-up PAC (cortex-to-thalamus) was found. Meanwhile, the Limbic and DN-B networks exhibited bidirectional PAC enhancement, indicating active cortico-cortical information exchange. Collectively, these findings highlight a hierarchical architecture where the thalamus provides the global timing structure necessary for coherent cortical social computation.

Conclusion

Our findings provide direct electrophysiological evidence for thalamic orchestration of the social brain. We demonstrate a functional dissociation where the MD nucleus may facilitate internal mentalizing through network segregation, while the ANT nucleus may support the synthesis of cognitive and affective information through network integration. Importantly, the absence of local thalamic activation (i.e., HFA), combined with robust top-down PAC effects, suggests that the thalamus does not compute ToM content itself, but rather coordinates distinct cortical processing modes via global oscillatory control. This highlights a hierarchical cortical-subcortical architecture supporting human social cognition.
