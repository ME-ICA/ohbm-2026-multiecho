Introduction

Brain cells generate waste that must be efficiently cleared to preserve neural homeostasis. Recent evidence have suggested that the glymphatic system uses fluid channels known as perivascular spaces (PVS), for waste-clearance.3 PVS facilitates the flow of cerebrospinal fluid that clears metabolic waste and potentially pathogenic proteins from the brain parenchyma.1,3 When this system becomes impaired, harmful proteins accumulate, contributing to the development of motor-neuron neurodegenerative diseases, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).4

ALS is an aggressive disease that selectively targets motor neurons. As these neurons deteriorate, patients experience profound functional motor decline. The disease advances rapidly, with most patients surviving less than five years from symptom onset. Evidence suggests that a dysfunctional glymphatic system may fail to clear away misfolded proteins, a hallmark of ALS, which in turn, could exacerbate neuronal stress and further drive motor neuron degeneration.4 Understanding how glymphatic impairment contributes to ALS onset and progression in a multi-centre cohort is therefore critical.  

A minimally invasive technique to address this uses magnetic resonance (MR) diffusion tensor imaging – along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS). It leverages information from the predictable orientation of fibers near the brain ventricles, where the PVS runs perpendicular to the surrounding white matter fibre tracts. By comparing estimates of water movement along the PVS with those along the adjacent tracts, DTI-ALPS yields a quantitative index of glymphatic activity.5 The clinical utility of DTI-ALPS as a biomarker, however, requires good reproducibility: The measures must perform consistently across repeated acquisitions as well as across scanners/sites, if it is to serve as reliable metric in clinical or multicentre trials. Here we assess the test-retest repeatability and inter-scanner/site variability of the DTI-ALPS index. 

Methods

Study Design: The Canadian ALS Neuroimaging Consortium (CALSNIC) provides a unique dataset to address this query.2 Specifically, the CALSNIC traveling-heads cohort was drawn from two studies, CALSNIC-1 and CALSNIC-2 where 17 healthy adults (10 males, 7 females; mean age ≈ 40 years) travelled to multiple sites over the course of ~1 to 2.5 years. CALSNIC-1 included five sites (Calgary, Alberta; CAL; Edmonton, Alberta; EDM; London, Ontario; LON; Montreal, Quebec; QUE; and Toronto, Ontario; TOR) and CALSNIC-2 included two additional sites, Miami, Florida (MIA) and Quebec, Quebec (QUE). At each site, participants underwent two diffusion MR (dMR) acquisitions in a single visit (denoted as Scan A and Scan B) less than 1 hour apart, yielding 88 scans in total.

MR Acquisition: All imaging was acquired on 3-T clinical scanners (General Electric, Siemens, or Philips), with each site following the CALSNIC harmonized diffusion protocol.2 Protocols were matched as closely as possible across scanners with respect to dMR voxel size (2 mm isotropic), field of view, and echo time. CALSNIC-1 and CALSNIC-2 included multiple b = 0 s/mm² images and 30 diffusion-encoding directions at b = 1000 s/mm2. In CALSNIC-2, all sites additionally acquired 30 directions at b = 2000 s/mm2.2 

Pre-processing and Tensor Estimation: Raw diffusion volumes were denoised using Marchenko–Pastur principal component analysis to reduce thermal noise in low-signal regions (MRtrix denoised). Gibbs ringing was then suppressed (MRtrix mrdegibbs). Eddy-current and motion-induced distortions were corrected (FSL eddy). Brain extraction was performed (FSL bet) to avoid inclusion of skull or dura. Post-processing QC involved a visual review of corrected images. 

Diffusion tensors were fit voxel-wise to the pre-processed dMR images (MRtrix) and maps of fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), and axial (AD) and radial (RD) diffusivities reconstructed. For DTI-ALPS analysis, we used the three principal diffusivities aligned with image field-of-view axes (Dxx, Dyy, Dzz). 

Regions-of-interest Placement: Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined in projection and association tracts adjacent to the body of the lateral ventricles. Pre-defined ROI masks (JHU atlas, JHU-ICBM-FA-2mm.nii.gz) were used to capture (1) projection tracts running predominantly superior–inferior and (2) association tracts running predominantly anterior–posterior (Figure 1). Each FA map was linearly and then non-linearly registered to the masks; the inverse of this transform was then applied to the template ROIs to bring them into native diffusion space (FLIRT, FNIRT, applywarp). Each ROI had a diameter of approximately 5 mm and were placed in both the left and right hemisphere for bilateral analysis. 

Computation of the DTI-ALPS Index: For each scan, we extracted mean Dxx, Dyy, and Dzz within the ROIs (Figure 1). In our analysis, Dxx reflects diffusivity along the left–right (perivascular) axis, whereas Dyy and Dzz reflect diffusivity along axes aligned with association and projection tracts, respectively. The DTI-ALPS index was computed as = (𝐷𝑥,projection + 𝐷𝑥,association)/(𝐷𝑦,projection + 𝐷𝑧,association)

Higher index values indicate relatively greater diffusivity along perivascular spaces compared to the average diffusivity in the orthogonal fibre directions. Lower values suggest reduced perivascular water movement and potentially impaired clearance. A global DTI-ALPS index was calculated by averaging across hemispheres. 

Statistical Analysis: To quantify intra-scanner test–retest reliability, we compared Scan A and Scan B from each site using Bland–Altman analysis, estimating mean bias and limits-of-agreement (95% LOA; defined as bias ±1.96 × SD of the differences) and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for the DTI-ALPS index. Inter-site variability was assessed by comparing subject-level mean DTI-ALPS values across sites using Bland-Altman plots and ICCs to evaluate systematic offsets and agreement.

Results

The overall mean bilateral DTI-ALPS was 1.75, with a median of 1.68 and a range from 1.03 to 2.57 (Figure 2). Across all sites, the overall distribution of DTI-ALPS values agreed with the range found in the literature for healthy adults.1

Across the 7 sites, the test–retest biases between scans A and B were small. Absolute biases ranged from 0.0009 to 0.0648 DTI-ALPS units. When expressed relative to the mean DTI-ALPS at each site, these corresponded to ≤4% of the site-specific mean value, indicating no systematic shift between repeat acquisitions on the same scanner. The 95% LOA for A vs B were -0.460 to 0.335 (CAL), -0.113 to 0.115 (EDM), -0.217 to 0.273 (LON), -0.576 to 0.446 (MIA), -0.311 to 0.323 (MON), -0.480 to 0.445 (QUE), and -0.216 to 0.264 (TOR) DTI-ALPS units (Figure 2). These intervals correspond to expected absolute scan-to-scan differences (half the LOA width) of 0.398, 0.114, 0.245, 0.511, 0.317, 0.463, and 0.240 DTI-ALPS units at CAL, EDM, LON, MIA, MON, QUE, and TOR, respectively, which represent 6.5 to 29.2% of the mean DTI-ALPS value. In practical terms, if a subject were scanned twice at the same site, their DTI-ALPS values would usually be expected to lie within ~0.3 units of each other (roughly 20% of the mean value at most sites), which is an encouraging finding for longitudinal applications and for using DTI-ALPS as a biomarker in clinical trials. Site-specific ICC (2,1) values for A/B scans were 0.79 (CAL), 0.98 (EDM), 0.89 (LON), 0.83 (MIA), 0.83 (MTL), 0.84 (QUE), and 0.87 (TOR) (all p ≤ 0.002). 

Across all 21 site pairs, Bland–Altman biases ranged from -0.29 to +0.44 DTI-ALPS units. For example, in the site pair of CAL-EDM, DTI-ALPS values were higher at EDM (bias -0.26), with 95% LOA from -0.64 to +0.11. In contrast, comparisons between CAL and LON, MIA, MON, QUE, or TOR showed smaller absolute biases (-0.13 to +0.13), indicating closer inter-site agreement. Site pairs involving EDM and QUE tended to display larger biases and wider 95% LOA; for instance, EDM vs QUE had a bias of +0.44, with 95% LOA from -0.19 to +1.07, indicating that for some subjects the difference in DTI-ALPS between these two centres exceeded 1.0 unit (Figure 2). Other site pairs, such as LON vs TOR and MIA vs TOR, exhibited smaller biases and narrower 95% LOA (-0.35 to +0.40 DTI-ALPS units). Across all pairs, the magnitude of inter-site disagreement (the average distance between the bias and each LOA) was 0.37 DTI-ALPS units, compared with 0.25 units intra-site, confirming that site-to-site differences are larger than test–retest variability on a given scanner. 

Inter-site reliability quantified with ICC (2,1), using the A/B-scan averaged data, was lower than intra-site reliability. ICC values ranged from 0.34 (EDM-QUE) to 0.91 (MIA-TOR), with a mean of 0.70 across all 21 site pairs. Ten pairs showed good to excellent agreement (ICC ≥ 0.75), eight showed moderate agreement (0.50–0.75), and three pairs, predominantly involving EDM and QUE, showed poor agreement (ICC < 0.50), consistent with the larger biases and wider 95% LOA observed in the Bland–Altman analyses. 

Conclusion

These results carry several important implications for the use of DTI-ALPS as a biomarker. The low intra-site bias and relatively tight 95% LOA indicate that this metric is internally consistent at the scanner level. Intra-scanner biases were <4% of the mean DTI-ALPS value, and 95% LOA for most sites fell within ±0.35 units, indicating that repeated acquisitions under the same conditions are reproducible. Given a mean DTI-ALPS of approximately 1.75, even the largest intra-scanner bias (4%) corresponds to an error of 0.07 units, so systematic differences between repeat scans on the same scanner were small relative to the overall ALPS range. 

Intra-site ICC (2,1) values ranged from 0.79 to 0.98 across sites (mean ICC across sites 0.86), indicating that most of the variance in DTI-ALPS within a given site reflects true differences between subjects rather than measurement noise. The high test–retest agreement supports the use of DTI-ALPS for tracking disease progression within a given centre, for monitoring individuals over time, and for repeated-measures or within-subject designs in clinical studies. From this, we can infer that a longitudinal change that clearly exceeds the usual site-specific variation is unlikely to be driven by measurement noise alone and therefore is more likely to represent a true biological shift. 

The larger inter-site biases and wider limits of agreement highlight the substantial impact of site and scanner vendor/model on DTI-ALPS. Across 21 site pairs, inter-site biases ranged from -0.29 to +0.44 DTI-ALPS units, corresponding to 25% of 1.75, the cohort mean DTI-ALPS value. The typical magnitude of disagreement between sites was 0.37 units, compared an intra-site disagreement of 0.25 units. In our data, some centres systematically yielded lower DTI-ALPS values (e.g., EDM), whereas others tended to yield higher values (e.g., QUE), and several EDM-QUE and EDM-MON comparisons showed both large biases and low inter-site ICCs. Inter-site ICC (2,1) values (0.34 to 0.91, mean 0.70) further confirm that agreement between scanners is only moderate on average, and clearly lower than intra-site reliability. This pattern suggests that each site has its own characteristic offset or scaling compared with the group average, likely due to hardware, acquisition protocol differences, or local calibration and shimming practices. 

In a multi-centre study, these site effects are of significance. If raw DTI-ALPS values were pooled without adjustment, part of any observed group difference may simply reflect site where participants were scanned rather than differences in underlying biology or disease. For example, an ALS cohort scanned mainly at a lower-than-average DTI-ALPS site could appear worse than controls scanned mainly at a higher-than-average site, even in the absence of differences in glymphatic flow. Because of this, our results argue for building site and scanner effects directly into both the analysis and the study design. On the analysis side, that might mean modelling site as a fixed or random effect or applying harmonization tools like ComBat. On the design side, it reinforces the value in acquiring travelling-heads or phantom data and for conducting regular cross-site calibration experiments.  

These analyses lay the groundwork for the interpretation of DTI-ALPS as a biomarker of change in glymphatic function. They support the idea that the index is technically robust within scanners and is well-suited for longitudinal tracking and individual-level inferences at a single site. At the same time, they underline the need for careful harmonization and statistical adjustment when combining data across centres. By establishing this reliability profile, we can interpret future ALS vs control or subtype analyses with more confidence that observed differences are indicative of a glymphatic or microstructural change, rather than where or on which scanner the data was acquired.
