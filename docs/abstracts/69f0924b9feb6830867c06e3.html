Introduction

Declarative memory enables humans to consciously recall the past, which has been shown to depend on the integrity of the hippocampus. However, a longstanding debate concerns whether the hippocampus is uniquely important for episodic memory (re-experiencing past events in their original spatiotemporal context) or also supports semantic memory (abstract facts and conceptual knowledge)1. Traditional models suggest episodic memory relies on the hippocampus while semantic memory depends on adjacent cortical structures, but alternative views propose that the hippocampus might support both memory types.

Contemporary theories reconcile this debate by proposing that cognitive maps may constitute a domain-general mechanism for organizing relational knowledge. Supporting this view, extensive research revealed systematic organizational principles along the hippocampal-long axis for encoding both spatial and episodic memory: the anterior hippocampus encodes coarse information while the posterior hippocampus represents fine details2. This evidence indicates that gradient organization may constitute a fundamental architectural feature for the representation of all knowledge types, including conceptual knowledge.

In light of this background, here we investigated the latent architecture of semantic knowledge representation in the human hippocampus using a data-driven approach. For that purpose, we designed a dense-sampling experiment with ultra-high-field 7T fMRI scanning across multiple sessions during a continuous object recognition task. Using the THINGS image dataset3, we achieved broad coverage of the conceptual space to assess whether semantic memory also exhibits a gradient-based organization in the hippocampus. Collectively, our findings may bridge cognitive map theory, hippocampal neuroanatomy, and semantic memory, revealing how organizational principles of the human hippocampus governing spatial navigation may further extend to encode abstract conceptual knowledge.

Methods

In a dense-sampling experiment, we acquired ultra-high-field 7T fMRI data from a group of 20 participants (24.56 ± 2.42 years, 14F/6M) across five consecutive sessions. Each session incorporated 12 runs of a continuous object recognition task (Old/New), during which participants were presented with high-quality object-centric images from the THINGS image database. Following the application of HCP pipelines for preprocessing, and the use of GLMsingle to estimate trial-specific neural responses, we subsequently constructed a hippocampal voxel-by-concept representation matrix by averaging single-trial beta estimates across repetitions and across 1,854 concepts.

With the aim of characterizing the architecture of conceptual knowledge representation in the hippocampus, we then employed Principal Component Analysis (PCA), which yielded a primary gradient, defined by voxel-wise principal component scores and concept loadings. While the voxel-wise principal component scores delineate the topographical organization (visualized on the unfolded hippocampal surface)4, the concept loadings quantify how specific concepts are compressed onto this gradient. 

Subsequently, we utilized Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression to determine if the identified principal gradient is grounded in the intrinsic organization of the hippocampus. In particular, we sought to predict voxel-wise principal component scores from a comprehensive set of multimodal features of the hippocampus (e.g., microstructural profiles and functional connectivity gradients). The predictive validity of these features was assessed using five-fold cross-validation with a spatial split, where the model was trained on 80% of voxels and tested on the remaining 20% in each fold.

In a final step, we used a ridge regression-based encoding model to link observed compression patterns to interpretable semantic dimensions, thereby characterizing the semantic content governed by the principal gradient. Utilizing 66 semantic features derived from an independent dataset of human behavioral similarity judgments, we modeled the relationship between conceptual features and the identified gradient’s concept loadings. Model performance was evaluated via five-fold cross-validation across concepts (training on 80% of concepts, testing on 20%). By analyzing the model weights and the response of each concept (calculated as the cosine similarity between the concept’s weight vector and the feature dimensions), we explicitly characterized the specific semantic information encoded along the principal gradient of hippocampal knowledge representation.

Results

Latent organization of conceptual knowledge in the human hippocampus

The application of PCA on the voxel-by-concept representation matrix revealed a principal gradient of semantic memory embedded within the hippocampus. When visualized on the unfolded hippocampal surface, the voxel-wise principal component scores delineated a continuous topographical gradient extending from the anterior to the posterior hippocampus along its longitudinal axis (Fig. 2a). Further analysis revealed that this spatial organization is deeply rooted within the intrinsic architecture of the human hippocampus, including microstructural profiles and functional connectivity gradients, robustly predicting the spatial topography of the identified semantic gradient (R2 = 0.91, permutation test, p < 0.05; Fig. 2b). Furthermore, bootstrap-based feature importance analysis demonstrated that this gradient is not driven by a single factor, but it is rather constrained by a synergistic combination of multiple features, confirming its intrinsic validity (Fig. 2c).

Coarse to fine-grained semantic encoding along the hippocampal long axis

In order to characterize the semantic content of the identified principal gradient, we constructed an encoding model to predict component scores from 66 interpretable conceptual features governing similarity amongst THINGS objects. This model achieved a significant prediction accuracy of 0.21 (permutation test, p < 0.05). Inspection of the model weights revealed a functional organization mirroring longitudinal gradients previously observed in the encoding of both spatial and episodic memory: the anterior hippocampus was dominated by coarse, global, and schematic features such as large spaces with beams or valuable objects, whereas the posterior hippocampus was characterized by fine-grained, perceptual object features such as tools or plants (Fig. 2d). Projecting individual concepts onto the principal gradient space further illustrated this continuum through specific image exemplars (Fig. 2e). Overall, the principal gradient tracked a clear semantic shift from the representation of large-scale, complex structures such as carousels and ships (global schemas) to small, manipulable tools like scissors and peelers (local details).

Conclusion

Using a dense sampling approach at ultra–high-field 7T fMRI and a large, naturalistic image dataset, here we identified a robust gradient architecture along the hippocampal-long axis for the organization of conceptual knowledge in humans. This principal gradient encodes representational granularity along the hippocampal long axis: anterior segments represent coarse, contextual, and schematic semantic structure, whereas posterior segments preferentially represent fine-grained, perceptually rich local features. Importantly, this organization emerges during the processing of highly naturalistic, semantically diverse visual input using a data-driven analytic approach, rather than dichotomic manipulations employed in most experimental settings, indicating that the observed gradient reflects an intrinsic principle of long-term semantic memory organization.

Moreover, by linking the identified gradient’s spatial topology to multimodal intrinsic features, we demonstrate that this semantic gradient is deeply rooted in the microstructural profile and large-scale connectivity architecture of the human hippocampus. These findings integrate the well-established long-axis specialization observed in spatial navigation and episodic memory together with semantic representations within a unified neural coordinate system. The hippocampus appears to employ a shared computational machinery to compress and reorganize concrete, spatiotemporally bound experiences into abstract, cross-contextual knowledge. In line with recent perspectives, these findings indicate that the theorized cognitive map is not merely a metaphor for physical navigation, but a domain-general organizing principle for knowledge representation in humans5.

In addition, our results open up exciting new avenues for investigation. The present work focuses on the principal semantic gradient, which constitutes the dominant axis capturing a highly generalizable gradient of granularity. However, additional manifolds with lower-variance are likely to encode complementary semantic structures. Future studies should systematically chart the geometry and biological underpinnings of these secondary semantic manifolds, and examine how they interact with the primary axis to form a flexible conceptual space. Together, this work will further clarify how the hippocampus implements multiscale transformations from specific experiences to abstract, generalizable knowledge.
