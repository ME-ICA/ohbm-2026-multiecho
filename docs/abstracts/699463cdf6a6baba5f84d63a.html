Introduction

Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD) is characterized by excessive fear and avoidance of social situations and is often accompanied by heightened cognitive and physiological arousal.^1 Neuroimaging studies suggest that dysregulation of large-scale functional brain networks plays a key role in SAD; however, findings based on mean functional connectivity (FC) differences across groups have been inconsistent.

Structural–functional coupling (SFC), which quantifies the relationship between structural connectivity (SC) and FC, has emerged as a promising framework for understanding how anatomical constraints shape functional interactions.^2 Yet, most SFC studies rely on static connectivity measures and largely ignore within-subject temporal variability, a limitation particularly relevant for SAD, where symptom expression is closely linked to fluctuations in arousal.

Importantly, FC estimates are influenced by the amplitude of BOLD signals, reflecting global functional energy or variability. Variations in BOLD amplitude across individuals or time may confound SFC estimates if not explicitly modeled, raising concerns about interpretability and potential circularity. To address these gaps, the present study explicitly models functional energy derived from BOLD time series and examines its interaction with structural–functional coupling in a dynamic framework. We aimed to estimate window-wise SFC while controlling for functional energy and to test whether energy–coupling relationships differ between individuals with SAD and healthy controls, moving beyond static group comparisons toward a dynamic, mechanistic understanding of network regulation in SAD.


Methods

Participants and data acquisition

Participants included individuals diagnosed with Social Anxiety Disorder(N = 74, age = 25.47 ± 4.30) and age-matched healthy controls(N = 67, age = 25.20 ±3.58). All participants underwent multimodal MRI acquisition, including T1-weighted structural MRI, diffusion MRI, and resting-state functional MRI. Social anxiety symptom severity was assessed using standardized clinical scales such as the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale or Social Interaction Anxiety Scale.

Structural and functional connectivity and Functional energy estimation

Functional connectivity was estimated using a sliding-window correlation approach, yielding dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) matrices across time windows. Functional energy was quantified as mean of the BOLD time series variability for each time window and across regions. For each subject and time window, functional connectivity values were modeled as a linear combination of structural connectivity metrics and functional energy. Structural–functional coupling was defined as the regression coefficients associated with structural predictors, representing the strength of anatomical constraint on functional interactions after accounting for energy-related effects. This modeling approach yielded window-wise SFC estimates for each individual.

Energy–coupling interaction analysis

To assess group differences in the energy–dependent SFCs, linear mixed-effects models were employed with subject-specific random intercepts. Fixed effects included functional energy, group, and their interaction. This framework allowed us to account for repeated window-level measurements within individuals while testing whether the coupling response to energy differed between groups. 

Results

Group comparison of energy and mean coupling

Mean functional energy did not differ significantly between the SAD and NC groups. Similarly, average structural–functional coupling values, after controlling for energy effects, showed no significant group differences.

Energy–coupling interaction

Linear mixed-effects modeling revealed a significant interaction between functional energy and group (p < 0.001). In healthy controls, increases in functional energy were associated with stronger structural–functional coupling, indicating adaptive integration of functional activity with underlying anatomy. In contrast, individuals with SAD exhibited reduced coupling as energy increased, reflecting a maladaptive response. Likelihood ratio testing confirmed that inclusion of the interaction term significantly improved model fit relative to a model without the interaction.

Individual energy–coupling sensitivity

Energy–coupling sensitivity varied substantially across individuals, with the SAD group showing a tendency toward more negative slopes. This variability motivated further examination of clinical relevance.

Clinical association and group difference

Within the SAD group, stronger negative energy–coupling sensitivity was significantly associated with greater social anxiety symptom severity, indicating that individuals whose coupling deteriorated more strongly under increased energy exhibited more severe symptoms. No such association was observed in healthy controls. Regression-based interaction analysis confirmed that the relationship between sensitivity and symptom severity differed significantly between groups.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that alterations in structural–functional coupling in Social Anxiety Disorder are not reflected in mean connectivity differences but rather in abnormal responses of coupling strength to fluctuations in functional energy. Although overall energy levels were comparable between groups, individuals with SAD showed a paradoxical decoupling of structure and function as energy increased, in contrast to the adaptive coupling observed in healthy controls.

Importantly, individual differences in energy–coupling sensitivity were selectively associated with symptom severity in SAD, linking a dynamic network regulation property to clinical expression. These findings challenge static connectivity models of anxiety disorders and instead support a dynamic systems perspective, in which pathological states emerge from impaired regulation under high-energy conditions.

Future work may extend this framework using state-space or stochastic dynamical models to capture temporal dependencies in coupling dynamics, and to examine how therapeutic interventions modulate energy–coupling regulation. Overall, this study highlights energy-dependent dysregulation of structural–functional coordination as a key mechanism underlying social anxiety disorder.
