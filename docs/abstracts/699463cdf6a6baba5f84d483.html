Introduction

Transforming natural sounds into a knowledge of the sound-generating objects and events in the environment requires converting acoustic signals into semantic representations. A key goal of research on the perceptual and cerebral representation of natural sounds is then to pinpoint the chain of sound-signal transformations involved in this process. Recent research approached this problem within a model-comparison framework, revealing that intermediate layers of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are particularly effective at predicting the perceived dissimilarity and post-primary fMRI responses to natural sounds.1 Extensive model comparison approaches have however yet to be applied to time-resolved cerebral responses to natural sounds,2-3 leaving the temporal dynamics of this process unspecified. Here, we addressed this problem by carrying out a model-comparison analysis of magnetoencephalography (MEG) responses to a heterogeneous set of natural sounds.

Methods

Experimental design. Twenty-one participants (11 females; median age = 25 years) listened to 150 natural sounds (2 s duration), each repeated eight times across two MEG sessions, while performing a one-back repetition detection task (mean accuracy = 0.84 ± 0.17 SEM). Sounds were selected from an initial pool of 1,378 stimuli drawn from the FSD50K database, chosen for recognizability and recording quality. Each sound was annotated following auditory-event taxonomies along two semantic dimensions: sound-generating object or agent (“what/who,” e.g., dog) and sound-generating action (“how,” e.g., barking). The final stimulus set was optimized to minimize distance covariance between intermediate acoustic representations (Yamnet, layer 8) and semantic representations (concatenated Word2Vec embeddings of what/who and how), maximizing statistical dissociation between acoustic and semantic hypotheses.

Computational models. We evaluated three model classes to predict MEG responses: acoustic, text-based semantic, and CNN-based sound models (Fig. 1).

Acoustic models included complementary signal-processing representations: cochleagram and modulation transfer function (MTF), approximating subcortical and primary auditory cortex processing, and the Auditory Dimensions model (AudDim), estimating psychoacoustic features (pitch, loudness, periodicity, brightness, roughness) associated with STG processing.1 All acoustic features were computed in three 1-s windows (0–1 s, 0.5–1.5 s, 1–2 s).

Text-based semantic models consisted of continuous vector embeddings for what/who and how labels, including Word2Vec (Google News), GloVe, GUSE, BERT, GPT-2, Mistral, and OpenAI embeddings. We also included a categorical semantic representation comprising 15 categories derived from clustering concatenated Word2Vec what/who and how embeddings.

CNN models included two sound-processing networks: CatDNN, trained for sound-event classification (one-hot outputs), and SemDNN, trained to predict Word2Vec embeddings. For both networks, embeddings were extracted for three 1-s patches (0–1 s, 0.5–1.5 s, 1–2 s) at each layer.

Model–representation analyses. MEG responses were predicted using cross-validated representational similarity analysis at the sensor level and at the source level (volumetric beamformer) within ventral auditory ROIs (200 Hz temporal resolution). Crossnobis distances between sound-specific MEG responses were computed, whitened using pre-stimulus activity, and cross-validated across repetitions⁴. Model representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) were computed using Euclidean and cosine distances. For acoustic models, distances were calculated separately for each feature and temporal window. For each of the text-based continuous semantic models, we computed the Euclidean and cosine distance between sound stimuli considering either the what/who embeddings, the how embeddings, or between what and how embeddings of different stimuli. In addition, we computed the Euclidean and cosine distance between the CLS tokens of the what/who+how sentence in BERT, as well as the categorical RDM based on the 15 categories defined above. For CNNs, distances were computed for each layer and time window. All MEG and model RDMs were further whitened based on their theoretical covariance structure, improving model identifiability⁵.

Model prediction relied on leave-one-participant-out cross-validated linear regression predicting MEG RDMs from model RDMs. Variance partitioning was performed using commonality analysis on cross-validated R² estimates¹. To correct biases due to differing predictor dimensionality, null distributions were generated using Monte Carlo null-hypothesis model (random Gaussian MEG features processed using the same RSA pipeline as for the real MEG data; N Monte Carlo samples = 10,000), resulting in R²cv estimates z-scored based on the mean and standard deviation of the Monte Carlo distributions (zR²cv).

Results

Figure 2A shows the ability of the three model classes to predict MEG dissimilarities at both the sensor and source levels. Acoustic models (red) and text-based semantic models (blue) are grouped as classes, while the two CNNs are presented individually (CatDNN in orange; SemDNN in green). Across sensors and sources, CNNs generally achieved higher zR²cv values than both the acoustic and semantic model classes. More specifically, we observed an increasing dominance of CNN-derived representations from primary auditory regions (HG) toward post-primary auditory areas (PT and STG/STS) at relatively early latencies (~200–250 ms). At the sensor level, SemDNN showed predictive predominance around 500–700 ms, potentially reflecting activity originating in HG and STG/STS.

Interestingly, acoustic models outperformed CNNs in a specific temporal window between 700 and 1100 ms. Complementary analyses (not shown in figures) indicated that this effect was driven by the psychoacoustic features captured by the AudDim model. At pre-offset latencies (1–2 s), CNNs again clearly outperformed all other models across all regions-of-interest (ROIs). Notably, none of the models were able to explain neural representations in frontal regions such as the IFG.

To further elucidate the underlying processes, we performed a variance partition analysis (VPA) across the acoustic, semantic, and CNN model classes. The commonality analysis (Figure 1B) revealed that the CNN model class accounted for a substantial amount of the variance predicted by both acoustic (red) and semantic (blue) models. This shared variance increased from HG toward STG/STS. A late peak in shared variance between acoustic models and CNNs was observed around 600–1100 ms, particularly in PT, suggesting a late acoustic contribution to sound-based semantic processing. In contrast, acoustic and text-based semantic model classes (purple) shared virtually no variance across ROIs, indicating that they represent largely distinct processes.

The unique variance components provided the clearest dissociation between model classes and allowed us to characterize the temporal dynamics of natural sound representations (Figure 1C). We observed an early acoustic-driven categorical representation in STG/STS, which transitioned into a continuous semantic representation, peaking in strength at around 500–600 ms in HG and STG/STS. This semantic contribution then declined, giving way (by approximately 1200 ms) to a renewed predominance of sound-driven categorical representations. The late phase appears the strongest in HG and STG/STS, although spatial specificity is less clear in this window.

Conclusion

By jointly evaluating acoustic, text-based semantic, and CNN-derived representations, our results reveal a temporally and anatomically structured progression in the neural encoding of natural sounds. CNN models consistently provided the strongest overall prediction of MEG dissimilarities across both sensor- and source-level analyses, outperforming acoustic and semantic models throughout most of the stimulus duration. This predominance emerged early, around 200–250 ms, and increased along the auditory hierarchy, from primary auditory cortex (HG) toward posterior regions (PT, STG/STS), indicating that hierarchical CNN representations capture transformations aligned with cortical processing streams.

The VPA further clarified the shared and unique contributions of the model classes. CNNs captured a substantial portion of the variance explained by both acoustic and semantic models, and this shared variance increased from HG to STG/STS, highlighting that CNNs embed information spanning low-level acoustics to higher-level categories. The late shared variance between CNNs and acoustic models (600–1100 ms), especially in PT, underscores the persistence of acoustic-driven processes during stages commonly associated with semantic integration. Conversely, acoustic and text-based semantic models showed virtually no shared variance, reinforcing that they capture fundamentally distinct dimensions of sound representation.

Most critically, the unique contributions revealed a coherent sequence of representational stages. Early after stimulus onset, neural patterns were best explained by acoustic-driven categorical structure in STG/STS. This was followed by a transition to continuous sound-based semantic representations, peaking around 500–600 ms, consistent with the timing of semantic access during auditory processing. As the sound unfolded, these semantic representations gradually weakened, giving way, by ~1200 ms, to renewed dominance of sound-driven categorical representations. This late shift may reflect the engagement of higher-level decision, categorization, or task-related processes as the sound approaches offset.

Together, these findings outline a dynamic representational trajectory in which acoustics, semantics, and learned hierarchical features contribute at different stages and anatomical locations. CNNs capture a large fraction of this trajectory, yet specific temporal windows reveal meaningful contributions from psychoacoustic and semantic models that CNNs do not fully subsume. This highlights the value of combining multiple computational models to parse the evolving structure of auditory cortical representations and provides a fine-grained account of how the brain transforms natural sounds from early acoustic encoding to intermediate semantic processing and late-stage categorical organization.
