Introduction

Functional connectome “fingerprinting” postulates that individuals can be reliably identified from gray-matter (GM) connectivity patterns within resting-state datasets.^1 However, almost previous work has focused on GM only. In fMRI studies, white-matter (WM) BOLD signals are typically discarded as noise despite accumulating evidence that they covary with task-evoked activation in GM, show tract-aligned synchrony, and structured resting-state connectivity.^(2,3) WM BOLD signals may thus also carry complementary information about individual brain organization, but low SNR and strong global co-activation make WM fingerprints difficult to detect with standard functional connectivity pipelines.

In parallel, masked autoencoders and masked graph autoencoders have emerged as powerful self-supervised tools for separating shared structure from instance-specific variation in images and graphs.^(4,5) We hypothesized that a spatiotemporal graph masked autoencoder applied jointly to GM and WM time series could learn common connectivity patterns across subjects and conditions, and that the residual signal after removing these common patterns would reveal subject-specific “fingerprints” in WM.

Here we test this hypothesis in a large Human Connectome Project cohort with resting-state and task fMRI. We ask three questions: (i) how strong are WM-only fingerprints compared with GM-only fingerprints; (ii) can graph representation learning recover WM fingerprints that are weak or invisible in raw functional connectivity; and (iii) does combining GM and WM in this framework improve cross-condition identification relative to GM alone.

Methods

Data and Processing: We analyzed the 3T HCP young-adult data (n = 630), with six scans per subject including two resting-state scans (Rest1 and Rest2) and four task scans (Language, Emotion, Motor, and Working Memory). Preprocessing mainly comprised global signal regression and temporal filtering (passband = 0.01–0.10 Hz). For each scan, we extracted time-series from ROIs of GM (Shen-268 parcels) and WM (JHU-48 bundles).1 For each scan, we constructed a ROI-to-ROI FC matrix using Pearson correlations and applied a Fisher z-transformation, yielding a raw FC matrix Z (Figure 1A). 

Matching and Identification Rate (ID): We split scans into two sets: test (TEST: {Rest1, Working Memory Motor}) and database (DB: {Rest2, Language, Emotion}). For each scan, we vectorized the upper triangle of its Z, yielding z. For a TEST subject s and DB subject j, similarity was defined as \rho\left(s,j\right)=corr(z_s,\ \ z_j). The predicted identity for s was the DB subject with the highest similarity. The identification (ID) rate was the proportion of correctly matched TEST subjects. 

Spatio–temporal Graph Masked Autoencoder (st-GraphMAE): Each scan is a graph G=(V,E)with ROI nodes Vand node features x_i\in\mathbb{R}^T(time series). Edges come from Fisher-z FC by symmetric k-NN on magnitude: 

E=\left\{\left(i,j\right):j\in N_k\left(i\right)\ or\ N_k\left(j\right)\right\}\cup\left\{\left(i,i\right)\right\}.

Features are projected to hidden space h_i^((0))=W_"in"\ \ x_i. We sample a spatial node mask m_i\in{0,1}and a temporal mask M_{it}\in{0,1}(rates \pi_s,\pi_t); encoder inputs are

{\widetilde{h}}_i^{(0)}={\begin{matrix}W_{\mathrm{in}}(x_i\odot M_i),&m_i=1\\e_{\mathrm{mask}},&m_i=0\\\end{matrix}

with learnable token e_{\mathrm{mask}}. A Graph Attention Network (GAT) encoder–decoder yields \hat{x}=W_{\mathrm{out}}\mathrm{\thinsp} f_{\mathrm{dec}}(f_{\mathrm{enc}}({\widetilde{h}}^{(0)},G)). The objective combines a masked-node cosine loss and a masked-time MSE:

\mathcal{L}=\lambda_{\mathrm{sp}}\frac{1}{\mid V_0\mid}\sum_{i\in V_0}{\mathrm{SCE}({\hat{x}}_i,x_i)}+\lambda_{\mathrm{tm}}\frac{1}{\mid\Omega\mid}\sum_{(i,t)\in\Omega}{({\hat{x}}_{it}-x_{it})^2},\bigmwhere  V_0=\left\{i:m_i=0\right\},\Omega=\left\{\left(i,t\right):m_i=1,\mathrm{\ } M_{it}=0\right\},\ and

\mathrm{SCE}\left(u,v\right)=-\left(\frac{1+\cos{\left(u,v\right)}}{2}\right)^\alphalog\left(\frac{1+\cos{\left(u,v\right)}}{2}\right),\ where\ \alpha=2.

A single “union” model is trained jointly across GM and WM and across sessions, with padding masks applied when needed. The graph construction pipeline and model architecture is shown in Figure 1.

Residual FC identification: From the reconstructed time-series \hat{x}, we computed the reconstructed FC matrix \hat{Z}\ and defined the residual FC as Z_{\mathrm{res}}=\hat{Z}-Z_{\mathrm{raw}}. We then repeated the DB→TEST matching procedure using \hat{Z}\ and Z_{\mathrm{res}}\ to obtain residual-FC ID, respectively, thereby isolating individual-specific structure amplified by the model while down-weighting shared and low-informative components.

Results

For baseline fingerprinting using raw functional connectivity, gray-matter (GM) matrices showed the strongest performance. Rest1–Rest2 matches achieved high identification rates, while cross-condition matches involving rest and tasks (e.g., Rest1→Language, Rest1→Emotion, and Motor→Language/Emotion) reached moderate identification levels. The rates were lower than those reported by Flinn et al.^1

White-matter (WM) raw connectivity produced substantially weaker fingerprinting. Rest–rest identification was clearly above chance but much lower than in GM, and most rest–task and task–task pairs showed only low identification rates. When GM and WM were combined at the raw-connectivity level, the overall pattern largely followed GM: rest–rest matching remained strong and cross-condition performance was similar to GM alone, indicating only limited additional benefit from simply adding WM to the raw FC matrices.

Using residual connectivity derived from the spatiotemporal graph masked autoencoder changed this pattern across all tissue configurations. In the GM-only residual matrices, rest–rest identification increased compared with the corresponding raw GM matrices in both matching directions. Cross-condition matches also improved, with rest–task and task–task pairs generally showing higher identification rates than in the raw GM condition.

Residual WM connectivity showed the largest relative change. Rest1–Rest2 identification increased substantially compared with raw WM, and many rest–task and task–task pairs that were close to chance in the raw WM matrices became clearly above chance in the residual WM matrices.

For the combined GM+WM condition, residual connectivity yielded the highest and most consistent identification rates overall. Rest–rest pairs remained strong, and cross-condition matches (including rest–task and task–task combinations) were higher than in both raw GM and raw GM+WM, in both DB→TEST and TEST→DB directions. Across all three tissue configurations, residual connectivity thus produced systematically higher identification rates than the corresponding raw connectivity, with particularly pronounced gains in WM and in cross-condition matching.

Conclusion

This study addresses whether white-matter (WM) BOLD carries reliable individual “fingerprints” and whether graph-based representation learning can uncover them. Using standard functional connectivity, WM-only fingerprinting was weak compared with gray matter (GM), and simply appending WM to GM offered little advantage. After applying a graph representation learning approach to remove the common pattern, identification rates increased systematically in all tissue configurations, with the largest relative gains in WM and in cross-condition matches.

These findings show that WM BOLD connectivity contains stable, subject-specific structure that is largely obscured in conventional FC but becomes accessible when shared patterns are modeled and subtracted. Residual GM connectivity strengthens classic GM fingerprints, while residual WM connectivity reveals additional individual differences that are not redundant with GM. The combined residual GM+WM representation provides the most robust identification across rest and task states.

Methodologically, this work highlights spatiotemporal graph representation learning and residualization as a practical way to separate common from individual-specific signal in multi-tissue fMRI. Conceptually, it supports treating WM as an integral component of the functional connectome rather than as noise. Future work will test whether these WM-enhanced fingerprints improve longitudinal stability and sensitivity to aging or disease, and whether similar residual graph approaches can generalize to other parcellations, modalities, and clinical cohorts.
