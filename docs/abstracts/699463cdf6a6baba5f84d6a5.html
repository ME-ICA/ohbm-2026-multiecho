Introduction

1. Introduction

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has revealed that resting-state neural fluctuations are highly organised, consistently giving rise to multiple large-scale networks with well-defined spatial patterns. Even without engaging in an explicit task, the brain continues to operate dynamically, constantly adjusting in response to current environmental input and internally generated processes. The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) has been adopted to characterise whole-brain configurations that emerge over time, commonly referred to as brain states. Within this framework, states are modelled as multivariate Gaussian distributions that capture not only distinct activation but also, importantly, unique patterns of functional connectivity (Vidaurre et al., 2017). This approach facilitates examination of brain dynamics without relying on the assumptions and biases inherent in sliding-window approaches.

Despite the success of HMMs in characterising dynamic brain activity, the genetic mechanisms underlying these states remain largely unknown. Using data from ~52,000 UK Biobank participants, we aimed to determine whether (1) HMM-derived brain states are heritable and biologically interpretable, mapping to meaningful genes and showing enrichment in relevant cell types and neurotransmitter systems; (2) their variability is driven by shared versus state-specific genetic influences; and (3) they are genetically linked to behaviour and diseases.

Methods

2. Method

1. Genotype 

UK Biobank genotype array and imputed data, which had undergone prior quality control, were used. Analyses were restricted to individuals of self-reported European ancestry. Individuals were excluded based on outlying genetic principal components (±5 SD), low genotyping rate (<95%), excessive heterozygosity, or sex mismatch. Variants were filtered by minor allele frequency (>1%), call rate (>95%), Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (p > 1 × 10⁻⁶), and imputation quality (r² > 0.4). After quality control, 9,142,950 SNPs from ~52,000 individuals were included in the analysis.

2. Imaging 

Denoised resting-state fMRI data were available for ~52,000 participants, acquired on a 3 T Siemens Skyra scanner (multiband = 8; 490 timepoints; TR = 0.735 s; 2.4 mm isotropic resolution). Standard preprocessing, including motion correction, filtering, and EPI unwarping, was applied, followed by ICA-based artefact removal. Group ICA identified 55 non-artefact components from 100, and subject-specific time series were obtained using dual regression. A multivariate Gaussian HMM was fitted to the subject‑specific node timeseries derived from the group‑level parcellation, thereby segmenting each participant’s resting-state fMRI time series into a sequence of discrete, recurring brain states. In this framework, each state  is described by a mean activation vector  and a covariance matrix , where  captures the state-specific pattern of functional connectivity across parcels. The HMM assigns, at each time point, posterior probabilities over the 12 states to each subject, yielding a state time course for each participant. For each subject and each of the 12 states, a fractional occupancy (FO) was computed as the temporal mean of the posterior probability of that state — i.e., the proportion of time that the brain is estimated to spend in that state during the scan. These subject-level FO values for all 12 states were then used as quantitative phenotypes in a genome-wide association study (GWAS). 

3. GWAS

Goal 1: Characterise the genetic architecture of brain states.

A genetic relatedness matrix (GRM) was constructed from LD-pruned SNPs using GCTA, and GWAS was performed with fastGWA-mlm (Jiang et al., 2019), adjusting for demographic, head motion, and genetic ancestry covariates. Independent loci were clumped via PLINK at p < 5 × 10-8 (1 Mb LD window). SNP-based heritability was estimated using both LD Score Regression (LDSC) and GCTA-GREML. Putative causal variants were prioritised through functionally informed fine-mapping (PolyFun + SuSiE) for significant loci (±500 kb). SNPs were assigned to genes if supported by at least two approaches, including MAGMA positional mapping, H-MAGMA for long-range chromatin interactions, xQTL mapping which integrates molecular, splicing, expression, and methylation QTLs, or mBAT-combo which detects multi-SNP associations while accounting for masking effects. Finally, enrichment of genes was assessed across 31 superclusters and 461 clusters of cell types derived from single-nucleus RNA sequencing data, each annotated with relevant neurotransmitter profiles (Siletti et al., 2023).

Goal 2: Identify shared and distinct polygenic signals across brain states.

Genetic correlations between fractional occupancy (FO) of brain states were estimated using LDSC. Shared genomic regions were identified by extracting LD blocks around sentinel SNPs and performing Bayesian colocalization (coloc.abf), which calculates posterior probabilities of different causal variant configurations under the assumption of a single causal variant per trait.

Goal 3: Assess relevance to behaviour and diseases

Genetic correlation, colocalisation, and causal inference – bidirectional TwoSample Mendelian Randomisation and MR-PRESSO - were conducted between FO of states and 48 psychiatric, neurological, cognitive, and neuroimaging phenotypes. 

Results

3. Results

Clumping analyses identified 41 independent loci across the 12 brain states, with the number of loci per state ranging from 1 (States 5, 8, and 11) to 12 (State 4). Chromosome 2 harboured the largest number of associated loci. The total number of genome-wide significant SNPs per state varied from 1 to 560. SNP-based heritability () ranged from 0.0398 to 0.0734 (standard errors 0.008–0.0107) using LD Score Regression, indicating modest but significant genetic contributions across all states. Estimates from GREML were generally higher, ranging from 0.0355 to 0.1332 with highly significant p-values, consistent with LDSC results and confirming a modest but significant genetic influence on FO of brain states. At the gene level, loci were mapped to genes using multiple complementary approaches (MAGMA, mBAT-combo, sQTL, eQTL, and Hi-C data). The phenotypic relevance of these genes—defined as prior evidence of association with specific traits or diseases—was then assessed by querying the GWAS Catalogue (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/). Among the overlapping results across states, PSD4—previously associated with blood cell counts and C-reactive protein (CRP)—emerged in multiple states (2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, and 12). IL1A, a cytokine within the interleukin-1 family, appeared in States 2, 5, and 7. POLR1B, linked to CRP levels and smoking-related traits, was identified in States 2, 5, and 12. In addition, APOE, widely implicated in neurodegeneration and cognitive performance, was detected in States 1 and 4. Complementing these gene-level findings, cell-type enrichment analyses indicated that FOs of brain states are primarily driven by excitatory and inhibitory neurons—including spiny neurons, splatter neurons, and interneurons—characterised by GABA- and glutamate-mediated actions. Taken together, these findings indicate that dynamic brain states are shaped by coordinated genetic mechanisms involving immune–metabolic, inflammatory, and neurodegeneration-related pathways acting through key neuronal cell types.

In addressing our second goal, we observed abundant pleiotropy across dynamic brain states. Under PP.H4.abf > 0.5, States 9 and 2 shared eight loci, and States 9 and 8 shared five. Beyond these, three state pairs shared four loci, seven shared three, thirteen shared two, and nine shared one. In addition, genetic correlations among FOs pointed to a hierarchical structure underlying the organisation of dynamic brain states. Collectively, these results suggest that, despite their definitional differences in activation and connectivity profiles, brain states are shaped by overlapping genetic architectures.

Turning to our third goal, we found that FOs of brain states are genetically correlated with a range of phenotypes. For example, educational attainment showed genetic correlations with 10 of the 12 states, whereas the personality trait extraversion showed correlations with eight. Sleep duration, anxiety, and schizophrenia each displayed correlations with five states. Genomic colocation analyses further supported these links; for instance, sleep duration shared two causal loci with State 4, and in total, we identified 31 instances in which a brain state and one of the studied phenotypes colocalised at a putative causal locus. Mendelian Randomisation also showed that educational attainment and sleep duration have causal effects on specific brain states. Taken together, these findings highlight a shared genetic architecture linking dynamic brain states to behavioural and disease phenotypes. Conclusion

4. Conclusion

In summary, this study provides comprehensive insights into the genetic architecture of dynamic brain states, as defined by Hidden Markov Models of resting-state fMRI. First, we showed that brain dynamics are heritable, map to biologically meaningful genes, and are primarily driven by excitatory and inhibitory neurons—including spiny neurons, spatter neurons, and interneurons—with GABAergic and glutamatergic neurotransmission playing central roles. Second, these fractional occupancies are shaped by both shared and state-specific loci, exhibiting extensive pleiotropy across states. Third, the genetic effects of these loci are associated with a wide range of phenotypes, including educational attainment, personality traits, sleep duration and diseases. Overall, these findings illuminate the genetic underpinnings of human brain dynamics, linking genomic variation, neuronal circuitry, and phenotypes.
