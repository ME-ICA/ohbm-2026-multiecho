Introduction

The negative impact of head motion on resting-state fMRI studies is well documented and a limiting factor for studies that investigate cohorts with higher levels of motion (e.g. children). Head motion affects the individual volumes and the time series in many complex forms because several aspects of the MR acquisition are sensitive to motion, such as multi-slice acquisition, spatial encoding, B1 transmit and receive, and the B0 field.^1 Many of these artifacts persist after the volumes have been anatomically co-registered with retrospective or prospective motion correction (PMC). B1 receive artifact is one such “secondary effect” of head motion due to the head moving within changing array coil sensitivities. The resulting changes in observed signal intensity bias in the coil-combined magnitude images can be substantial (~10% in peripheral brain regions from ~5 degree rotations) and are not corrected explicitly in typical analysis pipelines, although some methods have been proposed based on bias correction.^2 These intensity changes are indirectly corrected to some extent by general methods for correcting or “denoising” motion artifacts, including simple regression methods and more data-driven approaches like independent component analysis (ICA).^3

In this work we introduce an explicit between-volume bias correction for fMRI timeseries based on the tissue-based bias correction used in SAMSEG.^4 The method can be added as a preprocessing step following volume co-registration and optionally combined with motion regression and ICA. We examine its performance for correlation-based functional connectivity on a dataset with intentional large head movements as well as ground truth “still” scans acquired for each subject.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were acquired from nine subjects under different movement and PMC conditions using 2D multi-slice EPI on a 3T MAGNETOM Skyra with a 64-channel head coil (Siemens). Each run included 180 volumes (9:48 min). Acquisition parameters: 3mm isotropic resolution, 44 slices, TR=3.25s, TE=30ms, echo spacing=0.50ms, matrix=64×64; to avoid motion-related artifacts, no in-plane parallel imaging or SMS was used. Volume navigators (vNavs) were acquired once per TR for PMC^5 (8 mm isotropic, GRAPPA R=3, TR=6ms, TE=2.5ms, echo spacing=0.30ms, matrix=32×32, feedback delay=100ms).

For the “still” conditions, the subjects were instructed to minimize their head movements, while for the “move” conditions, subjects rotated their head about the B0 axis (i.e., shaking their head in a “no” gesture). This specific movement pattern targets the B1 receive intensity change (the focus of this study). These conditions were repeated for both PMC on and off.

Data preprocessing followed a standard pipeline implemented using the Nipype framework in Python. In this pipeline, the data was passed through a combined slice-timing correction and EPI volume co-registration step. Next, the EPI data was registered to the T1 space by finding the registration transformation between the average EPI and the skull stripped structural image using FMRIB's Linear Image Registration Tool (FLIRT). In parallel with this step, the subject’s T1 image was registered to MNI space using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). The EPI image was registered to MNI space using the combined transformations. As a last step of the pre-processing, the data was smoothed using a 3D Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum of 4mm.

ICA-based correction and regression-based correction were performed on the data in MNI space, while the bias-field correction step was performed after the slice timing and volume registration steps described above (i.e., in the native space). In the bias-field correction algorithm, first the data was temporally segmented based on the motion. Segments were determined by thresholding frame-wise displacement with a threshold of 0.4mm. Then, for each temporal segment, i.e., head pose, a bias field was calculated by 1) obtaining a tissue segmentation in the native EPI space (SynthSeg was used); 2) estimating a bias field that provides more homogeneous intensities (narrower histograms) within tissue classes (inspired by SAMSEG)4. All bias fields were divided by the bias field from the first temporal segment, and volumes in subsequent segments were divided by the corresponding bias for that segment.

For confound regression, we first extracted white-matter (WM) and CSF time series by spatially averaging the voxel time series within their respective masks (eroded to ensure no overlap with gray matter exists). Then, 6 rigid body motion parameters were obtained from the vNav estimates (calculated in real-time for PMC) so that the same method for motion measurement was used for PMC on and off data. The final 26 confounds were 6 motion parameters, their derivatives, squared parameters and derivatives, and the two WM and CSF time series. All 26 confounds were removed by projection onto the signal space.

ICA-based Automatic Removal of Motion Artifacts (ICA-AROMA) was also used for denoising the data. The number of ICA components was set to 100 as the amount of motion in the data is significant, and the vNavs motion parameters were used for the confound classification step.

Figure 1 shows the time series for a voxel, illustrating the effect of the artifact we aim to mitigate, and after applying bias field correction, regression, and ICA. The top row shows a ~10% intensity change after each large motion that volume registration is unable to fix. The bias field correction algorithm (second row) reduces the intensity changes, but some spikes remain in the data. Confound regression reduces the motion-related intensity changes, and ICA reduces them further.

Results

Figure 2 demonstrates the correlation map estimated with a seed located in the auditory region, for different cleaning methods. Auditory networks were chosen in this work as they are located laterally and are strongly affected by coil sensitivity changes induced by yaw head rotation around the B₀ axis. The left two columns show the correlation maps of the still cases, while the right two columns show the move case. In the still condition, without additional cleaning, there is a high correlation across the brain, a known effect when global signal contributions are not removed. This is why, when the data is cleaned using regression (which includes WM and CSF time series) and ICA-AROMA (which also removes functional components that overlap with structural brain segments; specifically, components whose spatial maps predominantly cover white matter and CSF are flagged for removal) the high correlation is confined to auditory regions. Note the bilateral nature of the correlation. The correlation maps of the move case without any additional cleaning steps show widespread false positives, largely due to the B1 receive artifact intensity changes. All three methods result in cleaner maps, but for the bias field correction, regression or Bias Field Correction + regression method, the maps show less focal connections and bleed into other regions (compared to the maps for the clean still conditions). ICA-AROMA applied alone or to bias field corrected data produces more focal connectivity maps, while the combined Bias Field Correction + ICA-AROMA approach results in more widespread connectivity patterns. Note that none of the cleaning methods results in the bilateral connectivity maps we expect to see, which would mean that even in the ICA-AROMA case, we are losing vital information in the cleaning process. More investigation is needed to explore the reason behind this loss of information.

The impact of PMC was negligible, as the applied PMC method here does not correct for the B1 receive artifact.

Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the effectiveness of segmentation-based bias field correction, motion regression, and ICA-AROMA in mitigating the influence of B1 receive artifacts in fMRI data with high levels of head motion. As auditory regions are in lateral regions of the brain strongly affected by these coil sensitivity changes from yaw head rotation around the B0 axis, we elected to focus on this network. We confirm that standard motion correction (volume registration) and PMC do not remove B1 receive intensity changes that are a major secondary effect of large head movements, as expected. Our results show that although using only bias field correction and motion regression reduces spurious correlations, they are likely not enough, and there remain high seed-based correlations outside the expected regions. We found that using ICA-AROMA (both alone and on the bias field corrected data) cleans the connectivity maps; however, the expected bilateral auditory connectivity is lost with all methods, suggesting that these approaches fail to recover the information when there are substantial intensity changes from B1 receive artifacts. Future work is needed to develop methods that both clean the data and preserve vital information in fMRI data affected by large head motion.
