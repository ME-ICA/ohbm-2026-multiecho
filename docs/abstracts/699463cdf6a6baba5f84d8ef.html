Introduction

Functional connectome fingerprinting has established that individual brains possess unique, stable connectivity profiles that act as an indentifiable signature.1 Current theoretical frameworks posit that this identifiablity relies on the complex interplay of whole brain distributed networks.2 However, the extent to which this functional identity depends on the integrity of the underlying neural substrate remains unknown. Hemispherotomy—the surgical disconnection of a cerebral hemisphere—presents an interesting case to test how the brain forces the remaining neural architecture to function separated. Previous approaches have succesfully shown that individuals with a single hemisphere retain their canonical resting-state networks.3, 4 In this study, we employ this extreme model of substrate reduction to test the limits of connectome fingerprinting. By applying identifiability metrics to a cohort of hemispherotomy patients, we are the first to describe how identifiable and unique the brain fingerprint becomes after such a massive intervention.

Methods

We analyzed resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data from 26 hemispherotomy patients and 24 healthy controls. Data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep and projected to MNI space; motion, CSF, WM, global signal and aCompCor regressors were removed and data were band-pass filtered (0.01–0.08 Hz). 

Functional connectome fingerprinting workflow consisted in applying the Schaefer-100 parcellation atlas5, adjusted with subject-specific lesion masks, to preprocessed data. Mean BOLD signals were extracted from the intact and isolated hemispheres independently, then split into two halves, to compute functional connectomes per half. Following the methodology of Amico and Goñi,2 we then computed identifiability matrices for each hemisphere and both groups to extract fingerprint metrics.

These metrics include within-subject similarity (Iself), between-subject similarity (Iothers), and Idiff which quantifies how identifiable the FC of a subject is. To characterize FC edge-wise idiosyncrasy, we consider an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of > 0.6, calculated as shown in Figure 1B, only for the intact hemispheres. Additionally we grouped the edges by the seven canonical networks, to measure within- and between-network differences.

Differences in fingerprint metrics, mean ICC values within and between each resting-state network were tested with Welch t-tests and FDR correction (α= 0.05). For the analysis of reliable edge counts, a Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the proportion of high-ICC edges between hemispherotomy and control subjects.

Results

No significant differences were found on the stability of the fingerprint for both left (LH) and right healthy (RH) hemispheres (Iself) (LH: t = −0.99; p = 0.327, RH: t = −1.87; p = 0.071). Moreover, the similarity of the fingerprint to other peers (Iothers) was significantly lower in the patient group (LH: t = −4.30; p < 0.001, RH: t = −10.57; p < 0.001). This difference in Iothers made the FC fingerprint significantly more identifiable within the patients group in the right hemisphere (RH: t = 4.70; p < 0.001) but not in the left hemisphere (LH: t = 1.92; pFDR = 0.094).
In the isolated hemisphere comparisons to controls, the results revealed no significant differences in neither of the metrics for the left hemisphere (Iself: t = −2.05; pFDR = 0.310, Iothers: t = −1.48; pFDR = 0.310, Idiff: t = −0.26; pFDR = 0.805). However on the right hemisphere, Iothers was significantly lower in the patient group (t = −9.61; pFDR = 0.024), and Iothers was significantly higher in the patient group (t = 5.27; pFDR = 0.04); Iself showed no significant differences (t = −1.14; pFDR = 0.363) across groups. Paradoxically, the intact hemisphere ICC analysis revealed that HT patients exhibited a significantly higher number of reliable edges than controls, for both the left (33% vs 18%: p < 0.001) and right (43% vs 17%: p < 0.001) hemispheres. As well, patients showed widespread higher ICC, particularly in higher-order networks from the right hemisphere (DMN, pFDR < 0.001; FPN, pFDR < 0.001; DA pFDR < 0.001). Following the workflow, we observed higher idiosyncrasy within- and between-networks in the patient group, with higher significant differences in the right hemisphere as observed in Figure 2.

Conclusion

Here, we demonstrate that hemispherotomy patients fingerprint is significantly more identifiable than controls, in both conditions of intact and isolated hemisphere. This is further corroborated by the emergence of hyper-reliable edges within high-order networks (DMN, FPN), suggesting that the intact hemisphere settles into a rigid, highly idiosyncratic configuration. We interpret that neural identity does not require a complete anatomical substrate; rather, the reduction of substrate forces the brain into a unique functional corner. Elucidating the precise drivers of this functional uniqueness remains a critical next step for understanding how neural networks compensate for massive substrate loss.
