Introduction

Multi-parameter mapping (MPM) provides high-resolution quantitative maps of tissue properties in the brain, including magnetization transfer saturation (MTsat), proton density (PD), and relaxation rates (R1, R2*), in a clinically feasible scan time (~20 min)1. In longitudinal studies, high test-retest reliability of MPM metrics is required to detect pathological small changes over time, and stable segmentation-derived volumetric estimates across visits are a practical requirement for downstream morphometry. In many studies, an additional whole-brain 3D T1-weighted scan is acquired to support morphometry and tissue segmentation, but this adds scan time beyond the MPM protocol2. 

Synthetic T1-weighted images derived from MPM data may improve the stability of segmentation-derived volumetric estimates by providing a segmentation-friendly T1w-like contrast and reducing dependence on scanner-specific intensity scaling. Here, we evaluate two complementary synthetic T1 approaches: an MPM-based synthetic T13 and a SynthSR-based synthetic T14, and compare both against using the quantitative R1 map directly as the segmentation input. We assessed segmentation quality and across-visit stability of segmentation-derived total intracranial volume (TIV). Conventional MPRAGE was not acquired in this protocol; however, prior work has shown that synT1-MPM yields morphometric outcomes comparable to those obtained from acquired T1-weighted images3.

Methods

We used MPM data from the multicenter Nogo Inhibition in Spinal Cord Injury (NISCI) clinical trial, which was acquired across 8 sites on 3T Siemens systems in participants with acute traumatic cervical spinal cord injury (neurological level C1 to C8). The MPM protocol provided whole-brain coverage at 1 mm isotropic resolution and was acquired in ~20 minutes. MRI was acquired at three longitudinal visits (screening, 1 month, 6 months post-injury; mean 17.2, 53.3, 190.1 days). Across the multi-site NISCI cohort, we included 77 participants contributing 199 visit sessions (up to three sessions per participant). 

MPM data were processed with the hMRI toolbox to generate quantitative parameter maps. For each visit, we generated two synthetic T1w images. First, synT1-MPM was reconstructed from the PD, R1, and R2* maps using an MPRAGE signal equation 3. Second, synT1-SynthSR was generated from the MPM T1w image using SynthSR. SynthSR transforms a heterogeneous T1w anatomical image into a high-resolution, contrast-standardized T1w image via learned harmonization and super-resolution 4.

Brain tissue segmentation was performed with CAT12 using three different images (synT1-MPM, synT1-SynthSR, and R1). CAT12 produced tissue segmentations and volumetric measures, including an estimate of total intracranial volume (TIV), using a consistent default configuration across images. Segmentation quality was quantified using CAT12’s internal segmentation quality rating (SQR) extracted from the CAT12 reports. SQR is a scalar (0–5) summary of segmentation quality, derived from how well the tissue segmentation fits the data (e.g., residual boundary errors, consistency), where higher values indicate better segmentations.

Longitudinal stability was quantified within subjects (≥2 visits) by computing the coefficient of variation (CoV = SD/mean) of TIV across visits, separately for each segmentation input. Segmentation quality was assessed using within-visit paired comparisons, restricted to visits with a valid CAT12 SQR for all compared segmentation inputs. For each contrast, we report the mean paired differences between segmentation inputs, 95% confidence intervals computed using a normal approximation, two-sided p-values, and Cohen’s d effect sizes.

Results

Segmentation quality differed across images when quantified using the CAT12 SQR. Across visits with valid SQR values for all three segmentation inputs (n = 199 visits), synT1-SynthSR showed the highest SQR, followed by R1 and synT1-MPM (median SQR: 3.70, 3.68, and 3.51, IQR: 3.60–3.76, 3.55–3.80, 3.42–3.60, respectively). In paired comparisons, synT1-SynthSR yielded a higher SQR than synT1-MPM (mean paired Δ = +0.153, 95% CI [0.126, 0.180], p < 0.001, d = 1.28), while synT1-SynthSR showed a smaller, non-significant advantage over R1 (Δ = +0.074, 95% CI [−0.012, 0.160], p = 0.091, d = 0.19).

Within-subject longitudinal stability was quantified using the CoV of TIV across visits, where lower CoV indicates greater stability. In subjects with at least two visits per segmentation image, both 3 and 4 showed lower CoV of TIV than R1 (median CoV: 1.0%, 1.13%, 1.6%; IQR: 0.49–2.42%, 0.39–2.26%, 0.79–3.66%, respectively). In paired comparisons, synT1-SynthSR reduced TIV CoV versus R1 (median paired Δ = −0.54 percentage points, 95% CI [−5.9, −1.2], p = 0.0029, d = −0.36) and synT1-MPM showed a similar reduction (Δ = −0.58 pp, 95% CI [−6.0, −1.1], p = 0.0044, d = −0.34), while synT1-SynthSR and synT1-MPM did not differ significantly (Δ = −0.05 pp, 95% CI [−2.1, 0.8], p = 0.37, d = −0.11).

These findings indicate that synthetic T1 inputs, synT1-SynthSR and synT1-MPM, yield more stable longitudinal segmentation-derived volumetric estimates than R1, while synT1-SynthSR also provides the highest CAT12 segmentation quality ratings.

Conclusion

Our results show that the choice of segmentation input affects longitudinal segmentation performance in a multi-site longitudinal MPM dataset. Both synthetic T1 inputs (synT1-MPM and synT1-SynthSR) improved the across-visit stability of segmentation-derived volumetric estimates relative to using the quantitative R1 map. On CAT12’s Segmentation Quality Rating (SQR), synT1-SynthSR achieved the highest ratings, R1 was similar, and synT1-MPM was lower. For CAT12-based longitudinal brain segmentation in MPM studies, we recommend using a synthetic T1 input rather than R1, with synT1-SynthSR preferred when available.

This work is limited by reliance on CAT12-specific QC metrics and by focusing on a global volume endpoint (TIV) rather than regional gray and white matter measures. Future work should validate these findings against an acquired structural T1-weighted reference (MPRAGE) in an independent dataset where available.
