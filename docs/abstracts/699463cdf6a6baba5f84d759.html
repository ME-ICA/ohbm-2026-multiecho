Introduction

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) applies weak electrical currents via scalp-attached electrodes to modulate excitability in the human brain1. Beneficial effects of tDCS on motor and cognitive functions have been demonstrated in healthy and clinical populations2. However, while the basic neurophysiological mechanisms of tDCS are well understood3, its effects on the neural networks underlying human brain functions requires further investigation.

Therefore, combining tDCS with fMRI is essential to reveal its effects on large-scale brain networks. By administering tDCS concurrently with task-based or resting-state (RS) fMRI, it is possible to investigate immediate stimulation effects on brain function. However, the vast majority of concurrent tDCS-fMRI studies have employed conventional montages using relatively large electrodes. This results in widespread current flow between electrodes, potentially affecting multiple brain regions and simultaneously4. Consequently, the origin of the observed changes in brain function (network modulation vs. current spread) remained difficult to ascertain.

Currently, there is no established framework for testing hypotheses regarding network-specific neural modulation5. To address this gap in knowledge, we developed a novel analytical framework using data from a large-scale, multicenter consortium (MeMoSLAP RU 5429). First, we employed seed-based RS analysis to identify the functional networks associated with target regions for focal tDCS in the relevant projects and also established their test-retest reliability. Second, we identified task-based activity patterns elicited by the given learning tasks. Third, we assessed the overlap between the RS functional networks and the task-active regions, to determine which task-based effects would represent true network-level effects of focal tDCS. Hence, we aimed to develop an empirically informed network-based framework, suited for planning of future focal tDCS-fMRI studies.

Methods

Four concurrent tDCS-fMRI sessions (two RS, two tasks-based) were completed by 37 right-handed, healthy participants (19 female; mean age=25.78 years) at least one week apart. In the task sessions, participants were asked to either perform an Object-Location Memory (OLM, n=18) or an Associative Picture, Pseudoword Learning (APPL, n=19) task. Both tasks used block designs with a total of six experimental blocks (OLM or APPL tasks, see Figure1) and two randomly interspersed control blocks (left/right decisions or a simple lexical decision task). To minimize learning effects, parallel versions were administered. A Sham focal tDCS (3×1 montage) consisted of a 10-second ramp-up to 2 mA, followed by 20 seconds of stimulation, a 10-second ramp-down immediately prior to commencing the functional scans. Electrode montages were individually optimized for each participant. The overall procedure ensured precise targeting and participant blinding, and provides a direct comparator for future active tDCS arms within the MeMoSLAP consortium.

 MRI data were collected on a 3T Siemens Vida scanner. RS fMRI preprocessing was done using fMRIPrep v24.1.1, followed by denoising with 27 nuisance regressors comprising a comprehensive set of 24 motion parameters, to capture both linear and nonlinear motion artifacts while maintaining orthogonality between regressors, global signal (GS), and mean signals from white matter (WM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Seed-based functional connectivity was calculated for left ventral inferior frontal gyrus (lvIFG) and right occipito-temporal cortex (rOTC) seeds. Group-level one sample t-tests were conducted (p<0.001, cluster, FWE p<0.05). Test-retest reliability (TRR) was measured through intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

Task-fMRI data were investigated with the help of SPM12 (first-level GLM, second-level one sample t-test, p<0.001, cluster, FWE p<0.05). The extent of overlap between RS networks and task activation was determined by Dice similarity coefficient and voxel-wise Pearson correlation.

Results

Resting-state functional connectivity analyses revealed distinct large-scale networks: A ventral visual-limbic network was associated with the rOTC seed, which connected most strongly to the right occipital fusiform gyrus (peak T = 17.70, cluster size = 8,973 voxels, p<0.000001 FWE-corrected) and left occipital pole (peak T = 11.19, cluster size = 6,682 voxels, p<0.000001). A left lateralized language-related and executive control network was identified for the lvIFG seed. Both networks exhibited high TRR: 72.0% of voxels in the rOTC network and 77.3% in the lvIFG network were shown good-to-excellent TRR (ICC ≥ 0.75). In the OLM task, the activation was most prominent in the left parahippocampal gyrus and superior parietal lobule. The APPL task showed the strongest activation in a large bilateral cerebellar cluster and a left-dominant fronto-parietal network. The similarity analysis revealed significant regions of overlap between RS connectivity networks and task-based activation, where the rOTC network demonstrated a positive correlation with OLM task activation (OML>Control: r=0.289, Dice=0.249; OLM>Baseline: r=0.365, Dice=0.349, see Figure 2A) and the lvIFG network exhibited a significant correlation with APPL task activation (APPL>Control: r=0.133, Dice=0.112; APPL>Baseline: r=0.061, Dice=0.121, see Figure2B).

Conclusion

We describe a cross-modal fMRI-based framework to guide planning of network-specific neuromodulation and empirically guided hypothesis testing in focal tDCS-fMRI studies. We illustrated this approach using two datasets from the MeMoSLAP consortium. (1) RS-data delineated the functional networks originating from the envisaged focal tDCS targets (rOTC, lIFG) for two distinct experimental learning paradigms (OLM, APPL). These networks showed substantial consistency across scanning sessions, with good-to-excellent ICCs in over 70% of voxels of either network, highlighting the usability of seed-based RS-fMRI approaches for network-based targeting and hypothesis testing. (2) Subsequently, we determined the spatial overlap of the identified seeded-RS networks with activity elicited by the respective learning paradigms. This step identified regions in the task-active networks that could be affected by focal tDCS, either via direct application of current flow to the target region or downstream effects on functionally connected areas. Notably, only a subset of voxels identified by the seed-based RS analysis overlapped with activity elicited by either paradigm (ranging from 7-55%), with overall larger correspondence for the OLM task. Therefore, the degree of potential tDCS network effects varied substantially depending on the selected target region, the extent of its functional network and also task-specific activity patterns. (3) Finally, the degree of correspondence between imaging modalities was in part mediated by the chosen contrasts-of-interest in the task-based analyses, with more conservative control conditions resulting in reduced overlap between task-related activity and the seeded RS-network. This suggests that the choice of contrast has direct implications for revealing potential stimulation effects in tDCS-fMRI studies.

This study thus established a principled, cross-modal fMRI framework that bridges a critical gap in neuromodulation research. By integrating reliable intrinsic connectivity maps with task-evoked activity, we provide a method to prospectively identify network-level targets for focal NIBS and to generate hypotheses for analysis in tDCS-fMRI studies. This approach paves the way for investigating the causal modulation of specific functional networks, facilitating a shift from stimulating single brain regions to targeting predefined functional pathways.
