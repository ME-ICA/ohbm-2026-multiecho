Introduction

Teaching (bio)medical science students the craft of neuroimaging: segmentation, use of atlases, fMRI analysis, and fiber tracking is a challenging task. While the theory of neuroimaging acquisition and methods,  can be taught in theoretical lectures, hands-on experience and interacting with neuroimaging analysis and software is an indispensible learning experience, and will prepare students for a potential career in neuroimaging. 

Teaching in our institute is based on constructive alignment, meaning “bringing into alignment the predetermined competencies, the learning and teaching activities, and the assessment types.”. The competencies of our course in biomedical sciences “Structural Aspects of the Central Nervous System” (40-50 students), contain a.o. “To link anatomy of brain sections to medical imaging (MRI)”, and ”To have insight and can use neuroimaging software”. 

User-friendly access to neuroimaging software packages is key, since (bio-)medical sciences students have limited experience with scientific software, nor with programming or scripting. A key requirement is therefore a graphical user interface for the software. In this work we demonstrate the use of centrally managed computing infrastructure to host Neurodesk for teaching purposes and report on our, and the students experiences.

Methods

The students are taught brain anatomy through lectures and guided dissection practicals, and assessed with spotter tests, that require students to interpret anatomical specimens. Neuroimaging is taught through lectures on structural MRI, functional MRI, diffusion MRI and coregistration, guided seminars, and assessed with a fiber tracking assignment. Secondly, the students complete a 2-day project involving a live task fMRI session with multiple subjects, and 1-day workshop on fMRI data preprocessing.

Guided Neuroimaging Seminars. One of the fundamental problems with neuroimaging software is to get it installed and running on a local PC. Students have their own laptops with a variety of operating systems, limited memory and drive space. Neuroimaging software is usually developed for Linux/Unix environments, which may be a hurdle for non-technical students.

Neurodesk^1 [https://neurodeslk.org] is a solution to these issues. It is a containerised environment, making neuroimaging tools available without having to install software locally. By using the supercomputing infrastructure of the Vlaams Supercomputer Center VSC (as opposed to students using their laptops) there is no worry anymore about laptops crashing or students panicking about losing their work - only a decent internet connection is required. User accounts on VSC are created by the Ghent University HPC team, based on the student enrollment. The students interact with Neurodesk through a web browser, thanks to the Open OnDemand tool [https://www.openondemand.org] offered by VSC that allows users to interact with supercomputer clusters within a web browser. 10GB of storage is provided per student, and data can be uploaded, copied and moved in an interactive session of the supercomputer cluster (fig 1). 

The DW-MRI seminar is built around exercises, loosely based on^2 and using publicly available data^3. The students get a preprocessed dataset, and are asked to create a mask, calculate the diffusion tensor and FA map, estimate the response function, calculate FODs, perform whole brain fiber tracking and track the corpus callosum using MRtrix3^4. The fiber tracking assignment is designed to make the student familiar with the practicalities of fiber tracking. Goal is to explore the differences in fiber tracking quality between preprocessed DTI and CSD whole brain tractography data. The students are asked to tract two fiber bundles based on recommendations given in literature, with the tckgen and tckedit commands of MRtrix3.

The structural MRI and fMRI guided sessions are based on the Example Boxes provided by https://www.neuroimagingprimers.org/. Students are guided through working with brain atlases, loading segmentation results data in fsleyes^5, and exploring preprocessed fMRI data an statistical maps in FSL FEAT^5. 

During the 2 day workshop, in the first day a standard, 30s block design finger tapping experiment was performed on 3 healthy volunteers on a Siemens PrismaFit. 160 volumes were acquired with a TR of 1.5s. Left hand and right hand finger tapping was alternated. A 3D MPRAGE was acquired for anatomical reference. 

On the second day, students are given the NIfti converted T1 and fMRI data and now are instructed to figure out how to preprocess and analyze the task-based fMRI datasets from the three subjects. 

Results

For each topic (structural MRI, fMRI and DWI), within one 1.5h seminar, students can complete the exercises. The main questions students ask are about commands and moving data around in a terminal. The assignment results are beyond expectation; most students have produced fiber tracts without many spurious tracts, and that are anatomically correct, based on knowledge gained in the anatomy classes. Students use include and exclude operations with self-drawn regions of interest and find out first-hand how difficult it is to produce high quality results. None of the students failed the assignment.

For the 2 day workshop, students reported that the experience was realistic and useful. Students were especially astonished by the complexity of analyses that were not prepared beforehand by the supervisors, as was the case in the neuroimaging seminars.

Conclusion

Neurodesk combined with Open Ondemand on a supercomputing cluster revolutionizes acces to advanced neuroimaging software in a containerised environment, accessible with a web browser. Within one hour, a complete novice can analyse datasets and display MRI data in a graphical user environment.

Student's feedback is very positive; it gives them the opportunity to experience first hand what neuroimaging analyses really involves. Most students in biomedical sciences at our institute are relatively unfamiliar with use of a terminal, inspecting data, and interpreting workflows.  

In the near future, a modified and extended version of the fiber tracking part of this course will be used to train neurosurgeons and neuroradiologists in white matter anatomy and tractography. The request was made after demonstrating the platform to this clinical audience.

More advanced courses also benefit as neuroimaging pipelines and statistical software are also available on https://neurodesk.org/example-notebooks/intro.html. Research projects can use the power of supercomputers to speed up analysis on large datasets with Neurodesk. As the environment is containerised, we foresee future implementation. in a hospital environment, which combined with an XNAT instance, can open up powerful and safe analytical and teaching tools to a clinical audience.
