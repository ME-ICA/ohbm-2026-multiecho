Introduction

Primary open-angle glaucoma is a progressive retinal disease resulting in anterograde degeneration in the primary visual pathway.1 A notable feature of the optic nerve is its retinotopic organization2; glaucomatous loss of sensitivity in a specific part of the visual field is reflected in structural loss in a specific section of the optic nerve corresponding to that part of the visual field. If we could quantify this structural loss in vivo using diffusion MRI, this could result in novel biomarkers for disease progression and treatment response. Here, we present a method to do so, based on a diffusion MRI analysis technique called fixel-based analysis (FBA). FBA provides biologically interpretable metrics of white matter, such as fiber density and cross-section (FDC).3 In this work, we present a method for mapping FDC values onto the cross-section of the optic nerve. By comparing the maps of participants with glaucoma to those of controls, cross-sectional degeneration patterns may be revealed.

Methods

We investigated diffusion MRI data of 17 participants with glaucoma and 21 controls similar in age and sex.4 Acquisitions were performed on a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3T MRI scanner. The main acquisition parameters for this study were a voxel size of 2.0 mm isotropic; b-values of 1000 and 2500 s/mm²; 64 directions per shell; and three b0 images. To perform fixel-based analysis, we applied the recommended pipeline in MRtrix3,3 using a customized mask that included the optic nerves. In all participants, we performed cross-sectional mapping in the intraorbital part of the optic nerve. Constructing cross-sectional maps of the optic nerve is a multi-step process, as shown in Figure 1. Firstly, we performed tractography on each participant, seeding from the optic nerve head and tracking until the optic canal (Figure 1b). Secondly, we constructed a core streamline by averaging the tractogram’s streamline coordinates along the optic nerve (Figure 1c). Finally, to generate cross-sectional slices, we created perpendicular planes at multiple positions along the core streamline (Figure 1d) and sampled the neighboring FDC values onto these planes (Figure 1e). We represented these cross-sectional slices as discretized polar plots to allow for a group-wise comparison (Figure 1f). In this study, we used a Mann-Whitney U-test with false discovery rate correction to compare FDC values across the two groups. 

Results

The results of the group comparisons are shown in Figure 2. Due to the relatively low spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio of the scans, patient-specific degeneration patterns were not observed, but average group comparisons were possible. Compared to healthy controls, the glaucoma group showed reductions of FDC along the majority of the intraorbital optic nerve, as reflected by the p-value maps in Figure 2. Within the cross-sectional slices, a circular area of significant reduction (pFDR < 0.05) can be observed. The shape and size of this area change along the optic nerve and reductions are less pronounced at the most anterior and posterior parts. At the center of the intraorbital optic nerve, the circular area with significant reductions reaches a radius of 3 mm. Moreover, we observed greater differences in the inferior-temporal region of the cross-section, as shown in Figure 2a and 2b. 

Conclusion

Applying cross-sectional mapping to the optic nerve revealed group differences within the study population that align with clinical expectations based on conventional methods. The vast majority of participants with glaucoma were in the early stages of the disease. The fact that the clearest changes were seen in the inferior-temporal part of the optic nerve fits in what is to be expected in glaucoma, as the thickest retinal nerve fiber layer is found in that region. Interestingly, the significant FDC reductions were clearly localized within the optic nerve only, showing the robustness of our mapping technique. We expect that our method can observe patient-specific degeneration patterns if it were to be applied to higher-quality, higher-resolution data acquired at ultra-high field strengths (7T), or at 3T systems equipped with higher-strength gradients, which we plan to test in the near future. Finally, while in the present study we applied our cross-sectional mapping technique to the retinotopically organized optic nerve, in principle it could be applied to any white matter tract of interest. We demonstrated that our cross-sectional mapping of the intraorbital optic nerve is a promising approach to quantify clinically meaningful white matter changes in individuals with glaucoma. Our non-invasive method can provide information beyond what current clinical examinations can provide to capture neuroplasticity. This, in turn, may help towards providing a more accurate prognosis and fine-grained evaluation of treatment responses.
