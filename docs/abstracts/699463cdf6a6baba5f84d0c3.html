Introduction

Pooling diffusion MRI (dMRI) data across studies and sites holds the potential to boost statistical power and enable analyses of population level variability and normative trajectories. However, differences in acquisition protocols and scanners introduce non-biological variability that can obscure true microstructural effects and limit the sensitivity of pooled analyses.^1 Diffusion protocols vary substantially across studies due to competing constraints on scan time, hardware capabilities, and scientific priorities, and full standardization is often impractical. However, such between-protocol variability is an additional source of noise in multi-cohort data analyses. While scanner related effects have been widely studied, the extent to which pooling large datasets with different acquisition protocols alone including differences in b-values, angular sampling, and resolution drives variability in diffusion tensor (DTI) and kurtosis metrics (DKI) remains insufficiently characterized.

Here we aim to quantify the between-protocol variability to (i) guide data selection for the most sensitive pooled analysis and (ii) provide pointers for optimal design of multi-site studies. We acquired a unique dataset of healthy adults scanned with multiple multishell diffusion protocols, previously included in major public neuroimaging studies. The dataset complements previous efforts such as ON-Harmony.^2 Indeed, by holding scanner, subject population, and processing pipeline constant, we isolate the effects of acquisition protocol differences on common dMRI metrics. 

Methods

Data: Thirty-five healthy volunteers (17 Male, 18 Female, mean age = 43.71 ± 18.51 years; range = 23-80 years) underwent brain dMRI on a 3T Siemens Prisma MR scanner after signing consent. Each participant was scanned using seven different diffusion MRI acquisition protocols (Figure 1) within a single imaging session, enabling direct within-subject comparison of protocol-induced variability while controlling for biological differences. The acquisition protocols were selected to represent the diffusion imaging schemes employed in large-scale neuroimaging initiatives. Protocols include those defined for the Human Connectome Project on Aging (HCPa) as well as HCP study on development, Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study, Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) the UK Biobank, NYU’s Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center (ADRC), and two NYU Langone Health protocols that were used to scan 8000 clinical patients and research volunteers, referred to as MESO TE=72 and MESO TE=96, the latter acquired twice to assess baseline variability.

Preprocessing: All data were preprocessed using the DESIGNER pipeline including denoising, normalization, and correction for Gibbs ringing, Rician Bias, eddy currents, EPI distortion and motion artifacts.^3 DTI estimation using b=0 and b=1000 s/mm2 (1500 s/mm2 for the HCPa protocol) was performed to extract DTI maps including fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD) maps. Diffusion kurtosis imaging (mean kurtosis (MK), radial kurtosis (RK), and axial kurtosis (AK)) metrics were derived from the kurtosis tensor through angular averaging of the dimensionless cumulant tensor, rather than directional kurtosis, as it results in fewer outliers in parametric maps. All b-values were included in computation of DKI metrics. Regional diffusion metrics were extracted from 21 white matter regions defined by the JHU-ICBM white matter atlas Twenty-one white-matter regions were selected from the JHU-ICBM atlas. Selected JHU regions chosen based on association to early and late myelination in aging as well as those affected in aging neurological disorders including Alzheimer’s disease (See legend in Figure 2). Atlas labels were registered to each subject's native diffusion space using symmetric diffeomorphic normalization (SyN) implemented with ANTs. Mean values for each diffusion metric were computed within each atlas region for every protocol and every subject.

Analysis: The coefficient of variation (CoV) was calculated across protocols for each diffusion metric within each region. For a given subject and region, cross-protocol CoV was defined as the standard deviation of regional mean values across the seven acquisition protocols, divided by their mean, expressed as a percentage. A greedy algorithm approach was used to assess the cumulative impact of combining data from different acquisition protocols. Beginning with a reference protocol (MESO TE=96), additional protocols were sequentially added in the order that minimized the resulting cross-protocol CoV at each step. At each addition, the cross-protocol CoV was recalculated across all included protocols. 

Results

Figure 2 illustrates the variability in diffusion metrics across protocols in large multi-site neuroimaging studies. In Figure 2a, the CoV is computed across all seven protocols for each DTI and WDKI metric. The Manhattan plot displays the cross-protocol CoV for the average diffusion metric within 21 selected JHU white matter regions for each subject, including smaller regions with high variability, such as the fornix. Median cross-protocol CoV values calculated across the selected JHU regions are noted above the metric labels. These median values range from 3.75% to 7.13% for DTI metrics and 4.23% to 4.98% for DKI metrics.

In Figure 2b, the cross-protocol CoV (all protocols) from the Manhattan plot is illustrated regionally within the corresponding JHU-ICMB white matter regions for three selected diffusion metrics: FA, MD, MK. Each JHU tract displays the median cross-protocol CoV within each region, across subjects for the specified metric, with CoV represented on a color scale: white indicating low variability (0%) and dark red indicating high variability (>10%).

In a subsample taken from within 7 JHU regions (CST-L, CST-R, GCC, PLIC-L, PLIC-R, SLF-L, SLF-R) the greedy algorithm consistently selected for MD the addition of the HCPa protocol to have the largest single-step increase in CoV, with HCPa as the 7th introductory protocol across the subsampled regions. Similarly, for MK, the ABCD protocol was consistently the 7th introductory protocol across the subsampled JHU regions. On the other hand, stepwise increasing order in FA showed a more steady increase in protocols that varied across hemispheres and regions. This is illustrated in Figure 2c for the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) in the right hemisphere as a representative white matter region. The analysis begins with the MESO TE=96 protocol and sequentially adds other protocols, sorted by their ability to minimize variability. Green and blue dashed horizontal lines represent test-retest variability, which is 0.64% for FA, 0.25% for MD, and 0.79% for MK. CoV for FA increased to 3.31% when all seven protocols were included. MD, which initially demonstrated the lowest test-retest variability, rose to 3.88% with all protocols. MK exhibited the highest cross-protocol variability, reaching 4.35% with all protocols included. Across all metrics, the inclusion of the HCPa protocol produced the largest single-step increase in CoV. MD CoV more than tripled (from 1.16% to 3.88%) and MK CoV nearly doubled (from 1.72% to 3.43%) upon its introduction. The addition of the ABCD protocol maintained CoV below 2% for FA and MD but resulted in the largest increase in variability for MK, rising from 3.43% to 4.35%. 

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that diffusion metrics vary across imaging protocols, potentially affecting sensitivity to white matter microstructural differences in pooled analyses. Our results indicate that various acquisition parameters drive the cross protocol variability in diffusion metrics, with b-value emerging as the dominant factor, particularly for MD. Protocols with differing diffusion weightings, such as those approximating HCPa, showed variability patterns consistent with prior evidence showing that MD is strongly influenced by b-value selection.^4

Variability was found to be both region and metric dependent, with smaller white matter regions showing increased sensitivity to resolution and partial-volume effects, and kurtosis metrics possibly exhibiting greater susceptibility to noise and diffusion weighting differences. These findings underscore the need for harmonization of imaging protocols, particularly fixing the b-value to 1000 s/mm2 for DTI, considering its wide use, when pooling datasets to detect subtle biological effects. 

Overall, we observe that median CoV of diffusion metrics across protocols is below 5%, particularly when excluding HCPa. A median CoV of approximately 5% was previously observed in reproducibility assessments of cortical volumes, derived from structural MRI.^2 Therefore, we believe that dMRI provides an avenue to probe white matter microstructure with a precision as good as other quantitative MR-derived brain features. These results are in agreement with the ON-Harmony study that further includes variability in imaging hardware.^2

Future work employing more systematic acquisition manipulations will be critical for identifying protocol combinations that maximize compatibility and sensitivity in large scale and normative dMRI analyses. We will also evaluate whether harmonization tools such as ComBat might reduce between-protocol variability of dMRI metrics.^5
