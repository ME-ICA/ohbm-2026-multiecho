Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a complex condition influenced by genetic, environmental, and biological factors1. A consistent neurobiological finding in MDD is reduced white matter (WM) integrity2, which has been linked to neuroinflammatory processes. Systemic inflammation, often elevated in MDD, is associated with decreased WM volume and microstructural alterations3. Early-life adversity, such as childhood maltreatment (CM), is also implicated in both neuroinflammation and WM disruption. One contributor to this inflammatory profile may be B cell dysregulation. In MDD, alterations in B cell subsets suggest immune imbalance4. Memory B cells, a long-lived subset, play a key role in immunological memory by enabling rapid antibody responses and may also contribute to inflammatory mechanisms relevant to MDD-related brain changes5.

This study aims to investigate the relationship between switched memory B cells, childhood adverse events, and white matter microstructure.

Methods

We analyzed data from 126 age- and sex-matched participants with major depressive disorder (MDD) and healthy controls (HC) from the FOR2107 consortium. Participants were grouped by childhood maltreatment status, defined as scores above validated thresholds on two Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) subscales, resulting in four groups: HC without maltreatment (HCCTQ-, N=33), HC with maltreatment (HCCTQ+, N=25), MDD without maltreatment (MDDCTQ−, N=36), and MDD with maltreatment (MDDCTQ+, N=32).

Peripheral blood samples were collected before or after MRI acquisition, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation. PBMCs were cryopreserved and stored in liquid nitrogen until analysis. B-cell subsets were characterized by flow cytometry following standard protocols. B cells were identified as CD19⁺/CD20⁺ cells after exclusion of debris, doublets, T cells (CD3⁺), and monocytes (CD14⁺). Subsets were defined based on CD1d, CD5, CD21, CD24, CD27, CD38, and IgD expression. B-cell measures were expressed as percentages of total PBMCs and related to MRI-derived metrics. 

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) was used to assess white matter microstructure. Fractional anisotropy (FA) was analyzed using tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) and related to switched memory B cell counts. Group comparisons were conducted across diagnosis and maltreatment categories. Region-of-interest (ROI) analyses focused on the corpus callosum and the superior longitudinal fasciculus (ptfce-FWE < .05), supplemented by an exploratory whole-brain analysis (ptfce-FWE < .10).

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 29.0 and FSL 6.0.1. ANCOVAs were conducted for switched memory B cells, with cell proportions as the dependent variable, and group (HC vs. MDD), maltreatment (CTQ− vs. CTQ+), and their interaction as predictors (p < .05). Voxelwise analyses of fractional anisotropy (FA) were performed using a general linear model in FSL, including group, maltreatment, and their interaction as predictors. Tract-Based Spatial Statistics (TBSS) and Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) with 5000 permutations were used to correct for multiple comparisons (FWE p < .05). Significant clusters were localized using the JHU ICBM-DTI-81 atlas, and mean FA values were extracted for follow-up analyses.

Results

Switched memory B cell concentrations did not differ between individuals with major depressive disorder (MDD) and healthy controls (HC) (p = .778). A significant main effect of childhood adversity was observed (p = .004, η² = .067), whereas the interaction between diagnosis and childhood adversity did not reach statistical significance (p = .119, η² = .02). Participants with a history childhood maltreatment showed lower switched memory B cell proportions across groups. Fractional anisotropy (FA) in the corpus callosum was negatively associated with switched memory B cell counts (K= 226 voxels, ptfce-FWE = .038, coordinates x = 1.33, y = −30.4, z = 21.6). Observed FA reductions were located in the body (55.6%) and splenium (44.4%) of the corpus callosum. Exploratory whole-brain analyses revealed group differences in FA between HC and MDD (ptfce-FWE = .072) and between participants with and without childhood maltreatment experiences (ptfce-FWE = .081). No significant effects were observed in the superior longitudinal fasciculus, and no interactions between diagnosis and treatment were detected.

Conclusion

Switched memory B cell counts were negatively associated with white matter microstructure in the corpus callosum, independent of diagnostic group or maltreatment status. However, differences in B cell concentrations were observed between maltreated and non-maltreated individuals within the MDD group, suggesting a potential immunological pathway contributing to the neurobiological heterogeneity of depression. These findings underscore the need for future longitudinal and mechanistic studies to further explore immune-related processes as potential targets for novel therapeutic strategies.
