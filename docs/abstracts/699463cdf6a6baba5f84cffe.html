Introduction

Major depressive disorder is increasingly understood as a condition that involves widespread brain network disruptions1. The marked clinical and biological heterogeneity of depression2 makes it challenging to identify robust and reproducible neural signatures across independent cohorts acquired in different countries, scanners, and recruitment contexts. Polyconnectomic scoring3 (PCS) offers a framework for mapping whole-brain connectivity disease fingerprints into an individual-level score that reflects how strongly a subject’s connectome aligns with disorder-related patterns. We derived reproducible whole-brain dysconnectivity patterns for depression and examined how these disturbances retain clinical relevance across populations training a polyconnectomic score model in an Asian cohort and evaluating its performance in an independent European sample.

Methods

Polyconnectomic scores for major depressive disorder were derived by estimating a disorder-specific pattern of resting-state functional connectivity alterations in the open-access Japanese Strategic Research Program for the Promotion of Brain Science dataset (824 controls, 229 patients)4, controlling for age, sex, in-scanner motion, and site. Each participant’s resting-state functional connectivity profile was next estimated in an independent German cohort (923 controls, 694 patients)5 and weighted by the disease pattern, yielding a PCS per individual reflecting how strongly the patient’s connectome aligned with the depression-related pattern. PCS were evaluated in relation to diagnosis, remission status, overall symptom severity, and individual symptoms, with all analyses controlling for age, sex, in-scanner motion, and site. Sensitivity analyses were applied thresholding connections to include the most strongly altered connections from the discovery cohort (p < 0.1, p < 0.01, p < 0.001) to test whether major depressive disorder reflects distributed or focal network disruptions.

Results

Patients showed significantly higher polyconnectomic scores for major depressive disorder than controls (z = 5.1, p < 0.001), with classification performance above chance (AUC = 0.69) and unique variance explained beyond covariates (ΔR2 = 1.3%). Polyconnectomic scoring achieved its best performance when all functional connections were included, and accuracy declined when increasingly strict p-value thresholds were applied to select connections from the discovery dataset (p < 0.1, < 0.01, < 0.001: ΔR2 = 1.3%, 1.1%, 0.8%), indicating that major depressive disorder involves widespread, whole-brain functional dysregulation rather than effects restricted to a small subset of connections. Polyconnectomic scores tracked clinical state, with acutely depressed patients showing significantly elevated scores relative to controls (Hedges’ g = 0.32, pFDR < 0.001) and a trend toward higher scores than fully remitted patients (Hedges’ g = 0.18, pFDR = 0.073). Partially remitted patients also showed higher scores than controls (Hedges’ g = 0.27, pFDR = 0.006). Among patients, higher scores were associated with greater current symptom severity (r = 0.09, p = 0.022). At the level of individual symptoms, the strongest association was observed for loss of pleasure (r = 0.12, pFDR = 0.041), with additional nominal associations for suicidal ideation (r = 0.08, punc = 0.030), agitation (r = 0.09, punc = 0.024), indecisiveness (r = 0.10, punc = 0.008), and loss of appetite (r = 0.08, punc = 0.045). Polyconnectomic scoring models trained on bipolar disorder and schizophrenia showed no associations with depressive symptoms, underscoring the clinical specificity of the depression-derived score. 

Conclusion

These findings show that major depressive disorder involves distributed connectivity alterations that generalizes across populations. A functional dysconnectivity signature of depression derived in a discovery sample remained predictive and clinically meaningful in an independent cohort collected at a different site, with different scanners, protocols, and demographic characteristics. Polyconnectomic scoring provides a powerful and generalizable approach for quantifying whole-brain disturbances in depression and linking macroscale connectivity patterns to clinical variability.
