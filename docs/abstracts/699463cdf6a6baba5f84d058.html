Introduction

Understanding large-scale brain dynamics remains a central challenge in neuroscience.^1,2 Functional MRI captures whole-brain activity over time, yet its high dimensionality and nonlinear interactions hinder interpretable dynamical modelling.^2 

Traditional linear techniques, such as PCA or dynamic mode decomposition, offer limited expressivity, whereas deep nonlinear architectures often lack interpretability and control-theoretic grounding.^3 

The Koopman operator framework provides a principled bridge between these paradigms by representing nonlinear dynamical systems through an equivalent linear evolution in a suitably constructed latent space.^4 

In this work, we leverage Koopman theory to extract low-dimensional linear dynamics from fMRI data and reveal stroke-related alterations in latent representations of whole-brain activity.

Methods

We designed a Koopman auto-encoder (KAE) that jointly learns a nonlinear embedding and a state-dependent linear dynamics model. The encoder-decoder pair maps fMRI BOLD signals from an M-dimensional source space to latent coordinates z, while a state-dependent linear operator K(z(t)) approximates the local temporal evolution through the recurrence z(t+1) = K(z(t)) z(t). 

Conceptually, this defines a finite-dimensional family of linear operators that locally approximate the Koopman action on nonlinear dynamics.^4 The training objective combines reconstruction, prediction, and linearity enforcement losses^4,5, enabling identification of both the state-dependent operator K(z(t)) and its local spectral properties (eigenvalue decay rates μ and frequencies ω), thereby capturing time-varying stability characteristics of the latent dynamics.

Training was performed on synthetic oscillatory systems and on BOLD data from healthy controls and stroke patients, parcellated using the Schaefer-200 atlas.^6 

For each subject, latent trajectories and their associated operator fields K(z(t)) were analysed to extract eigenvalues (real part μ, imaginary part ω) , eigenfunctions, and controllability metrics. Linear control analysis in the latent space employed the controllability Gramian and energy-optimal control profiles, quantifying how easily each latent mode can be influenced over time.

Results

On simulated data, the KAE accurately recovered the true dynamical spectrum of the underlying systems, identifying Koopman eigenvalues consistent with theoretical predictions. Applied to resting-state fMRI, the model achieved high reconstruction accuracy and stable short-horizon predictions, outperforming conventional auto-encoders of comparable size.

Latent trajectories revealed interpretable oscillatory modes associated with large-scale cortical networks. Group-level comparisons showed significant differences between healthy and stroke subjects in Koopman eigenvalue distributions (μ and ω) and latent controllability metrics, indicating altered dynamical regimes and reduced control flexibility in specific latent dimensions.

These findings demonstrate that stroke induces a measurable shift in the linearised structure of whole-brain dynamics, which is captured robustly by the Koopman framework. Visualisation of the learned latent dynamics revealed coherent low-dimensional manifolds with smooth temporal organisation, supporting the interpretability and stability of the embedding.

Conclusion

The proposed Koopman-based generative model unifies nonlinear representation learning with linear control analysis, providing a mechanistically interpretable description of whole-brain dynamics. By enforcing linear evolution in the latent space, the framework enables the use of control-theoretic tools to characterise how latent modes can be modulated over time.^4 Stroke-related alterations in Koopman spectra and controllability metrics indicate reduced dynamical flexibility and impaired network reconfiguration.

These findings demonstrate that Koopman-linear embeddings can reveal hidden organisational principles of brain activity, bridging deep learning and theoretical neuroscience. Future work will extend the framework to dynamic functional connectivity, longitudinal recovery trajectories, and control-based interventions targeting specific latent dynamical regimes.
