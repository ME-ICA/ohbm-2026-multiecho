Introduction

In the search for robust biomarkers in epidemiological and clinical research and the development of effective machine learning models, complex multivariate confounding remains a critical methodological challenge. Often, demographic and lifestyle variables interact in non-linear ways and can thus obscure true associations such as those between brain and behavior, particularly in heterogeneous conditions like depression where inter-individual variability is high. Typical approaches to confound control adjust for a small number of individual variables. Not only does this pose the challenge of having to decide which variables to include as confounders, the approach also fails to account for the complex intercorrelations between confounders such as age, sex and lifestyle factors.1 Therefore, in the present work we aim to gain a better understanding of the confound-space by applying an unsupervised clustering approach that identifies subpopulations of individuals who share similar combinations of demographic and lifestyle characteristics, subsequently called archetypes. To overcome the common challenge of validating data-driven algorithms that do not enable comparison with a ground truth, our present work combines unsupervised machine learning for archetype detection with a novel validation approach that combines clustering with a supervised classification to test both stability and generalisability of lifestyle-to-cluster mapping. 

The present approach may not only aid the selection of confounding variables in future studies, it could also be used to uncover more homogenous subpopulations of individuals. In future studies, this can be leveraged in neuroimaging analyses to address the heterogeneity of both healthy individuals and patients, and facilitate the detection of brain associations with psychiatric disorders, potentially revealing differential neural correlates across lifestyle-derived subpopulations and increasing statistical power to detect them.

Methods

To identify archetypes of individuals who share similar combinations of potential confounding variables, we utilized data from 100,000 UK Biobank participants, split into test (n=50,000) and validation (n=50,000) datasets. 67 demographic and lifestyle variables were selected, encompassing socioeconomic status (e.g. education, income, deprivation index), health behaviors (e.g. physical activity, diet, smoking, alcohol consumption), employment characteristics, psychosocial factors, and basic demographics.

Subgroup identification was performed as a combination of the non-linear dimensionality reduction algorithm UMAP2 (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction) and the hierarchical density-based clustering algorithm HDBSCAN.3 The combination of UMAP and HDBSCAN was chosen for several reasons. For one, UMAP in combination with a Gower distance matrix is able to account for a mix of numerical and categorical data, which our UK Biobank sample consisted of. Secondly, as we could not a priori expect clusters within our sample to be of the same size or spherical, the fact that HDBSCAN as a density-based algorithm can accommodate non-spherical, differently sized clusters within the same dataset was essential. 

To validate our clustering solution, we developed a hybrid approach combining unsupervised and supervised learning. Using the validation cohort, we again performed clustering as described above. Then, we trained a gradient boosting classifier (XGBoost) on the validation cohort with the 67 lifestyle and demographic variables as features and cluster labels as targets. This classifier was then applied to predict cluster labels in the test cohort. Model performance was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with high AUC values indicating stable, well-separated, and generalizable clusters. Feature importance scores were extracted from the classifier to identify which variables are most informative for the classifier to distinguish between clusters.

Results

The clustering pipeline identified three robust clusters (n_C1 = 30633, n_C2 = 18137, n_C3 = 1223, unclustered = 7). Supervised classification validation yielded high performance: mean AUC of 1.00 (SD: 0.00) for both Cluster 1 and Cluster 2, and AUC of 0.77 (SD: 0.24) for Cluster 3, confirming high stability and generalizability of the clustering solution.

Feature importance analysis revealed employment status as the primary differentiating factor. Exploring variable distributions within each cluster revealed a complex triadic interaction between employment, age and sex. Cluster 1 consisted predominantly of middle-aged, employed individuals with balanced sex distribution (mean age: 52.2 years, 51.2% female). Cluster 2 comprised primarily non-working, elderly individuals with balanced sex distribution (mean age: 62.5 years, 53% female). Cluster 3 consisted of non-working, middle-aged, predominantly female individuals (93.5% female, mean age: 50.2 years). Due to its relatedness to employment, we furthermore compared household income distributions between the clusters. Cluster 1 exhibited a bell shaped distribution with a mode at £31,000-£51,000, Cluster 2 showed a distribution that peaks at the minimum income (<£18,000) and decreases steadily towards higher incomes, while Cluster 3 showed the most uniform distribution, with a mode at £52,000-£100,000. Lastly, when comparing the mean values of all numerical or ordinal variables between the clusters, small to moderate differences were seen in variables related to free time (time spent outdoors, driving, using the computer or watching TV), health behaviour (smoking and alcohol consumption) and psychosocial factors, specifically the ability to confide in others. 

Conclusion

Our findings show that it is possible to leverage unsupervised clustering to define archetypical groups of individuals based on patterns of lifestyle and demographic features which reflect complex interactions of potential confounds. This indicates that clustering algorithms may be a promising approach to tackle the multi-confound problem in epidemiological and clinical research by uncovering more homogenous subpopulations of individuals in a way that reduces the complexity of non-linear confound interactions. 

The finding that XGBoost’s feature importance measure revealed employment as the most informative factor for the classification of clusters implicates it as a key variable differentiating people in the present cohort, outweighing even the effects of age and sex, variables that are commonly considered as confounding factors in epidemiological studies. The fact that a further exploration of the variable distributions in each cluster revealed a three-way pattern of similarities between employment, age and sex backs our assumption that within the confound-space, complex interactions between a broad range of lifestyle and demographic factors exists. This likely reflects the complexity of human life in which employment is strongly interconnected with many other major factors of daily life, as further supported by the differences in health, free-time and psychosocial variables between clusters. 

Our validation approach of combining unsupervised and supervised learning provides evidence for the reliability and generalizability of these archetypes, exhibiting a high performance across all three clusters. The additional benefit of this approach, the ability to derive feature importance scores from the classifier, opens a window into the otherwise black box of nonlinear dimensionality reduction and clustering algorithms, which in our study revealed the major influence of employment on cluster differentiation.

In subsequent work we aim to build on these findings by combining our clustering framework with analyses of brain associations with psychiatric disorders in the UK Biobank, using it to guide our choice of confounding variables to consider as well as investigating whether additional insights and larger effect sizes can be achieved by reducing demographic heterogeneity through within-archetype as opposed to whole sample analyses.
