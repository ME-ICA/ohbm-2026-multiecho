Introduction

In major psychiatric disorders, including anxiety disorders (AXD), major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (BD), and psychotic spectrum disorders (SSD), educational and occupational attainment has consistently been described as a protective factor against unfavorable disease trajectories and cognitive impairment [1,2]. Cognitive deficits represent a core feature across these disorders and substantially contribute to functional disability and long-term outcome. Importantly, both cognitive impairment and an unfavorable course of illness have been linked to alterations in white matter fiber microstructure, suggesting a shared neurobiological substrate underlying clinical and cognitive dysfunction [3,4].

Educational and occupational attainment may therefore not only exert protective effects on a phenomenological level—such as symptom severity, cognitive performance, and functional outcome—but may also buffer adverse neurobiological processes associated with disease progression. In this context, education could contribute to maintaining white matter integrity and thereby support cognitive functioning across diagnostic boundaries. Such a neurobiological protective effect would make educational and occupational attainment a promising target for preventive and resilience-oriented strategies in psychiatry.

Furthermore, we hypothesize that the protective association between education and white matter integrity becomes increasingly apparent with advancing age, when cumulative exposure to disease-related stressors and neurobiological burden accelerates cognitive decline. Investigating these associations in a transdiagnostic framework may therefore provide important insights into shared protective mechanisms across major psychiatric disorders.

Methods

The present study included N=1424 participants from the transdiagnostic Marburg–Münster Affective Disorders Cohort Study (MACS, 5), comprising patients with anxiety disorders (AXD; N=141), major depressive disorder (MDD; N=468), bipolar disorder (BD; N=99), psychotic spectrum disorders (SSD; n=69), and healthy controls (HC; N=647). Diagnoses were established using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV-TR administered by trained raters. Healthy controls had no current or lifetime psychiatric disorder, whereas patients met criteria for a current or lifetime diagnosis. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the local ethics committees.

Educational and occupational attainment was assessed based on highest educational degree, current occupation, and household income. Income was transformed into deciles to ensure comparability with ordinal educational and occupational measures. An overall attainment score was calculated as the z-standardized sum of these variables. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using years of education and income separately.

Cognitive functioning was assessed using a comprehensive neuropsychological test battery covering executive function, attention, memory, working memory, and verbal fluency. A global cognitive performance factor was derived using principal component analysis based on established procedures.

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data were acquired on 3T MRI scanners at both study sites using harmonized protocols. Preprocessing and analysis were performed using FSL, including correction for motion and eddy current artifacts, brain extraction, and diffusion tensor fitting. Fractional anisotropy (FA) was used as the primary measure of white matter microstructure. The analyses were conducted using Tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS). Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) was used to correct for multiple comparisons.   Alpha-inflation was corrected using the family-wise error (FWE; p<.05) of permutated input data (5000 permutations) of the maximum TFCE scores.

Statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS and FSL. In a first step, a general linear model (GLM) was conducted with FA as the dependent variable and diagnosis (HC vs. AXD vs. MDD vs. BD vs. SSD) as the independent variable. Age, age², sex, TIV, and dummy-coded site/scanner variables were included as nuisance regressors. This model tested for diagnosis-related differences in white matter integrity across the skeleton. In a second step, a GLM was conducted with educational and occupational attainment, diagnosis, and their interaction term (attainment × diagnosis) as independent variables, and FA as the dependent variable. The same nuisance variables (age, age², sex, TIV, site/scanner) were included. This analysis examined whether higher educational and occupational attainment was associated with higher FA values and whether this association varied across diagnostic groups. In a final step, the interaction term between age and educational/occupational attainment (age × attainment) was included to examine whether the protective effect of educational and occupational attainment on white matter integrity changes across the lifespan.

Results

Analysis of cognitive performance revealed a significant main effect of diagnosis (F(4,1411)=25.69, p<.001, η²=.068) and a significant main effect of educational and occupational attainment (F(1,1411)=49.99, p<.001, η²=.034). The diagnosis × attainment interaction was not significant (F(4,1411)=1.90, p=.108, η²=.004), indicating a positive association between attainment and cognitive performance across all diagnostic groups. Simple slope analyses confirmed this association within each group (HC: b=.161, 95% CI [.091, .231]; MDD: b=.286 [.209, .363]; BD: b=.186 [.036, .337]; SSD: b=.313 [.094, .531]; AXD: b=.268 [.112, .424]). The model explained 34.2% of the variance in cognitive performance (R²=.342). Including the age × attainment interaction revealed a significant effect (F(1,1410)=24.46, p<.001, η²=.017), indicating that educational and occupational attainment had little effect in younger participants but exerted a protective effect on cognitive performance in older individuals.

Diffusion tensor imaging analyses showed a significant main effect of diagnosis on white matter integrity, with reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) in the corpus callosum and corona radiata (ptfce-FWE=0.002, η²=.009, k=1217 voxels; peak MNI: x=9, y=28, z=11). Post-hoc contrasts revealed significantly lower FA in SSD compared to MDD (ptfce-FWE=0.004, η²=.01, k=25301), AXD (ptfce-FWE=0.005, η²=.005, k=21758), and healthy controls (ptfce-FWE<0.001, η²=.01, k=35759). Additionally, BD showed lower FA compared to AXD (ptfce-FWE=0.042, η²<.001, k=383), resulting in a transdiagnostic pattern of SSD < BD < MDD < HC < AXD.

Educational and occupational attainment showed a significant positive association with FA in the retrolenticular part of the internal capsule (ptfce-FWE=0.035, η²=.008, k=26; peak MNI: x=−27, y=−28, z=11). Post-hoc contrasts confirmed higher FA values in participants with higher attainment (ptfce-FWE=0.005, η²=.005, k=33052). The attainment × diagnosis interaction was not significant (F-contrast: ptfce-FWE=0.101). Instead, a significant attainment × age interaction was observed, indicating a stronger positive association between attainment and FA in older participants (ptfce-FWE=0.029, η²=.006, k=10384; peak MNI: x=−25, y=−10, z=18, Figure 1), spanning widespread bilateral white matter regions.

Conclusion

The present findings demonstrate that higher educational and occupational attainment is associated with better cognitive performance and higher white matter integrity across major psychiatric disorders, supporting a transdiagnostic protective effect. This extends previous evidence of pervasive cognitive impairment in bipolar disorder and major depressive disorder [1,2] by identifying educational and occupational attainment as a factor linked not only to cognitive functioning but also to underlying brain structure.

Our results align with prior work showing robust associations between white matter microstructure and cognitive performance in psychiatric populations, particularly in major depressive disorder [3,4]. By demonstrating that educational and occupational attainment relates to fractional anisotropy across widespread white matter pathways, the present study integrates cognitive and structural findings within a unified framework. The absence of attainment × diagnosis interactions suggests that these associations operate similarly across diagnostic groups, consistent with transdiagnostic models of cognitive vulnerability.

Importantly, the association between educational and occupational attainment and both cognitive performance and white matter integrity was stronger in older participants. This age-dependent pattern is compatible with the notion that educational experiences may buffer cumulative illness-related and age-related burden on brain networks supporting cognition. Longitudinal evidence linking white matter alterations to cognitive decline in depression further supports the relevance of preserved structural connectivity for maintaining cognitive function over time [4].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Educational and occupational attainment represents a proxy measure and does not capture qualitative aspects of education or lifelong learning. Moreover, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although the sample included individuals across different illness stages, longitudinal studies—particularly beginning earlier in the lifespan—are required to disentangle developmental, illness-related, and experiential contributions.

In conclusion, educational and occupational attainment is associated with preserved cognitive performance and white matter integrity across major psychiatric disorders, with effects that become more pronounced with age. These findings support a transdiagnostic reserve framework linking educational experiences to the maintenance of structural brain networks underlying cognition. Longitudinal and interventional studies are now needed to test whether enhancing education- and occupation-related exposures can modify trajectories of white matter integrity, cognition, and functional outcome.
