Introduction

Head motion has widespread, pernicious effects on all MR imaging modalities that only worsen with increased spatial resolution and higher magnetic field. Scientific and technical effort has mainly centered on  computational algorithms to correct for motion effects, and methods to measure motion. However, relatively little effort has been directed to preventing head motion occurring. This is despite the fact that this would circumvent position-dependent effects, such as variable sensitivity modulation of the receiver coil, shim changes, and spatially varying gradient performance, all of which can present challenges to retrospective motion artifact correction.  Here, we introduce a new motion-minimization device (MR MinMo) and compare its performance to high-quality cushion sets commonly used in research and clinics worldwide. As shown in Figure 1, the MR MinMo device consists of a frame which conforms to the inner surface of the MRI head coil. It is fitted with a series of foam pads and inflatables (‘modules’), mounted to most effectively and comfortably secure the participant’s head. The frame has a hinged section which allows easy entry and egress; the inflatable modules are controlled by a custom manifold, hand-pumped by the MR operator to quickly and securely position the participant. The ear inflatables accommodate different earbuds for audio presentation, as well as being able to deliver sound pneumatically.

Methods

Here, in two experiments, we assessed the ability of the device to reduce motion via measurements of motion during fMRI. (Full details can be seen in the study pre-registration: https://osf.io/a6qbu/overview). In the first experiment,  participants performed a series of stereotypical movements routinely encountered in research and clinical imaging. We used whole-head EPI (TR=800ms) to estimate head motion in 25 participants scanned using standard cushions in one half of runs, and MR MinMo in the other half. Each made 10 different body movements typical of those observed during clinical and pediatric scanning (e.g. scratching the cheek, hip raise, leg cross, talking etc.).

Results

Across movement types, head motion was reduced by MR MinMo use (see Figure 2, top panel). Using standard motion correction parameters as a metric, we found that head translations and rotations were on average smaller and more tightly distributed when using the MR MinMo device, compared to the set of standard cushions. This was true for absolute displacement measures (reflecting shifting of the head over the entire run) and framewise displacements (volume by volume changes in position). Participants also rated the MR MinMo device highly on comfort at the beginning and end of the session. 

 In the second, retrospective study, we compared head motion in two cohorts of participants who watched feature-length movies, one cohort (N=84) who were scanned with standard padding (Aliko et al. 2020), and another (N=40, Levchenko et al., 2025) who were scanned with MR MinMo. We used the same realignment-based metrics as in the active study, and again found significantly less head movement when MR MinMo was used versus standard padding (Figure 2, bottom panel). 

Conclusion

More generally, the device has been used for long (>>1 hour) functional and structural scanning sessions for several hundred child, adolescent adult healthy and clinical participants, with positive results. The ability to reduce motion sensitivity across lengthy imaging sessions will benefit structural and functional MRI, enabling deeper characterisation of function, microstructural organisation and the nuanced relationship between the two.
