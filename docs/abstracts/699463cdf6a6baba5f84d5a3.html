Introduction

Imaging cerebral blood flow (CBF) is vital for managing various neurological conditions, such as stroke and traumatic brain injury. Currently, techniques such as ASL-MRI and PET are expensive and capture only static images, overlooking dynamic changes during treatment. Noninvasive optical approaches are promising alternatives. While traditional fNIRS measures concentration without specific CBF information, diffuse correlation spectroscopy (DCS) has yet to become a practical imaging tool due to noise and cost. Recently, speckle contrast optical spectroscopy (SCOS) has attracted attention for deep-tissue blood flow imaging, offering higher signal-to-noise ratios and lower costs. By combining multiple SCOS measurements and applying models of speckle correlation propagation through the head, we can produce tomographic images called speckle contrast optical tomography (SCOT). There has been no speckle-contrast-based device that has successfully mapped the human visual cortex through hair. Past studies have shown activation in the prefrontal cortex on the forehead, a hairless area [1]. Imaging with SCOT in humans is difficult because hair blocks light, and the head's shape requires multiple viewing angles. Our recent work with multi-mode fiber (MMF) bundles, using a single source-detector setup, demonstrated the ability to transmit blood-flow-related light for speckle-contrast measurement [2]. We also confirmed that CBF can be measured with a fiber-based system [3]. In this project, we created a high-density SCOT device with MMF bundles to navigate hair and head curvature, enabling feasible data collection from visual cortex regions and supporting SCOT as a functional brain imaging method.

Methods

The HD-SCOT system (see Figure 1) includes four sources and eight detectors. Each source employs a 785nm laser (Thorlabs LD785-SEV300) connected via a 5-meter multi-mode fiber (Fiberoptics Technology 50-4256-1-REV1, NA=0.48). Detector bundles (Fiberoptics Technology 50-4255-1-REV1, NA=0.66, 50 µm core) are paired with sCMOS cameras (Basler a2A1920-160umPRO), creating eight detection channels. The optical setup uses two achromatic doublet lenses (Thorlabs AC254-045-B-ML, AC254-030-B-ML) to focus scattered light onto the cameras, ensuring efficient collection and minimal aberrations. Slight defocusing at the fiber ends [4] improved image uniformity across fibers. Measurements involved imaging on the back of the head through hair on a person during resting-state (60 seconds) and visual retinotopy (12 minutes) for three subjects. During retinotopy, subjects viewed an 8 Hz flickering checkerboard wedge in the lower right visual field to stimulate the upper left visual cortex. The 12-minute stimulus comprised 20 repetitions, each with 10 seconds of stimulus and 20 seconds of crosshair display. Phase-encoded retinotopy with a rotating flickering wedge across the visual field using SCOT will be comparable to quadrant analysis of CW-DOT retinotopy responses [5]. 

Results

In vivo measurements from the four-source and eight-detector imaging array demonstrate pulsatile blood flow through hair on the back of a human subject's head (Figure 2A, B). The mean and speckle contrast light fall-off curves display the expected decline with increasing source-detector distance. Frequency-domain analysis of measurements at first and second source-detector distances (~12.9 mm and ~28.7 mm) reveals multiple harmonics, consistent with physiological dynamics. Block-averaged SCOT reconstructions in two subjects, during stimulus-on (t = 5:15 seconds) and off periods (t = 21:26 seconds) (Figure 2C, D, E), show similar activation responses in the left visual cortex, aligned with the timing of visual stimulus presentation to each subject.

Conclusion

Our HD-SCOT system can detect pulsatile blood flow from measurements on the back of the head through hair, achieving adequate SNR in SD-pair measurements up to the third nearest-neighbor (~38 mm). Visual cortex activations are evident in block-averaged measurements in both subjects, which establishes the feasibility of validating the brain specificity of SCOT. Future steps include expanding the HD-SCOT system to an 8-source by 18-detector instrument and performing additional visual stimulus studies using counterclockwise- and clockwise-rotating phase-encoded stimuli, which produce varying responses across the visual cortex. The results of this study confirm the feasibility of bedside monitoring of cerebral dynamics and validate brain specificity in SCOT responses to visual stimuli.
