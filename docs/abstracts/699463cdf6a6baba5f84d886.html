Introduction

Conventional functional MRI (fMRI) offers high spatial resolution of hemodynamic signals but only indirectly reflects neuronal activity and lacks biochemical specificity. Functional magnetic resonance spectroscopy (fMRS) complements this limitation by quantifying dynamic changes in neurometabolites, capturing aspects of neurotransmission and energy metabolism. However, fMRS suffers from low spatial resolution and limited capacity to map regional interactions at the network level. The result is a conceptual gap between the spatial mapping of brain activity and the mechanistic understanding of the molecular processes that sustain it.

To address this, the present study aims to integrate neurometabolic and receptor-based perspectives on brain function. We hypothesize that neurometabolic fluctuations measured with fMRS are modulated by the regional distribution of neuroreceptors that orchestrate the excitatory and inhibitory (E/I) signaling balance. By employing receptor-enriched fMRI analysis, we seek to identify functional gradients that trace how molecular architecture shapes brain dynamics under varying cognitive demands. 

Methods

Healthy participants (N = 36, 25.6 ± 3.2 yo, 17 females) underwent simultaneous fMRI and fMRS1 acquisitions using a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner during resting state, 0‑Back, 1‑Back, and 2‑Back working memory conditions to parametrically modulate cognitive load. The fMRS voxel was placed in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and fMRI data were modeled with a general linear model to derive graded changes in BOLD responses with increasing working memory load (Figure 1A). Subsequent analyses were restricted to parcels showing a significant group‑level parametric effect of working‑memory load.

To investigate receptor‑specific brain activity, we applied the Receptor‑Enhanced Analysis of functional Connectivity by Targets (REACT) framework2, which integrates PET‑derived receptor maps as spatial regressors to extract receptor‑enriched BOLD signals, followed by temporal regression to generate receptor‑specific functional connectivity (FC) maps (Figure 1B). Four neuroreceptor systems were investigated: GABA‑A, Ro15, mGluR5, and NMDA, capturing E/I and ionotropic/metabotropic receptor classes.  

Inter‑receptor similarity (cosine distance) from receptor‑enriched FC maps was first computed to generate session‑specific matrices, which were embedded via diffusion mapping to derive functional gradients3. These gradients were then aligned to resting‑state references to quantify receptor‑weighted task‑induced shifts, and finally projected into a two‑dimensional neurochemical space defined by E/I and ionotropic–metabotropic axes (Figure 1C). Parcels were then clustered within each run in the 2D neurochemical space using hierarchical clustering with Ward’s linkage, with the number of clusters chosen by maximizing the average silhouette coefficient. Cluster correspondence across runs was established via a Hungarian assignment of centroids, yielding globally indexed clusters whose 3D trajectories (G1, G2, run index) were used to assess mesoscale organization and stability. Associations between receptor‑enriched FC metrics and fMRS‑derived neurometabolite concentrations (Glx, GABA+) were assessed using Spearman correlations (rₛ).

Results

Diffusion embedding of receptor‑enriched maps revealed two principal functional gradients (G). G1 (Figure 2A) captured a continuous axis from inhibitory to excitatory receptor dominance (GABA‑A/Ro15 → mGluR5/NMDA), while G2 (Figure 2B) reflected the transition from ionotropic (fast) to metabotropic (modulatory) signaling. At rest, brain regions occupied a distributed configuration with balanced E/I and ionotropic–metabotropic representation, forming a triangular geometry in the receptor space (Figure 2D). 

During cognitive task engagement, both gradients underwent a systematic reorganization. G1 showed a transient shift toward inhibitory dominance during early task stages, followed by partial restoration of E/I balance under higher cognitive demand. G2 exhibited polarization toward ionotropic extremes during 1‑back and 2‑back conditions, suggesting increased reliance on fast synaptic signaling to support working memory updating and response selection. Examination of gradient interactions revealed an inverse‑U trajectory during task phases, indicating that brain regions with intermediate E/I balance diverged toward distinct ionotropic or metabotropic modes depending on task demands. This polarization was reduced during the most cognitively challenging condition, highlighting a dynamic reintegration of neurochemical systems under sustained load. Cluster‑level analyses in the 2D neurochemical space indicated that only a small subset of mesoscale configurations could be consistently tracked across runs and that these showed marked centroid shifts, pointing to flexible rather than stationary receptor‑anchored configurations under task demands (Figure 2C). Quantitative comparison of gradient shifts demonstrated that task sessions were more similar to one another than to rest (G1: mean rs = 0.44; G2: mean rs = 0.41), confirming the reproducibility of task‑dependent reorganization. Notably, no significant associations were observed between receptor‑enriched FC metrics and fMRS‑derived neurometabolite concentrations (e.g., glutamate/glutamine (Glx), GABA+) (rs = [–0.78- 0.94], all p > 0.05), implying that receptor‑based FC and in‑vivo neurometabolic activity constitute distinct yet complementary aspects of neurochemical brain function.

Conclusion

This multimodal framework reveals how molecular receptor architecture redefines the brain’s functional dynamics under cognitive demand, delineating two principal neurochemical axes, excitatory‑inhibitory and ionotropic‑metabotropic signaling, that anchor functional gradients. By integrating receptor‑enriched connectivity, fMRS, and gradient mapping, the study highlights how neurochemical architecture and metabolic dynamics jointly sustain cognitive flexibility and stability.
