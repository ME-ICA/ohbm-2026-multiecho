Introduction

With advancing age, the degree of segregation within brain structural systems increases, accompanied by a decline in both intra-network and inter-network connectivity, which in turn contributes to age-related deterioration. Previous studies have further demonstrated that age-related alterations in both within-network and between-network functional connectivity are modulated by sex differences 1. Exercise interventions have been widely proposed as an effective strategy to improve age-related changes in brain network activity 2.

Although cardiorespiratory fitness in older adults is associated with functional brain connectivity in a sex-specific manner 3, circulating brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and sex-related neurohormonal influences have been reported to support network-specific connectivity changes predominantly 4,5. These findings suggest that the neural support provided by exercise interventions in the context of aging and sex differences may arise from alterations within specific functional networks rather than from global reorganization of whole-brain network architecture.

Accordingly, the present study aims to investigate whether sex-related differences in brain networks under aging conditions are primarily expressed as functional connectivity adjustments within specific networks or instead reflect large-scale reconfiguration of overall brain network topology.

Methods

This study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (Approval No. 201805113RINC). A total of 43 participants (age range = 20-79 years, mean age = 46 ± 3.10 years; 13 males and 30 females) were recruited for this study.

Participants completed a 12-week aerobic exercise intervention consisting of supervised indoor spinning bike training conducted at National Taiwan University. The training sessions were performed three times per week, with each session lasting 30 minutes. Cycling resistance was automatically adjusted based on individual heart rate responses to maintain a moderate exercise intensity, corresponding to 40–59% of the target heart rate reserve.

Magnetic resonance imaging data were acquired at baseline (Week 0) and after completion of the intervention (Week 12) using a 3-Tesla MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens). High-resolution structural images were obtained using a T1-weighted three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (3D MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: matrix size = 256 × 256 × 192; isotropic voxel resolution = 0.93 × 0.93 × 0.93 mm³; inversion time (TI) = 900 ms; repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms; echo time (TE) = 2.3 ms; flip angle (FA) = 8°; bandwidth = 200 Hz/pixel; number of excitations (NEX) = 1. The total acquisition time for the T1-weighted scan was 4 minutes and 40 seconds. Resting-state functional MRI data were acquired using a single-shot gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (GE-EPI) sequence with the following parameters: matrix size = 64 × 64 × 31; slice thickness = 3.5 mm; field of view (FOV) = 224 × 224 mm²; repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms; echo time (TE) = 32 ms; flip angle (FA) = 90°; bandwidth = 3005 Hz/pixel.

All MRI data were preprocessed using the CONN functional connectivity toolbox (v 22; www.nitrc.org/projects/conn) following the standard preprocessing pipeline. Preprocessing steps included functional realignment and unwarping, slice-timing correction, outlier identification, direct segmentation and normalization to standard space, spatial smoothing of functional images (FWHM = 8mm), and denoising.

Functional connectivity analyses were conducted by computing ROI-to-ROI connectivity matrices based on a predefined whole-brain parcellation comprising 164 regions of interest (ROIs). Additionally, graph-theoretical analyses were conducted to characterize the network-level properties of brain functional organization.

Second-level analyses compared sex differences in exercise-induced brain network changes using a general linear model, with age included as a continuous covariate and sex modeled as a between-subject factor. Exercise effects were defined as the change from baseline (Week 0) to post-intervention (Week 12). Statistical significance was assessed using an FDR-corrected threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

After accounting for age as a covariate, significant sex differences were observed in exercise-induced network changes over the 12-week intervention period (Week 12 − Week 0), predominantly within the auditory cortical network (Heschl’s gyrus, planum temporale, and planum polare) and posterior cerebellar regions (Fig. 1A). The corresponding pattern of altered functional connectivity is illustrated in the ROI-to-ROI correlation matrix (Fig. 1B; uncorrected p < 0.00005; FDR-corrected p < 0.024). These effects were primarily confined to intra-network connections, indicating age-adjusted, sex-specific reorganization of functional brain networks following exercise.

Notably, the observed sex differences were spatially constrained and did not extend to global reconfiguration of whole-brain network topology. Graph-theoretical analyses further revealed no significant sex differences in exercise-induced changes in global or local efficiency at the whole-brain level after controlling for age (all p > 0.05, FDR-corrected).

Conclusion

Twelve weeks of exercise intervention induced sex-specific functional changes predominantly within auditory–cerebellar networks, without evidence of large-scale reorganization of whole-brain network topology. Sex-related differences were hypothesized based on prior evidence that neuroplastic responses to exercise are modulated by sex-dependent physiological and neuroendocrine factors across adulthood. The observed involvement of auditory–cerebellar connectivity may reflect the sensitivity of sensorimotor and timing-related circuits to aerobic training. Together, these findings indicate that exercise-related brain network plasticity across the adult lifespan is expressed through network-specific functional adaptations rather than global reconfiguration.
