Introduction

With the rapid acceleration of global urbanization, the built environment has become a critical determinant of human health. According to the World Health Organization, more than 55% of the world’s population currently lives in urban areas, a proportion expected to rise to 68% by 2050. However, poorly planned urban expansion and structural changes in cities can exacerbate environmental exposures, thereby increasing the burden of disease, particularly for respiratory and cardiovascular conditions.

Most existing studies examining the relationship between urban form and health rely on coarse classifications, typically distinguishing only between “urban” and “rural” areas. Such simplified approaches fail to capture the substantial heterogeneity within cities, including variations in building height, density, surrounding environments, and their evolution over time. This limitation constrains a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms linking urban morphology, environmental exposure, and human health.

To address this gap, this study proposes a novel 4D Urban Morphology (4D-UM) framework, which characterizes urban form across four dimensions: vertical structure (building height), horizontal structure (building density), environmental context (natural and artificial surroundings), and temporal dynamics (changes in residential environments over time). Using the Local Climate Zone (LCZ) classification as a unifying tool, we integrate multi-source remote sensing data with individual-level health data from the UK Biobank. This study systematically investigates the associations between 4D-UM and biological aging as well as disease risks, and further projects future health burdens under alternative socioeconomic development scenarios, providing evidence to support health-oriented urban planning.

Methods

This study employed a four-stage methodological framework: (1) construction of dynamic 4D Urban Morphology (4D-UM) exposure data, (2) system-specific biological age modeling, (3) statistical association and pathway analysis, and (4) future health burden projection under alternative socioeconomic scenarios.

First, 4D-UM data were constructed by integrating multi-source remote sensing imagery and geospatial datasets on the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. A Random Forest classifier was applied to generate annual Local Climate Zone (LCZ) maps for the United Kingdom from 2006 to 2024, with a spatial resolution of 1 km and a temporal resolution of one year. These dynamic LCZ products capture long-term spatiotemporal variation in urban morphology, including building height, density, and surrounding land cover. Residential coordinates from the UK Biobank were spatially matched to LCZ maps, enabling year-specific assignment of urban morphology exposures at the individual level.

Second, health risks were quantified using the Biological Age Gap (BAG), defined as the difference between predicted biological age and chronological age. Eight physiological systems were analyzed: nervous, cardiovascular, respiratory, digestive, urinary, endocrine, immune, and musculoskeletal systems. More than 90 non-imaging health phenotypes and over 200 imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) were extracted from the UK Biobank. Machine learning models, including Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), support vector machine (SVM), and elastic net regression, were trained to estimate system-specific biological age. Non-imaging BAGs were used as primary outcomes, while imaging-based BAGs were applied for validation.

For the neurological system, a dedicated brain age model was developed using 139 imaging-derived phenotypes, covering regional brain volumes, cortical thickness, surface area, and white matter microstructural measures. These features were selected to comprehensively characterize structural brain aging. Brain age predictions were generated using the same machine learning framework, and the resulting brain age gap was used to validate key associations observed in non-imaging analyses.

Third, associations between baseline LCZ categories and system-specific BAGs were examined using multivariable linear regression models with adjustment for relevant covariates. Estimated marginal means (emmeans) were employed for post hoc comparisons across LCZ types. To address multiple testing across systems and exposure categories, Bonferroni correction was applied.

To investigate potential mechanisms, a Partial Least Squares Path Model (PLS-PM) was constructed. LCZ types were encoded as dummy variables, while environmental mediators were standardized and imputed as needed. Three pathways were assessed: LCZ to environmental exposures, LCZ directly to health outcomes, and environmental exposures to health outcomes. Model stability was evaluated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples.

Finally, future health burdens attributable to urban morphology were projected by integrating 4D-UM data with Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs), ICD-10–coded disease data, and system-specific rates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study. Three scenarios—SSP1, SSP2, and SSP5—were analyzed to estimate disease burden trajectories from 2030 to 2100 using established modeling approaches.

Results

Analysis of 4D-UM distributions revealed that Open Low-Rise (LCZ 6) and Dense Forest (LCZ A) environments accounted for the largest proportion of UK Biobank participants. From 2006 to 2024, the overall population distribution across LCZ types remained relatively stable, indicating limited large-scale urban restructuring and low residential mobility among participants. High-rise and mid-rise compact zones (LCZ 1–3) were predominantly concentrated in Greater London and surrounding metropolitan areas.

Significant associations were observed between LCZ types and system-specific biological aging. Participants residing in LCZ 5 (Open Mid-Rise) exhibited significantly higher respiratory system BAGs compared with those living in other LCZs, suggesting accelerated respiratory aging in this urban form. In contrast, the same group demonstrated significantly lower endocrine system BAGs, indicating a protective or decelerated aging effect. These associations remained significant after Bonferroni correction in non-imaging BAG analyses and were consistently replicated using imaging-derived BAGs.

Pathway analysis using PLS-PM demonstrated that the effects of urban morphology on health were predominantly mediated through environmental factors rather than direct pathways. Among these mediators, air pollution exposure and green space availability emerged as the most influential contributors linking LCZs to health outcomes. Bootstrap validation confirmed the stability and robustness of the estimated pathways.

Future scenario projections indicated a consistent increase in urban morphology–related disease burden across all SSP scenarios from 2030 to 2100. However, substantial differences were observed between development pathways. SSP5 was associated with the highest projected health burden, while SSP1 consistently showed the lowest risk, with SSP2 falling in between. Decomposition of disease burden revealed that all-cause mortality and cardiovascular diseases were the primary drivers of future health impacts.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive, multidimensional assessment of how urban morphology evolution influences human health risks. By introducing the 4D-UM framework, it advances beyond traditional static or binary urban classifications and enables fine-grained characterization of urban form across spatial and temporal dimensions.

Our findings demonstrate that distinct urban morphology types are significantly associated with system-specific biological aging, with pronounced heterogeneity across physiological systems. The influence of urban form on health operates largely through environmental pathways, particularly air pollution and green space, underscoring the importance of environmental quality as a central lever for health-oriented urban planning.

Future scenario analyses further highlight that long-term health risks are highly sensitive to urban development trajectories. Sustainable development pathways (SSP1) substantially mitigate future disease burdens, whereas fossil-fuel–intensive growth (SSP5) exacerbates public health challenges. These results provide quantitative evidence to support integrated urban, environmental, and public health policy interventions.

Overall, this research represents the first systematic integration of dynamic 4D urban morphology data with individual-level UK Biobank health outcomes, identifies key mediating environmental mechanisms, and projects future health burdens under alternative development scenarios. It offers a robust scientific foundation for precision urban health interventions and forward-looking city planning strategies.
