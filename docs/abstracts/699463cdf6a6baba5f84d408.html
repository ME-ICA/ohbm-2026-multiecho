Introduction

Stroke is the leading cause of adult disability worldwide. The autonomic nervous system is responsible for a wide variety of critical functions, including cardiovascular regulation. Autonomic dysfunction affects most individuals after stroke, often manifesting as reduced vagal (parasympathetic) tone and heightened sympathetic activity that contribute to blood pressure instability, arrhythmias, and myocardial injury.1, 2 Impaired autonomic control increases mortality risk, worsens recovery, and limits functional independence;1,2 yet rehabilitation primarily focuses on motor systems (e.g., arm and hand function). However, it is likely that autonomic and motor systems work together to support recovery after stroke.

Supraspinal regulation of autonomic function is mediated by coordinated activity in specific cortical and subcortical brain regions, known as the central autonomic network (CAN).3,4 Although autonomic dysregulation is common amongst individuals with stroke, to our knowledge, no studies to date have investigated the impact of stroke and rehabilitation on CAN resting-state functional connectivity. This study aimed to address this gap by assessing CAN functional connectivity before and after a high dose of gamified motor practice with the paretic upper limb and examining its association with motor recovery.

Methods

Twenty-eight individuals with chronic (>6 months) stroke (65.6 ± 11.3 years old, 8 female) and 24 controls (64.0 ± 8.8 years old, 15 female) were included in the final analyses. All participants underwent a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) scan 24 hours before the first of 10 gamified motor practice sessions (week 0; pre-training) and 24 hours after the last session (post-training scan; week 5) (Figure 1). The Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT) was used to assess paretic arm motor function for individuals in the stroke group before and after gamified practice (Figure 1).

All participants engaged in 10 sessions of the Track and Intercept Task (TrAIT), a custom rehabilitation game that takes place in a semi-immersive virtual reality environment.5 Practice sessions took place 2-3 times per week for 4 weeks. Each session (30 min) consisted of 5 consecutive TRAIT blocks (200 movements/block, 1000 arm movements/session,10,000 total arm movements). TrAIT is a gamified arm rehabilitation intervention developed to: (1) deliver a high dose of practice in a workspace customized to each individual's reaching ability, and (2) progress difficulty with improved skill and provide an engaging training environment.5

Magnetic resonance data were acquired on a Philips Achieva 3.0 Tesla whole body MRI scanner (Philips Healthcare), using an 8-channel sensitivity encoding head coil (SENSE factor = 2.4) and parallel imaging. The following scans were acquired: (1) 3D T 1 turbo field echo scan (TI = 800 ms, short-TR = 1900ms, flip angle θ = 6°, FOV = 256 × 256 mm, 160 slices, 1mm slice thickness, scan time = 3.2 minutes), and (2) resting-state blood-oxygen level dependent (BOLD) scans (2)with a single shot EPI sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30ms, flip angle θ = 90°, voxel dimension = 3 mm3 with1-mm gap, 36 slices, FOV 240 × 240mm, scan time = 8.0min/scan). During the resting-state fMRI scan, all participants were instructed to fixate on a cross, think of nothing, and not fall asleep.

Resting-state functional connectivity analyses were performed using the CONN toolbox with SPM. Connectivity was assessed using a region of interest (ROI) to ROI approach to examine interactions within the CAN. Three CAN networks were examined: overall CAN (12 ROIs), parasympathetic CAN (9 ROIs), and sympathetic CAN (10 ROIs). Regions of interest for the CAN networks were derived from a prior meta-analysis defined as 5-mm radius spheres centered on published MNI coordinates.4 ROI-to-ROI connectivity matrices were constructed by computing Pearson correlations between the BOLD time series of all ROI pairs within each CAN network and were Fisher z-transformed prior to group-level analyses.

To characterize group differences (stroke vs. control) in functional connectivity at pre- and post-training, between-group ROI-to-ROI analyses were performed separately for the overall, parasympathetic, and sympathetic CAN networks. Pre-training and post-training resting-state fMRI data were analyzed in separate general linear models. Each model included two groups (stroke, control) and a single condition (pre or post), allowing group differences in connectivity to be evaluated independently at each time point (i.e. equivalent to two independent samples t-tests). 

Relationships between brain connectivity and motor function were then examined within the stroke group, separately for each CAN network. Training-related changes in ROI-to-ROI connectivity (post- to pre-training) were entered into regression models to test whether individual differences in motor recovery were associated with training-related changes in functional connectivity within the overall, parasympathetic, and sympathetic CAN networks. The change in upper-limb motor function (WMFT change score) was included as a continuous predictor. Statistical significance was corrected for multiple comparisons with the false discovery rate method for all analyses. 

Results

Before gamified motor practice, individuals with stroke exhibited several differences in overall CAN connectivity compared with controls (Figure 2A). Individuals with stroke showed reduced connectivity between the left amygdala/hypothalamus region and the right amygdala/hippocampal region (T(50) = -3.46, p = 0.0121). In contrast, connectivity was greater between the right amygdala/hippocampal region and both the left anterior insula (T(50) = 3.18, p = 0.0140) and the right anterior insula (T(50) = 2.83, p = 0.0243). 

Within the sympathetic CAN at baseline, individuals with stroke also demonstrated increased connectivity between the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (PFC)/pregenual and subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) region and the midcingulate cortex/paracingulate cortex/supplementary motor area (SMA) region (T(50) = 2.64, p = 0.0415), the right anterior insula/ventrolateral PFC region ((T(50) = 2.45, p = 0.0415), and the right dorsolateral PFC (T(50) = 2.56, p = 0.0415) (Figure 2B). No group differences were observed within the parasympathetic CAN at pre-training.

After gamified motor practice, most pre-training differences between individuals with stroke and control were no longer present (Figure 2A and B). The only persistent group difference was reduced connectivity between the left amygdala/hypothalamus and the right amygdala/hippocampal regions (T(50) = -4.02, p = 0.0022, [Figure 2A]). No group differences were observed within the sympathetic or parasympathetic CAN at post-training.

Gamified practice-related changes in overall CAN connectivity were significantly associated with improved motor function in the stroke group (Figure 2C). Greater WMFT change indicating better paretic arm motor function was associated with reduced connectivity between the left amygdala/hypothalamus and the right angular/supramarginal region (T(25) = -3.47, p = 0.0210), reduced connectivity between the right anterior insula and the ventromedial prefrontal/subgenual ACC (T(25) = -3.24, p = 0.0360), and increased connectivity between the ventromedial prefrontal/subgenual ACC and the pregenual ACC (T(25) = 2.97, p = 0.0360). 

Within the sympathetic CAN, increased connectivity between the amygdala/subgenual ACC/nucleus accumbens/caudate/hippocampal formation and cerebellar Crus I related to greater WMFT improvement (T(25) = 3.04, p = 0.0385, [Figure 2D]). No relationships were observed between parasympathetic CAN connectivity changes and paretic arm motor function as indexed by the WMFT.

Conclusion

This study examined how stroke alters functional connectivity within the CAN and how training-related changes in resting-state functional connectivity relate to motor function after stroke. Before gamified motor practice, individuals with stroke showed disrupted function within the CAN, including reduced connectivity between the left and right amygdala, and increased connectivity between the right amygdala and insula bilaterally. In healthy individuals, coordinated activity within these circuits supports autonomic regulation.3,4 The sympathetic CAN was also altered by stroke; higher connectivity was noted between the PFC, anterior insula, ACC, and the SMA regions. Increased sympathetic CAN connectivity is consistent with reports of heightened sympathetic activity after stroke,1,2 and may reflect a shift toward heightened salience processing and sympathetic drive. Although our findings are preliminary and based on a modest sample, they are consistent with reports of autonomic dysfunction after stroke.

Importantly, our data provide evidence of plasticity within the CAN networks; multiple group differences at baseline were mitigated by gamified motor practice. Only bilateral amygdala connectivity remained reduced after gamified motor practice. These findings suggest that gamified motor practice may partially normalize CAN connectivity in people with chronic stroke. This observation aligns with studies showing that motor learning, cognitive, and behavioural training can modify functional connectivity within limbic, prefrontal, insular, and cerebellar regions. Future work should include measures of heart rate and blood pressure variability to directly determine the relationship between CAN functional connectivity and autonomic nervous system activity post-stroke. 

Practice-related changes in connectivity were meaningfully related to motor function for both the overall CAN and sympathetic CAN. In the overall CAN, WMFT improvement was associated with reduced connectivity between limbic and parietal regions as well as insula and vmPFC pathways, alongside increased connectivity between subgenual and pregenual ACC regions. Together, these findings suggest that motor function can be supported by reductions in maladaptive hyperconnectivity and strengthening of regulatory prefrontal pathways. In the sympathetic CAN, higher motor function was associated with increased connectivity between a limbic/striatal region and cerebellar Crus I. This may suggest strengthened connectivity between emotional, motivational and sensorimotor areas that guide motor function.4 These data are consistent with emerging evidence that the cerebellum contributes to autonomic regulation as well as sensorimotor control. 

Parasympathetic CAN connectivity did not change with training and was not associated with motor outcomes. This suggests that circuits controlling sympathetic function, rather than parasympathetic control, are sensitive to stroke-related disruption and responsive to rehabilitation.

Overall, our findings indicate that stroke alters overall and sympathetic CAN connectivity, particularly within bilateral amygdala networks and sympathetic regulatory pathways. These changes in connectivity were restored by gamified motor practice. Interestingly, improved motor function with practice was associated with changes in connectivity among limbic, striatal, prefrontal, and cerebellar cortical regions known to regulate salience, emotion, motivation, sensorimotor, and autonomic control. Our results highlight the importance of considering autonomic cortical networks in stroke rehabilitation and suggest potential targets for future interventions aimed at enhancing recovery.
