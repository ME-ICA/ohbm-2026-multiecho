Introduction

Early-life adversity (ELA) is a major determinant of psychiatric disorders and suicide risk, yet individuals exposed to similar adversities show strikingly divergent mental health outcomes. This heterogeneity suggests the presence of resilience and vulnerability factors that buffer or exacerbate the effects of ELA. Identifying such moderators is critical for understanding heterogeneous risk trajectories and for informing personalized prevention strategies. Adolescence represents a particularly critical window to investigate such moderators, as ongoing brain maturation coincides with increased vulnerability to psychopathology.

While psychological and environmental moderators of ELA have been widely studied, increasing evidence highlights the brain as an active moderator rather than a passive endpoint of early stress. Specific neural features have been shown to amplify or attenuate adversity-related risk, indicating potential neural vulnerability and protective factors.1 However, most existing work relies on hypothesis-driven and single-modality approaches, limiting systematic identification of neural moderators and obscuring how integrated brain systems shape susceptibility and resilience.

ELA is inherently heterogeneous. In adolescence, adversity extends beyond the family to include non-familial interpersonal and non-interpersonal experiences,2 which recruit partially distinct neurodevelopmental pathways. Consistent with transdiagnostic frameworks, ELA-related risk may be better reflected in dimensional symptom domains—internalizing, externalizing, and the general psychopathology (p) factor—than in discrete diagnostic categories.3

Here, we leverage a large neurodevelopmental dataset to systematically examine whether multimodal brain features moderate associations between distinct adversity subtypes and transdiagnostic psychopathology in adolescence, with the goal of identifying neural markers that confer vulnerability or resilience to early adversity.

Methods

Participants

Data were obtained from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (Release 3.0), including ~11,878 children aged 9–10 years. 

Early-life adversity

Three ELA categories were examined: familial interpersonal adversity (FIA), non-familial interpersonal adversity (NFIA), and non-interpersonal adversity (NIA). FIA included abuse, neglect, and domestic violence within the family; NFIA comprised interpersonal adversity outside the family (e.g., bullying, community violence); and NIA included non-social stressors (e.g., natural disasters, accidents, major losses). Relevant items were identified from validated ABCD baseline instruments and aggregated to generate one total score per adversity subtype for each participant.

Psychopathology

General psychopathology (P) and orthogonal internalizing (INT) and externalizing (EXT) factors were derived from baseline parent-reported CBCL data using a bifactor model. Factor scores were extracted for subsequent analyses. Psychiatric diagnoses at baseline and 2-year follow-up were obtained via parent-reported Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for DSM-5 and coded dichotomously. Change in diagnostic status indexed clinical progression.

Neuroimaging measures

More than 1500 curated multimodal neuroimaging features provided by the ABCD consortium were used, including structural MRI, diffusion MRI, resting-state fMRI, and task-based fMRI. Participants failing quality control were excluded. Brain measures captured cortical and subcortical structure, white-matter microstructure, functional connectivity, and task-evoked activation across reward (monetary incentive delay task, MID), inhibitory control (stop signal task, SST), and emotional working memory tasks (emotional n-back, ENBACK).

Moderation analysis

Linear mixed-effects models tested whether brain features moderated associations between ELA subtypes and psychopathology dimensions:

Psychopathology = β1×brain + β2×ELA + β3×brain×ELA = β1×brain +( β2 + β3×brain)×ELA.

In this formulation, the marginal effect of ELA on Psychopathology is given by β2+β3×brain. Traits that attenuate adverse effects are considered protective, while those that amplify them confer vulnerability. Accordingly, a positive β3 indicated vulnerability, whereas a negative β3 indicated protection.4 Models included age, sex, race, socioeconomic variables, BMI, scanner manufacturer, modality-specific covariates, and random intercepts for family nested within site. Bonferroni correction was applied within each adversity × modality × psychopathology domain. Model robustness was evaluated using 80/20 train–test splits with ten-fold cross-validation.

Relative Resilience Index (RRI)

To capture cumulative effects across all moderators and adversity types, we developed a Relative Resilience Index (RRI)—a whole-brain metric quantifying the balance between protective and vulnerability factors at individual level, which was computed as:

RRI = ∑i ai (∑m βm(res)xm(res) − ∑n βn(vul)xn(vul)),

where ai indicates the presence (1) or absence (0) of adversity i. Each resilient feature xm(res) and vulnerable feature xn(vul)  was trichotomized by using±1 standard deviation (SD) cutoffs to define high- and low-trait resilience groups. Specifically, values exceeding one SD above the mean were coded as 1 (presence), those below one SD as −1 (absence), and values within ±1 SD as 0 (neutral). The weights βm(res) and βn(vul)  represent standardized coefficients from the interaction terms involving the respective features in moderation analyses, reflecting their relative contribution.

Clinical progression

Ordinal logistic regression examined associations of RRI and individual brain features with clinical progression (improved, unchanged, worsened), with ten-fold cross-validation. 

Results

We identified 57 brain features that significantly moderated the association between ELA and psychopathology after Bonferroni correction (Fig. 1a). Of these, 34 showed negative brain×ELA interaction coefficients (standardized β = −0.086 to −0.048), consistent with protective effects, and 23 showed positive coefficients (β = 0.047 to 0.073), consistent with vulnerability effects. These features spanned multiple modalities (Fig. 1b), with the highest proportion observed in reward-related functional activation (MID, 5.8%) and the lowest in white matter connectivity (DTI, 1.3%). Protective brain features were concentrated in limbic, sensory integration, and regulatory circuits [including amygdala, insula, hippocampus, parietal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)], while vulnerability features were predominately localized in frontal and temporal systems, particularly during threat and loss avoidance contexts.

Neural moderators differed markedly across ELA types and psychopathology dimensions (Fig. 1c–f). Most features moderated non-familial interpersonal adversity (NFIA; 46 features), whereas fewer moderated non-interpersonal adversity (NIA; 10 features), and only one moderated familial interpersonal adversity (FIA). NFIA-related features spanned structural, resting-state, and all task-based modalities, whereas NIA-related effects were confined to DTI and resting-state connectivity. Across psychopathology dimensions, more features moderated the general psychopathology factor (P) and externalizing symptoms (EXT) than internalizing symptoms (INT), with modality-specific patterns across dimensions.

Spatially, distinct ELA types were associated with separable neural moderation profiles. NFIA involved both allostatic interoception (insula, amygdala, hippocampus, thalamus and ACC) and high-order social cognition, emotion regulation, cognitive control, and reward systems. In contrast, NIA-related moderators were mainly concentrated in allostatic interoception, and FIA only showed a single vulnerability-related effect in right caudal ACC activation during negative face processing.

Most features moderated only a single ELA–psychopathology combination. Notably, three features in the right ACC showed significant effects across multiple combinations, with either consistent or opposing directions, indicating region-specific but context-dependent moderation.

Model generalizability was supported by moderate-to-strong correlations between predicted and observed psychopathology scores in the test set (r = 0.37–0.87). 

In longitudinal analyses, six brain features prospectively predicted psychiatric progression over two years in the hypothesized directions. Three protective features were associated with a reduced risk of deterioration, including activation of the right rostral ACC during loss anticipation (OR = 0.27, p = 0.010), cortical thickness of the right paracentral gyrus (OR = 0.39, p = 0.033), and activation of the right inferior parietal cortex during inhibitory control (OR = 0.76, p = 0.045). In contrast, three vulnerability features were associated with increased risk, including activation of the left transverse temporal cortex (OR = 6.18, p = 0.021) and left insula during loss feedback (OR = 1.42, p = 0.028), as well as functional connectivity between the sensorimotor hand network and right hippocampus (OR = 1.30, p = 0.034). Finally, a whole-brain Relative Resilience Index (RRI), integrating protective and vulnerability features across adversity types, predicted a reduced risk of overall psychiatric deterioration at follow-up (OR = 0.69, p = 0.013), supporting a cumulative, whole-brain model of resilience.

Conclusion

This large-scale multimodal moderation study demonstrates that the adolescent brain actively shapes the psychiatric impact of ELA in an adversity-specific and system-specific manner, conferring resilience or vulnerability rather than serving solely as a passive outcome of exposure. Distinct neural circuits selectively moderated familial interpersonal, non-familial interpersonal, and non-interpersonal adversity, with protective features concentrated in limbic, sensory integration, and regulatory systems, and vulnerability features primarily in frontotemporal regions, providing a comprehensive map of neural resilience and vulnerability to ELA.

Importantly, the identified moderators align with the developmental mismatch model of brain maturation. Resilience to non-familial interpersonal adversity was associated with relatively advanced prefrontal structural features, including increased sulcus depth and white-matter contrast in the right ACC and inferior frontal gyrus, suggesting that accelerated maturation of regulatory circuits may facilitate top–down control over subcortical and interoceptive systems. The ACC emerged as a cross-modal hub, integrating cognitive, emotional, and interoceptive signals and exerting bidirectional influence across adversity types, highlighting its central role in moderating developmental trajectories.

Beyond individual features, integrating cumulative environmental burden with whole-brain balances of protective and vulnerability factors revealed that higher neural resilience predicts reduced longitudinal psychiatric risk. This system-level characterization bridges exposome and biological state, conceptually consistent with integrative frameworks such as One Health,5 and shifts the conceptualization of the brain from a downstream consequence of adversity to an active, developmentally embedded moderator of mental health trajectories. Together, these findings highlight adolescence as a sensitive window for precision-targeted interventions aimed at enhancing neural resilience and long-term psychiatric outcomes.
