Introduction

Functional connectivity patterns estimated from resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)– referred to as functional connectomes (FCs) – are unique to each individual 1. These so-called brain-fingerprints allow to identify individuals from a population, have been linked to cognitive traits 2 and the features driving identifiability are altered in neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s Disease 3 and Parkinson's Disease 4. However, most studies to date have identified fingerprinting features using either within-session estimates (by splitting a single session into two halves) or by comparing only two sessions acquired on two separate days. It therefore remains unclear whether the features driving individual variability differ between within-session and between-session timescales, and whether aggregating multiple sessions cumulatively alters the spatial organization of FC fingerprints or their ability to predict behavioural and cognitive traits. Understanding how FC fingerprints evolve across time and relate to cognition is critical for the development of individualized biomarkers, particularly as multi-session neuroimaging datasets are increasingly common. Here, we leveraged the Midnight Scanning Club (MSC) dataset5, comprising ten subjects each scanned across ten sessions, to assess FC reliability across three temporal scales and to test whether highly reliable FC edges predict cognitive performance.

Methods

This data was obtained from the OpenfMRI database5. Its accession number is ds000224. The dataset included N=10 healthy participants who underwent n=10 resting-state fMRI sessions and behavioural measures (detailed below). FC matrices were computed in each subject's native space using Pearson correlations between 278 brain regions (Shen parcellation plus subcortical and cerebellar regions). We calculated edgewise intra-class correlation (ICC), which quantifies the stability of connectivity between repeated measurements within subjects. Higher ICC values indicates more stable within-subject connectivity patterns and greater between subjects variability in the group 3. We calculated edgewise ICC(1,1) to assess: (1) within-session reliability (n = 10 matrices), by splitting each session’s acquisition into two halves; (2) between-session reliability (n = 9 matrices), by comparing consecutive sessions (sessions 1–2, 2–3, …, 9–10); and (3) cumulative reliability (n = 8 matrices), by progressively increasing the number of sessions included (sessions 1–3, 1–4, …, 1–10). For behavioral prediction, we identified high-reliability edges (99.9th percentile ICC) and computed their summed FC strength (averaged across sessions) for each subject. For within- and between-session reliability, high-reliability edges were extracted from the average ICC matrix across all respective matrices, whereas for cumulative reliability, we used the final ICC matrix (sessions 1–10). Linear models were used to test whether high-ICC FC values predicted behavioural performance across 17 measures, while controlling for age, sex, and education (one linear model per measure). Behavioural outcomes included: the Kaufman Brief Intelligence Test-2 (KBIT-2) IQ score; Behavioral Activation System (BAS) composite score (drive, fun-seeking, reward responsiveness) and Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS) score (sensitivity to punishment); NEO Five-Factor Inventory (NEO-FFI) Big Five personality dimensions (neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, conscientiousness); NIH Toolbox cognitive battery (crystallized cognition composite, 9-hole pegboard dexterity, dimensional change card sort, flanker inhibitory control and attention, list sorting working memory, oral reading recognition, pattern comparison processing speed, picture sequence memory, picture vocabulary). 

Results

We computed ICC matrices at three temporal scales (Figure 1A,1B,1C). Within-session reliability (test vs. retest) showed the highest ICC values, between-session reliability (consecutive pairs) showed lower – but still high – stability, and cumulative reliability (sessions 1-3 to 1-10) systematically increased with more sessions. The spatial organization of FC fingerprints across the three temporal scales varied (Figure 1), but high-reliability edges (99.9th percentile ICC) were predominantly within functional networks. Within-session and between-session matrices showed noisier spatial patterns, with many high-ICC points scattered across the brain. In contrast, cumulative reliability revealed more spatially coherent patterns, with minimal changes when increasing the number of sessions from 1–4 to 1–10. Linear models tested whether summed FC from these high-ICC edges predicted 17 behavioral measures (cognitive, personality, IQ). NIH Picture Sequence Memory showed the only significant association with positive high-reliability FC across all three ICC types (within: p=0.014; between: p=0.049; cumulative: p=0.021; Figure 2.B). However, no associations survived FDR correction across the 34 models tested (17 measures × 2 edge types (positive vs. negative connections); all p_FDR > 0.49). The convergence of this finding across three independent ICC calculation methods suggest a potential FC-fingerprint - behaviour relationship, in line with previous work 4. All other behavioral measures showed no significant associations (all p > 0.05).

Conclusion

This work shows that the spatial organization of FC fingerprints stabilizes after four sessions (i.e., when using cumulative reliability), providing more spatially coherent and less noisy estimates than single- or between-session analyses. Nevertheless, we observed a consistent association between high-reliability FC and episodic memory performance across all three ICC calculation methods (within, between, and cumulative), suggesting connections with high ICC value across the three FC fingerprint estimates still support a potential FC–behavior relationship. While further analyses are needed to support this, current results suggest that within-session FC fingerprints are sufficient to capture a potential FC–behavior relationship.
