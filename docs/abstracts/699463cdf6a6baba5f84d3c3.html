Introduction

Primary headaches (PH) are a prevalent neurological disorder inflicting a significant economic burden and reducing quality of life by affecting physical, emotional, and social functioning.^1 This suffering is hypothesized to arise from altered brain physiology within the pain network, including regions such as the thalamus, anterior cingulate, insula, somatosensory cortices, periaqueductal gray, prefrontal cortices, and amygdala.^2 Structural changes in overlapping areas have likewise been documented in primary headache disorders.^3 The structural and physiological contributions to long-term suffering and disability remain poorly understood, and medications are primarily used for symptom management. Overuse of these medications can cause medication overuse headaches, worsening symptoms and potentially leading to chronic headaches.^4 Osteopathic Manipulative Treatment (OMT) offers a non pharmacological alternative for the prevention and management of PH.^5 A major barrier to developing a personalized OMT approach is the incomplete understanding of how changes in brain structure and function contribute to patient symptoms. Identifying the changes associated with PH has a potential of providing biomarkers of long term suffering and thereby yield objective ways to evaluate treatment effects.

Our overall goal is to characterize how OMT affects both brain function and brain structure in individuals with PH. Specifically, this pilot study aimed to (1) assess OMT-related brain changes using resting-state fMRI and structural morphology measures, and (2) determine whether OMT alters connectivity within the pain network in a direction consistent with patterns observed in healthy controls.

Methods

Structural and resting-state fMRI data were collected on a Siemens 7T Terra.X scanner (MPRAGE; multiband EPI, TR = 1000 ms, TE = 20 ms, 2-mm^3 voxels) from 15 PH patients and 10 controls (ages 21–28). Patients received four OMT sessions and were rescanned within one week post-treatment.

fMRI data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep, denoised in FSL, deconvolved using HRF blind deconvolution, and band-pass filtered (0.01–0.1 Hz) in CONN. Functional connectivity matrices were generated using the Power atlas and analyzed in MATLAB with SPM12 tools.

Edge-wise statistics were performed in Fisher z-space using paired t-tests for PRE vs POST and Welch tests for CTRL comparisons. Multiple comparisons were controlled with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR (q = 0.05) within a 171-edge pain-network mask under a one-sided hypothesis. Structural morphology was processed with FreeSurfer, and regional volume, thickness, and surface area measures were tested using the same statistical framework.

Results

The analysis of the resting-state data identified a significant POST > PRE increase in connectivity between a sensorimotor node near the left postcentral gyrus (Power node 8) and a superior/medial frontal node (Power node 14). This edge survived within–pain-network FDR correction. The paired one-sided t-test yielded an uncorrected p = 0.0001. Connectivity for this connection increased from r = 0.21 (± 0.21) at PRE to r = 0.43 (±pm 0.11) at POST, representing a numerical increase in 13 of 15 subjects (86.7%).

Group-level analysis indicated that fMRI comparisons did not reach statistical significance (PRE vs. POST: p = 0.17; CTRL vs. PRE: p = 0.58; CTRL vs. POST: p = 0.57). Even so, mean connectivity increased after OMT, and the post-treatment distribution more closely resembled that of healthy controls, as shown in Fig. 1, left. This suggests a trend toward normalization of pain-network function following OMT.

For the morphology analysis, subtle PRE–POST differences were detected within the pain network. Six regions showed raw p < 0.05, including decreased surface area in the left precentral gyrus and reduced subcortical volume in the brainstem and right thalamus, as well as small increases in cortical thickness across frontal regions (pars orbitalis, medial orbitofrontal, and precentral cortex). Although none of these effects survived FDR correction, the pattern of local structural modulation anatomically overlaps with the significant fMRI connectivity change.

Across all four morphological metrics, PRE, POST, and CTRL groups showed very similar values, with no statistically significant differences (Fig. 1, right). The small numerical fluctuations observed are within expected measurement variability and do not indicate a treatment-related effect.

Conclusion

The results presented in this abstract provide preliminary evidence that OMT modulates functional connectivity in young adults with PH. A significant increase in connectivity between somatosensory and medial frontal regions was found, and this functional change occurred in the same general areas where subtle, raw p-value structural differences were observed in the morphology analysis. As the anatomical changes did not survive FDR correction, the findings indicate that the primary measurable impact of OMT in this time frame is functional rather than structural.
