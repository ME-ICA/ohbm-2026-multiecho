Introduction

Memory is vulnerable to transient disruptions. One of these disruptions is the tip-of-the-tongue (ToT) state, where access to information feels imminent yet is momentarily out of reach. This state can provide a window to examine how representational and control systems interact during successful and unsuccessful semantic and episodic retrieval. Prior studies have examined this experience only in semantic memory and have consistently implicated left hemisphere control regions¹, suggesting that ToTs recruit control mechanisms when representational systems fail to deliver target information. Here, this is extended to episodic transient retrieval failures to determine whether these failures share common mechanisms across representational systems or differ by memory type.

Semantic retrieval depends on a hub-and-spoke system centred on the anterior temporal lobe, while episodic retrieval may involve hippocampal mechanisms that support detail-rich recollection and gist-like construction2,3. To support retrieval, the semantic control (SCN) and frontoparietal (FPN) networks aid these systems through lexical selection, controlled retrieval and post-retrieval monitoring. Evidence for hemispheric biases also suggests that semantic retrieval tends to engage left-lateral control regions, whereas episodic retrieval draws more on bilateral or right-lateralised networks.

These distinctions in representational and control demands can also be examined within the broader organisation of the cortex. Principal cortical gradients capture transitions from unimodal to transmodal regions and from the default-mode network (DMN) to control systems4, offering a framework for understanding how different forms of retrieval are embedded within whole-brain architecture. Examining semantic and episodic known and ToT responses within this gradient structure may highlight how variations in representational content and control engagement influence the likelihood of transient retrieval failure.

Methods

Thirty healthy right-handed adults (mean age 25.19 ± 4.72 years, 18 females) took part in the study. All provided written informed consent. The experiment was approved by the ethics committee at the Basque Center on Cognition, Brain and Language and followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

The experiment was conducted over two days and involved a 2 (Memory: semantic vs episodic) x 4 (Response: known, ToT, familiar, unknown). On day 1, participants learned 220 non-famous face name pairs through a test–retest procedure. On day 2, they completed an fMRI task followed by a post-scan verification and recognition phase. The participants were presented with 220 famous and 220 recently learned faces. Famous and non-famous items were balanced across different dimensions. A prior normative study ensured 15-20% recognition of all famous names. Within the scanner, the experiment was divided into four functional runs containing a combination of 55 famous and 55 non-famous trials. Participants indicated whether they knew the name, were in a ToT state, judged the face as familiar or did not know the name using an MRI-compatible button box. Each trial consisted of a fixation cross for 2500 ms followed by the face for 2500 ms and then a 1500 ms response screen  (See Figure 1). After scanning, all 440 faces were re-judged outside the scanner using the same responses, followed by a verbal and multiple-choice name test to verify the response.

MRI data were acquired on a 3-T Siemens PRISMAfit system with a 64-channel coil. Functional images were collected with a multiband T2* echo-planar sequence (TR 1000 ms, TE 35 ms, voxel size 2.4 mm) and structural images with a 1 mm MPRAGE sequence. Preprocessing followed two pipelines in SPM12. The native space pipeline included slice timing correction, realignment, 3 mm smoothing, motion repair, coregistration and an additional 4 mm smoothing step. A high-pass filter removed low-frequency drift. The normalised pipeline followed the same steps but used 4 mm initial smoothing and MNI305 normalisation with 7 mm final smoothing (total smoothing of 8.06 mm).

A GLM estimated condition-specific responses for semantic and episodic known and ToT trials. Familiar and unknown events were modelled but not analysed. Events were modelled as 4-second epochs convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function and movement parameters were included as nuisance regressors. One-sample t-tests produced group-level contrasts with voxelwise FWE correction at p < .05. 

ROI analyses focused on representational and control regions and were all defined in individual space using HCP parcellation and automated hippocampal segmentation. Functional connectivity was estimated with the beta series correlation method and Fisher-transformed correlation values were compared across conditions using t-tests with false discovery rate correction at q < .05.

To examine how the responses are positioned within large-scale cortical organisation, specific contrasts of interest were aligned with canonical gradients that capture the transition from sensory-based processing to transmodal integration and the differentiation between DMN and control networks. Following removal of noninformative voxels and standardisation of voxel intensities, spatial correspondence with the gradients was estimated through voxel-wise Pearson correlation and FSLCC coefficients for each contrast of interest. Given the spatial dependence of cortical data, significance was evaluated through spherical spin permutation testing. Each contrast and gradient map was projected onto an FSL 32k surface and subjected to 5000 permutations, which provided a spatially informed null distribution against which the empirical correlations were assessed.

Results

Behavioural Results
     A 2 (Memory) × 2 (Response)  ANOVA revealed a statistically significant main effect of Memory (p =.02) and a Memory by Response interaction (p ≤ .001). Within semantic memory, post-hoc analyses revealed a higher percent of known responses versus ToTs (p ≤ .001). Within episodic memory, a reverse pattern emerged where a higher percent of ToT responses were observed compared to known responses (p ≤ .001). Between memory systems, there was a higher percent of semantic known responses compared to episodic known responses (p = .01), while there were more episodic ToTs compared to semantic ToTs (p ≤ .001).

ROI Analysis
    ROI analyses were conducted using a series of 2 (Memory) × 2 (Response) repeated measures ANOVAs. Control regions (dlPFC, dmPFC and IFG pars triangularis) and representational regions (temporal poles and hippocampus) were examined. In the left dlPFC, a significant Memory by Response interaction (p ≤ .001) showed stronger activation for episodic known and ToTs versus semantic known (p ≤ .001) and ToTs (p = .006), respectively, with semantic ToTs also exceeding semantic known responses (p ≤ .001). The left dmPFC had a similar interaction (p ≤ .001), where episodic known had stronger regional engagement versus semantic known (p = .007), as well as semantic ToT having stronger regional engagement versus semantic known (p = .001).

The left IFG pars triangularis showed a significant interaction (p ≤ .001) driven by greater activation for semantic ToTs compared with both semantic known (p ≤ .001) and episodic ToT (p ≤ .001) responses.

Representational regions showed a significant main effect of Memory (ps ≤ .001) for both temporal poles, with consistently greater activation during semantic retrieval compared to episodic retrieval. Within the hippocampus, anterior and posterior subregions showed significant main effects of Memory and Response (ps ≤ .05), with semantic greater than episodic across response types. 

Functional Connectivity
    To test whether retrieval engages overlapping representational and control regions, we conducted pairwise functional connectivity analysis to compare coupling strength among ROIs (See Fig. 2). For semantic known responses, stronger connectivity was observed between the right anterior hippocampus and left (t = 2.87, q = .02) and right temporal poles (t = 3.57, q = .01) and between the right posterior hippocampus and the left temporal pole (t = 2.76, q = .02). Semantic ToTs also showed increased coupling between the right posterior hippocampus and the left temporal pole (t = 2.92, q = .02).

Episodic ToTs demonstrated stronger coupling among SCN and FPN regions, including left pars triangularis and right pars triangularis (t = 2.82, q = .02) and right dlPFC (t = 4.50, q = .01). Within semantic memory, ToTs showed greater coupling between the right posterior hippocampus and left dlPFC compared to semantic known responses (t = 2.77, q = .02). Within episodic memory, ToTs produced stronger bilateral pars triangularis coupling compared to episodic known responses (t = 3.79, q = .02). 

Gradient Analysis

Gradient analyses indicated that the different mnemonic responses occupied distinct positions within large-scale cortical organisation. Along Gradient 1, episodic known responses showed a clearer shift toward the transmodal end than episodic ToTs after spin permutation testing (r = .693, p ≤ .001), suggesting greater integration within higher-order systems when retrieval succeeds. For Gradient 3, semantic known responses aligned more strongly with DMN regions (r = -.326, p = .001), compared to semantic ToTs, which showed a relative alignment toward control regions. This distinction extended between memory systems, with successful (r = -.615, p ≤ .001) and unsuccessful (r = -.535, p ≤ .001) semantic retrieval consistently positioned toward DMN regions when compared to episodic retrieval, which showed greater alignment with control-related gradients for both known and ToT states.

Conclusion

Using a face-naming paradigm, the present work examined the interaction between semantic and episodic systems during successful and unsuccessful retrieval and how these processes are embedded within large-scale cortical organisation. Findings showed that (1) ToTs involved differential engagement of prefrontal control regions, with the SCN and FPN operating in tandem to support retrieval attempts; (2) functional connectivity revealed strengthened coupling between posterior hippocampus and left temporal and prefrontal regions during semantic ToTs and enhanced bilateral frontal coupling during episodic ToTs, indicating greater demands on control networks; and (3) gradient analyses showed that, on the third gradient, semantic retrieval, both known and ToT, was positioned toward DMN regions, whereas episodic retrieval occupied more control regions. This suggests that semantic and episodic ToTs depend on different control mechanisms, with semantic ToTs drawing primarily on left-lateralised SCN regions and episodic ToTs recruiting a wider interhemispheric network incorporating both SCN and FPN regions.

During semantic ToTs, left pars triangularis, dlPFC, and dmPFC formed a left-lateralised control network that supported selection and monitoring during partial access, reflecting an SCN that resolves competition when conceptual representations remain accessible but incomplete. However, episodic ToTs did not show marked regional increases relative to episodic known responses but were distinguished by stronger functional coupling between left pars triangularis and right-hemisphere control regions, including right IFG and right dlPFC. This interhemispheric organisation suggests a broader SCN and FPN organisation that compensates for weaker representational evidence, suggesting that episodic retrieval depends more heavily on distributed monitoring and selection mechanisms. Together, these point to a continuum in which semantic failures draw on focused, left-lateralised control, whereas episodic failures recruit wider domain-general systems according to the strength and organisation of the underlying representations.

Semantic ToTs also revealed hippocampal coupling with frontal and temporal regions. Stronger connectivity between the right posterior hippocampus and left temporal pole aligns with long-axis reorganisation, where retrieval shifts from anterior to posterior hippocampal regions as memories become more semantic. Stronger coupling between the right posterior hippocampus and left dlPFC suggests that the hippocampus retrieves relational content through pattern completion while dlPFC maintains the retrieval goal and adapts the search when access is weak.

Regarding the gradient analysis, semantic retrieval, successful and unsuccessful, was positioned toward DMN-related transmodal regions on the third gradient, reflecting reliance on integrated conceptual structure and associative coherence. Episodic retrieval was shifted toward more control-associated regions, suggesting greater dependence on processes that guide search and evaluation when representational evidence is weak. This division may occur because semantic memory is supported by densely interconnected representations, while episodic memory depends more heavily on control mechanisms to stabilise access.

Conclusion
    Semantic ToTs involved increased activation of left-lateralised control regions, including pars triangularis and dlPFC, whereas episodic ToTs were characterised by stronger coupling between left pars triangularis and right-hemisphere control regions. This highlights the left pars triangularis as a flexible hub, shifting from localised engagement during semantic ToTs to interhemispheric connectivity during episodic ToTs. These findings demonstrate that although ToT states share a common phenomenology, their neural mechanisms differ by memory system, reflecting a shift from regionalised to distributed control as retrieval becomes more effortful.
