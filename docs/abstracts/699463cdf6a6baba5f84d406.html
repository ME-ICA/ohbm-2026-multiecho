Introduction

Phantom limb pain (PLP) relates to perceived pain in the missing body part that occurs after severe peripheral nerve injury or amputation in up to 80% of amputees.1 PLP has been associated with peripheral and central alterations as well as with psychological factors. However, it is still unclear how PLP unfolds over time and how potential alterations of the sensory and motor maps of the body in the brain contribute to its development, as well as how these processes causally interact.2 To address this gap, a longitudinal study was conducted to assess cortical excitability in nine lower-limb amputees during the first six months after amputation and with a follow-up 1.5 years post-amputation. The study aims to assess whether changes in the magnitude of neural activation over time, spatial shifts in cortical activation, and the extent of activated cortical regions predict subsequent PLP.

Methods

Nine individuals with lower-limb amputations (age: M = 50, SD = 10.7; 4 females) were assessed within the first six months after amputation (M = 4.3 months, SD = 1.9; session 1) and reassessed 1.5 years post-amputation (M = 17.1 months, SD = 3.2; session 2). Four participants reported phantom limb pain at session 1, and five at session 2. An additional group of nine age- and gender-matched healthy individuals served as a comparison group to examine changes in neural activity over time. Brain responses to noxious heat stimulation were evaluated using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) in a block design. The stimulation was applied over the groin ipsi- and contralateral to the side of amputation at a suprathreshold pain level. The regions of interest (ROIs) include the somatosensory cortex, primary motor cortex, Insula, ACC as well as thalamus. PLP intensity was assessed by a modified German version of the West Haven–Yale Multidimensional Pain Inventory (MPI)3, which assesses  PLP intensity as the mean of current pain, pain over the past week, and pain-related suffering by 7-point Likert scales.FMRI data from right-sided amputees (n = 2) were mirrored along the mid-sagittal plane prior to spatial normalization, ensuring that the deafferented hemisphere was uniformly aligned left-sided for subsequent analyses. To assess potential changes in brain activation over time unrelated to amputation, we performed a whole-brain paired t-test (cluster-forming threshold z = 2.3), comparing the two measurement sessions separately for healthy controls and amputees. This control-group analysis controlled for potential confounding effects of repeated scanning, habituation, attention, or scanner-related variability. Masks from the Juelich histological atlas (binarized, thresholded at 15%; BA3b, BA4a, insula, thalamus, ACC) were used to identify the individual activation peaks for each participant and session. An 8 mm-radius spherical ROI was centered on this peak, within which the mean %BOLD signal change, number of active voxels, and peak coordinates were extracted. This approach enables assessment of intra-individual changes over time and cortical shifts of peak activation within predefined ROIs.For each region of interest (ROI) and corresponding body side, separate linear mixed-effects models were fitted to examine the relationship between PLP and cortical activation. PLP was modeled as the outcome variable, with time, percentage of BOLD signal change, and the number of activated voxels (NVox) included as fixed effects, and subject as a random intercept. Interaction terms between time and mean %BOLD signal change were included to assess whether the relationship between baseline cortical activity and PLP changed over time. To assess whether shifts in the spatial location of activation peaks were associated with changes in PLP, the Euclidean distance between the peak coordinates at session 1 and session 2 was computed for each ROI. Results

Neural activity in the left and right ACC was negatively associated with PLP (right ACC: β = -2.48, p = 0.034 and left ACC: β = –2.08, p = 0.026). In the right insula, contralateral to the amputation site, a higher mean %BOLD signal change was linked to reduced PLP (β = –2.69, p = 0.008) across time. These effects represent the relationship between mean %BOLD signal change and PLP at session 1, which served as the reference level in the model. Importantly, neither the main effect of time nor the time × mean %BOLD signal change interaction was significant, indicating that the magnitude of PLP did not differ significantly between sessions and that the association between mean %BOLD signal change and PLP was stable across session 1 and session 2 on the group level (see Figure 1 for summary).In the left primary somatosensory cortex (BA3b), ipsilateral to the amputation site and contralateral to the thermal stimulation on the right groin, a greater number of activated voxels (NVox) was associated with lower PLP at session 1 (β = -0.012, p = 0.011). The time × NVox interaction was not significant, indicating that this negative association was statistically stable across both sessions. Across ROIs, the Euclidean displacement of activation peaks in the right somatosensory cortex (right BA3b), contralateral to the amputation site, significantly predicted longitudinal PLP changes. Larger shifts of the peak were associated with higher PLP at follow-up (β = 0.029, p = 0.004), while no other ROIs showed significant associations.To control whether changes in brain activation over time could be attributed to general repetition effects rather than amputation-related processes, a whole-brain paired t-test comparing the two sessions was performed. Healthy controls showed no significant differences between sessions, indicating stable activation patterns over time. In contrast, amputees exhibited significant activation changes in several clusters.

Conclusion

In summary, healthy controls showed stable whole-brain activation over time, while amputees exhibited significant longitudinal change. Regarding individual activation changes within predefined ROIs in amputees, reduced PLP was associated with greater neural activation, reflected by higher number of activated voxels in the primary somatosensory cortex and greater mean %BOLD signal change in the ACC and the insula, stable across time. This pattern contrasts with studies reporting positive correlations between PLP intensity and cortical activation, yet aligns with more recent evidence showing heterogeneous, and at times hypoactive, responses in chronic PLP.4 Thus, these regions may contribute directly to PLP maintenance, yet the absence of a significant session × PLP interaction raises the possibility that activation changes occur even earlier post-amputation or are influenced by pre-amputation pain, beyond our assessment window.5 Further, greater shifts of activation peaks in the primary somatosensory cortex were associated with higher subsequent PLP. This suggests that deviations from canonical topography in the contralateral sensorimotor cortex may contribute to persistent pain. To address these findings in more detail, pre- and post-amputation longitudinal designs including larger sample sizes are needed.
