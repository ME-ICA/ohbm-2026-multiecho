Introduction

A central research objective in neuroscience is to understand how molecular and cellular processes support the large-scale organization of brain function. Previously, the molecular substrates influencing functional organization have been predominantly explored from a transcriptomic perspective. However, constrained by cellular translational regulatory activity, transcriptomics cannot directly reflect actual protein abundance or establish direct links with brain function. Meanwhile, the formation of brain functional network phenotypes is complex, and contributions from other scales cannot be overlooked. As the ultimate biological effectors of human brain function, the proteome possesses inherent advantages over the transcriptome for functional characterization, such as better reflecting cortical spatial parcellation (Xiao Q. et al. 2025) and capturing more significant changes in certain neuropsychiatric disorders (Johnson ECB. et al. 2022). Nevertheless, limited data have hindered studies of whole-brain proteomics and functional networks. To address this gap, we utilized the first comprehensive human brain proteomic dataset to elucidate how protein co-expression patterns influence brain functional network organization.

Methods

First, based on the Brainnetome Atlas (Fan L. et al. 2016), we collected proteomic data from the Spatially-resolved Human Brain Proteome Atlas (Xiao Q. et al. 2025) and transcriptomic data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) (Hawrylycz MJ. et al. 2012). After retaining common genes, we obtained gene expression profiles and protein abundance profiles for 4,719 genes across 186 brain regions. The differences in information between the cerebral cortical transcriptome and proteome were examined by comparing correlations between the two profiles across brain regions and genes. Subsequently, 1) we constructed a protein co-expression (PC) matrix by calculating Spearman correlations of protein intensity across brain regions. The functional connectivity (FC) matrix was derived from HCP (Smith, SM. et al., 2013) rs-fMRI data by computing inter-regional time-series correlations using the same cortical parcellation. Using Yeo's 7 networks, we explored the FC-PC relationships within and between functional networks (Figure 1A). 2) Through the "simulated protein depletion" procedure, we quantified the contribution of each protein to FC-PC correlation, identifying key contributory proteins that either promote or inhibit FC formation and their underlying biological processes (Figure 1B). 3) We integrated transcriptomic data to explore the differential influences between molecular levels. 4) We explored network-specifically expressed proteins, analyzing their unique contributions to FC-PC correlation and their roles in corresponding functional networks (Figure 1C). 5) We assessed the associations between key contributory proteins and task performance, as well as their potential implications in brain disorders (Figure 1D).

Results

Here, we utilized the first comprehensive human brain proteomic dataset to elucidate how protein co-expression patterns influence brain functional network organization. We found that protein abundance in the human brain cannot be reliably predicted from mRNA levels alone. Meanwhile, spatial protein co-expression affects functional network formation and further establishes a coupling relationship with network connectivity patterns, which exhibits a hierarchical decline from primary to heteromodal cortex. Through further “simulated protein depletion”, we identified two subsets: a promotive proteome (PCCR_act) and an inhibitory proteome (PCCR_inh). Moreover, compared to the transcriptome, proteins establish a stronger synergistic relationship with functional connectivity (FC) and capture more molecular basis information of FC: co-expression of proteins involved in interneuronal information transmission influences functional network connectivity, and these contributed proteins are enriched in various neuropsychiatric disorders, which was not observed in the transcriptome (Figure 2). Furthermore, differential expression analysis revealed that network-specific proteins, particularly those overexpressed in heteromodal networks, play a more critical facilitating role in the FC-PC correlation. The facilitative effect of key proteins on FC further exhibits associations with behaviors such as emotion and multiple neuropsychiatric disorders.

Conclusion

In summary, this is the first study to explore the foundation of brain functional networks using the new whole-brain human proteomic dataset. Our results demonstrate the necessity of using the proteome, as compared to the transcriptome, to explore functional network organization. Concurrently, we provide convergent multimodal evidence indicating that resting-state functional networks connectivity correlates with the co-expression of proteins linked to interneuronal information transmission, and that these proteins further encode behavioral performance and neuropsychiatric diseases. These results not only expand our understanding of neuroimaging-molecular relationships, but also provide a priority scope for future understanding of how proteins contribute to large-scale functional connectivity, and offer possible micro-macro interacted mechanistic interpretations for brain function-behavior and dysfunction.
