Introduction

Musical skill acquisition is a common framework for studying learning and retention, involving complex cognitive, sensory, and motor integration. Playing an instrument requires coordination among motor skills, auditory processing, spatial perception, cognitive control, and memory 1 , and has been demonstrated to induce widespread experience-driven alterations in brain activity and connectivity 1,2. Some alterations, particularly in auditory and motor areas, persist long after training ends 3. In contrast, without sufficient practice essential to retain the acquired skill, functional connectivity within the modulated sensorimotor networks often returns to baseline 3. 

In this work, we investigated whether targeted audio-visual memory reactivations following initial musical learning can facilitate skill retention and preserve neural plasticity in the absence of ongoing practice. Previous studies have shown that brief memory reactivations can enhance basic visual and motor skill acquisition4, however the practical relevance to daily life ecological skills and their underlying neural mechanisms remains unknown. We used a piano training protocol for initial acquisition of the musical skill, followed by brief audio-visual memory reactivations spread over several weeks. fMRI scans were acquired before and after initial learning (pre-learning, post-learning), and after one month (retention). Behavioral retention was evaluated by comparing post-learning and retention performance. We hypothesized that reactivations will enhance skill retention and induce distinct functional connectivity patterns relative to controls not undergoing reactivations. Identifying the neurobehavioral mechanisms underlying reactivation-based facilitation of such skill retention could ultimately inform strategies to enhance learning efficiency and support the long-term retention of complex daily-life skills.

Methods

Thirty-eight healthy, right-handed young adults with no prior musical experience participated in the study (mean: 26.34, sd: 4.13, 20 females). Participants completed a five-session piano-learning protocol. During the first four sessions, participants learned to play Beethoven’s Für Elise on an electric keyboard using ‘pyano’, an in-house program designed for gradual and uniform piano training, progressing from single-handed practice to full bimanual performance. All keystrokes were digitally recorded for note-level analysis. Resting-state fMRI scans were acquired during the first (pre-learning) and fourth (post-learning) sessions, and in a fifth session (retention) following a month during which participants were instructed not to practice. Behavioral retention was evaluated by comparing post-learning and retention performance. Participants were randomly assigned after the post-training session to either a reactivation group or a passive control group without reactivations. 

The reactivation stimulus consisted of a 30-second audio-visual clip of the learned melody, identical to its presentation during training. Participants received reminders to watch the clip once in a quiet environment at high volume every 4 days (7 exposures in total) (Figure 1). Behavioral measures included accuracy (correct notes divided by total notes) and rhythm (timing deviation between consecutive notes relative to the required tempo). These scores were computed in post-learning and retention sessions. To evaluate performance under increasing demands, we analyzed accuracy and rhythm for the complete melody under different tempo conditions: No-Tempo, Tempo=60, Tempo=80, and Tempo=100. As an additional measure of task efficiency, we counted the extra attempts beyond the minimum required to complete the session at each time point, allowing us to capture both the rate of progress and the number of repetitions required to achieve flawless performance. Group-level and within-subject comparisons were performed using independent and paired t-tests, respectively, for each behavioral measure. 

Resting-state fMRI data were analyzed to extract functional connectivity matrices using the Schaefer-100 cortical parcellation5 . Parcel-wise time series were extracted, and the pairwise Pearson’s correlations were computed across brain regions, for each participant and time point (pre-training, post-training, and retention). To account for inter-individual variability in baseline connectivity, post-learning and retention connectivity maps were residualized with respect to each participant’s pre-training connectivity, isolating the component of neural change attributable to learning or to the reactivation manipulation. Paired t-tests were then used to assess post-learning-to-retention changes within each group, and independent t-tests were used to evaluate between-group differences. Correlation values were Fisher Z-transformed, and multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure (p = .05). Both thresholded and unthresholded Z-score matrices were visualized to characterize the spatial distribution of connectivity changes. We further examined group differences in resting-state connectivity changes from post-learning to retention. Specifically, we identified edges showing the largest between-session modifications and the greatest divergence between the reactivation and control groups. The top 10 edges for each group were visualized as circular graphs to illustrate the strongest network-level interaction. To characterize large-scale network reorganization during retention, we quantified the number of significantly modulated functional connectivity edges between all pairs of canonical resting-state networks. For each network pair, edges were counted separately for positive and negative changes in connectivity and analyzed independently within the Reactivation and Control groups.

Results

Behavioral analyses demonstrated significant group differences in retention under challenging tempo demands. The control group exhibited significant deterioration in both accuracy and rhythm (accuracy: 0.47 ± 0.06 to 0.29 ± 0.06, p = .0037; rhythm: 0.52 ± 0.06 to 0.26 ± 0.06, p < .001; Fig. 2A–B), whereas the reactivation group showed no significant decline (accuracy: 0.44 ± 0.06 to 0.41 ± 0.07, p = .35; rhythm: 0.48 ± 0.06 to 0.44 ± 0.07, p = .17; Fig. 2A–B). This divergence produced a significant between-group rhythm difference at retention (p = .024; Fig. 2B), indicating superior long-term retention following brief reactivations. As expected, group differences were not robust for less-challenging tempo conditions, with performance either remaining stable across groups or declining similarly over time. Finally, task-efficiency analyses showed that the reactivation group exhibited a significantly greater reduction in extra attempts across sessions than controls (p = .0187; Fig. 2C), reflecting more efficient retrieval of the acquired skill during the retention session.

Resting-state connectivity analyses revealed distinct, group-specific patterns of connectivity change from post-learning to retention (Figure 2D-E). In the reactivation group, the 10 strongest edges linked the default mode and frontoparietal control networks, as well as default mode interactions with sensorimotor and visual networks, alongside selective decreases in limbic–salience and limbic–control coupling (Figure 2G). In contrast, the strongest edges in the control group were characterized by increased connectivity among the somatomotor, visual, dorsal attention, and salience networks (Figure 2G). To quantify these network-level patterns beyond individual edges, we next examined the distribution of significant connectivity changes across pairs of large-scale networks (Figure 2H). This analysis revealed that the Reactivation group exhibited a higher prevalence of positively modulated edges involving default mode, frontoparietal control, and sensorimotor networks, whereas the Control group showed relatively more negative connectivity changes across several network pairs, thereby providing a quantitative complement to the edge-level effects observed in Figure 2G.

Together, results show that brief reactivations stabilize musical skill after learning, particularly when task difficulty increases, and modulate offline long-term functional‑network reorganization.

Conclusion

Brief audio‑visual reactivations during offline periods support long-term retention of musical performance. They are associated with the modulation of resting‑state networks, particularly involving large-scale systems such as the default mode, frontoparietal control, and sensorimotor networks. Rather than merely protecting against decay, memory reactivation appears to facilitate network reorganization, potentially strengthening motor-auditory connections over time. Notably, these changes at the network level differ from those seen in the control group, indicating that reactivation doesn't simply maintain existing connectivity patterns. Instead, it triggers a distinct mode of large-scale reorganization during retention. Importantly, these effects were achieved through a highly minimal intervention, brief audiovisual reactivations totaling 2.5 minutes distributed across an entire month, yet were sufficient to produce measurable differences in both behavioral retention and large-scale resting-state network organization. These findings identify reactivation as a scalable mechanism for maintaining complex skill learning over time. Beyond the musical domain as a model for ecological skills, this work highlights the possibility of using low-effort interventions to stabilize daily-life human skills, with potential applications for rehabilitation, expertise acquisition, and education.
