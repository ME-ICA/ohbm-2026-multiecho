Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is characterized by persistent anxiety-inducing thoughts (obsessions) that can lead to repetitive anxiety-reducing behaviors (compulsions). The current task-based fMRI literature on OCD implies dysfunctional interactions within and across the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuit, which notably helps the brain to adaptively generate and update internal models that guide action selection1. This ability to internally model the temporal and contextual structure of its environment is a form of “forward modelling” that mitigates the demands on prediction error monitoring2. However, changes in stimulus – response predictability uncouple the stimulus from the required response and shift brain function from being prediction-driven to being reactive. Plausibly, OCD impacts both predictive and reactive competence3, and therefore certainty (or uncertainty) in the perception-reaction-action cycle may exert differential effects on brain network interactions4. These interactions are usually captured on a bivariate basis (with methods such as traditional 2nd order undirected functional connectivity, uFC). Recent findings suggesting that global brain function can also be captured higher-order functional connectivity (HOFC) methods motivate an extension of these methods to fMRI data in OCD5. HOFC defines the functional resemblance between two regions based on the similarity or dissimilarity of their connectivity profiles across the entire network. Here, we provide the first application of HOFC to study the effects of certainty or uncertainty in the perception-reaction-action cycle on brain network function in OCD youth and typical controls.

Methods

A total of 63 participants (HC = 26) were enrolled in this study (all provided consent or assent, and all methods were approved by the WSU Institutional Review Board). Gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) fMRI data were obtained [3 Tesla Siemens Verio system; 12-channel volume head coil (TR: 2.6 s, TE: 29 ms, FOV: 256×256mm2, acquisition matrix: 128×128, 36 axial slices, voxel dimensions: 2×2 x 3 mm3); 3D T1-weighted anatomical MRI image was also acquired (TR: 2200 ms, TI: 778 ms, TE: 3 ms, flip angle = 13°, FOV: 256×256mm2, 256 axial slices of thickness = 1.0 mm, matrix = 256×256)]. Image processing was performed in SPM12 using established methods for temporal (slice timing correction) and spatial correction (realignment and normalization). In first-level models, task epochs were modeled as boxcar functions convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function forming regressors of interest. In each participant’s first-level model, the six motion parameters (3 for translation and 3 for rotation) derived from realignment were modeled as covariates of no interest. Images exceeding 4 mm of movement (<1% of all images) were excised from analyses without replacement.

The task (Figure 1a) manipulated the uncertainty of the stimulus-response mapping by a) altering the response schedule (based on stimulus identity) thereby requiring participants to respond to every stimulus in the sequence (Go) or respond only to stimuli with a specific identity (NoGo), and b) maintaining a fixed inter-stimulus interval between successively presented stimuli (Periodic) or pseudo-randomly varying this interval (Pseudorandom). The four conditions formed from this factorial combination ranged from high certainty (C3U0) to high uncertainty (C0U3). 

From the modelled fMRI data, we averaged the time series data of voxels within each of the 246 functionally derived cerebral parcels of the Brainnetome atlas. The pairwise zero-lag Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated from the time series data for all possible region pairs (30,135 unique pairs). This resulted in four condition-specific symmetric uFC matrices (246x246 elements)(Figure 1b). Fischer’s r to z transformation was used to transform all FC matrix values into z-scores. The estimated uFC matrices were submitted for HOFC estimation. Here, from the 246 distinct uFC vector (containing the connectivity of each region with the remaining 245 regions) we calculated pairwise Pearson's correlations. The resultant HOFC matrices (Figure 1c) represent the similarity between the functional roles of all pairs of regions (again, 30,135 unique pairs). A region pair with high HOFC indicates that two regions play similar integrative roles with respect to all other regions in the connectome, whereas a low HOFC value indicates antagonistic roles across the connectome.

Inter-group (HC ≠ OCD) differences in HOFC were estimated using 2-sample t-tests. Significance testing was derived by creating null distributions from the data (3,496 x 104 permutation experiments). Observed t-values were labelled as significant if ±2.5 s.d. from the t values in the null distribution. We also identified regions where changes in HOFC were monotonically related to changes in task uncertainty using Spearman’s Rho (ρ; p<.05).

Results

There was widespread loss of HOFC in the OCD group regardless of task condition (C1U2 excepted). This effect was pronounced across frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital, and sub-lobar regions (we do not show these results for limitations of space). Figure 2 depicts the results from investigating monotonicity. As seen in Figure 2a, in HC, the HOFC between several pairs of regions distributed across the cortex and sub-cortex was highly responsive to increases in uncertainty in the perception-reaction-action cycle, suggesting that cooperative reconfiguration of sensory and cognitive networks in the neurotypical brain is flexibly related to task context. Converse effects were observed in OCD (Figure 2b), with increasingly antagonistic HOFC as a function of increased uncertainty.

Conclusion

In this study, we manipulated the uncertainty of the perception-reaction-action cycle (in four task conditions), and assessed its effects on HOFC in HC and OCD participants. Regardless of task-condition, HOFC was lower in the OCD group. Moreover, HC HOFC was highly responsive to changes in the uncertainty of the stimulus response relationship, with a converse result seen in OCD.

These findings reveal how task certainty and uncertainty disrupt global functional network architecture in OCD. This system-wide fragmentation provides a mechanistic framework for understanding why individuals with OCD experience persistent difficulties adapting to changing environments and may explain the rigidity and compulsivity central to the disorder’s clinical presentation.
