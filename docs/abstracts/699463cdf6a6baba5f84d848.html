Introduction

Music has the remarkable ability to elicit bodily sensations, which are intertwined with the accompanying emotional experience. Previous research has highlighted the importance of both emotions and bodily sensations in shaping our perception and appreciation of music. Recent research has revealed distinct patterns of bodily sensations elicited by various emotion categories,¹ providing compelling evidence for the embodiment of musical experiences. In parallel, neuroimaging studies have indicated that the somato-motor brain networks’ activity is modulated by the emotional effects of listening to music, and that distinct emotions can be decoded from the activity patterns.² Altogether, significant progress has been made in understanding the neural correlates of music-induced emotions and the bodily aspects of music perception, yet the neural underpinnings of embodied musical emotions remain poorly understood. This study aims to investigate the neural activity patterns underlying distinct embodied music-induced emotions. Specifically, we examine the similarity between the representational geometry of the neural patterns and the self-reported bodily sensations induced by distinct musical emotions. 

Methods

Female participants (n=70) underwent a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) while listening to emotionally engaging music excerpts. The emotion categories studied were aggressiveness, happiness, sadness, and fear. Stimuli consisted of five 45-second music excerpts for each emotion category (20 in total), presented in a fixed pseudo-random order, with 15-second silent breaks between excerpts. The music excerpts were selected from Western songs which had previously received high ratings for the corresponding emotion category.¹ Following the scanning session, participants reported their bodily sensations while re-listening to the musical excerpts outside the scanner. Using the emBODY tool,³ they colored the body parts where they experienced some activation. To identify brain areas activated by each music-induced emotion category as compared to periods of silence, a full-volume general linear model (GLM) fitting was performed. To map where the different emotional categories were felt in the body,  bodily sensation maps for each emotion category were obtained using pixelwise one-sample t-tests against zero across participants. The neural patterns and the bodily sensations associated with each song were compared using representational similarity analysis (RSA). Subject-wise first-level beta maps for each song (music vs. silence) were extracted and masked using predefined ROIs. To enable cross-subject comparison, the extracted beta maps were hyperaligned into a common representational space.⁴ Within this shared space, the dimensionality of the individual data was reduced using principal component analysis (PCA), thereby retaining the most informative components while minimizing noise. Then, using the RSA toolbox,⁵ representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMS) were constructed for each subject and each ROI by correlating the hyperaligned neural patterns of each song with those of every other song. The RDMs contained spatial correlation values (1 - Pearson’s correlation coefficient), reflecting the dissimilarity of neural patterns across songs. In parallel, bodily sensation maps were created for each song, and t-values extracted from pixels within the body maps were correlated across songs to generate a model RDM. Finally, for each ROI, neural RDMs were correlated with the model RDM using Spearman’s rank correlation. Statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. 

Results

The GLM analysis revealed significantly higher BOLD responses during music listening compared to silence across motor, auditory and emotion-related brain regions (Figure 1). Specifically, activation was observed within the bilateral precentral gyrus (PRECG), supplementary motor area (SMA), superior temporal gyrus (STG), middle temporal gyrus (MTG), Heschl’s gyrus (HG), insula, and limbic regions (putamen and caudate). The bodily sensation maps suggested emotion-specific bodily activation topographies: aggressiveness was associated with increased sensations in the head, happiness with widespread activation across the torso and extremities, sadness with sensations concentrated in the chest and the head, and fear with heightened activation in the chest and abdomen. RSA further revealed significant correspondences between the representational geometries of neural activity patterns and bodily sensations maps (Figure 2). Strong similarities were observed within bilateral PRECG and SMA, the left cerebellum, bilateral limbic regions and insula, left anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and bilateral STG, MTG, and HG. 

Conclusion

Together, these findings demonstrate that music-induced emotions engage a distributed network of motor, auditory, and limbic regions. The bodily sensation maps further highlight the embodied nature of musical emotions, suggesting that each category is associated with distinct bodily activation topographies. Crucially, the representational similarity analysis revealed that the neural representational geometries closely mirror the geometries of the bodily sensation maps, particularly within motor, interoceptive, limbic and auditory regions. This convergence suggests that embodied experiences of musical emotions are reflected in shared representational structures across brain and body, providing novel evidence for the neural basis of embodied musical emotion.
