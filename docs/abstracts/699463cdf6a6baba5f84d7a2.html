Introduction

It's a major challenge in neuroscience and clinical care to understand why some patients show signs of minimal consciousness after severe brain injury, while others remain unaware. In this study, we used a physiologically-informed cortico-thalamic model that describes how different neural populations fire and communicate to generate the electrical signals measured by EEG. By fitting the model to recordings of two large independent datasets of patients and healthy participants, we found that it could reliably reproduce the individual brain activity of different groups. The model parameters that describe the communication between the cortex and thalamus populations were found to be different between patient groups. Furthermore, the model was also able to reproduce important patterns of complexity of the real recordings, as the complexity measures obtained from the simulations were found to be highly correlated with the empirical ones and differed between patients who showed minimal consciousness and patients with unresponsive wakefulness. Together, these findings offer new insight into the brain mechanisms that support consciousness after injury and demonstrate the potential of biophysical modelling to create personalized models for future therapeutic applications.

Methods

Datasets

We analyzed two large, independent high-density EEG datasets comprising both resting-state and local–global (LG) paradigm recordings. The combined sample included 277 patients with unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (UWS), 340 patients in a minimally conscious state (MCS), and 74 healthy controls. These datasets provided complementary and independent cohorts to assess the robustness of the modeling approach across recording conditions and populations.

EEG Preprocessing and Spectral Analysis

Following standard preprocessing procedures, power spectral densities were computed from artifact-free EEG epochs. Spectral estimates were obtained for (i) the average signal across all 183 electrodes, and separately for (ii) frontal and parietal regions of interest ^1, defined as the average of electrode Fz and Pz together with their eight immediately surrounding electrodes, respectively. These regional analyses were motivated by theoretical frameworks emphasizing the role of fronto-parietal and posterior cortical networks in conscious processing.

Cortico-Thalamic Model Fitting

A cortico-thalamic neural mass model ^2 was fitted to the empirical EEG spectra using a Markov chain random walk optimization procedure. The fitted parameters reflect key aspects of neural firing dynamics and connectivity within cortico-thalamic circuits. This approach enabled the identification of parameter configurations that differentiate healthy controls from patients with disorders of consciousness (DoC), as well as distinctions between UWS and MCS.

Complexity Analysis

To assess whether the model captured higher-order temporal structure beyond spectral features, complexity measures were computed for both empirical and simulated time series ^3 across epochs. Specifically, we estimated Lempel–Ziv complexity (LZC), spectral entropy (SE), and permutation entropy (PE) ^4. Permutation entropy was computed in the theta band using an embedding dimension of 3, based on prior evidence demonstrating its sensitivity in discriminating between UWS and MCS patients. The implementation followed established procedures described in the literature.

Statistical Analysis

Model performance was evaluated by assessing the correspondence between empirical and simulated measures using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. In addition, we examined the influence of inter-patient variability on model parameters and performance, including clinical indices such as the Coma Recovery Scale–Revised (CRS-R) score and time since onset (TSO). Together, these analyses allowed us to evaluate the model's ability to reproduce both spectral and complexity profiles of EEG activity across different brain states and levels of consciousness.

Results

Group Differences in Model Parameters

The cortico-thalamic model accurately reproduced the empirical spectra across all groups, with low chi-square fitting errors and no significant differences in fitting quality between healthy controls (HC), minimally conscious state (MCS), and unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (UWS) patients.

Across both the Liège and Paris datasets, Kruskal–Wallis tests revealed significant group effects in several model parameters, primarily distinguishing healthy controls from patients. No significant differences were observed between frontal and parietal regions, and subregional results are therefore reported only in the supplementary material. Moreover, none of the model parameters showed significant correlations with time since onset (TSO) or Coma Recovery Scale–Revised (CRS-R) scores.

Five parameters consistently differed between controls and patients in both datasets. Compared with HC, patients exhibited a reduced excitatory corticothalamic loop gain (G_ese), increased inhibitory corticothalamic gain (G_esre), increased intrathalamic loop gain (G_srs), longer corticothalamic delays (t₀), and higher EMG-related components (P_EMG). Pairwise comparisons confirmed that these effects were significant between HC and both patient groups in both datasets.

Differences between MCS and UWS were less consistent. In the Paris dataset, MCS patients showed higher G_ese values and longer t₀ compared with UWS patients, whereas no significant MCS–UWS differences were detected in the Liège dataset, although median G_ese values followed the same trend.

Complexity Measures and Model Validation

We next examined whether the model could reproduce empirical complexity features. No significant differences were found between frontal and parietal channels for any complexity metric; therefore, results are reported for whole-brain signals only.

For Lempel–Ziv complexity (LZC), empirical data showed a significant group effect in the Paris dataset, with HC exhibiting higher complexity than both MCS and UWS. In simulated data, LZC was consistently higher in HC than in patient groups across both datasets. Importantly, empirical and simulated LZC values were significantly correlated within each group, with the strongest correlations observed in healthy controls and progressively weaker correlations in MCS and UWS.

Spectral entropy (SE) differed significantly across groups in both datasets, with HC showing higher entropy than both patient groups in empirical and simulated data. No significant differences were observed between MCS and UWS. Moderate-to-strong correlations were found between empirical and simulated SE values across all groups, again decreasing with reduced levels of consciousness.

Permutation entropy in the theta band (PE_θ) showed the clearest group separation. In both datasets, PE_θ was significantly higher in HC than in patients, in both empirical and simulated data. In the Paris dataset, MCS patients exhibited higher PE_θ than UWS patients, a pattern that was also reproduced by the model. Correlations between empirical and simulated PE_θ were high across groups, particularly in HC and MCS.

Influence of Clinical Variables

Supplementary analyses revealed higher complexity values in patients with TSO greater than one year, particularly in the Paris dataset. Correlations between CRS-R scores and complexity measures were weak or absent, indicating that model-derived and complexity-based markers capture information that is partly independent of behavioral assessments.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate that biophysically grounded neural field models provide a powerful framework for interpreting high-density EEG data in disorders of consciousness (DoC). By fitting a corticothalamic model to large, independent EEG datasets, we show that model-derived parameters capture systematic alterations in thalamocortical interactions that distinguish healthy controls from patients, and in some cases differentiate minimally conscious state (MCS) from unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (UWS). These findings are in line with the mesocircuit hypothesis ^5, and reinforce the central role of thalamocortical loops in shaping large-scale brain dynamics underlying conscious awareness.

Beyond reproducing spectral power features, the model successfully generated synthetic time series that preserved key complexity signatures of empirical EEG recordings. The strong correspondence between real and simulated complexity measures—particularly theta-band permutation entropy—indicates that the model captures not only second-order statistics, but also higher-order temporal structure of brain activity. This ability to link abstract model parameters to observable complexity metrics highlights the value of computational modeling for bridging mechanistic interpretations and data-driven biomarkers in brain imaging.

In this model, the parameters offer an interpretable low-dimensional representation of neural circuit function, allowing EEG signals to be understood in terms of excitatory–inhibitory balance, effective connectivity, and transmission delays within corticothalamic networks. Importantly, these parameters capture information that is partially dissociated from behavioral scales such as CRS-R, suggesting that modeling can reveal latent neural states not directly accessible through clinical observation alone.

The differential sensitivity of resting-state versus task-based (local–global) datasets further underscores the importance of experimental context when interpreting brain imaging data. Computational models provide a way to integrate such context by explaining how external stimulation or task engagement modulates underlying circuit dynamics, thereby enhancing sensitivity to residual consciousness.

Overall, this work supports the growing role of computational modeling in human brain mapping: not merely as a tool for fitting data, but as a means to interpret neural signals, test hypotheses about circuit dysfunction, and simulate individualized brain dynamics. By linking EEG spectra and complexity to specific corticothalamic mechanisms, such models offer a promising avenue for mechanistic biomarkers, virtual perturbation experiments, and ultimately personalized strategies for diagnosis and intervention in disorders of consciousness.
