Introduction

Functional language lateralisation is a hallmark of human brain organisation, yet its structural basis remains unresolved. While classical models emphasise left-hemispheric dominance for language processing, diffusion MRI has revealed substantial variability in the asymmetry of key language pathways1, raising the question of whether such variability reflects discrete subtypes or a continuous spectrum. Leveraging high-resolution Human Connectome Project data and advanced tractography, we investigated lateralisation profiles across major language-recruited white matter tracts. We combined Bayesian modelling, permutation-based variability analysis, and manifold learning to characterise both central tendencies and dispersion of structural asymmetry and to determine whether participants cluster into distinct lateralisation phenotypes or exhibit continuous variation.

Methods

The study involved 172 healthy participants (29.5 ± 3.6 years, 60.5% females) from the Human Connectome Project's (HCP) 7T diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) datasets2. Data underwent preprocessing using the HCP default pipeline (v3.19.03), addressing field, motion, and geometric distortions with FSL's TOPUP and EDDY functions. Whole-brain fibre orientation distribution (FOD) estimation used StarTrack software (https://nbl-research.github.io/startrack.html) in the native DWI space. Spherical deconvolutions employed a damped Richardson-Lucy algorithm4 with a fixed fibre response (α = 1.5 × 10–3 mm2 s−1) and a geometric damping parameter, involving 200 iterations.

For tractography, we set an absolute threshold (3x the spherical FOD of a grey matter isotropic voxel) and a relative threshold (8% of the maximum amplitude of the FOD; Thiebaut de Schotten et al., 2011). Streamline tractography used a modified Euler algorithm with a 35° angle threshold, 0.5 mm step size, and a 15 mm minimum streamline length. The fixed absolute threshold, 0.0036, aligns with previous studies5, informed by post-mortem Klingler dissections. Structural connectome data was registered to the MNI space.

Seven bilateral language-recruited tracts were dissected manually following established anatomical protocols: frontal aslant tract (FAT), inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), uncinate fasciculus (UF), and anterior, long, and posterior arcuate fasciculus segments (AFa, AFl, Afp see Figure 1). Dissections were performed by a trained rater and verified by expert neuroanatomists, followed by streamline cleaning via MegaTrack. Microstructural indices (HMOA) and macrostructural measurements (tract count, TC, and volume, VC) were extracted for each tract.

The lateralisation index (LI) was calculated as (R-L)/(R+L), with negative values indicating left lateralisation, positive values indicating right lateralisation, and an LI of 0 for bilateral distribution (Thiebaut de Schotten et al., 2011). Lateralisation indices were evaluated using Bayesian intercept-only models (brms/Stan) with truncated Cauchy(0,0.707) priors; evidence was quantified using Bayes factors, with robustness assessed across prior widths. This Bayesian framework allows for the direct quantification of evidence in favor of both the alternative and the null hypothesis - something that traditional frequentist tests cannot provide, as they do not offer evidence for the null. Interhemispheric dispersion was assessed using a Variability Index (VI) based on median absolute deviation (MAD) as follows: VI = (MAD(R)-MAD(L))/(MAD(R)+MAD(L)), with significance evaluated via 100,000 paired-sample permutations and Bonferroni correction.

Associations with handedness (Edinburgh Handedness Inventory) were tested using Bayesian hierarchical models with leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO). Behavioural correlates were assessed using NIH Toolbox, including Picture Vocabulary, Oral Reading, Tone Discrimination, and story-comprehension accuracy and response times. Dimensionality reduction (UMAP) characterised potential clustering of participants based on LI patterns.

Results

Bayesian analyses revealed robust tract-specific lateralisations. AFl, ILF, and FAT showed strong leftward lateralisation across metrics (Bayes factors >10⁴). AFa and UF showed strong rightward lateralisation (Bayes factors >10⁷). IFOF and AFp demonstrated mixed or metric-dependent evidence, indicating bilateral or weakly lateralised profiles (see Figure 2A).

Interhemispheric variability (VI) showed heterogeneous patterns independent of average lateralisation. ILF and FAT exhibited greater left-hemisphere variability (e.g., FAT (TC) VI = −0.58, p < 0.00001), whereas AFa and UF showed greater right-hemisphere variability (e.g., UF (VC) VI = 0.32, p = 0.00002). Other tracts showed balanced interhemispheric dispersion (see Figure 2B).

Handedness did not significantly predict LI patterns (BF₁₀ = 0.03), and structural lateralisation did not improve the prediction of any language behavioural measure (BF range = 0.86–1.09, see Figure 2C upper panel).; LOO did not favour full models (see Figure 2C lower panel).

UMAP revealed diffuse, continuous topography without distinct clusters, indicating that structural language lateralisation varies along a continuum rather than discrete subgroups (see Figure 2D).

Conclusion

The human language connectome exhibits a graded continuum of structural lateralisation rather than categorical subtypes. While population-level asymmetries are tract-specific and robust, interindividual variability and dispersion patterns reveal complex hemispheric specialisation not captured by mean lateralisation alone. The absence of clustering and limited behavioural correlations suggests that language lateralisation is an emergent, network-level property shaped by distributed dynamics rather than fixed anatomical types. These findings challenge binary classifications of language dominance and support a continuous model of hemispheric specialisation with implications for neurodevelopmental and clinical frameworks.
