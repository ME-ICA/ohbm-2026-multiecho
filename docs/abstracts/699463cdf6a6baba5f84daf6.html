Introduction

Successful social coordination requires not only motor synchronization but also a subjective state of shared attention, often described as "social flow" or "involvement." While hyperscanning studies have frequently identified inter-brain synchrony during group behaviors, the intra-brain oscillatory mechanisms that support this subjective experience remain under-explored. We hypothesized that the sensation of being "involved" in a group task is mediated by alpha-band inhibition, which suppresses irrelevant sensory streams to maintain a shared cognitive set.

Methods

We engaged 29 quartets of female participants (N=116) in an EEG hyperscanning study. Groups performed a joint music-playing task using single-string instruments under five conditions: Random (baseline), Synchrony (unison), Turn-taking (sequential), Imitation (delayed replication), and Cooperation (harmonized joint action). Subjective experiences were assessed using a validated questionnaire (Q2: Involvement), rating the level of engagement while playing. We analyzed Phase Locking Values (PLV) within subjects (Self-PLV) across Delta, Theta, Alpha, and Beta bands, correlating these neural markers with behavioral scores.

Results

A distinct spectral signature emerged for the Cooperation condition. We found a significant positive correlation between subjective "Involvement" scores and Alpha-band (8–14 Hz) PLV in the parietal cortex (P3: r=0.523, p=0.021; P4: r=0.603, p=0.006; Pz: r=0.470, p=0.042). This effect was condition-specific; it was absent in the Random and Synchrony conditions. Furthermore, higher involvement was associated with a downregulation of Delta-band activity in the right parietal region (P4: r=-0.581, p=0.009), suggesting a suppression of idling rhythms during high-engagement social states.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that the subjective feeling of being "involved" in a cooperative group is supported by a specific neurophysiological mechanism: alpha-band phase stability in the parietal cortex. We propose that parietal alpha acts as a "gating" mechanism during social cooperation, facilitating the integration of self-and-other information by inhibiting distraction. This provides a robust neural correlate for social flow in multi-person interactions.
