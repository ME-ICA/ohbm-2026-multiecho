Introduction

Magnetoencephalography (MEG) using optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) is becoming increasingly popular within the MEG community. This technology overcomes several limitations of conventional SQUID-based systems. In particular, OPMs eliminate the need for liquid helium, reducing both system cost and maintenance requirements, and allow sensors to be placed closer to the scalp with flexible positioning. However, OPM-based MEG systems still require a magnetically shielded room (MSR) for operation. MSRs are expensive—often costing as much as three or more complete OP-MEG systems—and impose strict constraints on installation locations. This work presents an attempt to eliminate the need for a magnetically shielded room in OP-MEG research, thereby enabling wider accessibility and adoption of MEG.

Methods

Instead of using a typical passively magnetically shielded room (built from mu-metal, copper and aluminium), we built an actively shielded OPM system (Fig. 1) that provides compensation of the magnetic field at a fraction of the weight (3000 Kg, vs 75 Kg). The system performs active control of the three orthogonal DC components of the magnetic field as well as the first eight field gradients. The system consists of six square Helmholtz coils (2 × 2 m), with two pairs in each orthogonal direction. These coils are used to control the three DC field components and two independent longitudinal gradient components. In addition, three sets of Golay coils (each loop measuring 2 × 0.4 m) [1] are used to control three independent transverse gradient components. The use of two pairs of Helmholtz coils in each direction allows for coarse passive field nulling, with active feedback control applied on top – AC control. Three tri-axial Bartington Mag-03 fluxgate sensors are used as reference sensors to provide feedback for the coil control system. The cage nulls the magnetic field within a 30 × 30 × 30 cm³ volume at its centre and is therefore designed for stationary (non-moving) experiments only. 
To evaluate the system, we used a simulated brain-like signal generated by a small coil. We simulated 1-second epochs: in the first half-second, our phantom is turned off, in the next half-second, the phantom produces a 15Hz burst of magnetic field. This signal is comparable in magnitude to a large inter-ictal discharge and shows that the system is capable of recording signals comparable in magnitude to brain activity. 

(Figure 1: Active shielding cage composed of Helmholtz coils (one set annotated as A) for DC field and longitudinal gradients compensation and Golay coils (cooper wire loops; one half of the set annotated as B) for transverse gradient control.)

Results

1) The active shielding system reduces the ambient magnetic field from 60,000nT at DC to <200nT. This allows for the operation of OPM sensors.

2) Using analog PID controllers, we reduce the interference from 1000nT to < 6nT.  

3) Further residual field changes are minimised with software methods (signal space projection) by a factor of 30.

4) Finally, we validated the approach with a phantom recording of signals comparable in magnitude to brain signals using a 32-channel Mag4health OPM system. (Fig. 2)

(Figure 2: Phantom recording with 16 OPMs operating outside of a magnetically shielded room. The Y axis shows the recorded field over time (x-axis) in units of pT. In the first half second, our phantom is turned off. In the next half second, the phantom produces a 15Hz burst of magnetic field. )

Conclusion

We show that, with active shielding, MAG4Health OPM sensors are capable of measuring brain-like signals, demonstrating the feasibility and potential of our approach. This solution has the potential to reduce the overall cost of an OPM-MEG system by about a factor of 10, significantly lowering the barrier to deployment and enabling wider adoption of MEG in clinical and research settings, including environments where magnetically shielded rooms are impractical or prohibitively expensive. Future work will focus on recording human brain signals. We also hope to validate other available OPM systems.
