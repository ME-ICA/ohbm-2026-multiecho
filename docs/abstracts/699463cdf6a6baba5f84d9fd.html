Introduction

A growing body of circadian neuroscience research shows that diurnal physiology, sleep–wake state, and associated fluid and microstructural dynamics are linked to measurable within-day fluctuations across multiple imaging contrasts and derived brain phenotypes. These findings highlight time of scan as a systematic, physiology-linked source of variance in neuroimaging data [1]. However, most existing studies of circadian brain change are limited to small samples and longitudinal designs with few individuals. leaving population-level evidence on the effects of time of day (TOD) remains largely lacking.

Normative modeling (NM) has been widely applied in neuroimaging to characterize brain measures across the lifespan [2,3] by estimating expected age-related trajectories and population variability. NM learns the conditional distribution of biological measures given relevant covariates in a healthy reference population, thereby establishing quantitative “reference charts.” Rather than focusing on group averages, NM quantifies individual-level deviations from an expected reference range, offering clear advantages over traditional case–control approaches.

In this work, we introduce a diurnal time-of-day normative model (todNM) as an extension of a baseline lifespan normative model (blNM). We fitted region-wise normative models across the brain and evaluated both models on an independent test set comprising healthy individuals and multiple clinical cohorts. To quantify the impact of TOD on normative estimates, we compared within-subject z-scores derived from todNM and blNM. Multiple brain regions showed robust differences between models. To assess whether TOD effects depend on clinical status, we tested model × diagnosis interactions. Finally, in a longitudinal test set, incorporating TOD altered which regions exhibited significant within-subject change between visits, suggesting that TOD-aware normative models may improve sensitivity for detecting longitudinal change.

Methods

We collected T1w MRI scans from various open access datasets, including ADNI, BANDA, CAMCAN, HCP1200, HCPAging, HCPDev, HCPEP, NKIRS, OASIS, PING, and 48 datasets from OpenNeuro. The acquisition time is extracted (if possible) from .json sidecar of the scans. All raw scans are preprocessed with FreeSurfer 7.4.1 on the Denbi server. The volume of aparc regions and aseg regions are extracted from the FreeSurfer results as response for NM. We modelled in total 108 regions with enough data: 38 regions from aseg, 68 regions from aparc, plus two global measures ICV and cerebellum volume. 

The whole scan collection is split into three datasets: i) Train set, which is composed of healthy cohort (HC), with each contributes one scan; ii) Cross test set, contains HC and disease cohort. This set also have one unique scan per subject, whom do not overlap with train set; iii) Long test set, this set serves the purpose of validating TOD effect’s within subjects. It contains 2 same-diagnose scans per subject with TOD difference of two scans >= 2 hours. Train set has subject N=10577, 55% female (F), age 3—100 y, mean=35.12, std=22.45; Cross test set has subject N=4726, 49% F, age 6—97.1 y, mean=61.46, std=18.38, with 1869 healthy, 1032 Parkinsonism, 612 AD,  552 MCI, 319 Depression, 168 Anxiety, 85 Schizophrenia, 64 ADHD, and 25 Bipolar; Long test set has subject (scan pair) N=909, including 450 Healthy, 123 MCI, 192 Parkinsonism, 74 Depression, 70 AD. Statistic analysis

Paired model comparison (blNM vs todNM)
To quantify the effect of TOD on normative deviation, we compared paired z-scores from blNM and todNM of the same scan in the cross test set. Analyses were stratified by diagnosis group. For each ROI, we tested the significance of within-subject model different using a two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. P-values were then corrected for multiple comparisons across ROIs within each diagnosis using Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH–FDR).

2×2 ANOVA for model×group interaction
To determine whether TOD alters normative deviations differently in patients than in controls, we fitted an ROI-wise 2×2 mixed ANOVA using z-score of cross test set. Model (blNM vs todNM) is within-subject factor, and group (Healthy vs one diagnosis, analysed pairwise) is between-subject factor. We focus on model×group interaction, which tests whether the model induced shift differs between the diagnosis group and controls. Interaction p-values were BH–FDR corrected across ROIs within each diagnosis.

Longitudinal paired scan analysis
To assess longitudinal change, we tested within-subject differences between two scans in the long test set, separately for blNM and todNM. For each ROI and diagnosis group, we applied two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to evaluate whether the within-model longitudinal change differed from zero. BH–FDR correction was applied across ROIs within each diagnosis×model. Differences in the pattern of significant ROIs between blNM and todNM were used to evaluate how TOD modulated inferred longitudinal effects relative to lifespan adjusted norms.

Results

For paired model test, we defined a standard significance criterion as BH-FDR q<0.05 (BH). We then applied a strict criterion requiring both BH-FDR significance and a meaningful shift: the Hodges–Lehmann (HL) paired-difference estimate had to show robust non-trivial displacement (strict). Across 108 ROIs per diagnosis, we observed omnipresent z-score shifts from blNM to todNM in multiple groups, including Healthy (94 BH; 57 strict) and several clinical cohorts, Parkinsonism (93 BH; 59 strict), AD (78 BH; 57 strict), MCI (71 BH; 56 strict), and ADHD (75 BH; 69 strict). Intermediate effects were seen in Schizophrenia (51 BH; 44 strict), Anxiety (50 BH; 34 strict), and Depression (42 BH; 21 strict). Bipolar showed no BH/strict-significant ROIs. Spatially, the strict effects were highly convergent: right inferior temporal and right middle temporal repeatedly ranked among the strongest shifts across diagnoses and healthy, typically with negative HL (z_tod < z_bl), alongside recurrent effects in right insula, left and right cerebellum cortex, and left rostral anterior cingulate. In contrast, left temporal pole was a frequent positive-HL ROI across several groups. Importantly, while the HL magnitudes were small (≈10⁻³–10⁻² z units), the associated rank-biserial effects were often large, indicating highly consistent direction of change across subjects rather than large per-subject swings. Diagnosis-specific nuances were also evident: ADHD was dominated by broadly negative shifts (e.g., RH inferiortemporal, RH middletemporal, RH precentral), Depression emphasized cerebellar cortex shifts, and Schizophrenia showed comparatively more positive strict effects (e.g., CC_Central and lateral orbitofrontal). (Fig2. a and b)

From ROI-wise 2×2 mixed ANOVA test, interaction effects were widespread and diagnosis-dependent across the 108 ROIs. Strong interaction appeared for Depression (81 ROIs), Anxiety (81), Schizophrenia (70), Parkinsonism (63), and AD (33), while Bipolar showed only 1 ROI and none for MCI and ADHD. A notable cross-diagnosis set of ROIs appears (with across ≥4 diagnoses), including ventricular/CSF measures, amygdala, thalamus/ventral diencephalon, striatal nuclei (caudate/putamen), corpus callosum, and fronto-parietal/DMN cortices. Directionally, the blNM - todNM shift tended to be larger in Depression/Anxiety/Schizophrenia/AD than in healthy for many shared ROIs, whereas Parkinsonism often showed the opposite sign, suggesting that TOD adjust abnormality differently by disorder. These interaction patterns are consistent with growing evidence that MRI-derived brain metrics vary across the day and can show regional rhythmicity (including cortical and subcortical effects), implying that scan time can couple to measured anatomy/biophysics. The disorder-specific directions also align with the fact that circadian/sleep regulation is commonly disrupted in mood disorders, PD, and AD. (Fig2. c)

Longitudinal effects were largely confined to Healthy, MCI, and AD. At the diagnosis level, Healthy participants showed a large number of significant ROIs under both models (43/108 in blNM and todNM), but with balanced reallocation between models (five ROIs significance gained and five lost from blNM to todNM). MCI showed a pronounced increase in sensitivity after TOD adjustment, with significant ROIs increasing from 18/108 to 28/108 with no losses. AD showed a similar but smaller effect (28/108 to 33/108), with 8 gains and 3 losses. In contrast, Parkinsonism showed only one gained ROI, and no significant ROIs were detected for Depression, Anxiety, or ADHD. At the region level, a shared cross-diagnosis set emerged across Healthy, MCI, and AD under todNM, involving medial temporal regions, ventricular/CSF measures, and temporo-occipital cortex. Model-dependent changes were diagnosis-specific: MCI gains were exclusively cortical and concentrated in posterior temporo-parietal, while AD gains spanned both cortical and subcortical structures. Conclusion

Paired model comparison (blNM vs. todNM) showed that incorporating time of day (TOD) systematically shifts individual-level z-scores in a substantial subset of ROIs across multiple diagnoses, including healthy controls. Importantly, these effects were not anatomically random. Temporal, insular/cingulate, cerebellar, and striatal regions repeatedly emerged as among the most consistently affected across diagnoses, indicating a widespread yet regionally structured influence of TOD on normative deviation estimates.

The 2×2 mixed ANOVA further demonstrated that TOD-related shifts are not uniform across groups. Many ROIs exhibited significant model X group interactions, indicating that TOD modeling can alter the magnitude of deviation differently in patients compared with controls. Notably, several interaction regions overlapped with well-established disease-sensitive systems, for example, medial and inferior temporal and limbic regions in Alzheimer’s disease and MCI [4], supporting biological plausibility rather than purely statistical artifacts.

 Longitudinal paired analyses showed that the set of ROIs identified as changing across visits can differ between blNM and todNM. These findings indicate that TOD adjustment does not simply inflate or attenuate statistical significance globally. Instead, incorporating TOD can re-map which regional longitudinal effects are most detectable, with changes that are diagnosis-specific in both extent and direction.

 Taken together, these results align with recent evidence demonstrating measurable circadian and diurnal modulation of human brain morphology and tissue properties, implying that scan acquisition time can influence apparent structural phenotypes [5]. The converging message is that time of day constitutes a subtle but non-negligible nuisance factor in normative modeling: although its effects are small in magnitude, TOD-related variation can reshape individual deviation estimates and does so differentially across clinical populations.
