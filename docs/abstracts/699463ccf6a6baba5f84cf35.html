Introduction

Anxiety is a prolonged, future-oriented response to ambiguous or distal threat, marked by sustained physiological, affective, and cognitive engagement 1. With a global prevalence of 3.6%, it is a major contributor to disability and is linked to impaired daily functioning and neurocognitive inefficiencies 2. Although early intervention can reduce progression to anxiety and depressive disorders, subthreshold anxiety often remains undiagnosed. Neuroimaging work identifies brain signal variability (BSV) of the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal as a promising marker in psychiatric conditions, yet no study has characterized how BSV relates to anxiety.

Executive functions (EFs) provide a link between BSV and anxiety. EF performance depends on the coordinated functioning of large-scale networks such as the frontoparietal network, salience, dorsal attention, and default mode systems. Optimal BSV supports flexible control, whereas excessive variability disrupts coherence and impairs EF efficiency 3. Anxiety is characterized by dysfunction within these same networks, reflected in slowed processing, impaired inhibition, and inefficient engagement of prefrontal regions. Prior studies also reported atypical BSV associated with anxiety disorder severity4 and changes with treatment response5. Yet it remains unknown whether EF deficits mediate the relationship between resting-state BSV and anxiety symptoms. Identifying this pathway may inform interventions that target the EF dysfunctionalities related to these large-scale networks in individuals with heightened anxiety. This study integrates resting-state BSV with EF measures and dimensional anxiety symptoms. Using data from the Enhanced Nathan Kline Institute Rockland Sample (N = 468; ages 18–85), we identified functional correlates of anxiety and tested whether BSV-related inefficiencies in core EF measures mediate the relationship between intrinsic neural dynamics and anxious affect.

Methods

We analyzed resting-state fMRI data from the Enhanced Nathan Kline Institute Rockland Sample. Of 574 participants initially scanned, individuals with poor-quality anatomical or functional data or excessive motion (mean FD > 0.5 mm) were excluded, yielding a final sample of 468 adults (64.7% female; mean age = 43.86 ± 18.54 years). Participants completed self-report measures, neuropsychological testing, and a SCID-I interview. Anxiety was assessed using the STAI-Trait measure. Executive functions were measured using D-KEFS indices of cognitive flexibility (TMT-B, CWI-Switching, Verbal Fluency Switching, Sorting), inhibitory control (CWI-Inhibition, CWI-Inhibition/Switching), and processing speed (TMT-A, Motor Speed), supplemented with Penn CNB measures of flexibility (PCET), sustained attention (CPT), and working-memory updating (letter n-back).

Resting-state fMRI (~10 min; Siemens 3T Trio; multiband EPI, TR = 1400 ms, TE = 30 ms, 2-mm isotropic) was preprocessed using DPARSF, AFNI, and FSL, including removal of initial volumes, slice-timing correction, skull stripping, realignment, ICA-FIX denoising, normalization, despiking, smoothing, motion regression, detrending, and 0.01–0.10 Hz filtering. The Schaefer 200-parcel atlas was used for a brain parcellation. Brain signal variability (BSV) was computed as rMSSD on z-normalized parcel time series.

To test whether EF mediated BSV–anxiety associations, we fit parcel-wise single-mediator models with anxiety severity as the dependent variable, parcel-level BSV as the predictor, and EF factor scores (inhibitory control, processing speed, working memory, cognitive flexibility) as mediators. Mediation was estimated for each EF domain across all 200 parcels, controlling for age, sex, and head motion. All variables were z-standardized. Indirect, direct, and total effects were computed using 20,000-iteration nonparametric bootstrapping, with indirect effects deemed significant when 95% CIs excluded zero.

Results

The four-factor EF model showed good fit (χ²(65) = 140.92, CFI = .954, RMSEA = .050, SRMR = .049), supporting working memory, inhibitory control, processing speed, and cognitive flexibility as distinct latent domains. Mediation analyses identified inhibitory control and processing speed as reliable mediators of BSV–anxiety associations. For inhibitory control, several frontoparietal parcels showed significant indirect effects: higher BSV predicted stronger inhibitory control (β = .10–.13), which in turn predicted higher anxiety, with all indirect paths significant and direct effects nonsignificant. Processing speed showed the most widespread effects; higher BSV predicted slower processing speed across frontoparietal, dorsal-attention, salience, and default-mode parcels (β = .08–.13), and slower processing speed predicted higher anxiety (β ≈ .12–.13). Cognitive flexibility showed a more selective pattern limited to inferior parietal, intraparietal, temporal, and default-mode regions, where higher BSV predicted poorer flexibility (β = −.11 to −.14), which in turn predicted higher anxiety.

Conclusion

This study shows that EF disruptions mediate links between BSV and anxiety in specific brain regions. Across EF domains - inhibitory control, processing speed, and cognitive flexibility, higher BSV within frontoparietal, dorsal-attention, salience/ventral-attention, and default-mode hubs was related to reduced executive efficiency, which in turn related to greater anxiety. The BSV parcels that exhibited significant indirect effects, including the inferior parietal lobule, intraparietal sulcus, lateral temporal cortex, and dorsal/mid-prefrontal regions, overlap with heteromodal control networks supporting working-memory updating, goal maintenance, and flexible behavioral shifting. Direct BSV–anxiety associations were negligible once EF was included, aligning with models in which anxiety emerges when unstable cognitive dynamics impair top-down regulation. These findings also help reconcile mixed reports of adaptive versus maladaptive variability, suggesting that resting-state BSV becomes detrimental when it undermines the stability required for efficient executive computation.
