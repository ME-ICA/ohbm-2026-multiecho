Introduction

The somatosensory cortex is a well-characterized region of the cerebral cortex that plays a central role in processing sensory information from the body. Histologically, the itcan be subdivided into distinct areas, each with characteristic laminar architectures [1]. Functionally, it contains somatotopic maps in which different cortical areas represent specific body parts in a systematic spatial arrangement [2]. Moreover, extensive studies [3, 4] have indicated a hierarchical processing of somatosensory information, sequentially from primary to secondary cortices and then to association areas. Owing to these highly organized features, the somatosensory cortex provides an ideal model for understanding the systematic organization of the brain and how it processes and integrates information.

Somatosensory processing does not rely on isolated areas. Instead, these areas are intricately woven into a complex network of anatomically connected and functionally interacting neuronal populations. Previous studies have extensively characterized both structural and functional connectivity of the somatosensory network, yet the molecular basis that supports and modulates the network-level interactions remain largely unknown. 

Neurotransmitters and their receptors are key molecules underlying neuronal transmission and supporting areal interactions [5]. The areal distribution of receptor density reflects the chemoarchitectonic balance and may shape how structural connections are translated into functional interactions. In this context, receptor covariance (RC), defined as the similarity of receptor profiles across areas, providing a powerful framework to quantify molecular-level relationship between brain areas. In the present study, we integrate the RC with structural (SC) and functional connectivity (FC) to quantify the RC-SC and RC-FC couplings. Comparing these three modalities will shed light on how neurotransmitter systems modulate the relationship between structure and function.

Methods

Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined based on established somatosensory pathways reported in literature. They were mainly located in the parietal lobe and the dorsal bank of the lateral fissure.

To quantify the RC, we first calculated a representative feature vector consisting of 14 receptor density values for each ROI. Receptor densities were z-score normalized within each receptor type to equalize their contributions. The RC between any pair of cortical areas was then measured by computing the Pearson correlation of their representative feature vectors. In this manner, we computed the similarities between each seed area and all areas across the cortex, resulting in an interareal correlation matrix. To characterize the RC patterns, we first mapped the average RC for all ROIs, and also performed dimensional reduction to capture continuous axes of variation (i.e. gradient) across all ROIs. Furthermore, hierarchical clustering and principal component analyses (PCA) were utilized to identify the grouping patterns among these areas.

We also reconstructed resting-state FC and tract-tracing-based SC enabling a multimodal characterization. For each ROI, connectivity profiles were thus represented as feature vectors in three modalities: RC, SC, and FC. To assess the relationship between molecular similarity and network connectivity, RC-FC and RC-SC couplings were quantified separately by computing the Pearson correlation between the corresponding feature vectors. Finally, 'leave-one-receptor-out' analysis was performed to evaluate the contribution of individual receptor types to the RC-FC and RC-SC couplings.

Results

Overall, all ROIs exhibited substantial similarities in their RC patterns with anatomically adjacent regions; however, the RC patterns still differed considerably across ROIs. For example, area 3bl showed strong positive correlations with primary visual and auditory areas. Area 1 displayed a more moderate RC pattern, with weaker correlations with visual areas but stronger correlations with posterior parietal and motor areas. In contrast, area S2 primarily correlated with the inferior parietal lobule and areas within the lateral fissure.

Regarding the averaged RC, lateral 3b stood out with the lowest value, indicating that it shares the least similarity with other cortical areas. Gradient analysis further highlighted the distinctiveness of lateral 3b within the entire somatosensory network. Hierarchical cluster analysis unveiled five distinct clusters within the somatosensory network. 

The coupling analysis revealed that the RC matrix was significantly correlated with both SC (r = 0.21, p = 7.5 × 10−8) and FC (r = 0.32, p = 1.3 × 10−109) matrices when considering all areas. For specifically areas, the RC-SC couplings ranged from 0.0190 to 0.4108, the RC-FC couplings ranged from 0.0915 to 0.4893. The kainate, GABAᴀ/BZ, and α1 receptors contributed most to overall RC-FC and RC-SC couplings, with α2 and D1 also influencing RC-FC, and 5-HT2 modulating RC-SC couplings.

Conclusion

In this study, we reconstructed the RC pattern of the macaque somatosensory network, revealing distinct molecular profiles across areas. By integrating multivariate analyses with existing evidence, we proposed an extended hierarchical framework among the somatosensory areas. Beyond the well-established cortical hierarchy from SI to secondary SIIcortices and further to the association areas, our results reveal additional hierarchical differentiation within each processing level.

Furthermore, by comparing RC with SC and RC, we demonstrated that molecular similarity is closely linked with SC and FC, yet it exhibits unique characteristics that are not fully explained by SC or FC alone and potentially reflect the processing hierarchy of the somatosensory system. Together, these findings highlight receptor covariance as a complementary modality that bridges molecular architecture and large-scale brain networks.
