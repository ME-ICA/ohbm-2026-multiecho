Introduction

Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition that manifests in social interaction and communication differences, as well as restricted interests and repetitive behaviors. Autism presents with extensive heterogeneity, suggesting multiple subtypes with different neurobiological and behavioral profiles. Prior work indicates that the reward circuitry of the brain contributes to core symptoms observed in autism, and of these reward regions, the ventral striatum (VS) has been notably implicated in autism pathophysiology. Regions that compose the dopaminergic reward system, including the VS are regulated by the habenula (Hb), a small pair of functionally distinct epithalamic nuclei.1,2,3

Acting as a node, the habenula has also been acknowledged for mediating neural systems, including emotional behaviors between the forebrain and midbrain regions and modulating dopaminergic and serotonergic systems.4 Additionally, the Hb may be a prominent region in motivated and adaptive behaviors in autism due to its connections with corticolimbic and midbrain monoaminergic regions.3 Thus, we propose that further investigation of altered Hb resting state functional connectivity (rsFC) may elucidate underlying mechanisms inherent to autism symptom manifestation.3 Here, we posit that data-driven subgroups of autistic individuals may be distinguished by different patterns of whole-brain Hb rsFC. Identification of such subtypes has the potential to provide proactive diagnoses and cultivate a supportive framework centered at the individual. Furthermore, to enhance the quality of life across an individual’s lifespan, a person-centered approach is tailored to meet the needs while following the values of the individual. 

Methods

Neuroimaging data for autistic participants (N=705; M=16.53; SD=8.33 years) were accessed within the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE I and II). A Hb seed ROI was manually distinguished on the Montreal Neurological Imaging (MNI152) T1 template brain (i.e., International Consortium for Brain Mapping, ICM152) on Mango Imaging Viewer as described by Lawson et al.1 Using the Hb seed ROI, each participant's Hb voxelwise time series was extracted via AFNI (v22.3.05) where each time series was unsmoothed, preprocessed, and denoised. Averaged ROI time series were created per participant by computing the mean voxel value for each time point across non-zero voxels and subsequently entered into a general linear model (GLM). The GLM produced Z-transformed participant-level whole-brain Hb rsFC correlation maps. 

To determine maximal subgroups (k), per participant Hb rsFC maps were converted from vector-form distance into a square-form data matrix (nparticipants x nvoxels). Based on whole-brain hb rsFC of participants, we employed agglomerative hierarchical clustering to negate cluster quantity a priori, supported by Euclidean distance and Ward’s linkage method.5 Pairwise distances between voxel pairs was computed via Euclidean distance and produced a condensed 1D array of the data matrix. For the Euclidean distance between rows of the data matrix to correspond to correlation distance, each participant vector was row-centered and L2-normalized. Ward’s linkage method was applied to the condensed 1D array to minimize cluster variance. Computed distances were then plotted as a heatmap and dendrogram. We assessed clustering solutions k=[2, 9] for parsimony and chose an optimal clustering solution based on agreement across several metrics. We looked for local maxima in silhouette score, increases in gap statistic, maxima in Variance Ratio Criterion (VRC), increases in cluster separation, maxima in hierarchy index, and increases in linkage distance (ΔLD) Once the optimal k was selected, thresholded t-statistic maps were generated using a FDR-corrected voxel-level threshold of 𝑞FDR-corr=0.01, and cluster-level threshold of 50 voxels. Brain surface plots were created with surfplot from matplotlib. Cluster maps were uploaded to Neurovault and functionally decoded via Neurosynth. 

Results

Clustering was performed and clustering metrics collectively indicated an optimal clustering solution of k=3 (Fig. 1A). Produced by successive k value merge heights, the greatest linkage distance jump (ΔLD=0.278) of the eight cluster groups (k=[2, 9]) was observed at k=3. Connectivity maps for the three clusters are shown in Fig. 1B. Cluster 1 included regions of the default mode network (DMN) and dorsal attention network (DAN), with peak Hb connectivity in the left angular gyrus (IAG, BA 39). Cluster 2 included regions of the visual network (VN), including the superior parietal cortex (SPC) and frontal eye fields, with peak connectivity in the right superior lobule (rSPL, BA 7). Cluster 3 included regions of the salience network (SN), with peak connectivity in the right insular cortex (rIC, BA 13). Defined by strength of association, functional decoding results indicated that cluster 1 was associated with “default mode network” (DMN), “autobiographical memory”, “posterior cingulate”, “episodic”, “medial prefrontal”, “dorsal attention network” (DAN), and “retrieval”. Cluster 2 was associated with “intraparietal sulcus”, “superior parietal”, “spatial”, “saccade”, “eye fields”, “movements”, “visual”, and “premotor”. Cluster 3 was associated with “insula”, “frontal operculum”, “nociceptive”, “painful”, “body”, “response inhibition”, and “pain”. 

Conclusion

Hierarchical clustering of individual whole-brain Hb rsFC patterns offers an alternative analysis of identifying subtypes of autism. We identified three patterns of Hb connectivity that differ between individuals, suggesting three subtypes of autism participants. 

The first subtype was distinguished by Hb connectivity with the default mode network; the second, by Hb connectivity with superior parietal cortex; and the third, by Hb connectivity with the salience network. Together, these patterns are aligned with the triple network model, suggesting that differentiation of autism subtypes via Hb connectivity patterns may correspond to significant differences in interaction and synchronization of large-scale brain networks responsible for higher-order cognitive functioning. 

We acknowledge that our approach is limited given our sample only consisted of autism participants and did not examine any neurotypical (NT) participants, thereby restricting inspection of possible overlap in connectivity amongst a combined sample of participants. Future analyses will examine brain-behavior associations between Hb rsFC and phenotypic measures of autism symptomatology. This work contributes towards parsing the heterogeneity of autism and examining potential associations of rsFC patterns that contribute to behavioral manifestations. Defining subtypes of autism may enhance current abilities to identify and distinguish autistic subgroups with the ultimate goal of understanding and support at an individual level.
