Introduction

Mild cognitive impairment with Lewy bodies (MCI-LB) is typically characterized by non-amnestic MCI coupled with one or two core clinical features of DLB, such as cognitive fluctuations, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, and REM sleep behavior disorder. Cognitively, the executive and visuospatial domains are usually altered early on. 

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) has the potential to classify pre-dementia stages of DLB and to identify discriminative patterns of brain network alterations. The functional connectivity (FC) is known to fluctuate significantly 1, and investigating these dynamic FC (dFC) patterns offers valuable complementary information beyond static FC (sFC) analyses 2, 3.

We aimed to investigate whether rs-fMRI dynamic connectivity parameters add to standard static connectivity analysis in classifying drug naïve mild cognitive impairment with Lewy bodies (MCI-LB) from healthy controls (HC), and to uncover the most discriminative features with respect to specific dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) changes and their cognitive correlates. We employed three different dFC methods 4: Clustering of Correlation Matrices (CCM), Coactivation Pattern Analysis (CAP), and Fast Eigenvector Centrality (FEC). We hypothesize that CCM and CAP introduce features with greater discriminative power (measured with effect size) than FEC-based features 5.

Methods

A total of 84 participants (32 MCI-LB, 52 HC) underwent clinical, neuropsychological, and resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) assessments. Inclusion criteria for MCI-LB required neuropsychological impairment (≥1 SD below norms on ≥2 tests) and at least one core DLB symptom. Exclusion criteria included other neurological diagnoses and MRI contraindications. After quality control for motion artifacts, 6 participants per group were removed, and age- and sex-matched subgroups were retained (26 MCI-LB and matched HCs). Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained.

Neuropsychological testing assessed global cognition, memory, attention, executive, and visuospatial functions. Domain-specific Z-scores were computed and compared using Wilcoxon rank sum tests and effect sizes.

Rs-fMRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma and preprocessed using SPM12 and MATLAB following standard pipelines. Static functional connectivity (sFC) was calculated as Pearson correlations between 32 hubs representing five large-scale resting-state networks: sensorimotor network (SMN), dorsal attentional network (DAN), default mode network (DMN), visual network (VN), frontal-parietal control network (FPCN). The used ROIs are visualized in Figure 1. Each resting-state network was then represented by the average signal. We computed within- and between-network connectivity. Here, within-network connectivity was evaluated as the mean correlation between hubs of each large-scale RSN separately, and between-network connectivity was the mean correlation between hubs of pairs of these networks.

Dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) was estimated using three complementary methods: clustering of correlation matrices (CCM) with sliding-window correlations (60 s, 10-scan step) clustered via k-means; coactivation patterns (CAP) clustering ROI timepoints without sliding windows (2–40 states tested); and fast eigenvector centrality (FEC), a template-free voxel-wise method clustered into recurring patterns. Temporal properties of dFC states (coverage, occurrence, mean duration, and number of transitions) were quantified.

Pairwise classification analysis (MCI-LB vs HC) employed Random Forest models, with Gradient Boosting tested secondarily. Highly correlated features were removed, and information gain identified the most discriminative dFC features. Cross-validation used a leave-one-pair-out scheme, with brain states estimated in training sets and projected onto testing data. Finally, partial correlations controlling for depression assessed relationships between selected sFC/dFC features and cognitive performance as well as core clinical DLB symptoms.

Results

Relying on static functional connectivity (sFC), discrimination accuracy was low: MCI-LB vs. HC (accuracy 0.538). Adding dFC improved the comparison: The average accuracy of 0.750 was reached, representing an improvement of 0.212 from the sFC baseline.

These data confirm that dFC parameters significantly contribute to classification performance and allow for the achievement of clinically relevant discriminative power (see Figure 2).

We found distinct patterns of dFC changes that best discriminated between relevant groups and were related to cognitive outcomes. The optimal number of states was identified using the SSE bend criterion, which selected four states for CCM and FEC, and seven states for CAP.

In the classification of MCI-LB vs HC, the largest effect size was observed for the decrease in CCM coverage of state 2 in the MCI-LB group (Cohen’s d = -0.767). In this group, the feature correlates with the memory Z-score (r(23) = .51, p = .009); whereas no such correlation appeared in the HC group. CCM state 2 is characterized by a positive correlation between the DMN with the DAN, VN, and motor networks, and a negative correlation between the FPCN and these three networks. 

Conclusion

Overall, dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) provides a valuable extension to static functional connectivity (sFC) for classifying dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) at its early pre-dementia stage. dFC analyses revealed distinct patterns of network dynamics that differentiate mild cognitive impairment with Lewy bodies (MCI-LB) from healthy controls, corresponding to specific cognitive deficits in MCI-LB. Incorporating dFC parameters substantially improved classification accuracy, highlighting the potential of functional connectivity as an imaging biomarker for prodromal DLB.
