Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a severe affective disorder characterized by recurrent mood episodes and substantial functional impairment. Early stages of the disorder are often marked by subthreshold affective symptoms and heterogeneous clinical presentations, making early recognition and targeted intervention particularly challenging. Identifying neural correlates of vulnerability before the onset of manifest illness is therefore a critical goal for improving prevention and early intervention strategies.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has revealed widespread alterations in large-scale brain networks in BD, including default-mode, cognitive-control, and sensorimotor systems. However, most existing studies focus on diagnosed patients and examine single modalities in isolation, limiting insight into how brain network alterations relate to emerging affective symptoms in at-risk populations. Multivariate data fusion approaches offer a powerful framework to characterize joint brain–symptom dimensions that may index latent vulnerability mechanisms.

In this study, we investigated individuals at risk for BD from the BipoLife consortium, including 207 participants classified as low or high risk based on the Bipolar Prodrome Symptom Scale (BPSS). Resting-state whole-brain functional connectivity measures were jointly analyzed with depressive (IDS-C30) and manic (ASRM) symptom scores using canonical correlation analysis combined with joint independent component analysis (CCA+jICA). This approach allowed identification of distinct functional connectivity–symptom dimensions, including a general sleep–affective regulation component and a polarity-related activation component that was selectively driven by high-risk individuals.

Methods

Data were drawn from  the Early-BipoLife consortium, a study designed to improve early recognition and characterization of individuals at increased risk for bipolar disorder.1 Early-BipoLife recruits help-seeking adolescents and young adults (aged 15–35 years) presenting with established clinical risk factors for bipolar disorder and follows them longitudinally within specialized early recognition centers across Germany. The present analysis included 207 participants with available resting-state fMRI and clinical symptom data. Risk status was determined using the Bipolar Prodrome Symptom Scale (BPSS), resulting in 160 low-risk and 47 high-risk individuals. The sample comprised 118 females and 89 males, with a mean age of 24.99 ± 4.28 years (range: 17–35 years). 

Affective symptom dimensions were assessed using item-level scores from the Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology – Clinician rated (IDS-C30) and the Altman Self-Rating Mania Scale (ASRM). The IDS-C30 captures a broad range of depressive symptom domains, including mood quality, sleep and circadian disturbances, psychomotor changes, cognitive symptoms, and somatic complaints, while the ASRM assesses activity levels and mania-related behavioral features. 

Resting-state fMRI data were acquired as part of the standardized Early-BipoLife neuroimaging protocol1 and analyzed based on a group independent component analysis (GICA) framework. Functional connectivity matrices were computed between 53 intrinsic connectivity networks based on a Neuromark template,2 covering seven large-scale brain networks: subcortical (SCN), auditory (ADN), sensorimotor (SMN), visual (VSN), cognitive control (CCN), default mode (DMN), and cerebellar (CBN) networks. All preprocessing and connectivity estimation steps were performed following established ICA-based Neuromark procedures.

To identify shared latent dimensions linking functional brain connectivity and affective symptomatology, we applied a canonical correlation analysis followed by joint independent component analysis (CCA+jICA) framework using the Fusion ICA Toolbox (FIT v2.0c) implemented in MATLAB R2025a.3,4

CCA was first used to identify maximally covarying projections across functional connectivity and clinical symptom modalities. These projections were subsequently decomposed using joint ICA to extract independent components representing joint brain–symptom patterns.3,4 Prior to fusion, both modalities were standardized to ensure comparable scaling.

The number of components was set to 9, based on the elbow criterion. ICA decomposition was performed using the Infomax algorithm and repeated 50 times. Component stability was assessed using ICASSO, allowing identification of robust and reproducible components across runs. Component loadings were converted to z-scores to facilitate interpretability. All other parameters were kept at default settings.

For each joint component, correlations between functional connectivity and clinical symptomatology loading coefficients were computed across the full sample. To assess risk-specific effects, subject loadings were subsequently stratified by BPSS risk group, and correlations were recomputed separately for high-risk and low-risk individuals.

Results

Multimodal fusion analysis identified two robust joint functional connectivity–symptom components showing significant cross-modal coupling.

Component 1 showed a strong and highly significant association between functional connectivity and clinical loadings (r = 0.37, FDR-corrected p < 10⁻⁶). Clinically, this component was dominated by sleep-related and affective symptoms, with opposing contributions from sleep-onset insomnia versus a cluster including mood quality impairment and nocturnal awakenings, suggesting a dissociation between sleep initiation difficulties and sleep maintenance/fragmentation patterns alongside affective symptom severity. The associated functional connectivity pattern prominently involved subcortical interactions with default-mode and cerebellar networks, consistent with large-scale regulation of arousal, sleep, and internally oriented affective processing. Importantly, this brain–symptom coupling was present in both high-risk and low-risk individuals, indicating a general sleep–affective regulation dimension rather than a risk-specific pattern.

Component 2 exhibited a significant functional connectivity–clinical association (r = 0.19, FDR-corrected p = 0.018). The clinical profile reflected a clear polarity-related activation dimension, contrasting depressive and psychomotor-retarded symptoms with manic/activated features such as elevated mood, increased self-confidence, increased activity and talkativeness, and reduced need for sleep. The corresponding functional connectivity (FC) pattern was distributed across subcortical, sensory–motor (auditory, visual, and sensorimotor), cognitive control, and default-mode networks, reflecting large-scale network reconfiguration rather than focal connectivity changes. Risk-stratified analyses revealed that this FC–clinical coupling was pronounced in high-risk individuals (r = 0.38, FDR-corrected p = 0.034), whereas the association was weaker and non-significant in the low-risk group (r = 0.14, FDR-corrected p = 0.107), suggesting a risk-enriched expression of this bipolar-spectrum activation dimension.

Conclusion

In this study, multimodal fusion of resting-state functional connectivity and affective symptom measures revealed two distinct brain–symptom dimensions in individuals at risk for bipolar disorder. The first component captured a general sleep–affective regulation dimension, linking heterogeneous sleep disturbances and affective symptom severity to large-scale functional connectivity patterns involving subcortical, default-mode, and cerebellar networks. The presence of this coupling across both low- and high-risk individuals suggests that alterations in sleep–affective regulation reflect a transdiagnostic vulnerability dimension.

The second component reflected a polarity-related activation dimension, characterized by opposing depressive/psychomotor-retarded versus manic/activated symptom profiles and a distributed functional connectivity pattern spanning sensory–motor, cognitive-control, default-mode, and subcortical networks. Importantly, this brain–symptom coupling was selectively pronounced in high-risk individuals, indicating that polarity-related network reconfiguration may represent a risk-enriched neural signature relevant to bipolar-spectrum vulnerability.

Together, these findings highlight the utility of multivariate data fusion approaches for disentangling shared versus risk-specific neural–symptom dimensions in at-risk populations. By moving beyond categorical diagnoses and single-modality analyses, this work contributes to a dimensional understanding of early bipolar vulnerability and underscores the importance of sleep regulation and polarity-related activation as key targets for future longitudinal and preventive research.
