Introduction

Females are twice as likely as males to be diagnosed with depression and anxiety disorders in their lifetime.1 While sex differences in psychiatric diagnoses and in the development of psychopathology have been well documented, we know less about the neurobiological mechanisms and markers that underlie these sex differences. Recently, researchers have examined the role of large-scale functional brain networks in influencing affective and cognitive functioning. One such approach, the triple network model, unifies three key brain networks that have been implicated in psychopathology: the Salience Network (SN), Fronto-parietal Network (FPN), and Default Mode Network (DMN).2 Aberrant intrinsic connectivity among these three networks has been found to be associated with a range of psychiatric disorders, including depression and anxiety. Although previous studies have identified sex differences in functional brain networks in specific clinical disorders, such as youth with autism spectrum disorder,3 fewer investigations have been conducted with more diverse samples. Although some studies have examined the triple network model in community samples, the primary focus of these investigations has been on connectivity within a single network, such as the DMN or the SN, rather than on interactions among the three networks. Elucidating sex-specific, between-network interactions in a community sample and delineating associations across several types of psychopathology promises to yield valuable insights concerning global patterns of brain functional connectivity (FC) that characterize sex-differentiated manifestations of psychopathology.

Methods

Participants in this study were 106 young adults (57 females) recruited from the San Francisco Bay Area as part of a larger longitudinal study examining the effects of early life stress on psychobiological development through early adulthood. At approximately age 20 years (Mage=20.59±1.17 years), brain scans were conducted using a 3T MRI scanner (GE Discovery MR750, GE Medical Systems) equipped with a 32-channel head coil (Nova Medical). We collected SPGR T1-weighted axial anatomical images (repetition time [TR] = 6.24 ms; echo time [TE] = 2.34 ms; flip angle = 12°; FOV = 230 mm; voxel size = 0.8984 × 0.8984 × 0.9000 mm; scan time = 5:15). We acquired 15 minutes of simultaneous multi-slice (multi-band) resting-state BOLD fMRI (rs-fMRI) data using two consecutive T2*-weighted echo planar imaging sequences. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes open and were monitored via webcam for the entirety of the scan. The raw functional images were quality checked prior to preprocessing. We used fMRIPrep v25.1.4 (RRID:SCR_016216) to preprocess and the XCP-D pipeline to post-process rs-fMRI data.4 Both pipelines were based on Nipype version 1.10.0 (RRID:SCR_002502). Resting-state networks were defined using the Schaefer atlas with a resolution of 256 parcels, each of which was assigned a functional network label based on the Yeo 7- networks. Pair-wise FC between all regions was computed using the Pearson’s correlation of each parcel’s timeseries data. Between-network FC was computed by averaging FC between all pair-wise correlations of two networks. To assess psychopathology, we administered the Adult Self Report (ASR), a 126-item self-report questionnaire assessing a wide range of emotional and behavioral problems in adults. To minimize the number of statistical analyses, we first conducted a Multivariate Analysis of Covariance (MANCOVA) to examine whether there were significant sex differences in scores on specific scales of the ASR (Internalizing Problems, Externalizing Problems, Total Problems, Depressive Problems, and Anxiety Problems), controlling for age and race. For those scales on which there was a significant sex difference, we then conducted linear regression analyses in R using the lm function in the base R stats package to examine whether sex moderated relations between FC among the SN, FPN, and DMN, and psychopathology. We followed up significant interactions of sex and FC (p<0.05) with simple slopes analysis using the sim_slopes function in the interactions package.

Results

Females had significantly higher scores than did males on the ASR Depressive Problems (t=2.17, p=0.032) and Anxiety Problems (t=2.01, p=0.048) scales, but not on the Internalizing, Externalizing, or Total Problems scales (ps>0.17). Results of the MANCOVA examining sex differences in the ASR scales are presented in Figure 1A. Following up on the significant sex differences in depression and anxiety, we found that sex moderated the associations between FC of the triple network model and depressive and anxiety problems (Figure 1B-D). Specifically, scores on the Depressive Problems scale were negatively associated with SN-FPN connectivity (β=-14.78, SE=7.38, p=0.048) in females but not in males (t=-2.51, p=0.014). Although scores on the Anxiety Problems scale were negatively associated with both SN-DMN FC (β=-7.88, SE=3.36, p=0.021) and FPN-DMN (β=-10.77, SE=4.97, p=0.033), only the relation between SN-DMN FC and Anxiety Problems was moderated by sex; again, SN-DMN FC predicted Anxiety Problems scores only for females (t=-2.21, p=0.029).

Conclusion

Our findings indicate a potential neurobiological process that contributes to the higher rates of anxiety and depression in females than in males. While all three between-network connectivity patterns (SN-FPN, SN-DMN, FPN-DMN) were significantly related to psychopathology, there were important distinctions in the nature of these associations. Whereas SN-DMN and FPN-DMN were related to Anxiety Problems, SN-FPN was associated with Depressive Problems. Further, sex moderated the associations between SN-FPN and Depressive Problems and between SN-DMN and Anxiety problems. Our results are consistent with findings of prior studies examining sex-specific network FC.5 Importantly, the specific between-network interactions we found to be involved in these sex differences support emerging evidence implicating the SN as a central hub connecting other large-scale brain networks like the DMN and FPN. The SN relays control signals to the DMN and FPN in response to salient stimuli, synchronizing the engagement of one network with the disengagement of the other. 2 Therefore, gaining a more comprehensive understanding of the nature of the relation of the SN with the DMN and FPN is becoming crucial in advancing our knowledge of affective and cognitive dysfunction. Our findings highlight these important between-network patterns of FC within the triple network model. We also assessed both depression- and anxiety-related psychopathology, which were characterized by significant sex differences in our study. By combining the triple network model, sex, and psychopathology into a single model, our study addresses a critical gap in the literature examining sex differences in both psychiatric outcomes and large-scale functional brain networks and, further, provides evidence of a neurobiological pathway through which females have higher rates of depression and anxiety disorders than do males.
