Introduction

The increasing value of multimodal neuroimaging, which integrates data like structural MRI (sMRI) and functional MRI (fMRI), lies in its capacity to enhance the understanding of brain mechanisms and improve the sensitivity of brain-based biomarkers for various disorders1. Combining these data sources enables the identification of information that is shared between modalities as well as unique to each. This research investigates a key methodological aspect: whether the spatial decomposition generated by the multimodal fusion is sensitive to the spatial scale utilized for functional network connectivity (FNC). The methodology involved fusing an identical sMRI dataset with FNC features at two spatial scales - a low resolution of 53 components (FNC-53) and a high resolution of 105 components (FNC-105). The primary research objective was to compare the results from these two distinct fusion models to explicitly determine if and how the resulting structural manifolds are dependent on the functional spatial scale.

Methods

We employ a multi-stage data fusion process using multiset canonical correlation analysis and joint independent component analysis (mCCA+jICA) to integrate the sMRI and FNC datasets. This method (Fig 1 (a)) is designed to yield independent components that capture both the shared and specific multimodal characteristics essential for distinguishing patients from healthy controls. The experimental design centers on a parallel comparison using two independent fusions:

  • Low-Resolution Fusion (FNC-53): mCCA+jICA is performed using sMRI and FNC features derived from the 53-component template.

  • High-Resolution Fusion (FNC-105): The same sMRI data is used, but this time it is fused with FNC features derived from the 105-component template.

With 10 independent sources extracted from both models, the crucial structural components are isolated. A correlation analysis is then executed between the gray matter (GM) sources of Fusion-1 and Fusion-2 to measure the spatial similarity resulting from the two different functional scale inputs. We then reordered these components based on their correlation values in decreasing order as shown in Fig. 1(b). This step is critical to quantifying the spatial overlap or divergence between the structural findings as a direct function of the input functional scale. 

We employ MRI data from Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)2 dataset with 83 patients suffering from Alzheimer's disease and 383 healthy controls. Gray matter (GM) images were derived from the sMRI data via the SPM12 software. For the same dataset, functional data was processed to yield two FNC matrices: a low-resolution FNC-53 and a high-resolution FNC-105, based on their respective ICN Neuromark templates.

Results

After ordering the components based on the correlation of the gray matter sources, the spatial components identified are observed to vary as the correlation decreases. For these variable sources, loading parameters are then computed for both the gray matter and FNC sources corresponding to the components in both the fusions, to identify their expression level in both patient and healthy control groups. Finally, t-tests are performed to identify the significant group differences, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Conclusion

Our preliminary research demonstrates that sources extracted after fusion have a variable dependence on the scale of FNC components. This variability indicates that the spatial decomposition is scale-sensitive, revealing both scale-invariant and scale-sensitive structural components. This finding presents an opportunity to study multimodal brain features across variable spatial scales and evaluate their utility as potential brain biomarkers.
