Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) and other psychiatric diseases can benefit from objective decision support in diagnosis and therapy. Machine learning approaches based on biomarkers extracted from electroencephalography (EEG) signals have the potential to serve as low-cost decision support systems. Despite the successful demonstration of this approach, contradictory findings regarding the diagnostic value of those biomarkers hamper their deployment in a clinical setting. One source of inter-study variability is the selection of participants. In addition to demographic factors, psychiatric patients show heterogeneous clinical characteristics. As a consequence, findings are not replicable between studies. Different choices in preprocessing steps between studies introduce further variability. In most studies, the data recording and processing pipeline consists of one specific choice out of several alternatives. Yet, different methodical decisions during this process can have an impact on the findings, rendering comparison between studies impossible1. One controversial group of biomarkers for MDD diagnosis are alpha band markers, which provide contradictory results regarding the usefulness of specific markers2.

The goal of this study is to assess two of the main types of reproducibility3 on EEG alpha band biomarkers for the support of MDD diagnosis. For the purpose of replication, we harmonize two publicly available datasets with eyes-closed resting-state EEG. To demonstrate robustness of the biomarkers against the processing pipeline, we systematically investigate the influence of selected preprocessing steps, different biomarkers, and classification algorithms with a multiverse analysis. The main goal is to shed light on the influence of processing steps and to examine analytical approaches to inform future studies on EEG biomarkers for psychiatric disorders.

Methods

Datasets and Harmonization

Two publicly available datasets were used (D14 and D25) with MDD patients (D1: n=33, D2: n=24) and healthy controls (HCs) (D1: n=30, D2: n=29). All patients were diagnosed based on the DSM-IV. Both datasets contain 5-minute resting-state EEG time series data with eyes closed. Electrodes of both datasets were matched, leaving 13 common channels: seven (pre)frontal (Fp1/2, F3/4, F7/8, Fz), two central (C3/4), two parietal (P3/4), and two occipital (O1/2). Both datasets were re-referenced to average and resampled to 250Hz. To harmonize the spectral bandwidth, both datasets were bandpass filtered from 1 to 40 Hz.

Multiverse Analysis

To investigate the impact of processing choices on the classification results, we adapted a multiverse analysis. For each step of the processing pipeline, we implemented variations and combined them fully. This procedure led us to a multiverse with 2.376 paths (Fig 1).

Artifact Removal

Three commonly applied variations in artifact removal were carried out: 1) No artifact removal. 2) Independent component analysis based automatic labeling (ICLabel) and removal of artifact categories like eye blinks. 3) Data cleaning with algorithms adjusted to the specific datasets and manual revision of the pre-cleaned data to handle remaining artifacts. 

Preprocessing

We applied three variations of z-normalization to capture common normalization strategies: 1) No normalization. 2) Subject-wise normalization to level putative differences across subjects. 3) Channel-wise normalization to level putative differences across EEG traces.

Afterward, we split the data into non-overlapping artifact-free segments of 20s. The optimal ratio from remaining subjects and remaining segments to retain the largest possible amount of data was selecting eight windows per subject. Subjects with less than eight segments were excluded from further analyses. This left 48 (MDD: 52%) / 32 (MDD: 56%) subjects for D1/D2. We utilized the first 5, 10 and 15 seconds of each segment for our analysis for segment length as another source of analytic variability.

Feature Extraction

We extracted eleven biomarkers from the alpha band (8-13 Hz, Welch filter): absolute and relative bandpower, absolute and relative centroid frequency, peak frequency, and from the envelope of the signal: kurtosis, skewness, median, interquartile range, variance and range. To calculate the envelope, we used a Butterworth filter with 8th filter order. 

Biomarker values were calculated individually for each single time segment and EEG channel. Each subject is represented by eight segments, corresponding to a data augmentation process commonly used in machine learning. Alternatively, we pursued an aggregation strategy, typically used for analyses with statistical interference tests, by taking the median over each subject’s eight segments, resulting in one single and more robust biomarker value per subject and EEG channel.

Classification

Both datasets were separately used to train classification models with three basic and commonly applied classification algorithms: Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine with a linear kernel, and Random Forest. The model inputs were 13-dimensional feature vectors containing the biomarker values for each EEG channel. The classification models were trained with six-fold cross-validation on a per-subject split basis. Balanced accuracy was calculated for each model.

Statistics

For a quantitative analysis of the replication across datasets, we calculated analyses of variance (ANOVA) with dataset as between-subject factor, and all other factors as within-subject factors. In the course of the analysis, we narrow down the factors and conduct further ANOVAs. Reported values are mean ± standard deviation unless stated otherwise. We do not correct the statistical results for multiple comparisons but report different thresholds of alpha levels to facilitate comparison to other studies.

Results

Replication of classification results with two datasets

The overall classification performance differs sign. between datasets (F1,10=4.760; p<.001). Classifiers operating on D1 achieve a mean accuracy of 59.4±9.4% across all paths with a rather wide performance range from 35.4% to 87.5% mean accuracy for the individual paths. The overall performance of classifiers operating on D2 is at chance level with 49.3±8.8% with mean accuracies for the individual paths ranging from 18.9% to 87.2%. It should be noted that only the best performing biomarker (relative centroid frequency) shows a meaningful above-average separation between HCs and MDDs for both datasets, while especially for D2 random effects in classifiers for biomarkers performing around chance level might bias overall statistical effects.

Impact of cleaning 

By manually revising the EEG signals, we found technical artifacts (high spikes and technical noise at ~5Hz across channels) in D1 that were not removed by automatic cleaning. While this has no significant impact on the overall performance of the classifiers (D1: F2,10=1.623, p=.245), biomarker performance is differentially affected by cleaning (D1: F20,100=9.469, p<.001). Analysis of D2 reveals similar statistical effects (main effect cleaning: F2,10=0.763, p=.492; interaction cleaning x biomarker: F20,100=3.390, p<.001). Importantly, the technical artifacts in D1 were unequally distributed across groups (before subject removal: MDD: 12 / HC: 1; X21=12.580, p<.01), heavily biasing the analysis. Therefore, the remaining analysis includes only manually revised data. 

Impact of processing steps on classification performance

The further analysis separates the biomarkers into two groups: For the ones not affected by normalization (absolute and relative centroid frequency, envelope skewness and kurtosis, relative bandpower, and peak frequency) the normalization factor was discarded. For biomarkers affected by normalization (envelope median, range, variance, interquartile range and the absolute bandpower) the factor normalization was included.

In D1, no significant main effects or interactions for biomarkers not affected by normalization were identified. The classification performance of the other biomarker group is sign. affected by normalization (F2,10=6.529,p<.05), segment length (F3,15=3.522, p<.05), and the classification algorithm (F2,10=4.606, p<.05). Additionally, the performance of the biomarkers is differentially modulated by normalization (F8,40=3.779, p<.01). 

D2 exhibits more vulnerability to processing steps. The performance of biomarkers unaffected by normalization sign. depends on segment length (F3,15=5.567, p<.01), biomarkers (F5,25=3.371, p<.05), and algorithm (F2,10=9.947, p<.01). Furthermore, the differential performance of biomarkers is modulated by the chosen algorithm (F10,50=2.777, p<.05). The performance of the other biomarker group is sign. affected by normalization (F2,10=4.534, p<.05) and biomarker (F4,20=13.102, p<.001). The differential performance of biomarkers is modulated by normalization (F8,40=3.210, p<.01), segment length (F12,60=9.427, p<.001), and algorithm (F8,40=4.836, p<.001).

Robustness of biomarkers across paths and folds

The less impact the processing steps have on the diagnostic accuracy of a biomarker, the more robust to analytical variability is this biomarker (Fig 2A, e.g., relative centroid frequency). We observed three kinds of divergences in the analyses. Lack of replicability across datasets: The biomarker performs good in one dataset and bad in the other (Fig 2A, e.g., absolute centroid frequency (t431=23.882, p<.001). Limited replicability within datasets: The performance within a dataset and path shows high variance across folds (Fig 2B, e.g., D2 envelope kurtosis). Analytical variability: The biomarker performance diverges widely within a dataset depending on the chosen processing path (Fig 2A, e.g., D2 envelope range).

Conclusion

This study assesses several types of reproducibility2 of alpha band EEG biomarkers for MDD diagnosis. We achieve diagnostic accuracies that range from below chance level up to 88% depending on the dataset, processing variations, biomarker, and classification algorithm. Furthermore, we demonstrate an approach to assess the robustness of biomarkers by analyzing the replicability and robustness to analytical variability. We propose that this approach should be used more frequently to shed light on the diagnostic value of biomarkers and to steer against the inflation of effect sizes in neuroimaging.

Cleaning the data of D1, one of the most widely used public datasets in MDD research, we found obvious technical noise in the signal that was not removed by automatic cleaning. This issue has not been reported before and this data is often used without removal of these artifacts.

The vast variability in processing and analysis pipelines for neuroimaging data is a source for lack of reproducibility. Our multiverse analysis revealed an influence of some of the investigated processing steps, though no discernible pattern of impact from specific steps was observable. We found that most biomarkers were not robust to analytical variability. If a biomarker is not robust against analytical variability and a good performing pipeline was chosen in a traditional study, this biomarker would be reported as a good EEG biomarker for MDD. Conversely, a biomarker with a bad performance in one pipeline might be disregarded for further analyses despite its potential with another processing choice. Performing a multiverse analysis is advantageous for processing options where the best variation is not known and can strengthen the credibility of a finding if it is rather robust across the multiverse space. While this study provides the multiverse analysis across five processing steps, further steps offer room for further variations and need to be considered in future research.

Replicability of biomarker research is important for generalizability and application in a clinical setting. Though both datasets are very small in sample size, they are widely used in biomarker studies. The performances of both datasets were sign. impacted by the biomarker choice with the relative centroid frequency being the best performing and rather robust biomarker across datasets. Further replication was severely hampered by the classifiers on D2 performing on chance level. Even though MDD patients of both datasets seemed similar, datasets behave differently according to performance, best performing biomarkers, and the impact of processing steps. Biomarkers whose diagnostic value would be assessed as good in one dataset, would perform bad in the other, even with the same processing pipeline. In order to understand the physiological underpinnings of a heterogeneous disease like MDD and to find reproducible biomarkers, richer datasets containing technical information, circumstances during recording as well as extensive demographic and neurophysiological characterizations of the subjects. This endeavor necessitates much larger datasets to achieve convergence in the findings. 

To conclude, this study demonstrates the large influence of choice of processing steps and their combinations as well as the usage of datasets. A multiverse approach in analyses of EEG resting-state data is therefore recommended, at least for the processing steps where an informed decision about a specific option cannot be made. The restricted replicability of our findings with two datasets mirrors the inconsistencies in the field very well and highlights the necessity for large and well-curated EEG datasets for MDD research. Along this line, this study is based on rather small datasets, which renders our findings primarily a methodological showcase restricted in generalizability.
