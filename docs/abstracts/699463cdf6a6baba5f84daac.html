Introduction

Analysis of magnetoencephalography (MEG) signals originating from different brain regions (neuronal populations) has revealed metastable states in certain frequency bands over time series.^1 These states are maintained for short periods and occur simultaneously across some regions, resulting in alternating synchronization and desynchronization, which is associated with cognitive flexibility.^2 This neuronal activity can be simulated using an analogy with coupled oscillators, where each oscillator represents a brain region, as in the Kuramoto model^3. In this model, the network topology determines how oscillators interact and shape the system’s dynamics. Before simulating oscillations, it is necessary to define the brain regions that will form the network. These regions must be spatially continuous, as they represent compact populations of neurons. Various parcellations of the cerebral cortex and subcortical regions have been defined based on anatomical characteristics, SC, and functional connectivity (FC). To date, the appropriate resolution for brain parcellation remains unknown. In fact, it is believed that a single parcellation cannot adequately represent the complex organization of the brain. Multi-scale hierarchical parcellations would offer greater traceability, flexibility, and scope. Simulating oscillations across different resolutions could help identify the scale that potentially provides better representation of functional brain networks. Considering this, this work seeks to implement a multiscale parcellation algorithm to analyze brain dynamics at different spatial resolutions and identify the parcellation level with the greatest metastability. Multiscale parcellation will be based on an anatomical atlas, geodesic distance, and clustering methods such as k-means++ and agglomerative hierarchical clustering. The metastability of the system will be calculated from the spectral entropy of brain oscillations simulated with the Kuramoto model.

Methods

We used preprocessed structural and diffusion MRI datasets from 99 healthy subjects (56 females, aged 22–35) from the 1200 Subjects Release (S1200) of the Human Connectome Project (HCP). Probabilistic tractography data were calculated for each subject from the dMRI data using MRtrix3. Base brain parcellation is composed of the 68 anatomical cortical regions of the (Desikan–Killiany) (DK) atlas and 14 subcortical regions from de aseg (Automatic Segmentation) atlas, both obtained with Freesurfer. Cortical brain regions were represented as surface meshes, which improves the robustness and accuracy of connectome mapping. The subcortical structures considered for each hemisphere include major nuclei and limbic components: the Thalamus, Caudate nucleus, Putamen, Globus Pallidus, Hippocampus, Amygdala, and Nucleus Accumbens. These regions were analyzed as volumetric images rather than surface meshes to better preserve their full three-dimensional shape.

To generate different resolutions of the base parcellation, we implemented two approaches: a surface-based model to represent the complex geometry of the cortex, and image-based methods for subcortical structures. The fine-grained parcellation of cortical regions was generated using the GeoSP algorithm^4 which applies K-means++ clustering and geodesic distance (GD) to subdivide a base ROI atlas. The DK ROIs were partitioned into varying numbers of subregions to produce regions of approximately equal size. Hierarchical clustering was applied to a GD matrix computed between the centroids of the subregions within each DK region to preserve the original anatomical boundaries. Different cortical parcellation levels were obtained by cutting the dendrograms. The different subcortical scales were generated by iteratively dividing each subcortical region, represented as a volumetric image, into two subregions of equal size^5. By combining cortical and subcortical scales, we generated different levels of brain parcellation, with the finest scale comprising 1000 regions.

To construct the structural connectome, we used a KDTree to identify the closest mesh vertex to each fiber endpoint in the parcellation level comprising 1000 ROIs. The MRtrix3 software was used to identify connections with subcortical regions. For cortico-subcortical connections, we combined the results from both approaches. Once the SC matrix for each subject at the 1000-ROI level was obtained, an average SC matrix across subjects was computed, retaining connections present in more than 50% of subjects. This average SC matrix was then aggregated into the other resolutions according to the hierarchical relationship between parcellation levels.

To simulate brain oscillations, the time-delayed coupled oscillators mathematical model of Kuramoto was used. The phase of each oscillator depends on its intrinsic frequency, the number of nodes N, the coupling coefficient and delay between regions, the global mean delay MD, the global coupling strength K and the interaction with other oscillators through the sine of the phase differences. The coupling strength between two regions was calculated as the number of fibers connecting both regions, which is contained in the SC matrix, normalized by the mean of their volumes. The volume for each region was calculated as its average volume across subjects in each parcellation level. The delay matrix was calculated as the average length of the fibers between each pair of regions, divided by a propagation speed equal to 6 m/s.

The Spectral Entropy (SE) of a system is an indicator of metastability, as it quantifies the diversity and distribution of oscillatory activity at different frequencies over a given period of time. The SE was calculated as the Shannon entropy Hn of the normalized power spectral density (PSD). The metastability of the system was calculated as the average of the SE across nodes. 

Results

We obtained 10 hierarchical parcellation levels ranging from 100 to 1000 ROIs, in addition to the base parcellation. As the parcellation resolution increases, the size of the regions decreases homogeneously, and the connectivity matrix gets less dense. Simulations were conducted for the 82-, 100-, 200-, 300-, 400- and 500-ROI parcellation levels until now, varying K from 0 to 1000 and MD from 0 to 30 ms. The intrinsic frequency of all oscillators was set to 40 Hz^3. For the six parcellation levels, we generated a heatmap of the SE values across the range of parameters K and MD. From each heatmap, an optimal pair of K and MD was extracted for further analysis, where the maximum SE was reached.

The average SE increased from the base parcellation (SE=0.5284) to the 400-ROI parcellation (SE=0.6130) and then slightly decreased at 500-ROI. The shape of the average PSD for each resolution shows differences in the number of frequency peaks, while the signal bandwidth remains similar.

The distribution of inter-regional connection average delays shifts toward lower values with increasing parcellation resolution.  A slight decreasing trend can be observed in the interquartile range. Additionally, the number of outliers increases slightly at higher resolutions, suggesting greater variability in rare long-delay connections. This occurs because getting smaller regions increases the representation of shorter fibers in the connectome, while simultaneously introducing longer average delays for connections between regions that are far apart. This may explain the need for a higher MD to achieve maximum SE as resolutions increase. 

Conclusion

The proposed hierarchical parcellation includes 10 levels, ranging from 100 to 1000 ROIs. As the number of parcels increases, their volumes become smaller and more uniform, reflecting a more consistent spatial resolution. As expected, finer parcellations also result in sparser SC matrices.

The main objective of the parcellation method is to generate multiple hierarchically organized scales. Although the only biological constraints are those imposed by the Desikan–Killiany atlas, which is used as the base atlas, the method is based on the geometry of the cortical surface.  The clusters obtained at each level were evaluated in terms of the homogeneity of regional volume, a characteristic commonly found in high-granularity parcellations employed for simulating brain dynamics.

The maximum average SE of the system increases with the parcellation resolution from 82 to 400 ROIs and then slightly decreased, suggesting richer dynamics at 400-ROI that may reflect a broader range of cognitive states. This observation raises the following questions: is the 400-ROI level the one with the highest SE in the multiscale parcellation, and is this feature alone sufficient to determine the most appropriate parcellation level for simulating brain dynamics?

Simulating the remaining levels to evaluate SE and compare the simulated and empirical FC networks (from MEG data) at each parcellation level constitute our next steps. The results of this comparison will help determine whether the level with higher metastability can also provide accurate estimates of real FC.
