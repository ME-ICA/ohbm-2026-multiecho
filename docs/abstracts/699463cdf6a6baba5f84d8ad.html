Introduction

Episodic memory (EM) enables the recollection of personally experienced events and plays a fundamental role in human cognition by linking past experiences to the self and supporting decision-making, future planning, and social interactions. A central question in memory research is why some events are vividly remembered whereas others are easily forgotten. The sense of agency (SoA)—the subjective experience of controlling one’s own actions and their consequences—may critically shape EM, as both rely on self-referential processes. Experiencing a strong SoA during an event may enhance memory encoding by increasing engagement and prioritizing self-relevant information, thereby strengthening memory representations. Despite growing interest, empirical evidence linking SoA to EM remains limited, and the underlying neural mechanisms are largely unknown.

Here, we developed a novel SoA–memory task to test whether perceived control over action outcomes during encoding modulates long-term item and spatial memory in healthy adults, while recording magnetoencephalography (MEG) during both encoding and retrieval. We focused on theta-band oscillations (4–7 Hz) as a candidate neural mechanism linking SoA and episodic memory. Theta activity has been implicated in action monitoring and action–outcome integration (core processes underlying SoA) as well as in episodic encoding and retrieval, particularly for associative and contextual information. Moreover, theta rhythms are thought to support hippocampal–cortical communication and neural reinstatement during recall. We therefore hypothesized that higher SoA during encoding would enhance subsequent memory performance and be associated with increased theta-band engagement during retrieval.

Methods

Thirty-three healthy right-handed participants (17 females; mean age = 25.15 ± 4.08 years, range = 18–34) took part in the experiment, which comprised two sessions (memory encoding and recognition) separated by a one-hour break.

Encoding Session (Figure 1A): On each trial, an object image appeared at the center of the screen, flanked by a left and a right box. Participants categorized each object into one box based on their own criteria by pressing the corresponding arrow key. Following the keypress, the object moved to either box. SoA was manipulated by varying the congruency between the keypress and movement direction: in congruent trials, the object moved in the chosen direction; in incongruent trials, it moved in the opposite direction. After each trial, participants rated their perceived control over the movement on a scale from 0 (no control) to 100 (full control). Participants were informed in advance that both the object and its final location would be tested after one hour and were instructed to intentionally encode both. This session consisted of 6 blocks of 20 trials (10 congruent, 10 incongruent), with unique objects per trial, and lasted ~30 minutes.

Recognition Session (Figure 1B): On each trial, participants viewed a previously encoded (“old”) object or a novel (“new”) object for 3 seconds. After its disappearance, participants indicated whether the object was old and had moved to the left (“old-left”), old and moved to the right (“old-right”), old with unknown location (“old-?”), or new. This session consisted of 10 blocks of 18 trials (6 congruent, 6 incongruent, 6 new), with unique objects per trial, and lasted ~30 minutes. After completing the task, participants rated their subjective feelings about their memory performance in the two conditions, and trait SoA was assessed using the Sense of Agency Scale.

During both sessions, brain activity was recorded using a 306-channel MEGIN TRIUX Neo MEG system (204 planar gradiometers, 102 magnetometers). A 3-min empty-room recording was acquired before each participant entered the laboratory, and 3-min resting-state data were collected prior to each session. Electrocardiogram (ECG) was recorded using two electrodes placed near the right and left clavicles, and respiration was monitored using a chest-mounted respiratory belt. Eye movements were recorded with an EyeLink 1000 eye tracker. All online signals (MEG, ECG, respiration, and eye position) were sampled at 1,000 Hz with a high-pass filter at 0.01 Hz and a low-pass filter at 330 Hz, and head position was continuously tracked. Participants sat upright in a magnetically shielded room. High-resolution T1-weighted structural MRI images were acquired for each participant using a 3T MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (3D MPRAGE, 1 × 1 × 1 mm³).

Behavioral analyses were conducted using JASP; MEG analyses were performed using MNE-Python; and T1-weighted MRI data were processed with FreeSurfer for anatomical reconstruction and construction of a single layer boundary element model to support MEG source localization.

Results

SoA Ratings: A paired-samples t-test confirmed successful manipulation of SoA during encoding, with significantly higher SoA ratings in the congruent compared to the incongruent condition (p < .001).

Memory Performance: Memory was assessed using (1) Old/New accuracy and reaction times (RTs), indexing item memory, and (2) Left/Right accuracy and RTs, indexing spatial location memory. Mixed-effects models were fitted to examine the effects of objective congruency (congruent vs. incongruent), subjective SoA ratings, and encoding duration (response time during encoding; control variable) on memory performance. Accuracy was analyzed using generalized linear mixed-effects models, and RTs using linear mixed-effects models. All models included random intercepts for participant and image. None of the three factors (congruency, SoA ratings, or encoding duration) predicted Old/New recognition accuracy or RTs (all ps > .073). In contrast, both congruency and SoA ratings significantly predicted higher Left/Right location accuracy (Figure 2A). Mediation analysis revealed that SoA ratings significantly mediated the effect of congruency on location accuracy, while a significant direct effect remained, indicating partial mediation (Figure 2B). No predictors affected RTs for correct location responses (all ps > .152). Control analyses ruled out alternative explanations related to encoding duration, spatial response biases, or guessing.

Subjective Ratings and Individual Differences: Post-task ratings indicated that participants believed they had better memory for objects encoded in the congruent condition, with a stronger perceived effect for location than item memory (p = .015). Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis showed that individual differences in trait SoA were positively correlated with the congruency-related enhancement in location memory (Figure 2C), such that higher everyday SoA was associated with a stronger spatial memory benefit in the present task.

MEG results: MEG source analyses primarily focused on trials with correct location responses during the recognition session. Theta-band activity (4–8 Hz) was examined in the hippocampus and midfrontal cortex, given its established role in action–outcome integration and episodic retrieval. Cluster-based permutation tests revealed significantly higher theta-band activity (i.e., reduced theta suppression) for objects previously encoded in the congruent compared to the incongruent condition. In the hippocampus, this effect emerged approximately 1.0–1.23 s following stimulus onset (Figure 2D), while in the midfrontal cortex it occurred between approximately 0.85 and 0.95 s (Figure 2E). These findings suggest enhanced theta-band engagement during episodic retrieval for events encoded under higher action–outcome congruency. 

Conclusion

Together, our behavioral and MEG findings demonstrate that visuo–motor congruency between actions and their consequences, and the resulting SoA during encoding, selectively enhance EM for spatial outcomes of self-caused events. This memory benefit was not observed for item recognition but was specific to source (location) memory, was partially mediated by explicit SoA ratings, and scaled with individual differences in trait SoA. At the neural level, events encoded under higher action–outcome congruency elicited increased theta-band activity during subsequent retrieval in the hippocampus and midfrontal cortex, consistent with enhanced neural reinstatement. These results suggest that the SoA acts as a cognitive binding mechanism that integrates actions and their consequences into coherent episodic representations, privileging memories of self-generated events.
