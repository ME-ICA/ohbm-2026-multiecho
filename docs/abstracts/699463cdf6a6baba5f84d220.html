Introduction

Dual-task performance declines early in healthy aging and is considered a sensitive marker of reduced executive control. Converging evidence suggests that these dual-task costs are linked to the integrity of the cholinergic basal forebrain, which plays a central role in attentional control, signal-to-noise regulation, and the stabilization of behavioral responses.

In particular, degeneration of the nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM), the major cholinergic projection nucleus to the cortex, is associated with early deficits in executive functioning in Parkinson’s disease and other neurodegenerative conditions. This raises the possibility that inter-individual variation in cholinergic function also contributes to age-related differences in dual-task performance in otherwise healthy older adults.

Fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations1 (fALFF) provides an index of regional intrinsic neural activity in the low-frequency range and may reflect the functional state of cholinergic nuclei. Here, we examine whether dual-task costs in motor and cognitive performance are associated with fALFF in the NBM in a sample of healthy older adults. It is expected that higher motor and cognitive dual-task costs will be associated with lower fALFF scores in the NBM.

Identifying such a relationship would suggest that spontaneous activity of the cholinergic basal forebrain is relevant for maintaining executive control during aging.

Methods

We conducted a one-group within-subject study with healthy older adults (N = 41, age ≥ 50 years, age M = 67.2; age SD = 7.5) from whom resting-state data were recorded in an MRI scanner as well as their performance under single and dual-task conditions inside the MRI. 

These tasks consisted of a cognitive Go-Nogo task and a motor task, in which foot pedals were pressed. They were performed individually (with the order balanced across participants) and subsequently performed simultaneously as a dual task. The outcome of the behavioral data are dual-task costs (DTC), which were calculated separately for performance (mean reaction time and reaction time variability, referring to standard deviation) in both the cognitive and motor components using the following formula: DTC = [(dual-task performance - single-task performance) / single-task performance] x 100%. 

MRI images were acquired on a SIEMENS 3T Prisma scanner. For structural scans the following parameters were used: 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 2.07 ms, voxel size = 0.75 x 0.75 x 0.75 mm³. For functional resting-state scans a 2D multiband EPI sequence with BOLD contrast was used, from the Center for Magnetic Resonance Research, University of Minnesota, TR = 850 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel size = 2.5 x 2.5 x 2.5 mm³. 

Imaging data were segmented and surface-reconstructed using fastsurfer version 2.3.0. and further preprocessed using fmriprep version 25.0.0. Datasets were excluded if participants moved excessively during the resting-state scan (head motion >3 mm translation or >3° rotation or >30% volumes show framewise displacement >0.5 mm). After discarding the first three volumes, detrending, and confound regression (including motion regressors, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals) fALFF1 was computed. Data for the NBM were extracted using the probabilistic Zaborszky NBM mask2 in MNI152NLin2009cAsym space. Mean fALFF within the NBM were correlated with each of the dual-task cost measures.

Results

One dataset was excluded due to excessive head movement, the results refer to a preliminary data analysis included 40 datasets. Participants showed clear dual-task costs in both modalities. Motor dual-task costs were larger and less variable than cognitive dual-task costs. Across participants, motor DTC (mean DTC: M = 53.2, SD = 26.7; variability DTC: M = 54.2, SD = 73.5) and cognitive DTC (mean DTC: M = 6.2, SD = 22.7; variability DTC: M = 110.5, SD = 67.1) showed substantial inter-individual variability. The fALFF scores did not vary substantially (M = 0.2, SD = 0.0).

None of the investigated relationships between NBM fALFF and the dual-task cost measures were significant: mean motor DTC and fALFF scores ρ = -.05, P = .74; standard deviation of the motor DTC and fALFF scores ρ = -.15, P = .36; mean cognitive DTC and fALFF scores ρ = -.14, P = .41; standard deviation of the cognitive DTC and fALFF scores ρ = -.15, P = .38. Nevertheless, all effects pointed in the expected negative direction.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate substantial inter-individual variability in dual-task performance in healthy older adults, but this behavioral variability was not reflected in resting-state fALFF of the NBM. This stands in contrast to findings in clinical and preclinical Alzheimer’s disease, where reduced NBM fALFF has been shown to differentiate individuals with abnormal cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers and predict functional decline in the entorhinal cortex3. One possibility is that cholinergic dysfunction in cognitively healthy aging is subtler than in preclinical populations, and therefore too small to detect with resting-state fALFF alone. Another explanation is methodological. Recent reproducibility work has highlighted that resting-state-fMRI–derived features such as fALFF and Regional Homogeneity are sensitive to analytic choices and often show limited robustness4. Given the NBM’s small size and proximity to susceptibility-prone areas, the absence of a detectable association in our study may reflect limitations of the measure rather than the absence of a physiological relationship.

Together, these considerations suggest that while cholinergic function remains a plausible contributor to individual differences in executive control during aging, resting-state fALFF in the NBM may not be a sufficiently sensitive marker in healthy populations. Future studies using larger samples, multimodal approaches (e.g., structural, diffusion-weighted and functional imaging measures), and harmonized processing pipelines may provide a more reliable assessment of cholinergic contributions to dual-task performance.
