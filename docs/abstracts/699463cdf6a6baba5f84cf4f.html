Introduction

Rapid threat processing is crucial for survival. In vision, neural models for emotional processing propose the existence of direct pathways, or “low roads”, from the visual thalamus to the amygdala, which may rely on magnocellular inputs and thereby enable fast processing of threat signals1–3. In audition, evidence from non-human animals points to the existence of a similar direct magnocellular pathway, but their presence in humans remains unclear4. Building on animal evidence that high-rate amplitude-modulated (highAM) sounds preferentially engage magnocellular auditory processing5, we combined behavioral, physiological and neuroimaging measures across two complementary studies to search for a magnocellular-associated “low road” for auditory threat in humans. Specifically, we tested whether highAM threat sounds elicit rapid affective responses (Study 1), and whether highAM emotional processing aligns with structural strength of specialized thalamo-amygdala connectivity (Study 2). Based on evidence in the visual domain, we hypothesized that threat-related EEG and pupillary responses would emerge earlier for highAM, relative to lowAM threat voices in Study 1. Finally, in Study 2, if a subcortical auditory pathway to the amygdala operates through magnocellular-associated mechanisms specifically sensitive to highAM signals, we predicted that neural sensitivity to highAM threat cues would covary with individual variability in the structural density of these thalamo-amygdala connections.

Methods

During fear conditioning, we recorded electroencephalography and pupillometry (Study 1) as well as functional and diffusion-weighted imaging (Study 2) from 28 and 31 healthy participants respectively as they detected voices. Human vocalizations were either paired (conditioned, CS+) or unpaired (not conditioned, CS-) with an unpleasant loud white noise (unconditioned stimulus, US), which determined their threatening significance. All voices were amplitude modulated at either highAM (40 Hz) or lowAM (10 Hz) rates.

Results

Across both completed empirical studies, convergent patterns emerged confirming our hypotheses. Behavioral and late neural and pupillary responses suggest that fear conditioning was effective; participants were aware of the pairing between the conditioned and the US stimuli (p < .001), they rated the conditioned stimuli as more negative and arousing than non-conditioned stimuli (p < .001), and showed differential pupil dilations (p = .007), auditory responses (p = .007), as well as differential number of hits (p = .011) and response times (p < .01) for CS+ versus CS- stimuli. Critically, only highAM cues triggered rapid neural and pupillary effects (Study 1), consistent with fast magnocellular-like inputs to the amygdala (p = .031; p = .024). Remarkably, analyses of the complementary functional and diffusion-weighted imaging data (Study 2) revealed that selective amygdala responses to highAM (and not lowAM) emotional voices were functionally associated with individual fiber density of a direct thalamo-amygdala pathway (MNI coordinates: 26, -6, -20; kE = 87; T = 4.23; pFWE-SVC = .002).

Conclusion

Together, these findings provide convergent functional and anatomical evidence supporting the existence of a fast direct subcortical pathway for auditory threat processing in humans, which is preferentially engaged by magnocellular-associated acoustic signals, and may potentially mirror the visual “low roads” long described in human affective neuroscience. Our results also bridge animal and human research on evolutionarily conserved subcortical systems for affective processing.
