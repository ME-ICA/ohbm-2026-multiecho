Introduction

Obesity is a chronic and multifactorial disease that results from alterations in the function of the organs or the whole organism directly induced by excess of adiposity and affects many children worldwide. Obesity has been associated with brain structure alterations, brain functional connectivity deficits, and executive functions (EF) impairments.^1 ^2 

EF refer to top-down mental processes necessary to coordinate goal‑directed behaviors. They encompass inhibition (the ability to suppress a reflex response), working memory (the ability to hold and use information for a short span), cognitive flexibility (the ability to shift between different strategies/mental sets based on environmental changes), planning (the ability to anticipate and organize a sequence of actions), and emotional regulation (the ability to use adaptive strategies in response to emotions).

Brain regions and networks alterations associated with obesity are mainly the salience network, the executive network, the DMN, and the reward network, which are involved in cognitive and affective functioning, interoceptive and homeostatic processes, self-control behaviors, and reward processing, respectively.^2

It has been established that both EF and brain functioning are modulated by several factors, including age and sex. While the impact of pediatric obesity on EF and brain functioning is being increasingly investigated, the question whether sex differences exist remains understudied. The aim of our study was to investigate sex differences in EF, brain structure and connectivity in children with obesity.

Methods

Participants

Forty-six children (19 girls) with obesity were recruited from two local hospitals of Barcelona. Inclusion criteria were: a homogeneous sample of preadolescents (to avoid the confounding factors induced by puberty) with an age between 9 and 12 years, having obesity (Body Mass Index (BMI) standard deviation >2 for age and sex), and an intelligence quotient (IQ) within the normal range (80–120). Exclusion criteria were: having a neurological, psychiatric, or developmental disorder, secondary obesity, and/or being under psychotropic medication.

Clinical assessments

EF evaluations, based on the work of Lezak and collaborators (2012), included:  

  1. Cognitive flexibility, using the shifting subtest of the Five Digit Test (FDT) and part II of the Children’s Color Trail Test (CCTT2);
  2. Working memory, using the backward digit (Indirect Digits and Ascending Digits) span of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children–5th edition (WISC-V), the backward spatial span of the Wechsler Non-Verbal Scale (WNV), and the correct responses sub-score of the Tower of London test (ToL);
  3. Inhibitory control, using the choosing subtest of the FDT, the commission errors and perseveration sub-scores of the Conner’s Continuous Performance Test 3 (CPT3), and the ToL initiation time sub-score;
  4. Planning abilities, using the ToL rule violations, total time and moves sub-scores.

All cognitive scores were z scaled based on normative data. Moreover, indices for each EF domain (Working memory, Inhibitory control, Cognitive flexibility and Planning) were computed using the means of the corresponding tests z-scores.

Emotional skills were assessed using the emotional regulation index (ERI) of the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function 2nd edition completed by the parents. 

Brain imaging

The following sequences were used to acquire the brain images, within less than a month after the clinical assessments:

High resolution three-dimensional T1-weighted images in the sagittal plane (MPRAGE sequence: TR=2400ms, TE=3.32ms, TI=1000ms, 256 slices, FOV=224mm; flip angle=8◦; 0.8mm isotropic voxel).

3D T2-weighted images in sagittal orientation (TR=3200ms, TE=298ms, 256 slices, FOV=224mm; 0.9mm isotropic voxel).

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) sequence: single-shot diffusion-weighted EPI, b=1000 mm2/s, 30 directions, TR=7700ms, TE=89ms, FOV=244mm, 2mm isotropic voxel. Each direction was acquired twice (AP and PA) and images were averaged, improving signal-to-noise ratio.

Resting state fMRIecho-planar imaging–EPI sequence (240 volumes, 40 axial slices, TR=2500ms, TE=28ms, FOV=240mm, flip angle=80◦, 3mm isotropic voxel).

Analyses

Clinical assessments 

Between-group comparisons were conducted using Student T or Mann-Whitney tests (and associated effect sizes) as appropriate. 

Brain imaging

Images were available for 42 children (19 girls).

Preprocessing of structural images was performed with Freesurfer. Volumetric measures were acquired with the AssemblyNet pipeline of Volbrain. Sex differences were assessed with a family-wise MANCOVA (regions were separated into subcortical, cortical frontal, cortical temporal, cortical parietal, cortical occipital, cortical limbic and insula, cortical operculum, and cerebellum-brainstem) controlling for intracranial volume (ICV) and BMI z-score, followed by ANCOVAs and a Benjamin-Hocheberg FDR correction for the significant families (using p<0.05).

Preprocessing and analyses of fMRI data were performed with the CONN Toolbox, using the default preprocessing pipeline and the AAL atlas for the ROI-to-ROI connectivity analysis. Whole-brain comparisons in connectivity measures between girls and boys were assessed controlling for the BMI z-score. A threshold of 0.001 was used as a p-value to assess the significance of the connectivity between ROIs. 

Results

Participants

There was no difference in age, IQ and economical background between boys and girls in our sample. 

 

Clinical assessments

As illustrated on Figure 1, children with obesity had worse performances than normative data on some but not all clinical measures.

Relative to boys, girls differed significantly more from normative data on ToL rule violations (p=0.005; ES(r)=0.4). Moreover, they had significantly lower planning index score (p=0.011; ES(d)=0.4). 

Brain volumetry

Boys had greater ICV than girls (p<0.001). Controlling for ICV, girls had greater amygdala volume (p=0.02). Conversely, boys had greater parietal lobule and supramarginal gyrus volumes than girls (both p=0.03).

Brain connectivity

Four ROI-to-ROI connections differed significantly between girls and boys. There was: 

- A stronger connectivity between n the right Heschl gyrus and the left temporal superior gyrus among boys;

- A stronger connectivity between 1) the left orbital part of the inferior frontal gyrus and the left inferior parietal gyrus, 2) the left rectus gyrus and the left precuneus, and 3) the right cerebellum lobule III and the left cerebellum lobule VIII among girls.


Conclusion

Overall, the present results suggest some sex-dependent differences in executive functioning, brain anatomy and brain connectivity. However, given that our study did not include a healthy-weight age-matched control group, no conclusion on the potential influence of obesity can be drawn. 

A recent meta-analysis^3 failed to identify clear sex differences in terms of general executive control, except for some potential task-specific differences, such as better performances on spatial working memory tasks for men but better performances in delay discounting task for women. In our sample, girls displayed worse planning strategies than boys, which is somewhat inconsistent with this meta-analysis. The discrepancy could be explained by the fact that obesity tends to be associated with lower inhibitory control and planning skills.^1 Furthermore, age and biological maturity may influence the timing of EF development such as inhibition and working memory. 

In line with the literature,^4 our sample of boys had a larger global brain volume. Regarding local regional differences, our results suggest that girls had greater amygdala volume than boys but too few studies are available in the literature to conclude. Conversely, our sample of boys had larger parietal lobule and supramarginal gyrus. Again, findings on sex differences in terms of cortical regional volumes are inconsistent.^4 Few studies revealed volume differences that are consistent with our results, specifically for the parietal lobule, which has been found to be larger in women then in men.^4  As these findings concern a healthy-weight population, here also the discrepancy with our results could be due to having obesity and a young age. 

On the contrary, our functional connectivity results tend to align with the literature, with girls displaying greater connectivity between regions involved in the DMN and the fronto-parietal network.^5 Yet, women have been found to present stronger connectivity in the superior temporal regions than men,^5 while in our study this was the case for boys. In addition, our results suggest stronger connectivity within cerebellar lobules in girls than boys. At this stage, we haven’t been able to identify studies in the literature on sex differences in these brain regions.

Finally, additional analyses are being currently performed on DTI data as well as correlations between brain and clinical measures. Hopefully, these complementary analyses may allow a better understanding of the link between cerebral and behavioral indices in this population.  

In sum, up to now, the literature on sex differences in terms of executive functions, brain structure and connectivity among children is too sparce, especially in pediatric obesity. Overall, some patterns and trends are emerging, but drawing firm conclusions is hampered by the variety of methods, sample sizes and ages used. Our results only partly align with findings among healthy-weight population. Knowing that obesity impacts executive functions, brain structure and connectivity, future studies should include a control group of normal-weight children.
