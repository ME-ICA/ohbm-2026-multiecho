Introduction

Whole-brain cellular mapping is essential for understanding how neuronal and glial populations vary across individuals and how these differences shape brain architecture and function. However, achieving this remains challenging as imaging techniques typically have trade-offs between field of view, spatial resolution, and specificity. For instance, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can provide whole-brain measures of tissue microstructure, but it lacks the cellular specificity offered by microscopy. Recent developments in light-sheet microscopy (LSM) have begun to bridge this gap by enabling 3D mapping of cell density and diversity in the intact rodent brain at micron scale resolution. Nevertheless, LSM remains limited to ex vivo applications and suffers from inherently low throughput.1  

In this work we introduce technical advances that increase throughput across tissue clearing, immunolabelling, and LSM of the intact mouse brain. By combining these approaches, we enable large-scale imaging of neuronal cell bodies and generate whole-brain LSM-derived cell-density maps. Using diffusion MRI (dMRI) acquired from the same brains, we further develop predictive models that link macroscopic MRI contrasts to microscopic cellular features. Together, these developments establish a framework for integrating MRI and 3D microscopy to infer microstructural specificity from MRI in ways that can ultimately be translated to living humans. 

Methods

We analysed 8 mouse brains, each drawn from a genetically distinct BXD strain selected for known variation in neuronal densities. Two weeks following perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde and 2mM gadolinium contrast agent, brains were MR-imaged, extracted, immunolabelled, and rendered optically transparent to allow for whole-brain LSM. An overview of the full workflow is shown in Fig.1A.  

We optimised active tissue clearing and immunolabelling protocols using a SmartBatch+ device (LifeCanvas Technologies),1 which employs stochastic electrotransport2 and reaction-modulation techniques (eFLASH and SWITCH).1,2 This active approach accelerates the transmission of clearing agents and antibodies across a sample, whilst also ensuring uniform distribution, compared to conventional passive approaches (Fig. 1B).  

Specifically, intact brains were preserved using SHIELD2 and subsequently delipidated with Clear+ reagents (LifeCanvas Technologies).2 Samples were incubated overnight at 4°C in 5% hydrogen peroxide. Mature neurons and astrocytes were simultaneously labelled for NeuN (6mg CST 3666SF and 12mg JIR 711-295-152 per brain) and GFAP (10mg Biolegend 644702 and 20mg Biotium 20177 per brain). After immunolabelling, brains were dehydrated in a methanol series (1hr each in 20, 40, 60, 80, 100%) and transferred to ethyl cinnamate (Sigma, 112372-100G) for refractive index matching (RI=1.56).  

Once samples were optically transparent, they were imaged with an Ultramicroscope BLAZE (Miltenyi Biotech), operated in Lightspeed mode, which was compared against conventional imaging using dynamic horizontal focus (Fig. 1C). Each brain was illuminated from both sides (L/R) using three sheets at maximum width (NA=0.163) and minimum thickness (3.9mm). Sample autofluorescence (Ex/Em: 488/520-50nm) and immunolabelling (NeuN Ex/Em:561/595-40nm, GFAP Ex/Em:640/680-30nm) were acquired with the 1x objective and provide low-resolution whole-brain image volumes (6x6x4µm³) for quality control and registration to MRI. Higher-resolution whole-brain volumes (1x1x4µm³), stitched from multiple overlapping tiles, of immunolabelling were obtained with the 4x objective for cell segmentation using Cellpose3. Finally, immunolabelling within a restricted region of the right hippocampus was imaged at maximum resolution (0.22x0.22x2.45µm3) with the 12x objective to assess cell morphology (Fig. 1D).   

Prior to LSM, all animals underwent ex vivo MRI on a 7T Bruker BioSpec 70/20 USR scanner with a 4-channel cryocoil and 660mT/m gradients. The protocol included high-resolution T2-weighted structural imaging (3D TurboRARE 60µm isotropic; TE/TR= 36/350ms, RARE factor=6, ESP=12ms, BW 60 kHz) and multi-shell diffusion MRI (150µm isotropic; 16 directions per shell at b = [1, 2.5, 5.5, 8.5, 12.5, 17.5] ms/µm2, 𝛿/Δ=7.5/20ms). Diffusion data were denoised in the complex domain using NORDIC, shell-normalised, Gibbs-ringing corrected, and distortion-corrected using FSL-eddy. Diffusion models (DTI, DKI, NODDI and SANDI)4 were then fitted to extract voxel-wise microstructural metrics in grey matter, including neurite density, soma density, and parameters related to non-Gaussian diffusion (e.g., MK). 

Results

The optimised methods for tissue processing and microscopy enabled a 3x reduction in clearing and immunolabelling time (Fig. 1B) and >2x reduction in LSM imaging time (Fig. 1C). This acceleration facilitated the staggered processing of samples and completion of whole-brain LSM imaging for all 8 samples within 6 weeks. The same protocol can be used to label multiple cell types, including neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes (MOG), microglia (IBA-1), and dopaminergic neurons (TH), as shown in Fig 1E. Importantly, the protocol remains compatible with samples perfused with gadolinium, which enhances MRI contrast and shortens acquisition time, without compromising downstream clearing or microscopy.  

The increased throughput enabled relatively large-scale collection of LSM data, which we used to train models that predict LSM-derived features from MRI contrasts acquired in the same brain. Neuronal cell bodies were segmented from the LSM data using a Cellpose model3 trained on manually annotated data. Neuron counts within 128×128×32 voxel patches were converted into voxel-wise neuron-density maps, which were non-linearly registered to the corresponding T2-weighted MRI using TIRL5 (Fig. 2A), facilitating direct comparison with dMRI metrics. 

To assess the cellular specificity of dMRI microstructural measures, we trained a random-forest model to predict neuron density directly from dMRI features across the grey matter, using the LSM-derived NeuN maps from the same sample as target. Modelling was performed independently for each mouse strain. Model performance, assessed on held out data, exceeded chance for all BXD strains, as shown in Fig. 2B, though with some inter-strain variability. Multiple dMRI metrics contributed to prediction accuracy. Notably, combining biophysical parameters from SANDI, related to cell-body density and size, with phenomenological measures from the DKI model, including FA, MD and kurtosis, improved performance, suggesting that there is additional cellular information in the signal that is not captured by a single dMRI microstructure model. 

Conclusion

We introduce an optimised pipeline for mapping cellular features in the intact mouse brain following MRI. All steps in this protocol, including fixation, delipidation, bleaching, immunolabelling, and index matching, can be completed in 2 weeks. This workflow approaches the turnaround time of conventional 2D histology while maintaining the advantages of 3D whole-brain imaging. In addition, by using accelerated 3D microscopy acquisition modes, we show that high throughput imaging of the whole brain at cellular resolution is feasible. 

We also highlight the value of combining high-resolution whole-brain 3D light-sheet microscopy with diffusion MRI. By integrating modalities that provide complementary information across multiple spatial scales, we establish a unified pipeline that links structural, microstructural, and cellular data within a common anatomical framework. Successful registration of LSM-derived neuron density maps to MRI enabled direct voxel-wise comparisons, revealing systematic associations between cellular organisation and dMRI-derived microstructural measures. As a proof-of-concept, we further demonstrate the feasibility of developing machine-learning models to predict LSM cellular features from dMRI contrasts. Although current predictive performance is modest, these results illustrate the potential for using multimodal datasets to validate, calibrate, and ultimately enhance the cellular specificity of MRI metrics. 

Together, these findings provide a foundation for cross-scale modelling of brain tissue, opening the door to MRI-based, cell-specific biomarkers and establishing a framework to translate cellular insights toward in vivo, and eventually human, neuroimaging.
