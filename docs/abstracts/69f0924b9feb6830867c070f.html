Introduction

Large multi-site pediatric neuroimaging studies must harmonize MRI acquisition protocols and data formats across institutions, often handled manually post-collection by individual labs. This ad-hoc curation can lag months behind data acquisition, delaying analyses and hindering early quality control (QC). Neuroimaging research would benefit from more datasets conforming to FAIR1 (findability, accessibility, interoperability, and reusability) principles while preserving patient privacy or local data governance. These pressures drive the need for automated, scalable workflows that continuously integrate multiple data sources in a secure, privacy-preserving manner.

The Canadian Pediatric Imaging Platform (C-PIP) is a national initiative linking three major pediatric hospitals across Canada to build a large neuroimaging cohort and bioinformatics platform. The project aims to recruit nearly 800 participants (ages 0-18) and collect a longitudinal multimodal dataset including brain MRI, genetic, and cognitive measures to help understand neurodevelopment.2 A core goal of C-PIP is to enable secure, efficient multi-site data sharing. We developed BIDS-Flux, a privacy-preserving distributed platform for automated data ingestion and processing with a federated design. As each site keeps its data on local secure servers, pushing only de-identified outputs to a federated repository, this approach enables harmonization without transferring sensitive information.3

BIDS-Flux employs containerized workflows orchestrated via GitLab continuous integration (CI) and DataLad,4 with modern object storage for scalability (MinIO). It automates data flow from conversion of raw data into BIDS format and de-identification, through quality checks, to generation of analysis-ready derivatives.5 Every step is logged with version control to preserve full provenance. By minimizing manual intervention, BIDS-Flux ensures that all sites contribute to a unified, high-quality dataset ready for analysis.

Methods

BIDS-Flux is built on community-driven open-source technologies, primarily the BIDS standard and the DataLad data-versioning framework.⁴ Together, these provide interoperability with established neuroimaging tools, repositories, and cloud-native compute environments. FAIR compliance is achieved through transparent, standards-based metadata describing datasets and analytical processes, while accessibility is enabled through a federated architecture accommodating diverse governance and data-sovereignty requirements.

The system operates as a modular, containerized workflow supporting continuous ingestion, standardization, anonymization, QC, reporting, and preprocessing of neuroimaging and phenotypic data. Project-specific heuristics deployed on local hospital or research servers coordinate data handling, while GitLab continuous integration (CI) manages execution of Docker-based pipelines. DataLad provides large-file tracking via Git-Annex and full provenance through Git version control (Figure 1).

Raw instrument-specific data (e.g., DICOMs) are automatically indexed and registered within a DataLad dataset. Lightweight non-sensitive metadata are synchronized with local DevOps services, while large or sensitive files remain in local S3-compatible object stores with tiered access for raw, BIDS-converted, and derivative data. CI pipelines fetch required content, execute containerized processes (e.g., BIDS conversion and defacing), and submit results through pull requests that include automated validation and interactive QC reports. Following human review, approved datasets are synchronized to a federated platform hosted on the Digital Research Alliance of Canada cloud infrastructure. Curator approval triggers downstream preprocessing pipelines and a second review cycle before derivative synchronization.

Although developed for neuroimaging and phenotypic data, the underlying DataLad-based framework is domain-agnostic and can support other file-based standards, time-series databases, or web-based survey data. All ingestion and curation occur under site-specific IT policies, with access control enforced locally and at the federation level through institutional identity-and-access-management systems.

Results

BIDS-Flux enables near real-time automated data flow across all participating C-PIP sites, reducing the time from acquisition to analysis from weeks to hours with minimal human intervention. Across sites, the full C-PIP MRI protocol requires approximately 5-10 minutes for indexing, 3-15 minutes for BIDS conversion, 3-4 minutes for defacing, and 2-3 hours for automated QC metric extraction and report generation. Phenotypic data collected via REDCap are ingested and structured in approximately 1-2 minutes. BIDS-Flux supports human-in-the-loop workflows as well, allowing you to deal with processing issues or tagging QC outcomes.

Federated repositories remain continuously updated and harmonized, providing audit-ready, BIDS-valid datasets with full data and process provenance (Figure 2). System uptime, processing metrics, resource utilization, and pipeline success rates are exported to Prometheus and visualized via Grafana dashboards, supporting monitoring of operational robustness and scalability.

Early evaluations demonstrate stable continuous-integration performance, early detection of protocol deviations, and successful reuse of the platform across additional local research projects, confirming its modularity and scalability. The code base will become publicly available through our GitHub organization BIDS-Flux (https://github.com/orgs/BIDS-flux).

Conclusion

BIDS-Flux provides a scalable, reproducible, and FAIR-compliant infrastructure for multi-site neuroimaging studies. Applied in C-PIP, automated QC pipelines and cross-site dashboards enable early detection of data inconsistencies and protocol drift, supporting rapid feedback and intervention. While deployment required coordination across heterogeneous IT environments, the platform’s modular design supports adaptation across diverse institutional settings without compromising privacy.

Beyond neuroimaging, BIDS-Flux generalizes to other data modalities while enabling privacy-preserving, federated research through rich exposed metadata. Planned integration with NeuroBagel annotations will enhance cross-dataset discovery across national and international neuroimaging repositories, while future JupyterLab- and NeuroLibre-based extensions may support executable publications and reproducible analytics. BIDS-Flux thus offers a blueprint for next-generation neuroinformatics infrastructures that are secure, scalable, and FAIR by design, distributing the operations of the platform across sites and enabling federated data management.
