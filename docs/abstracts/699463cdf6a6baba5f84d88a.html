Introduction

Criminal convictions require not only proof of a guilty act (actus reus), but also of a culpable mental state (mens rea). In many cases, distinguishing whether a defendant acted knowingly or recklessly determines their legal culpability and the severity of punishment. Prior work (Vilares et al., 2017) showed that fMRI data collected during a task designed to simulate the knowing vs reckless distinction can successfully differentiate these mental states—but only when brain activity is recorded in the moment, as the act unfolds in the scanner. In real-world legal contexts, mens rea must be inferred from post hoc information, and such mental states are not available for direct observation. For neuroimaging to have forensic relevance, we must determine if legally relevant mental states can be reconstructed from memory, after the fact. Here, we test whether delayed memory of evidence can reveal the mental state under which an act was committed.

Methods

Participants: Forty adults recruited locally completed a behavioral contraband task and a follow-up MRI scan.

Behavioral and fMRI Scanning Sessions: Participants were told a cover story in which they decided whether to carry a suitcase that might contain illegal “contraband” through a checkpoint. On each trial, they saw either one suitcase (Knowing condition; contraband probability = 1) or five suitcases (Reckless condition; contraband probability = 0.2), along with a cue indicating the likelihood of being searched at the checkpoint (Figure 1a). Participants then chose whether they were willing to carry a case randomly selected from the set (Figure 1b). The payout structure encouraged participants to avoid getting caught: if searched, carrying resulted in a monetary loss, whereas if not searched, carrying could produce a gain (Figure 1d). After completing the contraband task, participants were scanned either 3 days (“Near”) or 30 days (“Far), forming four cohorts defined by mental state (Knowing vs Reckless) and scan group (Near vs Far) (Figure 1c). During scanning, participants viewed repeated presentations of one familiar image from their task condition (one suitcase for Knowing; five suitcases for Reckless), as well as two unfamiliar images: the opposite suitcase set (five for Knowing; one for Reckless) and a novel three suitcase stimulus. All images were shown individually and in randomized order. 

fMRI Analysis: Participants returned for functional magnetic resonance imaging on a 3T Siemens Trio during the second study visit. Preprocessing of echo-planar images included slice timing correction, realignment, co-registration to each participant’s T1 anatomical image, normalization, and smoothing with a 6 mm FWHM kernel in SPM12. A first-level general linear model was fit for each participant, modeling onsets of case and tunnel images. To evaluate the Knowing vs Reckless distinction while collapsing across scan group, we compared classification performance across three linear models (support vector machine, logistic regression, and elastic net) using whole-brain response patterns extracted for the relevant stimuli (one suitcase vs five-suitcase images) and organized into four feature representations: familiar, unfamiliar, concatenated familiar + unfamiliar, and difference (familiar – unfamiliar). To evaluate the Near vs Far distinction within mental state, these same feature representations were assessed using elastic net only. In all analyses, whole-brain responses were z-scored within subject and condition. To estimate model generalization, we implemented a nested cross-validation framework with a five-fold outer loop and a three-fold inner loop for model calibration using Platt scaling. To quantify classification performance, models were evaluated using area under the ROC curve (AUC). To assess the statistical significance of classifier performance, we conducted 1,000-iteration permutation testing by randomly shuffling held-out labels to generate a null AUC distribution against which observed AUCs were compared. For visualization, classifier coefficient vectors were used to compute voxel-wise signed survival rates across outer folds, which were projected back into brain space. 

Results

For the Knowing vs Reckless distinction, classification performance using responses to familiar stimuli was modest (AUCs: 0.358-0.617 across models). Performance improved when using unfamiliar stimuli (AUCs: 0.720-0.780) and further improved when concatenating familiar and unfamiliar responses (AUCs: 0.728-0.915). The difference contrast outperformed all other representations (AUCs: 0.952-0.983; accuracies: 90-93%). Voxels with the highest survival rates for Knowing were in the right precuneus and left superior temporal gyrus, whereas voxels predictive of Reckless were concentrated in left lingual and occipital regions (Figure 2). For the Near vs Far distinction within mental state, training on the familiar responses yielded AUCs of 0.448 (Knowing) and 0.615 (Reckless); unfamiliar responses yielded AUCs of 0.292 (Knowing) and 0.458 (Reckless). Concatenated responses produced AUCs of 0.275 for both mental states. The difference contrast yielded AUCs of 0.552 (Knowing) and 0.500 (Reckless). 

Conclusion

The Knowing vs Reckless boundary was most strongly captured by differential neural responses to familiar versus unfamiliar stimuli. Representations within mental state did not significantly differ across near and far scan groups. Importantly, the absence of a Near vs Far distinction does not undermine the legal relevance of these findings: if we are able to point to mens rea, criminal culpability can be established irrespective of when the mental state occurred. These results suggest that the brain’s memory responses can preserve information about whether someone acted knowingly or recklessly, even weeks after the event. In particular, probing memory responses to familiar and unfamiliar evidence can reveal detectable traces of the prior mental state in the nervous system, offering a potential framework for inferring mens rea when the original moment of decision is no longer directly accessible.
