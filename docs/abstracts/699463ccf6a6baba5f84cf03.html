Introduction

Human brain charts across the entire lifespan are indispensable for advancing both basic neuroscience and clinical research in neuropsychiatric disorders. The continuous aggregation of large-scale neuroimaging datasets has enabled the construction of population-level brain charts. However, the currently available lifespan brain charts are predominantly derived from European and North American cohorts, with a severe underrepresentation of Asian populations 1-4. The largest lifespan brain chart study to date (involving 101,457 individuals across 286 global sites) included fewer than 4,500 participants (< 4%) from East Asian ancestry, with a strikingly low representation from China. This representative bias raises critical questions about the universality of current brain charts and their clinical utility across diverse populations.

Compared with individuals of European ancestry, Chinese individuals exhibit distinct neuroanatomical features. These inter-ancestry differences, shaped by complex gene‒culture‒environment interactions, fundamentally challenge the generalizability of Western-centric brain charts to Chinese population. Supporting this, recent studies have revealed ethnic disparities in deviation scores within the UK Biobank cohort and reduced accuracy in cross-population brain-behavior prediction. Together, these empirical failures challenge the presumption of neurodevelopmental universality across ethnic groups, necessitating the urgent establishment of population-specific reference frameworks. 

To address this gap, we establish the population-specific brain growth charts for Chinese individuals, based on neuroimaging data of 43,037 participants (0-100 years, 384 sites). These brain charts not only fill critical knowledge gaps in understanding population-specific milestones of brain maturation but also establish a blueprint for developing ethnically specific reference standards worldwide, thereby advancing equitable precision medicine in brain health.

Methods

Structural MRI data processing

All structural MRI scans underwent a unified four-stage quality control pipeline 3. 43,037 healthy Chinese participants aged 0–100 years from 384 scanner sites across 29 provinces were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1a, c). For cross-population comparison, we further assembled a non-Chinese dataset comprising 56,339 healthy participants aged 0–100 years from 174 scanner sites across eight predominantly Western countries (Fig. 1b, d). For individuals aged two years and older, all structural MRI data underwent uniform preprocessing with FreeSurfer v6.0.0. For participants aged 0–2 years, we employed the iBEAT v2.0 pipeline, which is optimized for early-age neuroimaging data. For neonates from the dHCP study, we used data processed with the officially recommended dHCP pipeline. The individual cortical surfaces from participants aged <2 years were aligned with the adult fsaverage standard space using a three-step registration method 3. We delineated lifespan trajectories of 296 global and regional phenotypes in a large Chinese population and compared them against Western-centric models (Fig. 1e).

Modelling the lifespan growth curves

We applied generalized additive models for location, scale and shape (GAMLSS) to estimate the normative growth patterns. Bethlehem et al 1 demonstrated that the generalized gamma distribution is the most suitable for brain structural phenotypic data. To ensure optimal model fit, we explored alternative model configurations by varying the number of polynomials (1-3) used to parameterize the μ and σ components of the GG distribution, and additionally selected whether to include random effects for site in these parameters. The model with the lowest Bayesian information criterion was selected as the optimal model. 

Differences in the peak age between the Chinese and Western growth curves

All Chinese and Western participants were combined into a single pool. Stratified random sampling was performed on the basis of age and sex, with 44,969 participants assigned to “Chinese” group and the remainder assigned to “Western” group. Normative growth curves were then separately re-estimated for these two pseudo-groups, and the peak age difference between the two curves was calculated. A null distribution of the peak age differences for each phenotype was generated (1000 permutation). The empirical p value for each phenotype was determined relative to the null distribution. Multiple comparisons across all phenotypes were corrected using a false discovery rate threshold of 0.05.

Predictive accuracy for brain phenotypes of the Chinese and Western growth models

An independent testing set of 2,591 healthy controls from Chinese case–control datasets was used. As these participants were part of the original Chinese normative model, all models were retrained to prevent data leakage. The controls were randomly divided into training (N = 1,223) and testing (N = 1,368) subsets, stratified by age, sex, and site. The retrained models included 41,669 Chinese and 57,562 Western samples. The held-out testing subset was used to evaluate model performance (Fig. 5a), with predictive accuracy quantified by the coefficient of determination (R² = 1 − SSE/SST).

Clinical relevance of Chinese-specific normative models

Structural MRI data from four brain disorders were included—AD (n = 399), MCI (n = 283), SCZ (n = 691), and MDD (n = 1,492)—using the same quality control and preprocessing procedures. Patient data served as independent tests for individual deviation scores, with extreme deviations defined as |z| > 2.6. The proportion of extreme deviations across all phenotypes was calculated per patient. To evaluate model generalizability, we compared Chinese and Western-centric models in Chinese clinical cohorts using two-sample t-tests and reported effect sizes.

Results

Population-specific normative growth of global brain phenotypes

Using C-LBM Phase I data (43,037 participants, 384 sites in China), we first constructed sex-stratified Chinese lifespan normative models for 296 structural brain phenotypes. For cross-population comparisons, we developed Western-centric models (56,339 participants, 174 sites across Europe/North America/Oceania). 

Our analysis of Chinese lifespan brain charts revealed distinct neurodevelopmental timelines across global phenotypes (for example, see Fig. 2a). Most volumetric measures (excluding ventricles) showed inverted U-shaped trajectories with phenotype-specific peaks. Although Western-centric models exhibited similar nonlinear patterns, permutation analyses identified significant cross-population differences in peak maturation ages. The Chinese models showed delayed maturation (0.9–1.7 years later, all PFDR < 0.001) in total cerebral, subcortical GM, and cortical WM volumes, as well as in cortical thickness, surface area, and folding index. In contrast, they exhibited earlier maturation in intracranial (2.1 years), cerebellar (3.6 years), and cerebellar GM (2.4 years) volumes (all PFDR < 0.001).

Population-specific normative growth of subcortical regional phenotypes

The neostriatum (caudate and putamen) exhibited earliest peak maturation, with the caudate peaking in late childhood and the putamen in early adolescence, followed by the paleostriatum (pallidum) peaking in early adulthood (Fig. 2b). The thalamus and limbic structures reached peak maturation in early adulthood. Permutation testing revealed significantly prolonged peak maturation in several subcortical structures (caudate, putamen, thalamus, amygdala, and hippocampus) (left hemisphere: 0.4–2.5 years; right hemisphere: 0.3–2.5 years) in the Chinese-specific models compared with the Western-centric models (PFDR < 0.001).

Population-specific normative growth of cortical regional phenotypes

The Chinese population presented prolonged peak maturation in higher-order association cortices (lateral temporal/frontal/parietal cortices, insula, and posterior cingulate) but accelerated development in the primary visual cortex across most metrics (Fig. 2c). Compared with the Western-centric models, feature-specific exceptions emerged: (1) in the inferior frontal gyrus, the Chinese population presented earlier peaks in the cortical volume and thickness but prolonged surface area maturation, and (2) in the anterior cingulate cortex, earlier peaks in the surface area and gyrification but prolonged thickness maturation were observed in the Chinese population.

Enhanced phenotypic predictive accuracy using Chinese-specific brain growth models

Overall, the Chinese model demonstrated significantly higher predictive accuracy than the Western-centric model (Cohen’s d = 0.66, PFDR < 10-24). Notably, the Chinese model exhibited at least a 10% improvement in the predictive accuracy over the Western-centric model for 41 phenotypes and at least a 5% improvement for 94 phenotypes (Fig. 2d). The largest improvements (> 20%) occurred in cortical thickness measures of the bilateral paracentral, postcentral, precentral, precuneus, and superior parietal regions.

Reduced misestimation of deviation scores using Chinese-specific brain growth models

In AD patients, the Chinese models identified significantly more extreme deviations than the Western-centric models (Cohen’s d = 0.58, PFDR < 10-26; Fig. 2e). To further characterize systematic biases in the Western-centric models, we examined whether specific phenotypes exhibited significant under- or overestimation of extreme deviations. We identified 82 phenotypes that were significantly underestimated and 28 phenotypes that were significantly overestimated in the Western-centric model, with underestimation most prominently affecting measures of the cortical thickness and cortical GMV (Fig. 2e). Split-half replication confirmed the robustness of these results for AD patients. 

Conclusion

Our models reveal precisely timed neurodevelopmental trajectories for 296 structural phenotypes, revealing previously unrecognized population-specific patterns. Through extensive sensitivity analyses, including bootstrapping, age-stratified balanced resampling, leave-one-site-out analysis, split-half replication, imaging quality-controlled modelling, and ethnicity-controlled modeling, we confirmed the robustness and reproducibility of our population-specific lifespan models. Crucially, these Chinese-specific models outperform Western-centric models in terms of both the phenotypic predictive accuracy and pathological detection sensitivity for Chinese populations. By providing validated ethnic-specific references, we address a critical gap in global neuroscience while establishing a framework for developing representative brain atlases. This work not only advances equitable precision medicine but also calls for a paradigm shift towards population-inclusive neuroimaging standards worldwide.

Comparative neurodevelopmental analyses of Chinese and Western populations reveal fundamentally conserved trajectories but distinct spatiotemporal maturation timelines. Systematic differences included (i) delayed cerebral and subcortical maturation and accelerated cerebellar development in Chinese individuals and (ii) divergent regional cortical maturation featuring later development of higher-order association cortices but earlier development of the primary visual cortex. These findings substantially extend prior reports of neuroanatomical differences between Chinese and Caucasian populations through comprehensive phenotyping and milestone comparisons. These population variations likely arise from complex gene–environment–cultural interactions. Genome-wide meta-analysis demonstrated gene-specific regulation of nonlinear brain volume trajectories, whereas population genetics studies revealed significant variations in single nucleotide polymorphism allele frequencies, linkage disequilibrium patterns, and polygenic risk architectures 5. These findings underscore the necessity of population-informed frameworks in neuroscience and the development of precision medicine approaches tailored to diverse global populations.

Ethnic bias in normative predictive algorithms is a well-recognized issue in several research domains. Most normative algorithms have historically been derived from Caucasian participants residing in Western cultural contexts, which limits their generalizability to individuals from other ethnic and sociocultural backgrounds. A straightforward and effective solution to mitigate such bias is to include sufficiently large and representative datasets from diverse populations. In this study, we demonstrate similar ethnic bias in applications of normative neuroimaging models. Specifically, the Western-centric lifespan model yielded a significantly lower predictive accuracy than the Chinese-specific model when applied to healthy Chinese individuals. Moreover, a comparison of deviation scores revealed that the Western-centric model misestimated the extent of structural brain deviations in Chinese patients. Notably, creating distinct brain charts for every global population is impractical; however, relying on Western-derived models as universal proxies is equally untenable. Although these models can provide approximate estimates in the absence of population-specific norms, they are fundamentally limited by introducing systematic errors that compromise clinical precision. This critical gap underscores the urgent need for more inclusive and representative modeling frameworks. Together, our findings highlight a critical and underrecognized source of bias in neurodevelopmental modelling and call attention to the need for population-representative brain charts to ensure valid and precise clinical evaluations in diverse groups.
