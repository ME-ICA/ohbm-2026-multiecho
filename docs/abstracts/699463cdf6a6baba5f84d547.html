Introduction

The brain’s ability to capture and process audiovisual stimuli depends on its capacity to track the dynamics or temporal structure of sensory inputs.1 Ongoing, naturalistic audiovisual stimuli often exhibit fractal dynamics, in which the input’s spectral power-law exponent (PLE) varies over time and gives rise to a non-stationary temporal structure, a phenomenon known as multifractal dynamics.2,3 This is opposed to monofractal dynamics, where the PLE remains relatively stable over time. Do the brain’s multifractal dynamics continuously track those of naturalistic audiovisual inputs? To address this question using the 7-Tesla Human Connectome Project movie-watching dataset, we introduce a novel sliding window analysis pipeline that computes separate PLE time-series for the movie’s auditory and visual inputs, as well as for brain activity, in order to quantify multifractal dynamics in each. This enabled us to directly examine the link between the fractal dynamics of naturalistic inputs and concurrent brain activity in early auditory (A1 and adjacent regions) and visual (V1–V4) regions, using brain–input correlation and input-to-brain Granger causality. We then used a second independent movie-fMRI dataset to replicate and extend our analysis beyond purely sensory-related brain–input tracking to investigate to what degree the brain’s multifractal dynamics are correlated with the subjects’ foreground–background perception of the movie, as measured by their repeatedly sampled perceptual rating scores over the course of the movie.

Methods

Dataset
We analyzed the 7-Tesla HCP movie-watching runs (MOVIE2_HO1, n = 182 participants; MOVIE1_CC1 replication, n = 179) and a resting-state run (REST1). We used HCP-preprocessed ICA-FIX–denoised data. Atlas: HCP MMP 1.0 atlas, focusing on early auditory (A1 and adjacent regions) and early visual (V1–V4) areas, as well as higher-order auditory association regions and dorsal and ventral visual stream regions.

Multifractal PLE analysis of brain activity
For each region, we computed time-resolved power-law exponents (PLEs) using a 60-s sliding window with 1-s step in the 0.05–0.5 Hz frequency range. The lower frequency limit of 0.05 Hz was chosen to include at least three cycles of the lowest frequency (0.016 Hz) within a 60-second window, while the upper limit corresponded to the Nyquist frequency of the dataset (TR = 1 s). PLEs were derived from log-log linear regression of power spectra obtained via the fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

Multifractal PLE analysis of the movie’s inputs
We developed a processing pipeline implemented in Python to compute multifractal dynamics from both the movie’s auditory and visual input streams, enabling direct comparison with the brain’s PLE time-series in auditory and visual regions, respectively.

    Auditory input: Stereo audio was converted to mono to align with bilateral cortical ROI averaging. A third-order Butterworth forward–backward band-pass filter (20–20,000 Hz) was applied to match the human hearing range, followed by downsampling from 44,100 Hz to 5,000 Hz to better align with the slow fMRI sampling rate and the sluggish BOLD signal dynamics. Next, the upper envelope of the auditory time-series was extracted. A 60-s sliding window with 1-s steps was then applied, and for each window, FFT-based log–log linear regression of the power spectrum was used to derive an auditory input PLE time-series.

    Visual input: Movie frames (24 frames per second) were extracted and converted to relative luminance according to the sRGB/ITU-R BT.709 standard. Briefly, we constructed a 3D matrix in which the first two axes (x and y) corresponded to the width and height of each frame, and the third (z) axis corresponded to successive frames over time, allowing us to derive a time-series for each pixel. Each pixel’s time-series then underwent a 60-s sliding window (1-s step), upper-envelope extraction, FFT, and log–log regression to compute a PLE time-series. Finally, pixel-wise PLE time-series were averaged across all pixels to yield a single frame-wise visual PLE time-series. Figure 1 illustrates the processing pipeline and brain regions.

Brain–input tracking and information flow: Spearman correlation and Granger causality
To quantify how the brain’s PLE time-series matches the movie’s auditory and visual inputs, we computed Spearman rank correlations between the input and brain PLE time-series. We then assessed input-to-brain information flow via Granger causality (GC) of multifractal dynamics, evaluating whether input PLE dynamics predict brain PLE dynamics beyond what can be accounted for by the brain’s own past PLE values. In particular, for each brain region and its corresponding auditory or visual input, we fitted a vector autoregressive (VAR) model, selecting the optimal lag using the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Prior to fitting, stationarity was achieved via first-order differencing and confirmed using the augmented Dickey-Fuller and Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin tests. Model residuals were checked for serial correlation using the Durbin-Watson statistic. GC statistics were computed via variance comparison between univariate autoregression and VAR models tested for significance using F-tests, with p-values corrected via the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Results

Early auditory regions exhibited very high correlations with auditory input dynamics (rho = 0.76–0.83) and significant input-to-brain GC effects (p < 0.001). Early visual regions also showed high correlations with visual input dynamics (rho = 0.70–0.88) and significant GC in V1–V3 (p < 0.001). Additionally, whole-brain–input correlation maps revealed modality specificity in input tracking: auditory input tracking was confined to early auditory cortex, while visual input tracking was localized in early visual regions, as shown in Figure 2. Moreover, the brain’s input matching was specific to shared multifractal dynamics with the inputs. It was not observable in a comparison of the raw BOLD time-series with the raw auditory and visual input time-series. When the inputs’ multifractal dynamics were recalculated using the time-reversed raw input time-series (followed by the sliding window PLE computation on this reversed time-series), both the strong brain–input correlations and the significant Granger causality disappeared.

    We next assessed “higher-order” sensory association regions. In auditory association areas (superior temporal gyrus), Spearman correlations progressively decreased with increasing anatomical distance from early auditory cortex: A4 (rho = 0.51), STGa (rho = 0.35), STSvp (rho = 0.12), and STSva (rho = 0.05). None of these regions exhibited significant GC effects, indicating that the multifractal dynamics of auditory input no longer predicted neural activity as they did in early auditory areas. In dorsal visual stream regions, correlations with visual input also declined with anatomical distance: V3A (rho = 0.56), V6 (rho = 0.24), and IPS1 (rho = –0.26). GC effects were non-significant and progressively weaker with growing distance. Ventral stream areas showed a similar pattern, with the highest correlation in PIT (rho = 0.63) and lowest in VMV3 (rho = 0.12), accompanied by no significant GC effects.

Replication and perceptual relevance of input tracking in an independent movie-fMRI dataset

    Early auditory regions: In a sample of n = 20 healthy Chines adults, we observed high Spearman correlations between the brain’s and auditory input’s multifractal dynamics, ranging from rho = 0.81 in LBC to rho = 0.68 in RIC. Input-to-brain GC analysis yielded significant effects in regions A1 (p = 0.008), LBC (p = 0.001), and PBC (p = 0.016).

    Early visual regions: Spearman correlations between early visual regions and the visual input’s multifractal dynamics were strong in V1 (rho = 0.75), V2 (0.62), and V3 (0.64), and moderate in V4 (rho = 0.31). Significant input-to-brain GC was observed in V1 (p = 0.002), V2 (p < 0.001), and V3 (p = 0.018).

    Next, we examined whether the visual input’s multifractal dynamics and their tracking in brain region V1 correlated with the participants’ 36 perceptual ratings, collected every 30 seconds, reflecting perceptual and attentional focus on the movie content in the foreground (e.g., people, objects) versus background (e.g., landscapes, cityscapes, indoor scenes). To do this, we downsampled the time-series of both visual input and V1 PLE by dividing each into 36 segments (matching the 36 perceptual ratings) and computing the mean PLE for each segment. We averaged the perceptual ratings across subjects and z-scored all three time-series to enable their visual comparison.

    Visual input and perception scores: The perceptual foreground–background rating scores generally mirrored the visual input’s multifractal dynamics, exhibiting similar peaks (higher input PLE values corresponding to greater foreground perception) and troughs (lower input PLE values corresponding to greater background perception), with a significant Spearman correlation (rho = 0.66, p < 0.001).

    Brain and perception scores: The perceptual ratings corresponded closely with the brain’s multifractal dynamics, showing a significant Spearman correlation (rho = 0.60, p < 0.001).

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that the brain’s multifractal dynamics in early auditory and visual regions continuously track the multifractal dynamics or temporal structure of their respective inputs in a modality-specific manner. This is evidenced by strong correlations between the auditory input and the activity dynamics in early auditory regions and, as well as between the visual input and the dynamics in early visual regions. Conversely, early auditory regions did not match the visual input, and early visual regions did not match the auditory input.

    Importantly, the movie’s multifractal input dynamics also predicted those of the brain. Significant input-to-brain GC showed that the PLE time-series of the auditory and visual inputs reliably forecast subsequent PLE patterns in the brain’s early auditory and visual regions. Together, these findings demonstrate a directional relationship in which the inputs’ PLE values actively drive the temporal structure of the PLE observed in the brain. From another perspective, the brain’s PLE values continuously align with, and closely track, those of the inputs with a short time delay.

    Beyond the brain’s multifractal-based input tracking, we observed that input tracking in the primary visual cortex may partially shape the subjective perception of foreground versus background movie content. Analyses of the replication dataset, which included perceptual foreground–background rating scores, support a connection of the brain’s input tracking with the subjects’ input perception beyond objective sensory features. Hence, by connecting brain–input tracking to subjective perceptual experience, our findings complete the loop from sensory inputs to brain activity to perception, emphasizing the relevance of multifractal-based input tracking for (conscious) perception.

    What mechanism might explain the brain’s input tracking observed here? This question brings us back to the complexity matching phenomenon mentioned in the introduction. Empirical and computational studies suggest that brain activity often operates near a critical point, at the boundary between order (overly regular or oscillatory activity) and disorder (excessive randomness), where the brain activity’s power spectrum exhibits a power-law distribution.2,3 At this critical regime, neural dynamics exhibit optimal processing properties, including heightened sensitivity to sensory inputs and efficient transmission of information from input to brain.2,3 According to complexity matching theory, maximal information transfer occurs when the scaling exponents (PLEs) of two interacting systems, such as sensory inputs and brain dynamics, are similar. This is because the nonlinear interactions in the driven system are most responsive to sensory input dynamics whose statistical structure resembles its own internal fluctuations.2,4 In this view, the brain’s ongoing activity actively reorganizes its temporal structure or dynamics to align or match with that of the inputs.

    Together, our findings provide strong evidence for the hypothesis that shared multifractal dynamics between both brain and naturalistic audiovisual inputs are a mechanism through which the brain processes sensory information, shaping the perception of our audiovisual environment.
