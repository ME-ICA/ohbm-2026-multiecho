Introduction

In structural brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), skull-stripping is a preprocessing step during which the skull is removed. It facilitates brain tissue identification during downstream processes, such as segmentation and quantitative analyses.1

Historically, skull-stripping relied on image processing methods such as edge detection between tissues, intensity thresholding based on histogram analysis, morphological operations or atlas-based methods using a predefined brain template.2 While these approaches are relatively simple to implement, they can be sensitive to image quality (noise, low contrast) or underperform on some pathologies. Recently, several deep learning (DL) approaches have been developed to perform skull-stripping. For instance, the 3D U-Net architecture is very popular in this field.2

The performance and computing resources required greatly vary across existing tools and imaging modalities,2 which makes the selection of the optimal method difficult. This choice is particularly relevant for Clinica (www.clinica.run), an open-source software for neuroimage processing that supports multiple pipelines which would benefit from integrating a skull-stripping step, such as T1-weighted (T1w) or FLAIR MRI preprocessing.3 The chosen method should be both accurate and computationally efficient, without requiring GPU resources, thereby ensuring Clinica remains accessible to all users.

In this work, we benchmarked both traditional and deep learning-based skull-stripping methods using multiple public datasets spanning T1w and FLAIR MRI. We evaluated each tool’s accuracy, processing time, and robustness to common artifacts and pathologies to guide the selection of a reliable approach suitable for integration into the Clinica platform.

Methods

Five software tools were selected for the benchmark based on ease of installation and diversity of techniques, accounting both for traditional image processing and deep learning approaches. Brain Extraction Tool (BET)4 relies on a thresholding technique and mri_gcut5 on graph cuts, while AntspyNet Brain Extraction (ABE)6 and SynthStrip7 are both 3D U Nets and hdbet8 is a 3D CNN (Figure 1).

Skull-stripping performance was evaluated for T1w and FLAIR MRI. The benchmark leveraged data from four publicly available datasets, sampling variability in patient age and pathologies. The NFBS9 dataset includes 125 healthy adults (77 females, 48 males) aged 21–45 years, with T1w images acquired on a 3T Siemens scanner. The UPENN-GBM10 dataset comprises 630 glioblastoma patients (60% male, 40% female; aged 18–88 years), including T1, T2, T1-Gd, T2-FLAIR, DTI, and DSC images, with skull-stripped and registered volumes, predominantly acquired on Siemens 3T scanners. The MSSEG1 (Multiple Sclerosis Lesion Segmentation 1)11 dataset contains 53 multiple sclerosis patients (38 females, 15 males; aged 24–61) collected across three sites using four scanners from Siemens, Philips and GE (three 3T, one 1.5T), including FLAIR, T1 and PD-T2 sequences. Finally, the BITE12 dataset consists of only 14 tumor patients with T1-weighted MRI and the corresponding ultrasound images, acquired before surgery. All of them except BITE provide a skull-stripped image reference that was used as a ground truth in experiments. In both NFBS and UPENN-GBM this reference was obtained by manually refining the result from a skull-stripping software.9,10 In MSSEG1 the reference was defined manually.11

To evaluate the robustness of the skull-stripping methods, artifacts were generated using clinicaDL13 (https://github.com/aramis-lab/clinicadl) which relies on torchIO.14 Noise was generated to match a Gaussian distribution. Small amplitude random movements were produced as either translation or rotation. Contrast artifacts were generated by increasing grey matter (GM) intensity based on white matter (WM)/GM proportion and a random coefficient. Each method was applied to both original and artifact-corrupted images and the resulting skull-stripped outputs were compared against the ground-truth mask using multiple metrics (DICE, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F-beta). As for the BITE dataset, only a visual review was performed as no ground truth is available. These evaluations were performed for both T1w and FLAIR sequences. 

Statistical differences were assessed using an independent two-sample t-test.

Results

As a preliminary selection step, DICE scores and processing times were evaluated on a subset of 100 T1w images from NFBS. mri_gcut and BET ran quickly but yielded inconsistent segmentation results: the average processing time was below 45 seconds per image but the average DICE was 0.82 and 0.74, respectively. On the contrary, hdbet achieved the highest DICE scores (0.98 on average) but required prohibitive CPU processing times (more than two hours per image). SynthStrip and ABE offered favorable accuracy-speed tradeoffs: an average processing time around two minutes per image and 20 seconds respectively and an average DICE of 0.91 for SynthStrip and 0.96 for ABE. Based on this initial analysis, SynthStrip and ABE were selected for more detailed evaluation and the other methods were discarded.

As a second evaluation step, the robustness to noise, movement and contrast artifacts of SynthStrip and ABE was assessed. Figure 2A shows that the sequence-specific ABE model tends to outperform SynthStrip, independently of the type of artifact. For instance, with T1w images, the mean DICE score for ABE was 0.97 ± 0.01, compared to 0.92 ± 0.01 for SynthStrip. This is probably due to ABE models being trained specifically on each sequence. However, a visual review of the BITE dataset, which contains T1w and FLAIR images with tumors, shows that ABE systematically strips part of the tumors or the brain (Figure 2B), regardless of the sequence.

Conclusion

Based on these results, SynthStrip emerges as the recommended default method, offering a favorable balance between accuracy and computational efficiency. However, in scenarios where the imaging modality and underlying pathology are well-characterized, the specialized models available in ABE may provide superior segmentation performance.
