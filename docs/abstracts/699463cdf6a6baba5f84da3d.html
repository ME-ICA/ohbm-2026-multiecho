Introduction

Many studies have established that selective attention systematically alters object representations in IT and ventral temporal cortex—amplifying task-relevant features, suppressing competing information, and biasing multivoxel neural patterns towards attended objects (e.g., Reddy et al., 2009). In parallel, deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) incorporate architectures of the primate ventral visual stream and have become an exciting and powerful framework for modeling its representational geometry (e.g., Yamins et al., 2014). Yet brain–CNN correspondences have been assessed almost exclusively under fixed viewing conditions, leaving open whether—and how—cognitive state modulates the alignment between biological and computational vision. We analyze data from an audio-visual continuous performance task (avCPT) to explicitly test this question.

Methods

We re-analyzed data from the Chicago Attention and Thoughts (CAT) dataset (Ke et al., 2025), collected at the University of Chicago MRI Research Center. Participants (N = 60) completed two scan sessions that included a sustained attention task (avCPT). Our analyses focused on the avCPT (Corriveau et al., 2025), in which participants viewed a stream of images paired with sounds over 500 trials during a 10-minute scan (1.2s per trial). In each trial, the image was presented for 1.2s while the sound was presented for 1s with a 0.2s inter-trial interval. Each participant performed two conditions: a visual CPT (vCPT), where they attended to images and ignored sounds, and an auditory CPT (aCPT), where they attended to sounds and ignored images. We treated vCPT as the “task-relevant” condition and aCPT as the “task-irrelevant” condition for the visual stimuli. Participants were instructed to press a button to the frequent category in the attended modality (indoor/outdoor images, natural/manmade sounds) while withholding their responses to the infrequent category.

We preprocessed the fMRI data using AFNI. After discarding the first three TRs of each run, we despiked, slice-time corrected, and motion-corrected the data. Nuisance covariates, including 24 motion parameters and mean signals from eroded white-matter, ventricle, and whole-brain masks were regressed out. We additionally censored volumes with excessive motion (> .25mm) or >10% outlier voxels. Functional images were then aligned to each participant’s skull-stripped MPRAGE and nonlinearly normalized to MNI space. We extracted IT voxels following masks from the HarvardOxford atlas (ROI ID 14-16), resulting in 2059 voxels per subject. To model IT responses, we processed images through CORnet, a convolutional artificial neural network designed to mimic the primate visual hierarchy (Kubilius et al., 2019), and extracted features from six layers: V1, V2, V4, IT, AvgPool and the final fully-connected layer.

We conducted a representational similarity analysis to quantify alignment between the model and brain representations of the presented images. For each participant and each CORnet layer, we computed between-trial brain similarity by correlating voxel-wise IT patterns and model similarity by correlating the feature vectors from CORnet. We then linked the two similarity matrices with Spearman correlation. This analysis was performed for vCPT and aCPT separately, and the resulting subject-wise distribution of r-values was compared to zero with bootstrapping (10,000 iterations). To test whether brain-CNN alignment is higher in the task-relevant condition (vCPT) than the task-irrelevant condition (aCPT), we compared the r-distributions using a paired t-test.

Results

First, we established that our chosen CNN represents images in a similar manner consistent with human IT when individuals were attending to them (i.e., during vCPT). Neural-feature similarity was significantly above chance in four of the six assessed layers (V1, IT, Avgpool and the fully-connected layer; p < 0.05), validating CORnet as a plausible digital twin of the human visual system. Critically, this correspondence emerged only under attention: when participants were not attending to the images in aCPT, none of the CNN layers showed above-chance alignment, indicating that attention is required for establishing the brain–CNN representational similarity. Furthermore, although the brain-CNN alignment was numerically higher in vCPT than aCPT in all the six layers, such attentional enhancement was statistically significant only in the fully-connected layer, suggesting attention selectively enhances the highest-level representational alignment that is the most abstract and relevant with downstream cognition.

Conclusion

Together, these results suggest that selective attention modulates the brain-CNN alignment in their representations of the visual input, resonating with earlier findings that population codes in the IT cortex are malleable and dynamically reorganized by attentional states to increase their fidelity. In ongoing work, we aim to test whether visual perturbations can noninvasively modulate IT representations in human fMRI along with their representations in CNN models, and whether these shifts influence the functional network dynamics underlying high-level cognition.
