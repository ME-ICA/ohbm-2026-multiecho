Introduction

The ability to flexibly regulate the intensity and duration of emotional experiences is fundamental to adaptive behavior, influencing decision-making as well as mental and physical well-being. Individuals, however, differ widely in their ability to successfully regulate emotions, and such variability is a key factor in vulnerability to stress and psychopathology. Identifying the neural mechanisms underlying emotion regulation—and the sources of individual differences in regulatory success—has therefore become a central goal in affective neuroscience. 

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies and meta-analyses have consistently shown that emotion regulation engages activity across a widely distributed network of cortical and subcortical regions, including prefrontal, parietal, temporal, and limbic regions. These regions are involved in diverse cognitive and affective functions, including emotional reactivity, interoceptive processing, attention, working memory, executive control, and language1. 

Despite these advances, relatively few studies have directly linked neural activation patterns during regulation to individual differences in behavioral regulation success. Moreover, it remains unclear whether neural activity associated with effective, momentary regulation (state-level) aligns with trait-level emotion regulation tendencies assessed via self-report measures. To address these gaps, we tested whether distributed neural activation patterns during reappraisal relate to state and trait measures of emotion regulation in a large-scale fMRI dataset.  First, we integrated data from seven independent fMRI studies (N = 245) using closely matched versions of a cognitive reappraisal task to examine how task-based emotion regulation success relates to whole-brain activation during regulation. Second, we examined wether neural activation during reappraisal was associated with habitual use of reappraisal measured by Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ; n = 205).

Methods

Participants. Data were aggregated from seven independent fMRI studies employing closely matched versions of a cognitive emotion regulation task. The combined sample included 245 healthy participants (153 female, mean age = 25.86 years, SD = 7.50). A subset (n = 205) additionally completed the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ). 

Emotion Regulation Task. Participants completed a well-established emotion regulation task during fMRI scanning (Fig. 1A). Participants were instructed to either passively look at negative stimuli (Look) or regulate their emotions using cognitive reappraisal (i.e., reinterpreting negative stimuli to reduce their emotional impact) (Regulate). Images were presented for 4–8 s and were drawn from standardized affective image databases (e.g., IAPS2, NAPS3). Afterward, participants rated their current emotional state on a rating scale from very negative (-200) to very positive (+200). 

Emotion Regulation Success. State-level regulation success was quantified as the difference between mean emotional state ratings in the Regulate and Look conditions (Regulate minus Look), such that higher positive values indicated greater success in downregulating negative affect. To account for differences in rating scales across studies, success scores were z-scored within each study prior to aggregation and used as the primary behavioral predictor.

Trait Measure. Trait emotion regulation tendencies were assessed using the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ)4, which measures the habitual use of reappraisal and suppression, for a subsample of participants (n = 205). ERQ reappraisal subscale scores were included in the second-level analyses.  

fMRI Acquisition and Preprocessing. Functional MRI data were acquired across seven independent studies using Siemens scanners (3T Trio, 3T Prisma, and 7T systems). Echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequences were used with repetition times ranging from 1400–2000 ms and echo times from 23–30 ms. Spatial resolution varied across studies, with voxel sizes ranging from 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.0 mm (7T) to 3 × 3 × 3 mm (3T). Flip angles ranged from 62° to 90°, and whole-brain coverage was achieved using 37–78 slices, depending on the study. Despite minor study-specific differences, all datasets underwent harmonized preprocessing using SPM12. Preprocessing included slice-timing correction, realignment for motion correction, coregistration to individual T1-weighted anatomical images, spatial normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing.

First-Level Analyses. For each participant, a general linear model was estimated including regressors for picture-viewing periods during Look and Regulate trials, instruction cue periods, and emotional state rating periods that were convolved with the haemodynamic response function. Six motion parameters and run-wise constants were included as nuisance regressors. Individual contrast images for the contrast [Regulate > Look] were computed to index reappraisal–related brain activation. The resulting single-subject contrast images were submitted to the second-level group-analysis

Second-Level Analyses. At the group level, whole-brain analyses were conducted using two separate multiple regression models predicting [Regulate > Look] activation from (1) state-level regulation success (N = 245) and (2) ERQ reappraisal (n = 205). For each model, age and gender were entered as control covariates. T-statistics were thresholded at an initial cluster-defining threshold p < .001, corrected for multiple comparisons with family wise error rate (FWE) at p < .05. 

Results

Task Activation. The [Regulate > Look] contrast elicited widespread activation across frontal (e.g., bilateral superior and inferior frontal gyri, bilateral superior motor areae), temporal (e.g., bilateral middle and superior temporal gyri), and parietal regions (e.g., bilateral angular gyri), alongside concurrent decreases in a network including bilateral supramarginal gyrus, bilateral insulae and occipital regions. This activation pattern replicates well-established meta-analytic findings on the neural basis of emotion regulation. 

Trait Measure. ERQ reappraisal showed no significant association with neural activation during regulation at corrected thresholds.

Emotion Regulation Success. Emotion regulation success was negatively associated with activation in the precuneus (MNI: x = -9, y = -52, z = 65, k = 50, pFEW-corr <.001) and right supramarginal gyrus/temporoparietal junction (TPJ) (MNI: x = 60, y = 31, z = 29, pFEW-corr = .02, k = 19) (Fig. 1B). Thus, higher behavioral success was associated with reduced activation in the precuneus and right TPJ during regulation. No significant positive associations emerged. 

Together, our findings suggest that effective emotion regulation is characterized by attenuated activation in posterior midline and temporoparietal regions, whereas habitual emotion regulation tendencies, as assessed by self-report, showed no association with neural measures during reappraisal.

Conclusion

Across seven harmonized fMRI datasets, we found that neural activation during emotion regulation using reappraisal tracked state-level regulation success but did not reflect individual differences in trait-level regulation tendencies assessed by self-report. Effective regulation was characterized by reduced activation in the precuneus and temporoparietal junction, regions commonly implicated in self-referential and social-cognitive processing5. This pattern suggests that successful downregulation of negative affect may rely on attenuated engagement of internally oriented and perspectival processes during reappraisal. In contrast, individual differences in habitual use of reappraisal, as measured by the ERQ, showed no detectable neural correlates during task-based regulation. Together, these findings reveal a dissociation between neural processes supporting momentary regulatory success and trait-level emotion regulation tendencies, indicating that commonly used self-report measures may not directly map onto the neural mechanisms engaged during active regulation and underscore the importance of task-based behavioral measures for identifying the neural substrates of emotion regulation.
