Introduction

Drug-induced parkinsonism (DIP) and catatonia are disabling motor syndromes that can emerge in patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD) and mood disorders (MOD)1,2. While clinical scales (e.g., Simpson–Angus Scale; Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale) remain standard, they are time-intensive, rater-dependent, and difficult to deploy at scale or in remote settings. In parallel, short standardized motor videos contain rich, quantifiable information about movement phenotypes3. We propose a subtyping framework that links intrinsic brain network organization to movement phenotypes by mapping resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) to a latent representation learned from motor videos4.

Methods

Participants (n≈150; SSD or MOD) completed standardized motor paradigms (e.g., gait, posture, limb coordination) recorded as short video clips (n≈25,000). Videos will be encoded into a low-dimensional latent space using a video transformer, yielding subject-level latent embeddings summarizing motor behavior across tasks. We train the video transformer to classify MOD, SSD, DIP, and Catatonia, from spatial image embeddings, skeleton features, and optical flows (Gunnar Farneback). Separately, rs-fMRI was acquired and transformed into functional connectivity (FC) features (whole-brain FC). After the video transformer is trained and achieves a significantly higher classification accuracy than chance, a partial least squares (PLS) regression is used to learn multivariate brain–behavior relationships by predicting video latent embeddings from rs-fMRI connectivity. The resulting PLS components defined a shared latent axis capturing maximal covariance between intrinsic connectivity and motor-video phenotypes. Patients were subtyped by kmeans clustering their PLS-derived scores (and/or predicted video-latent profiles), producing connectivity-informed motor subtypes without requiring clinician-rated labels.

Results

Preliminary results of our video transformer training show a growing accuracy by giving the model more degrees of freedom / input features. This means when using more camera angles, and tasks together the detection of DIP, Catatonia, SSD, and MOD rises significantly with an accuracy of 93%, 76%, 79%, 66% respectively. The next step is to take these embeddings that capture disease relevant information and perform the subtyping on the shared latent space the PLS produces.  

Conclusion

This proof‑of‑concept demonstrates that video‑based transformer models can automate, efficiently and objectively, the detection of both DIP and catatonia in psychiatric populations. This strategy may enable mechanistically grounded subtyping, facilitate targeted monitoring of motor syndromes such as DIP and catatonia, and improve personalization of treatment planning. Future work will validate subtype stability across sites, integrate clinical and medication variables, and test whether subtype membership predicts symptom trajectories and treatment response.
