Introduction

Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) is used to map the functional organisation of the human brain on a large scale. A critical methodological choice in rs-fMRI analysis is brain parcellation, yet it is often underappreciated in this exact use case. Brain parcellation defines how voxel-level signals are aggregated into regions prior to connectivity estimation. Anatomical and functional atlases differ substantially in their assumptions about brain organisation, yet connectivity results derived from different parcellations are usually interpreted in the same way. Here, I examine how standard parcellation choices influence functional connectivity maps when all other analysis steps are held constant.

Methods

Methods

Data source and participant selection

Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) data were obtained from the OpenNeuro repository (dataset ds000228). Participants watched Disney Pixar’s “Partly Cloudy” while lying in the scanner. There was no task; participants were simply instructed to lie still and watch the movie. One healthy adult participant was selected, and a single resting-state run was analysed to compare parcellation effects without introducing between-subject variability.

Preprocessing

Preprocessed fMRI data provided with the dataset were used to ensure standardised preprocessing and to isolate the effect of parcellation choice. Preprocessing steps included slice timing correction, motion correction, spatial normalisation to MNI space, nuisance regression, and temporal filtering, as described in the original dataset documentation. No additional preprocessing differences were introduced between conditions.

Brain parcellation schemes

Two distinct parcellation schemes were selected:

  1. Automated Anatomical Labelling (AAL) atlas. It consists of 116 anatomically defined cortical and subcortical regions.
  2. Schaefer 200-region atlas (7-network solution), a functionally derived cortical parcellation matched with canonical functional networks on a large scale.

These atlases were chosen to contrast anatomical versus functional approaches to brain parcellation that are usually used in human brain mapping studies.

Time-series extraction

For each parcellation scheme, voxel-wise BOLD time series were averaged within each region of interest (ROI) to obtain regional mean time series. Identical extraction procedures were applied across both atlases.

Functional connectivity

Functional connectivity was computed by calculating pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients between all ROI time series, which resulted in a region-by-region connectivity matrix for each parcellation. Correlation matrices were Fisher z-transformed for visualisation and comparison.

Visualisation and comparison

Connectivity matrices were visualised to examine differences in correlation structure, network organisation, and modular patterns between parcellation schemes. All analysis steps, aside from atlas selection, were held constant. This measurement was taken to ensure that observed differences could be attributed solely to parcellation choice.

Results

Results

Functional connectivity maps derived from the two parcellation schemes showed substantial differences, even though the underlying data and analysis pipelines are identical.

On Connectivity structure

The AAL connectivity matrix exhibited broader, anatomically distributed correlation patterns, with relatively homogeneous connectivity over neighbouring regions. In contrast, the connectivity matrix based on Schaefer-200 exhibited a more differentiated structure. It was with clearer segregation of canonical functional networks and corresponded to the attention and sensory-motor systems.

On Network organization

Differences were observed in the apparent modular organisation of the brain networks. The functional parcellation emphasised within-network coherence and between-network separation. However, the anatomical parcellation produced connectivity patterns that were less in line with large-scale functional network boundaries.

On Correlation distributions

The distribution of correlation coefficients differed over parcellation schemes. The Schaefer matrix shows a wider range of correlation strengths and more pronounced clustering patterns relative to the AAL matrix.

On Mapping Implications

These results say that parcellation choice alone can meaningfully alter the representation of large-scale functional brain organisation derived from rs-fMRI. The same neural data yielded distinct connectivity maps depending on how the brain was divided into regions, which indeed affected both the visual interpretation and the inferred network structure.

Conclusion

This analysis says that the choice of brain parcellation scheme has a huge impact on resting-state functional connectivity maps. Even when the underlying data and analysis pipeline are held constant, it still has a great impact. Anatomical and functional parcellations produced different representations of large-scale brain organisation when I used the same rs-fMRI dataset and identical preprocessing and connectivity estimation procedures.

Specifically, the anatomical AAL atlas gave patterns that elevated spatial proximity and gross anatomical divisions. This resulted in wider and more homogeneous correlation structures. In contrast, the functionally derived Schaefer-200 atlas produced connectivity maps with clearer network segregation. It also had a more distinct modular organisation that aligned with canonical functional systems. These differences were evident at the level of connectivity matrices, at the level of network boundaries, and in the correlation structure as well.

The findings highlight an important methodological consideration for human brain mapping studies: conclusions about functional organisation, network structure, and inter-regional relationships can be strongly influenced by how the brain is divided into regions. Therefore, differences observed across studies or even within the same dataset reflect parcellation decisions rather than the actual neurobiological differences.

By isolating parcellation choice as the sole varying factor, this work provides a clear picture of its influence on functional connectivity mapping. The results I am showing here speak to the need for transparency in reporting parcellation schemes and for cautious interpretation when comparing connectivity findings in different studies that employ different atlases. More broadly, the study braces the importance of methodological awareness in brain mapping research. In particular, as increasingly fine-grained parcellations are adopted in contemporary neuroimaging analyses.
