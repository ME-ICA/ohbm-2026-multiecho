Introduction

Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy (DCM) is a leading cause of spinal cord disorder, yet the mechanisms driving its widespread cerebral consequences remain poorly characterized [1]. While cortical gray matter (GM) thinning in motor and associative areas is well-documented, it remains unclear whether these changes arise solely from retrograde corticospinal degeneration or involve network-level disruption mediated by subcortical motor systems [2]. We hypothesized that spinal pathology in DCM induces two distinct patterns of cerebral reorganization: a direct, structural loss of the motor cortex and an indirect pathway involving degeneration of associative regions via thalamic and basal ganglia relays.

Methods

a 3T scanner and included high-resolution T2*-weighted axial spinal cord MRI and T1-weighted brain MRI. Spinal cord GM cross-sectional area (CSA) was quantified at the C3 level (non-compressed) using deep-learning segmentation (Spinal Cord Toolbox v7.0, sct_deepseg_gm).

Cortical reconstruction was performed using FreeSurfer v8.0.0. Cortical measures (34 parcels per hemisphere) were extracted using the Desikan–Killiany atlas, alongside subcortical volumes of thalamus, caudate, putamen, and pallidum [3]. Pearson correlations were used to assess the association between spinal GM CSA and subcortical volumes. Vertex-wise General Linear Models (GLMs) assessed cortical thinning associated with spinal cord atrophy. We examined two models; Model 1 tested the total effect of spinal cord GM atrophy on cortical thickness (Cortical thickness ~ Spinal GM CSA + Age) and Model 2 tested for Mediation (Cortical thickness ~ Spinal GM CSA + [Subcortical Volume] + Age). 

Clusters remaining significant in model 1 indicate cortical areas associated with spinal cord GM atrophy in DCM. Clusters remaining significant in Model 2 were defined as direct effects of spinal cord GM atrophy. Clusters no longer significant with subcortical covariates were defined as indirect effects. Clusters were defined using a cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.01 and corrected for multiple comparisons using Monte Carlo simulation (cluster-wise p < 0.05.

Morphometric Similarity Networks (MSNs) were constructed using five cortical features (thickness, area, volume, mean curvature, Gaussian curvature) to compute morphometric coupling between subcortical seeds and cortical targets [4]. Partial correlations tested for associations between spinal GM CSA and subcortico-cortical coupling.

Results

Spinal cord GM atrophy in DCM patients significantly correlated with reduced volumes in the subcortical nuclei. Normalized spinal GM CSA significantly correlated with volumes of the Thalamus (r = 0.44, p = 0.002), Caudate (r = 0.36, p = 0.011), Putamen (r = 0.37, p = 0.009), and Pallidum (r = 0.32, p = 0.025). Vertex-wise GLM analyses showed that spinal cord GM atrophy was associated with widespread cortical thinning. Significant clusters were observed in the precentral gyrus (RH Precentral: 5,151.65 mm2; LH Precentral: 3,749.15 mm2) and extended into associative cortices, including the Right Superior Temporal gyrus (2,087.55 mm2), Right Inferior Parietal Lobule (1,262.68 mm2), and Left Cuneus (1,178.70 mm2). 

When thalamic volume was added as a covariate, significant clusters in the superior temporal, parietal, and visual cortices were no longer present. In contrast, clusters in the precentral gyrus and supplementary motor area (SMA) remained significant, although reduced in size. For example, the left precentral cluster remained significant after thalamus adjustment (2,861.71 mm², p < 0.001), as did the right precentral cluster (1,783.45 mm², p = 0.002). Similarly, adjusting for putamen volume abolished effects in temporal and visual areas, but preserved large clusters in the motor cortex (left: 3,567.34 mm²; right: 2,441.88 mm²). These results support a direct degenerative pathway affecting motor regions likely independent of subcortical relay structures. To confirm an indirect pathway, we examined cortical atrophy associated with subcortical volumes, while controlling for spinal GM CSA. The caudate volume showed strong associations with cortical thinning in distributed regions, including Right lingual gyrus/visual cortex (7,975.96 mm², p < 0.001), Right superior temporal gyrus (4,220.69 mm²), Left middle temporal gyrus (3,853.97 mm²), Left inferior parietal lobule (1,918.21 mm²). Consistent with these effects, morphometric similarity network (MSN) analysis revealed reduced spinal GM CSA was associated with decoupling of structural connectivity between the thalamus and inferior parietal cortex (p = 0.032), thalamus and pars opercularis (p = 0.026), putamen and fusiform gyrus (p = 0.036), and caudate and superior temporal cortex (p = 0.037).

Conclusion

In this study, we used two complementary approaches, general linear models and morphometric similarity network analyses to investigate how chronic spinal cord compression in DCM affects brain structure. Our findings from both approaches support a dual-pathway model of supraspinal reorganization in DCM. We demonstrate that motor cortex atrophy occurs through a direct, subcortically independent pathway, likely reflecting retrograde degeneration along disrupted corticospinal tracts. In contrast, atrophy in sensory, visual, and associative cortices is mediated by degeneration of subcortical relay hubs particularly the thalamus and striatum supporting an indirect thalamostriatal mechanism. The persistence of motor cortical thinning despite subcortical volume control, and the disappearance of non-motor cortical associations when subcortical hubs are accounted for, highlights this dissociation. Moreover, decoupling in subcortico-cortical structural networks further reinforces the functional role of these deep gray matter structures in cortical integrity. Together, these two patterns paint a clear picture that the motor system degenerates independently due to spinal injury and the non-motor systems degrade secondarily, driven by breakdown in structural network coupling between subcortical hubs (thalamus, striatum) and the cortex.
