Introduction

Brain aging is a complex and dynamic process influenced by various biological and environmental factors. Assessing the difference between brain-estimated age and actual age could aid in the early detection and diagnosis of age-related brain disorders and help to distinguish between different groups of patients ^1 ^2 ^3. In this study, we investigate the effectiveness of machine learning models in predicting brain age from MRI-derived features in the segmentation process and compare different hearing states groups.  The methodological challenge of this study arises from the high dimensionality of MRI tabular data. Such datasets often include thousands of features and exhibit intercorrelations, far exceeding the number of observations and leading to the well-known curse of dimensionality, which can impair model generalization and stability. To address this issue, dimensionality reduction techniques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) are commonly applied. While PCA helps reduce feature redundancy, it may also exclude potentially relevant biomarkers that do not conform high variance or to normal distribution assumptions. Second, different anatomical atlases provide varying levels of granularity in feature extraction, influencing model performance and interpretability. Lastly, feature importance analysis is crucial to understanding which brain regions contribute the most to age prediction and for improving model transparency in medical applications. Furthermore, we identify brain structures that exhibit the most pronounced differences across hearing groups using the A2009 and ASEG atlases. This analysis is conducted across multiple morphometric parameters, including average cortical thickness, thickness variability, and regional volume.

Methods

To allow a fair assessment of the different models, 15% of the data was randomly reserved as an independent holdout set. The remaining 85% was used in five-fold cross-validation, either split into an 80% training set and a 20% testing set, or for SVM model into an 80% training set, 10% validation set for model calibration, and 10% testing set. PCA was utilized to reduce the feature dimensionality. Since it assumes normally distributed features, the median absolute deviation (MAD) and median were computed to identify and remove outliers. Then a Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test was performed on the filtered attributes. Features that did not pass the normality test (based on p-values) were excluded from further analysis. This criterion was applied to better align with PCA assumptions, although this step may have excluded potentially relevant biomarkers. PCA was also performed on the full dataset without feature exclusion, yielding similar results; applying the criterion thus provides a more stable and robust outcome. Subsequently, the PCA was applied to 875 features, retaining 35 principal components that captured the highest proportion of variance in the data. While additional components can contain relevant biomarkers, including more components adversely affected model training. The models were trained exclusively using pathology-verified images. Additional models were trained without PCA on five different variants of features based on brain atlases: from A2009 (446 features), DKT (199 features), APARC (206 features), WM (426 features), ASEG (279 features). Moreover, interpreting feature importance in model predictions is easier without dimensionality reduction. Furthermore, two variations of the subsets: images verified for pathology subset and the entire dataset, were used to assess the influence of the dataset size and manual verification. This approach could help identify a limited set of features that may be optimal for future training. Brain age prediction was performed using regression models: Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Feedforward Neural Network with 4 layers (FNN (4)) and Feedforward Neural Network with 1 layer (FNN (1)). For RF and SVM, hyperparameters were optimized using RandomizedSearchCV with 5 fold cross validation and 10 randomly sampled hyperparameter combinations. The FNN models were implemented and trained using PyTorch optimizers: a 4-layer FNN with torch.optim.SGD, a 1-layer FNN with torch.optim.Adam. Torch.nn.L1Loss criterion was used as a loss function for neural networks. Optionally, for the SVM model, post-training adjustments were made by modeling the quadratic trend between predictions and actual age on the validation set. This trend was subsequently applied to adjust predictions on the test set.
The Mean Absolute Error (MAE) measure was used to assess model performance. Finally, an age gap correlation matrix was computed. For each individual, the differences between the predicted and biological age was computed, followed by the calculation of Spearman correlation between each of two atlases. Based on previous evaluation and correlation analyses, a combined feature set from the most informative atlases was utilized, resulting in further improvements in prediction MAE.

Subjects were divided into five groups according to the BIAP hearing loss classification system: Normal hearing; Mild hearing loss (difficulty understanding soft speech); Moderate hearing loss (speech understood only when spoken loudly); Severe hearing loss (speech understood only with loud speech at close distance; loud noises are perceived); and Very Severe to Total hearing loss combined.

For each hearing loss group and each brain morphometric feature from A2009 and ASEG atlas, the deviation (gap) from the healthy aging trajectory was calculated. Based on these deviations, maps of differences in morphometric features across varying degrees of hearing impairment were generated.

Results

SVM and FNN with one hidden layer achieved the best performance, leading us to focus on these models for further analysis. Notably, removing the age trend (calculated on the validation set) significantly improves the model's performance. This is particularly evident on the holdout set, which provides a reliable basis for comparison. In general, models utilizing PCA-transformed features outperformed those trained on individual atlases. However, it is important to note that PCA was applied to 875 features to reduce to 35 components, whereas individual atlases, such as ASEG, contain 279 features. This discrepancy suggests that applying PCA to the entire feature set may not yield optimal results. These findings point to the atlases that are likely to be the most valuable for further feature selection, making it easier to narrow down the search space in future work. Regarding input data, the lowest MAE was observed in models trained on the morphometric data from A2009 and ASEG atlases.  The results show a high correlation (close to 1) among the predictions made using data from the A2009, APARC, and DKT atlases. However, a notably lower correlation (approximately 0.5) is observed between predictions made using the WM and ASEG datasets. This finding aligns with expectations, as A2009, APARC, and DKT atlases contain similar brain structures, whereas WM and ASEG represent distinct anatomical features. Despite A2009 and ASEG achieving the best overall results, their predictions exhibit minimal correlation. This allowed to create model trained on these two connected atlases achiving better scores. The cortical regions in which the largest group differences emerged were identified by directly comparing longitudinal aging trajectories (i.e., changes in mean cortical thickness over time across a wide age range) between individuals who are “healthy agers” with age-appropriate normal hearing and individuals of similar aging range but with profound hearing loss. Thus, these areas index where brain aging proceeds differently as a function of hearing status, rather than reflecting a static baseline difference. The pattern of effects encompassing core language-semantic regions (left inferior frontal gyrus, superior temporal sulcus/middle temporal gyrus, angular gyrus), dorsal attention and cognitive control hubs (bilateral intraparietal sulcus), and default-mode regions supporting memory and integrative processing (bilateral precuneus and mid–posterior cingulate) is consistent with an account in which profound hearing loss is linked to steeper age-related thinning in higher-order association cortex. One mechanistic interpretation is that long-term reduction in high-fidelity speech input and linguistic context limits ongoing enrichment of the language semantic system, while communication under degraded auditory conditions increases “listening effort,” thereby placing sustained demands on domain-general attentional control and working-memory resources to maintain comprehension. Importantly, profound hearing loss also commonly co-occurs with reduced social engagement and cognitive stimulation, and with vascular-metabolic risk factors, which can broadly affect cognitive control circuits and the DMN and may contribute to lower cognitive reserve.

Conclusion

The presented results indicate that specific brain regions from FreeSurfer's ASEG and A2009 atlases are significantly predictive of an individual's age, based on either structural volume or cortical thickness. These findings align well with existing neuroimaging literature on brain-age prediction models. Therefore, the observed trajectory differences in hearing disorder groups likely reflect an interplay of stimulus deprivation, compensatory control recruitment during speech processing, and broader health- and lifestyle-related influences on brain aging, rather than a simple “use-dependent” thickening or thinning of individual regions.
