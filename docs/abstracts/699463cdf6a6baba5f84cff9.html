Introduction

The laboratory mouse (Mus musculus) remains the most commonly used mammalian model organism in neuroscience. It offers unparalleled opportunities for investigation of brain organization from microscopic cellular composition to macroscopic network architecture at experimentally tractable scales. Unlike human neuroimaging, rodent neuroscience permits invasive measurements that directly probe genetic, molecular, and circuit-level mechanisms, enabling validation of findings that would otherwise remain correlational. However, the very richness of available rodent data presents a significant challenge: datasets describing the mouse brain are intrinsically multiscale and multimodal, scattered across disparate scientific subfields including imaging, genetics, electrophysiology, and cellular anatomy. This fragmentation creates barriers for rodent and human neuroscience researchers alike, limiting the ability to contextualize new findings across modalities and species.

Here we present neuromaps-mouse (github.com/netneurolab/neuromaps-mouse), an extension of the neuromaps ecosystem designed to address these challenges. Building on neuromaps for human brain annotations, neuromaps-mouse provides a unified framework for comparing and visualizing a large collection of mouse brain data across scales and modalities. The toolbox serves both rodent and human neuroscience communities: for rodent researchers, it offers standardized access to curated reference datasets and rigorous statistical tools; for human researchers, it enables translational comparisons and validation of findings using invasive measurements only available in animal models. By lowering technical barriers and promoting data accessibility, neuromaps-mouse aims to accelerate multiscale, multimodal understanding of brain organization.

Methods

We developed neuromaps-mouse around three core components: a curated data repository, flexible region-matching utilities, and publication-ready visualization tools. The template and atlas conventions are based on the Allen Mouse Brain Common Coordinate Framework (CCFv3), providing a standardized anatomical reference. We aggregated datasets from public databases, published supplementary materials, and direct collaborations with investigators, then semi-manually mapped original region labels to standard CCFv3 regions while correcting for duplicates, ontology updates, and labeling errors. To facilitate comparisons between annotations defined at different parcellation levels, we implemented functions that match region sets by traversing the hierarchical ontology tree, enabling seamless integration of datasets acquired at different spatial resolutions.

To adapt to the diversity of rodent neuroscience data, we extended beyond scalar-valued brain maps to accommodate three data categories: scalar (regional maps), tabular (panels of regional measurements such as gene expression profiles), and matrix (inter-regional connectivity data). For statistical inference, we implemented spatial autocorrelation-preserving null models based on volumetric Moran spectral randomization, ensuring rigorous hypothesis testing that accounts for the spatial structure of brain maps. We built visualization utilities from the ground up with minimal dependencies, supporting orthographic and lightbox section views with flexible customization options. The package is openly available at https://github.com/netneurolab/neuromaps-mouse.

Results

The neuromaps-mouse repository currently includes curated annotations spanning a broad range of scales and modalities available in rodent neuroscience. Molecular and genetic data include transcriptomic profiles from in situ hybridization (ISH), spatial transcriptomics, and multi-omics measurements. Cellular-scale annotations include morphometric features, synaptome characterization, and immunofluorescent marker distributions. At the circuit level, we provide structural connectivity from tract-tracing experiments, as well as upcoming functional connectivity derived from calcium imaging, resting-state fMRI, and perturbation-based approaches including chemogenetic and optogenetic fMRI. Additional curated and prospective modalities include vascular architecture, microstructural measurements, diffusion MRI, neurotransmitter receptor distributions, and disease propagation patterns.

Using this integrated resource, we demonstrate several types of analyses this package enables. First, by comparing independent studies using different experimental approaches, we assess the evolution and reliability of measurement techniques for biological attributes. For example, regional PVALB gene expression shows strong correspondence across in situ hybridization and spatial transcriptomics datasets spanning nearly two decades, and parvalbumin-expressing cell density estimates converge across five independent whole-brain imaging studies using distinct tissue clearing and reconstruction methods. Second, we show how regional properties relate across modalities and scales, by displaying a selection of maps from transcriptomic profiles and cell type distributions, through immunofluorescent marker expression such as c-Fos following pharmacological perturbations, to task-evoked activation patterns derived from standardized behavioral paradigms. These maps can support a range of additional analyses, including region-wise similarity analyses with multimodal connectivity and similarity matrices, as well as cross-species translation by aligning annotations across mouse and human brains.

Conclusion

The neuromaps-mouse toolbox provides unified access to a comprehensive, curated collection of brain annotations across scales and modalities. For human neuroscience researchers, the toolbox offers a valuable resource for validating hypotheses using invasive measurements and for conducting translational analyses across species. The curated collection also provides a systematic survey of available data, revealing both what is well-established and what remains underexplored, and thereby highlighting opportunities for future investigation. We envision neuromaps-mouse as a growing, community-driven resource that will be continuously updated as new datasets become available.
