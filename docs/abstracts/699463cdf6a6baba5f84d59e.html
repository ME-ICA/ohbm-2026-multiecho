Introduction

Anticipatory postural adjustments (APAs) are considered to be a valuable component of postural control while standing, walking, or sitting ^ 1. Anticipatory postural control is the activation of postural adjustments before voluntary movement to minimize expected potential disturbances to balance caused by the movement in a feedforward manner ^ 2. 

Many factors can influence APAs, including the inertia of the moving body segments; initial and final body position, and the velocity of voluntary movements.

Rising from a chair is a common daily activity essential for independent living. Analysis of sit-to-stand in healthy adults found that the task involves a distinct anticipatory postural component to move the centre of mass over the base of support, prior to a centrally programmed sequence of events to raise the body from the chair. 

Sit-to-stand (STS) is widely used in clinical settings to screen older adults, since it is useful to evaluate low strength, poor balance and mobility problems associated with fall risk ^ 3.

The opposite movement, Stand-to-Sit (STTS), has been less investigated. However, biomechanical studies suggest that STTS may be even more discriminative of functional disability than STS (Sit‐to‐stand and stand‐to‐sit kinematics in older adults with and without functional disability) ^ 4. 

In this study, we aimed to investigate the cortical correlates of APAs during the preparation to STS and STTS task using functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) in a group of healthy young subjects. Further, we investigated possible relationships of cortical activity with biomechanical parameters describing task execution.

Methods

Twenty-six (age = 27.31± 5.95 years) were recruited to perform the STS and STTS tasks, using a force platform to measure force, velocity and impulse of the different movement phases ^5. In the STS condition they were asked to remain seated as relaxed as possible. After 15 seconds a recorded voice stated to expect the signal to get up. After other 15 seconds, a noise was emitted and subject as to naturally get up on the force platform. In the STTS condition, participants started by standing, and a recorded voice stated to expect the signal to sit down. 

Cortical activation was assessed through fNIRS during the REST (before the recorded voice) and the PREPARATION (after the recorded voice) phases of the two tasks. 

The 16 sources and 16 detectors array was arranged into 58 standard channels (3 cm) and 8 short-separation channels (8mm), covering frontal, prefrontal, sensorimotor and parietal areas. The fNIRS signal was pre-processed using MATLAB version R2021b (MathWorks, MA, USA) through custom scripts including some of the Homer3 NIRS processing package functions. Channels with low signal-to-noise ratio were discarded (SNR < 8). Intensity data of the remaining channels were converted to optical density changes, then motion artifacts were identified and with a combination of spline and wavelet motion correction techniques.

A bandpass filter (0.01–3 Hz) was applied to remove slow drifts, and high frequencies contribution, then the hemodynamic response function (HRF)was calculated.

by removing noisy channels, converting to optical density changes, correcting motion artifacts by applying spline and wavelet motion correction techniques, band-pass filtering (0.01-3Hz), converting to Oxy-hemoglobin (HbO) concentration changes, and regressing short channel signals. HbO concentration changes of channels belonging to the same hemisphere and Brodmann’s Area (BA) were averaged obtaining 28 regions of interest (ROI).  To identify active ROIs, a paired t-test with respect to zero was performed for all ROIs, task and conditions. 

To obtain reliable results, false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons was performed. 

A linear mixed model was applied to compare the cortical activations related to the tasks, with TASK (2 levels: STS/STTS), CONDITION (2 levels: REST/PREPARATION) and ROI (23 levels: L-BA10, L-BA9, L-BA8, L-BA44, L-BA6, L-BA4, L-BA5, L-BA7, L-BA40, C-BA10, C-BA9, C-BA4, C-BA7, R-BA10, R-BA9, R-BA8, R-BA44, R-BA6, R-BA4, R-BA5, R-BA7, R-BA39 and R-BA40) as fixed factor, and PARTICIPANTS as random factor. 

Results

The result of the statistical analysis revealed a significant effect of TASK (F(1, 2290) = 160.41; p<0.001), showing a general grated activation during STS than STTS, and CONDITION (F(1, 2290) = 26.27; p<0.001), indicating that the cortical activity is significantly lower during the PREPARATION phases that during the REST phases. Also, results showed a significant interaction of TASK* CONDITION  (F(22, 2290) = 119.89; p<0.001), that revealed no significant differences between the REST condition of the two tasks (RESTSTS vs RESTSTTS, p = 0.61), and a significant, a significant difference for the REST and the corresponding PREPARATION (RESTSTS < PREPARATIONSTS, p<0.001; RESTSTTS > PREPARATIONSTTS, p<0.001) and a significant difference between the PREPARATION condition of the two tasks PREPARATIONSTS > PREPARATIONSTTS, p<0.001).

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that cortical activity, as measured by fNIRS, differs between the Sit-to-Stand (STS) and Stand-to-Sit (STTS) tasks, particularly during the preparation phase associated with anticipatory postural adjustments (APAs). 

STS elicited greater widespread cortical activation than STTS, suggesting that preparation for standing up requires stronger engagement of cortical resources with respect to sitting down. 

This may reflect a preparation for a more precise control of descent and energy absorption during the eccentric task of STTS, and greater force production during the concentric task of standing up. These results support the role of APAs in cortical motor planning and suggest that fNIRS can provide valuable insights into the neural mechanisms underlying functional postural transitions, with potential applications in clinical assessment and rehabilitation of tailoring rehabilitative protocols
