Introduction

The corpus callosum (CC) is the largest white matter tract in the brain. It plays a critical role in connecting cognitive processes between the left and right cerebral hemispheres. Aging is a known risk factor for cognitive disruption and these changes are thought to be preceded by microstructural brain changes including in the CC.3 Magnetic resonance (MR) diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)-derived metrics like fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD) and axial diffusivity (AD) are sensitive to these microstructural changes. Prior work has found in adults age-related decreases in FA, and increases in diffusivity markers in the CC, with anterior segments experiencing earlier onset of decline for FA and greater increase in diffusivity. Cross-sectional studies consistently show an anterior-posterior gradient of decreasing FA and increasing diffusivity.1.2 Fewer studies have used longitudinal data to look at within-person microstructural changes in the CC segments over the adult lifespan. A lack of longitudinal study results in the trajectories being poorly defined, affected by cohort bias and individual variability. Sex differences are also understudied and thus findings are inconclusive. This study aims to examine age and sex changes in the microstructure of the CC using longitudinal DTI data to provide a more accurate understanding of the CC trajectory.

Methods

Healthy adults (N=284, mean age ~47 y, range 18–92 y; 59.5% female) enrolled in the Calgary Normative Study were scanned on a 3-T MR scanner (Discovery 750; General Electric).5 Longitudinal diffusion imaging data (b = 1000 s/mm², 30 directions) were obtained at a total of 495 time-points over 10.5 y (time-points 1-4: N1=284, N2=162, N3=42, N4=7). These data were processed (MRtrix3) by applying denoising, Gibbs artifact removal, brain extraction, Eddy current and motion corrections, and diffusion tensor estimation. Quality checks were done at each processing step, and images were transformed to a standard space (MNI152). White matter tract masks were generated (Tractseg) and visually inspected. In ~10% of cases manual correction of the masks was necessary. Mean FA, MD, RD and AD values were extracted for the whole CC and seven segments (rostrum, genu, rostral body, anterior midbody, posterior midbody, isthmus and splenium). A linear model (Metric ~ age2 + age + sex + age´sex + 1|Individual) was fit to the metrics with a = 0.05 used as the level of significance. Holm-Bonferroni correction was used to correct for multiple comparisons across the segments. Trajectory inflection points (maximum FA or minimum diffusivity) were calculated from the fit coefficients βage / (2βage²)).

Results

After Holm-Bonferroni correction, age2 and age were significant (pcorr<0.05). Sex differences were not significant after correction except in the genu where age×sex was significant for MD, RD, and AD (pcorr<0.05) where males had decreased diffusivity in early life and a steeper decline in late life. FA exhibited an inverted U-shaped pattern in all segments (Figure 1), with FA increasing until midlife and then declining thereafter. The earliest maximum FA occurred in the genu (23.4 years), while the latest occurred in the splenium (45.3 years). Apart from the rostrum and isthmus age of maximum FA followed an anterior-to-posterior gradient. MD, RD, and AD followed a U-shaped pattern in all segments (Figure 1), with diffusivity decreasing until midlife and then increasing thereafter. The earliest minimum MD occurred in the posterior midbody (27.3 years), while the latest occurred in the splenium (37.7 years). The earliest minimum RD occurred in the genu (29.3 years), while the latest occurred in the splenium (38.5 years). The earliest minimum AD occurred in the posterior midbody (20.1 years), while the latest occurred in the splenium and isthmus (35.7 years). Across all metrics, inflection points occurred later in the more posterior CC segments, while earlier inflection points showed some variability by segment. However, overall, a general anterior-posterior pattern in inflection point timing was observed. 

Conclusion

Age-related (CC) microstructural changes followed a general anterior-posterior gradient temporal change. Anterior callosal segments exhibited microstructural degradation at a younger age than posterior segments, consistent with their earlier developmental myelination and greater vulnerability to metabolic and vascular stressors across adulthood. This observation supports the proposed “last in, first out” trajectory that is suggested in the literature.4 Sex-specific analyses indicated that aging trajectories were similar across CC segments apart from the genu. Sex effects were minimal and highly localized, consistent with prior work showing that sex differences in CC microstructure are small and thus rarely consistent across studies.1,2 The isolated age×sex interaction in the genu aligns with reports that this CC segment exhibits inconsistent sex-related patterns, with both the direction and magnitude of effects varying across cohorts and imaging metrics. Overall, CC aging followed broadly similar trajectories across males and females, indicating shared underlying aging mechanisms. These longitudinal aging benchmarks provide a more accurate model of CC microstructure and may potentially help distinguish healthy aging from pathological decline in clinical populations.
