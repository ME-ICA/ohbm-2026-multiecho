Introduction

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is prevalent, leading to high morbidity and mortality across the globe1. However, not everyone who uses alcohol develops AUD, as it results from complex interactions of genetic and behavioral factors with neurobiological and psychosocial development. For instance, other psychiatric disorders increase the risk of AUD and its severity, when present2. Recent research efforts prioritize incorporating these factors to a transdiagnostic view of AUD etiology, prevention, diagnosis and intervention. Findings indicate that individuals with AUD tend to exhibit altered behavioral, self-report, and neuroimaging indices of executive functioning, reward valuation, and emotional processing3. Specifically, (1) impulsive decisions about reward and craving measured behaviorally using delay discounting (DD) and the Alcohol Purchase Task (APT) (2) trait impulsivity and psychiatric symptoms measured via self-reports such as the Urgency-Premeditation-Perseverance-Sensation Seeking-Positive Urgency (UPPS-P) scale, Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) scale, Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), and the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for the DSM-5 (PCL-5); and (3) integrity of cognitive and motivational networks measured with neuroimaging such as resting state functional connectivity (RS-FC) of approach, avoid, default mode, executive control, and salience networks. However, the unique predictive utility of these factors in diagnosing AUD remains unknown. A machine learning approach to assess the prediction and accuracy of a binary outcome generated from a forest of decision trees (random forest classification; RFC) has been successfully utilized to determine disease and disorder prevalence in clinical and community populations4. Therefore, the behavioral, self-report, and neuroimaging indices of cognitive, motivational, and psychiatric functions described above were used in an RFC model to determine their relative utility in determining AUD diagnosis.

Methods

Measure averages and standard deviations are presented in Figure 1.

A sample of 116 adult (mean age: 32.1, 44% male, 80% White) weekly alcohol consumers who were classified into an AUD group (n=56; operationalized as >2 symptoms endorsed on the DSM-5 Diagnostic Assessment Research Tool (DART) and mean drinking frequency above NIAAA high risk guidelines on an alcohol Timeline Follow-Back assessment5) and a control group (n=60). The AUD group and control group did not differ on other baseline sample characteristics. Participants completed behavioral, self-report and MRI assessments.

Whole brain fMRI was conducted using a GE Discovery 750 3T scanner. A 9min resting state echo planar fMRI (EPI) run of 270 volumes (TR=2000/TE=30ms, 22.4cm FoV, 64×64 matrix, 40 3.5-mm-thick axial slices, MB=2) was acquired after a T1 scan. MRI processing was performed using an afni_proc.py pipeline. This included slice-time correction, registration to the minimal outlier volume, censoring outlier volumes and those exhibiting >0.02mm inter-volume movement, alignment to the T1 volume in Talairach space, and application of a 5mm full-width half-maximum Gaussian filter. Participants with >25% censored volumes (i.e., <6.75min EPI data) were excluded from further analysis. GLM was used to control known sources of EPI signal variance, including observed movement, yielding residual time series for each participant used in seed region functional connectivity analyses. Seeds were the ventral striatum (approach), amygdala (avoid), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC; default mode), middle frontal gyrus (MFG; executive control), and anterior insula (salience). Mean EPI time series were extracted from each of these seed regions of each participant for five voxelwise cross-correlation analyses. Five resulting maps per person were used in five one-sample t-tests vs zero (i.e., no RS-FC) at the full sample level to empirically identify target regions of interest (ROIs) for each network. The 5 most intense (i.e., strongest connectivity) of the resulting significant (FDR corrected q<.05) clusters were chosen for each network. Mean GLM beta values were then calculated across all target clusters of each network for use as RS-FC measures in the RFC.

Results

Results of the behavioral, self-report, and RS-FC assessments are presented in Figure 1. 

The tested RFC model revealed a 92.8% classification accuracy at determining AUD vs control participants, with high a score of accuracy, sensitivity (i.e., the true positive number of cases detected), specificity (i.e., the true negative number of cases detected), and F1 (i.e., combined value representing precision and recall). Among variables, the APT, psychiatric disorder assessments, and subjective and behavioral measures of impulsivity remained top predictors contributing the most variance to the model, followed by RS-FC networks contributing additional significant variance to the model. Specifically, APT intensity, PCL-5 total, APT elasticity, GAD-7 total, PHQ-9 total, and APT Omax remained the top five predictors and contributed to 56.89% of the model’s predictive accuracy. Together, APT measures contributed to 37.71% of the model’s predictive accuracy, making it the top domain of predictive accuracy in determining AUD. Specifically, individual APT intensity contributed 16.16%, APT elasticity contributed 11.45%, APT observed maximum expenditure (Omax) contributed 6.40%, and APT breakpoint contributed 3.70%. Psychiatric measures contributed to 29.28%, with PTSD (PCL-5) total contributing 11.78%, generalized anxiety (GAD-7) total contributing 9.76%, and depressive symptoms (PHQ-9) total contributing 7.74%. UPPS-P measures contributed to 12.79%, with positive and negative urgency contributing to 9.09%. Together, RS-FC networks contributed to 11.44% of the model’s predictive accuracy, with the default mode and salience networks each contributing to 2.69% of the model’s predictive accuracy, while avoid, approach, and executive control networks each contributed to 2.02% of the model’s predictive accuracy. Delay discounting measures contributed 4.38%. 

Conclusion

The current study established a highly accurate, sensitive, and specific model to differentiate those with AUD from controls. Specifically, differences in specific brain networks coupled with heightened self-report and behavioral measures of psychiatric disorders, reward valuation (delay discounting), and motivation to drink (APT) characterized individuals with AUD. Not only do the current results support previous findings that impulsivity traits and brain network synchronies may be used to detect AUD, but also provide novel evidence that the additional behavioral and self-report measures differentially contribute to predictive model accuracy. Behavioral and self-report measures provided the strongest predictive utility for identifying those with AUD. However, total RS-FC network synchrony contributed less to the model’s accuracy. Even after preprocessing, residual noise could have contributed to a reduced predictive accuracy of RS-FC regions as fMRI signal is known for high variability and low signal to noise ratio. Further, RS-FC does not map directly onto behavior because there is no experimental challenge. Therefore, alterations in neural activity may be a more distal predictor compared to self-report and behavioral measures, leading to a weakened observable link between neural function and AUD diagnosis. It is also noteworthy that self-report and other behavioral measures appear to have better predictive utility in identifying those with AUD because it is diagnosed based on self-report behavioral criteria. Psychiatric and APT measures contributed the most to model predictive accuracy. This aligns with previous literature highlighting the predictive power of PTSD, anxiety, and depression in AUD. APT measures reflect the motivation to drink alcohol, which is directly related to AUD DSM-5-TR criteria, including craving and impaired control, and is largely established by previous literature to be a proxy measure of AUD severity. Interestingly, DD and impulsivity measures had lower predictive accuracy compared to other measures. Given both measures relate to impulsive decision-making, it appears that generalized impulsivity is not as predictively accurate as psychiatric and APT measures. Future directions include incorporating task-based fMRI measures to the model’s predictive accuracy as well as additional neural networks associated with AUD. However, it is important to note that the current results do not negate the importance of neuroimaging methodology in aiding diagnostic prediction. In sum, the current study reveals RS-FC networks do contribute to the predictive accuracy of identifying AUD.
