Introduction

The excitation-inhibition (E/I) imbalance framework proposes that an increased E/I ratio underpins autistic characteristics, with evidence across molecular, cellular, and human studies. Homeostatic regulation of excitation and inhibition is important in encoding and processing internal and external input, and disruption in this balance may lead to changes in information filtering and integration. 

However, the majority of work examining E/I in humans has relied on proximal measures, including magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) or EEG. A recent novel approach allows for directly estimating dynamic excitatory and inhibitory synaptic activity from fMRI data1, providing a different perspective for understanding E/I differences using a feedback inhibition control (FIC) model simulating the synaptic ensemble firing activity. 

To validate the model, we provide a pharmacological challenge as a means to measure E/I responsivity. Here we use the psychotropic compound psilocybin. Psilocybin is particularly relevant to our interest as it acts as an agonist of 5HT2A receptors, which are clustered in autism-related brain regions. Psilocybin is also proven to be able to increase cortical extracellular glutamate and GABA levels in preclinical models2 by acting on 5HT2A receptors found on both excitatory pyramidal and inhibitory interneuron cells. 

Our objectives therefore were to examine differences in fMRI-derived measures of synaptic excitatory and inhibitory (E/I) populations firing activity between autistic and non-autistic adults at baseline and in response to single dose psilocybin. 

Methods

Data were derived from a broader study using COMP360 psilocybin, a proprietary synthetic formulation of crystalline psilocybin owned by Compass Pathways plc, London, United Kingdom. 

 

Participants: Sixty-seven adults (37 autistic, 30 non-autistic; age > 18 years) were recruited. Autistic participants either had a clinical diagnosis of autism or were assessed using the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised or Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule to confirm diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included known genetic causes of autism (e.g., Fragile X syndrome), psychiatric illness, history of seizures and epilepsy, and use of medications affecting serotonin. 

Psilocybin: Psilocybin is a 5-HT2A agonist used as a pharmacological probe. The low doses of 2 mg and 5 mg were chosen based on evidence from other studies showing that low-dose psilocybin alters cognition and that low doses of the serotonergic psychedelic lysergic acid diethylamide affect resting-state fMRI measures3,4. All participants completed up to three visits, each involving a single oral dose of synthetic placebo, 2 mg psilocybin, and 5 mg psilocybin. Visit order was pseudorandomized with the constraint that participants always received 2 mg psilocybin before 5 mg to minimize unwanted side effects or adverse experiences.

fMRI: Participants underwent resting-state fMRI scans at each visit on a 3T GE scanner. An echo-planar imaging sequence was used with the following parameters: TR = 0.933 s, 524 total volumes (514 used in final analyses); TE = 32 ms; 52 interleaved slices; 2.70 mm slice thickness; 2.70 mm³ isotropic voxel size; FOV = 22.1 cm; FA = 60o and multiband 4. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes fixed on a cross. fMRI preprocessing was performed using fMRIPrep 22.0.2. Motion-related noise was removed by FSL_regflit, additional steps including high-pass filtering, white matter and CSF regression were performed. 

Model: A parameter feedback inhibition control (pFIC) model1 simulating ensemble synaptic firing activity was applied to derive the fraction of open channels for excitatory and inhibitory populations as E/I markers across 68 cortical regions of interest (ROIs) from the Desikan-Killiany (DK) atlas. The model was fit on grouped data for each diagnostic group and dose combination (‘group fitting’), yielding six group-level conditions (2 groups × 3 doses) and, subsequently, to each participant's fMRI data separately (‘individual fitting’). 

Analysis: To assess group-averaged differences, and shifts in E/I across conditions, we used permutation-based statistical analyses on pFIC output with False Discovery Rate correction for multiple comparisons across ROIs, we generate the null distribution for permutation by shuffling the labels of different visits of same participant to assign the data to different dose groups as input for pFIC model, the actual non-shuffled group observations were used to compare with the null distribution for the significance of the permutation test. To assess individual differences in E/I markers distribution across drug conditions, we applied Wilcoxon rank-sum tests due to skewness of the data.  

Results

Group differences: No significant group differences in E/I markers across ROIs were observed in the placebo or 2 mg conditions. Under 5 mg psilocybin, the distribution of E/I ratios across the brain was significantly lower in autistic compared to non-autistic groups (all:  Wrange = 397-424, p < 0.05) (See Results figure A). This appears driven by increased inhibition markers in autism (all:  Wrange = 155-184, p < 0.05) (See Results figure A), as no differences in excitation markers were observed.

Responsivity: No significant between doses differences in E/I markers across ROIs were observed in the autism or neurotypical group (See Results figure A). We then examined shifts in E/I markers between placebo and psilocybin using permutation-based analysis. Only autistic individuals showed a significant decrease in E/I ratios under both drug conditions, not observed in the neurotypical group. In the autism group, the directionality of shifts was consistent between 2 mg and 5 mg with more ROIs changing in the 5 mg conditions (21 ROIs) than the 2 mg condition (7 ROIs) with all ROIs showing shifts at 2 mg also showing shifts at 5 mg) (See Results figure B). Separate analysis of E and I markers showed a decrease in excitation (2 mg: 39 ROIs; 5 mg: 63 ROIs) across large numbers of regions and an increase in inhibition across fewer regions (2 mg: 6 ROIs; 5 mg: 3 ROIs) suggesting that the shift in E/I was predominantly driven by reduced inhibition. Although no significant shifts of E/I ratio were found in the nonautistic group with either 2 mg or 5 mg, inhibition increased between placebo and 5 mg across most of the brain (46 ROIs). 

Conclusion

We observed significant differences in global E/I markers between autistic and non-autistic participants under 5 mg psilocybin, with a lower E/I ratio in autism, seemingly driven by more inhibition at 5 mg. Autistic people showed significant decreases in E/I markers ratio between placebo and drug conditions which were predominantly driven by suppression of excitatory markers across the brain. Consistency across brain regions supports the increasing extent of psilocybin on E/I with increasing drug dose. Neurotypical people showed a moderate increase in inhibition instead, although this did not result in overall changes in E/I balance. The difference across doses between autistic and neurotypical participants was driven by differential responsiveness to the drug rather than baseline E/I differences, as no group differences were observed under placebo.

Our finding aligns with prior work by us and others showing that the autistic brain responds differently to pharmacological interventions. Given that these were low doses of psilocybin, our results suggest the autistic brain may be more sensitive to neurochemical perturbation. fMRI-derived dynamic E/I markers highlighting the importance of examining how excitation and inhibition change in response to perturbation rather than focusing solely on static ratio measurements.
