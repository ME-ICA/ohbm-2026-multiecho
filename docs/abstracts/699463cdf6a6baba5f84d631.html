Introduction

Puberty is a major neurobiological transition initiated by neuroendocrine processes that drive physical changes. Variation in the onset and progression of puberty exert unique influences on brain development, behaviour, and psychological well-being, beyond chronological age 1,2. Although most research has focused on pubertal timing, an equally important but understudied dimension is pubertal tempo, defined as how fast individuals progress through puberty. Faster tempo has been linked to mood difficulties, externalizing behaviours, and heightened developmental risk, yet its implications for brain maturation remain poorly understood 1.

Despite emerging neuroimaging research linking rapid tempo with accelerated cortical thinning and earlier maturation of selected subcortical regions 2,3, little is known about how tempo shapes the development of resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC). This represents a critical gap, given that rs-FC supports domains of adolescent functioning often sensitive to atypical pubertal tempo, including cognition, emotion regulation and social behaviour. Moreover, most prior work assumes a single, uniform pubertal tempo across adolescence, an assumption that oversimplifies the dynamic biology of pubertal maturation.

In the current study, we addressed these limitations by applying a novel method for estimating varying tempo across pubertal maturation, using both hormonal and physical indicators. We investigated how pubertal tempo relates to the development of rs-FC within large-scale cortical networks across adolescence.

Methods

Participants were drawn from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study, the largest ongoing longitudinal project which follows a diverse cohort of nearly 12,000 children (47.84% females), aged 9-10 years at baseline4, recruited from 21 research sites across the United States. This study includes annual assessments of mental and physical health, biospecimen data, along with bi-annual MRI scans. Data from the baseline to the 6th year follow-up waves (Release 6.0) were used in the current study.

After quality control, data cleaning, and harmonization procedures, the final analytic sample comprised 3,586 participants (1660 females). Exclusion criteria include: (1) missing pubertal measures, rs-fMRI data, or demographic information; (2) MRI or hormone data failed quality control; (3) no consecutive waves of valid data (because pubertal tempo was modelled longitudinally as change across intervals). To ensure compatibility with longitudinal harmonization procedures, analyses were restricted to waves with available MRI data (baseline, 2nd, 4th, and 6th year follow-ups).

Pubertal tempo was defined as the change in biological development divided by the change in chronological age. Using the Puberty Age Gap framework5, we generated longitudinal estimates of puberty age at each wave based on hormonal concentrations (i.e., DHEA, estradiol, testosterone) and physical indicators (i.e., Pubertal Development Scale [PDS]). For each available interval (baseline to Year 2, Year 2 to Year 4, and Year 4 to Year 6), we calculated the changes in each PDS items and hormone levels and used these changes in puberty indicators to model the corresponding change in chronological age. The resulting predicted change in puberty age provided a biologically grounded index of maturation change for that period. Pubertal tempo for each interval was then operationalized as the ratio of change in puberty age to change in chronological age, producing interval-specific estimates of maturation rate rather than a single static tempo across adolescence (Figure 1).

For rs-fMRI, pre-processed connectivity matrices from the ABCD repository were used. Seed-based Pearson correlations were computed between ROIs defined using the Gordon cortical parcellation, spanning 12 intrinsic cortical networks. Correlation values were Fisher-z transformed. Network-level rs-FC was summarized as mean within-network (n=12) and between-network (n=66) connectivity, yielding 78 features. ROIs labelled “none” were excluded, and only scans passing ABCD’s quality control were included. Longitudinal batch effects related to scanner were addressed using LongComBat harmonization method.

Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) were fit separately for males and females to examine whether pubertal tempo predicted rs-FC development across adolescence. For each rs-FC, the model included linear effect of tempo from the interval immediately preceding the imaging wave (e.g., rs-FC at Year 4 was predicted from tempo from Year 2 to Year 4 only). A smooth term of age was included as a covariate, with participants and site specified as random intercepts. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was used to control for multiple comparisons.

Results

Pubertal tempo showed sex-specific associations with rs-FC, after accounting for nonlinear age effects. In females, three cortical-cortical connections survived FDR correction. Faster tempo between baseline to 2nd year follow-up was associated with decreased connectivity between the Auditory–Salience network (t = -3.63, pFDR = 0.023), and the Salience–Sensorimotor-Hand network (t = -3.29, pFDR = 0.040). In contrast, the tempo between 2nd year to 4th year follow-ups was positively associated with connectivity between Cingulo-Opercular and Cingulo-Parietal networks (t = 3.58, pFDR = 0.028; Figure 2). In males, no cortical-cortical connectivity associations with tempo survived FDR correction, although uncorrected effects were observed across 19 connections.

Conclusion

Findings indicate that pubertal tempo exerts sex-specific and interval-dependent influences on the development of large-scale functional brain networks, above and beyond chronological age. In females, faster tempo during early adolescence (baseline to 2nd year follow-up) was associated with reduced rs-FC, mostly involving primary sensorimotor networks. This pattern shifted across adolescence where during mid-adolescence (2nd to 4th year follow-up), faster tempo was linked to increased rs-FC, particularly between higher-order control networks. In contrast, no cortical-cortical associations survived FDR correction in males, which may partly reflect their later pubertal onset. Together, these results suggest that the pace of pubertal maturation has a stronger and more temporally specific impact on functional network organization in females than in males, and highlight pubertal tempo as a potentially important dimension of pubertal maturation for understanding individual differences in adolescent brain development.
