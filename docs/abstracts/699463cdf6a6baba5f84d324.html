Introduction

Resting-state functional connectivity (FC) is used to relate spontaneous brain activity to inter-individual differences in behavioral and neurological traits. FC is typically estimated to use static Pearson correlation capturing bivariate synchrony. Recently, measures incorporating temporal dynamics, such as first-order multivariate autoregressive (AR1) models^1 have gained attention. Using a variance component model (VCM), a previous study reported that AR1-based FC explains individual differences in behavioral traits better than Pearson correlation–based FC.^1

However, it remains unclear whether this advantage reflects incorporation of temporal dynamics. In addition to temporal dynamics, Matkovič et al.^2 noted that AR1 provides multivariate measures isolating direct effects, whereas Pearson correlation is a bivariate measure that does not exclude indirect effects. In fact, they showed that partial correlation, a static and multivariate FC, and AR1 have comparable explanatory power, suggesting that multivariate modeling, rather than temporal structure, may drive the improvement.

Furthermore, to clarify the utility of FC measures incorporating temporal dynamics or multivariate direct connectivity, it is essential to evaluate predictive power. VCM used in the previous comparison of static vs. dynamic FCs focused on in-sample model fit without separating training and test data, thus only evaluating explanatory power. It remains unclear whether the advantages in VCM are also applicable to prediction, i.e., generalization to unseen individuals.

To address these points, in this study, we focused on predictive performance and conducted a 2×2 factorial comparison of four FC measures: static bivariate Pearson correlation, static multivariate partial correlation, bivariate AR1, and multivariate AR1. By assessing performance on unseen individuals, we tested whether AR1-based and multivariate FC measures offer more information for predicting traits than Pearson correlation.

Methods

We analyzed resting-state fMRI data and 58 behavioral measures from 896 healthy adults drawn from 1,003 participants in the Human Connectome Project (HCP). Participants were included if they had complete behavioral data and at least one resting-state run with minimal head motion. We used the preprocessed fMRI dataset released by Matkovič et al.^2. The cortex was parcellated into 360 regions of interest (ROIs) using the Glasser atlas. To mitigate motion-related artifacts, frames with framewise displacement (FD) > 0.3 mm were censored and motion parameters were regressed out. To preserve fast temporal dynamics, no temporal filtering was applied beyond the standard high-pass filter (cutoff = 2,000 s) used in the HCP pipeline (Fig.1A).

According to a previous study^2, we constructed four FC measures in a 2×2 factorial design (bivariate vs. multivariate; static vs. dynamic): static bivariate FC (Pearson correlation), static multivariate FC (partial correlation), bivariate AR1, and multivariate AR1 (Fig.1B). Static bivariate FC was defined as the pairwise Pearson correlation between ROI time series. Static multivariate FC was the corresponding partial correlation matrix, obtained from the inverse of the sample covariance matrix (i.e. precision matrix). Bivariate AR1 FC was defined as the lag-1 cross-covariance between ROI time series. Multivariate AR1 FC was obtained by fitting a first-order multivariate autoregressive model to ROI activity over time and using the estimated coefficient matrix as the FC representation.^3

For each FC measure, we computed an inter-subject similarity matrix by correlating the vectorized FC matrices between all pairs of subjects and used this matrix as a kernel (Fig.1C). To quantify explanatory power and replicate previous findings on model fit, we applied a variance component model (VCM; Fig.1D).^1,2 Nuisance covariates (age, sex, and FD) were regressed out from both the FC-based subject-similarity kernels and the behavioral similarity kernels before fitting the VCM. Explained variance was defined as the proportion of covariate-adjusted trait variance captured by each FC kernel relative to the total covariate-adjusted trait variance (sum of all kernel-specific and residual variance components).

We evaluated predictive performance on unseen individuals using kernel ridge regression (KRR) with 10-fold nested cross-validation (Fig.1D).^4 The value of the regularization parameter λ was selected using inner-loop cross-validation over a grid of values between 0 and 20.^4 To respect the family structure in the HCP data and avoid information leakage, subjects from the same family were always assigned to the same fold in both the inner and outer loops. Predictive accuracy was assessed as the Pearson correlation between predicted and observed trait values. To statistically compare predictive performance across the four FC measures, we performed a repeated-measures ANOVA, with all post hoc tests corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. 

To investigate how the strength of regularization affected predictive performance, we examined validation-set prediction accuracy across the full range of λ values explored in the grid search. Furthermore, to diagnose potential overfitting, we plotted predicted versus observed values separately for the training and validation data under the selected λ and visually inspected discrepancies between model fit on the training data and generalization to the validation data.

Results

Standard Pearson FC outperformed other FC measures incorporating temporal dynamics and multivariate direct connectivity in prediction

We first replicated previous VCM results showing that multivariate FC explained a larger proportion of variance in behavioral measures than did bivariate FC (Fig. 2A). Differences between static and dynamic measures were negligible, suggesting no advantage of temporal dynamics in terms of explanatory power.^2

We next proceeded to assess predictive performance of the four FCs by applying KRR to the same data used for the VCM analysis. KRR showed a result whose pattern is the opposite to that of the VCM analysis: bivariate FC significantly outperformed multivariate FC (Fig. 2C). Specifically, static bivariate FC (Pearson) yielded higher predictive accuracy than static multivariate FC (partial correlation; mean difference = 0.0223, p = 0.007), and bivariate dynamic FC (bivariate AR1) outperformed multivariate dynamic FC (AR1) (mean difference = 0.0554, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, no significant difference was observed between static and dynamic bivariate FC (mean difference = 0.0002, p = 1.0), whereas static multivariate FC significantly outperformed dynamic multivariate FC (mean difference = 0.0328, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2C). In both cases, dynamic FC did not yield better predictive performance than static FC. 

To examine whether these effects were driven by a small subset of traits, we selected ten behavioral measures with the highest predictive accuracy under static bivariate FC. We then visualized their VCM- and KRR-based results as representative examples (Fig. 2B,D). For most of these traits, multivariate FC again showed higher explained variance in the VCM, whereas bivariate FC achieved higher prediction accuracy in KRR, mirroring the pattern observed in the trait-wise averages. Taken together, these findings indicate that, given the current implementations of FC measures, explicitly modeling temporal dynamics or multivariate direct connectivity does not confer a predictive advantage over standard Pearson FC.

Impaired generalization in multivariate FC was due to overfitting

To investigate the reason for the low performance of multivariate FC in KRR, we visualized the relationship between the regularization parameter (λ) of KRR and predictive accuracy on the validation data (Fig. 2E). For bivariate FC, the accuracy profile showed a clear peak, allowing the identification of a λ that maximized predictive performance. In contrast, for multivariate FC, the profile was relatively flat with minimal variation across λ values, rendering the selection of an optimal regularization strength difficult. Consistent with this, when we plotted predicted versus observed values for the training and validation data under the selected λ, we observed clear signs of overfitting for multivariate FC (Fig. 2F,G). The model achieved near perfect accuracy on the training data but failed to generalize to the validation data. These results indicate that, under the present sample size and estimation scheme, the increased flexibility of multivariate FC mainly manifests as overfitting rather than as improved out-of-sample prediction, limiting its practical utility for trait prediction.

Conclusion

Within the broader literature on associations between resting-state FC and individual traits, an important unresolved question is whether the high explanatory power of models incorporating brain dynamics or multivariate direct connectivity translates into improved generalization to unseen individuals. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether brain dynamics carry more trait-related information than static FC alone. To address these questions, we performed a comprehensive evaluation of explanatory and predictive performance using a 2×2 factorial design. In line with previous study questioning the added value of dynamic FC, our results of both VCM and KRR indicate that incorporating temporal dynamics via AR1-based models does not confer a significant advantage over static FC. Furthermore, our KRR results suggest that multivariate models are vulnerable to overfitting and are thus limited in their ability to predict. This stands in contrast to previous VCM study reporting the superiority of multivariate FC measures. The divergent results between the VCM and KRR analyses indicate that the explanatory and predictive powers of FC measures could significantly diverge. The divergent result was partly explained by overfitting.

In conclusion, researchers should explicitly distinguish whether their primary goal is to explain covariance between brain and behavioral data or to achieve prediction of behavioral data using brain data. Depending on the objective, researchers can select FC measures and modeling strategies that are aligned with that specific objective.
