Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an immune-mediated disease, characterized by neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. Symptoms include motor impairment, gait disturbances, depression and cognitive impairment. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on the brain and spinal cord is routinely used to diagnose and monitor the disease.

In later disease stages, lesion burden and brain atrophy are known to be predictive of disability progression and cognitive decline.1,2 In addition, studies on body fluid biomarkers have highlighted the predictive value of serum neurofilament light chain (Nfl) and glial fibrillar acidic protein (GFAP) levels for these clinical outcomes.3 However, the specific relevance of these biomarkers of disease progression in early MS remains unclear due to a crucial lack of longitudinal data in early disease stages. Uncovering this relevance is important, because in early disease stages specifically, decisions on starting and/or escalating treatment can have lasting consequences for long-term clinical outcomes. Therefore, novel biomarkers are needed.

We hypothesize that in early MS, lesion accumulation will lead to increased serum biomarker levels and later to atrophy of crucial network structures,4 especially the thalamus, coinciding with clinical progression. To address this, we investigated the evolution of these biomarkers in a cohort of newly diagnosed people with MS (pwMS) in the first year after diagnosis, compared to healthy controls (HC). We specifically investigated how longitudinal biomarker trajectories relate to clinical and cognitive outcomes. Additionally, we investigated how MRI and serum biomarker outcomes are interrelated as well as explorations of disease modifying treatment (DMT) effects.

Methods

Cohort

This study is a subset of the Temprano cohort—an ongoing, longitudinal observational study including pwMS diagnosed 5–13 months earlier, and HC. Assessments occur annually and include recording current DMT use, neurological (pwMS only) and neuropsychological testing, blood collection, and MRI (figure 1). Additional blood samples are collected every three months. For the current study, out of the full Temprano cohort, only those participants with a complete first (baseline) and second timepoint (after 12 months) at the point of analysis were selected (N=67 MS and N=34 HC).

Clinical outcomes

Physical disability was assessed, in pwMS only, using the telephone Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS), the Timed 25-Foot Walk Test (T25FWT), and the 9-Hole Peg Test (9HPT). For both T25FWT and 9HPT, the averaged time of all attempts was used.

Cognition

Cognitive functioning was assessed using an adjusted version of the Minimal Assessment of Cognitive Function in Multiple Sclerosis (MACFIMS) using available alternate versions. Raw test scores were converted into demographically-adjusted z-scores using local normative datasets from Amsterdam UMC. For this initial dataset exploration, the full test battery was reduced to the three domains most affected in MS: information processing speed (IPS; letter-digit substitution test (oral version); LDST, and the 3-second interval-paced auditory serial addition test; PASAT), visuospatial memory (brief visuospatial memory test; BVMT-R) and verbal memory (California verbal learning test; CVLT-II). Domain specific scores were computed by averaging the relevant z-scores.

Serum biomarkers

After collection, the samples were transported to the Amsterdam UMC, where they were processed and stored at -80 degrees Celsius pending analyses. To quantify NfL and GFAP, the samples were measured with Simoa® immunoassay (N2PB Advantage PLUS, ref #104670, lot#504383) on the HD-XTM platform (Quanterix Corp., Billerica, MA, USA).

Imaging outcomes

MRI data was collected using a Philips Ingenia MR7700 3T scanner with a 32-channel head coil (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands). For each participant, a T1 weighted Magnetization-Prepared RApid Gradient Echo (MP-RAGE) sequence (repetition time [TR]/echo time [TE] = 7.0/3.2 milliseconds (ms); inversion time = 877 ms; flip angle = 9°; FOV = 256 × 240 ×180, voxel size = 1 mm3), and a 3D fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence (TR/TE=4800/340 ms; inversion time =1650 ms; flip angle = 40°; FOV = 250 × 250 ×182.56 mm; voxel size of 1.12 mm3) were obtained. Lesions were segmented from the 3D T1-weighted- and FLAIR images using LST-AI. The filled T1 images were processed using a predefined Freesurfer pipeline, and whole brain-, grey matter-, white matter-, thalamus- and lesion volume were extracted. In the analyses, lesion volume and normalized thalamus volume were included.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2). We checked the data for normality and displayed values in the demographics table as mean ± standard deviation, median [interquartile range] or n (%) where applicable. P value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To explore lesion- and thalamus volume evolution over time, we used linear mixed-effects (LME) models with the volumes as dependent variable and a time x group variable as independent, correcting for sex, age and education. Then, in pwMS only, we explored the effect of different disease modifying treatment (DMT) types (i.e. none, low/moderate efficacy, high efficacy) on the volume trajectories by adding a DMT x time interaction to the uncorrected models.

To explore the relationship between the selected brain volumes and clinical (pwMS only), cognitive and serum outcomes, we performed LME models with the aforementioned outcome variables and the brain volumes as predictor with a timepoint x volume interaction. The models were corrected for sex, age and education, where applicable.

Results

Cohort description and clinical progression

The cohort consisted of 67 pwMS (82.1% female, age 35.67 (standard deviation (SD)=10.40) years, and 32 HC (65.6% female, age 38.1 (SD=11.0). At baseline, pwMS had a median EDSS of 2.5 and significantly lower visuospatial memory compared to HC (MS z=-0.30, HC z=0.21, p=0.002), although no significant decline was seen over time in pwMS nor the whole group. For verbal memory, practice effects were observed, irrespective of group (MS ∆ z=0.89, HC ∆z=0.79, p < .001). In pwMS, EDSS and T25FWT remained stable in pwMS, while 9HPT times significantly improved (∆=-1.28s, p < .001).

Brain volumes

At baseline, pwMS had a median lesion volume of 0.99 mL [0.46; 3.06] and had lower thalamus volume compared to HC (MS: 14.13 mL, HC: 15.21 mL; p=0.006). No effect of time was seen for volumetric measures.

Serum biomarkers

For serum biomarkers, pwMS showed higher NfL levels at baseline (MS: 10.58pg/mL, HC: 7.15pg/ML; p=0.001), and significant group × time interactions were observed (β = –1.83, p = .004), where MS showed a steeper decline over time than HC. GFAP showed no significant group differences nor interactions over time.

Correlations with clinical, cognitive and serum outcomes

None of the other clinical outcomes were associated with lesion- or thalamus volume.

For cognition, in the MS-only models, higher baseline lesion volume was only associated with worse baseline verbal memory performance (β ≈ −0.045, p ≈ .047), but not visuospatial memory functioning nor IPS at baseline nor over time. Thalamus volume similarly showed no significant associations with cognition in pwMS. In the whole-group models, both lesion volume (β = –0.0415, p = .043) and thalamus volume (β = 107.76, p = .016) were significant correlates of worse verbal memory performance, although no significant time × volume interactions were observed.

Higher NfL levels at baseline were related to higher lesion volume at baseline in the MS-only model (β = 0.376, p = 0.004) as well as in the whole-group model (β = 0.389, p = 0.0011). Moreover, the time × lesion-volume interaction was significant, indicating that patients with a higher baseline lesion burden showed a steeper decline in NfL over time (MS-only: β = –0.268, p = 0.004; whole-group: β = –0.325, p < 0.0001). Higher lesion volumes were also related to higher GFAP levels at the whole-group level (β = 1.232, p = 0.038) but only a trend in the MS-only analysis (β = 1.184, p = 0.055). No significant effects of time or group were found.

Thalamus volume was related to GFAP levels in both MS-only and whole-group models, but not to NfL levels, with smaller thalamic volumes (more thalamic atrophy) associated with higher GFAP levels (MS-only: β = –6137.9, p < 0.001; whole-group: β = –4527.9, p < 0.001). No significant time interactions were found.

Treatment effects

Compared to those following no treatment, pwMS on low/moderate efficacy medication had significantly lower lesion burden (std.β = -0.17, p = 0.031), whereas those on high efficacy medications had significantly higher thalamus volume (std.β = 0.37, p = 0.032) and lower serum GFAP levels (std.β=-0.65 p=0.004). No effects of DMT type on NfL levels were found, and neither outcome showed a main effect of time, nor DMT-type × time interaction.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined the earliest brain changes in pwMS shortly after diagnosis, compared to HC, and explored how these relate to clinical, cognitive and blood biomarker outcomes over one year. We found that recently diagnosed pwMS already show signs of neurodegenerative disease in the form of significantly lower thalamic volume, as well as worse visuospatial memory and higher serum NfL levels. Over time, the cohort remained relatively stable, seeing only an improvement in manual dexterity in pwMS and a decrease in NfL over time. Further, in pwMS, lesion burden was related to higher serum NfL levels and worse verbal memory performance, whereas thalamic volume was associated with GFAP only. Treatment was related to lower lesion volumes and preserved thalamic volume.  

Higher lesion burden was related to higher NfL levels at baseline and a steeper decline of NfL. This is expected as NfL closely reflects axonal damage3. Therefore, higher lesion burden at diagnosis may result in higher NfL levels, which in turn leads to a greater absolute NfL decrease when the levels return to an individual’s baseline over time. GFAP showed no significant increase in this cohort in MS compared to HC, possibly due to our limited sample size. However, higher GFAP levels were related to worse thalamic neurodegeneration. These findings are in line with previous work, as GFAP is a biomarker of astrocytic activation and therefore reflective of atrophy in grey-matter structures, such as the thalamus3.

Looking at cognition in pwMS, cognitive dysfunction was only seen in visuospatial memory, whereas lesion burden was related to lower verbal memory levels. Interestingly, we did not observe a strong relation between thalamic atrophy and cognition, possibly due to our sample size and short time-window. Further, the lack of IPS impairment is surprising, as IPS is often hypothesized to be at the core of cognitive impairments in MS5. This could indicate that the ordering of cognitive domains affected in early MS might be more memory focused than previously considered.  

This study was not without limitations. First, the limited sample size may have impaired power, despite repeated measures. Although the cohort clearly shows differences in pwMS compared to controls, other changes at baseline or over time may be more subtle, or could be actually absent, warranting a re-evaluation of our conceptual framework of how cognitive impairment develops in MS. Second, short follow-up time may have hampered the ability to study atrophy over time, as one year is hardly enough to capture atrophy on a group-level. Therefore, we will follow up on these analyses in future work, utilizing the complete cohort, for which data acquisition is ongoing.

In summary, these results highlight the early occurrence of cognitive and neurodegenerative events in MS, warranting further evaluation in larger samples over a longer timeframe.
