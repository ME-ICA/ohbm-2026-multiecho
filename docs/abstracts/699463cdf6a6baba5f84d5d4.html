Introduction

When presented with sensory evidence that requires choice among more than one outcome, humans tend to use the available evidence and internal biases to decide upon the most likely action. However, humans sometimes revise their initial decision, perhaps due to internal neural computations that countermand the initial decision. Such “Change-of-Mind” events (CoM) permit investigation of cognitive flexibility. Crucially, CoM can occur even after external sensory information ceases and after the initial commitment occurs. This phenomenon of decision revision would reflect evidence accumulation mechanisms and self-correction of decision uncertainty1.

Previous neuroimaging research has provided some indications of the neural substrates of CoM. Studies employing EEG have implicated the medial frontal cortex in processing post-decision evidence accumulation, linking it to the self-correction of initial decision2. More recently, stereotactic EEG have revealed neural correlates of CoM in the supplementary motor area (SMA) and the insula3. Despite these advances, the whole-brain neural network governing CoM, particularly areas central to cognitive control, remains to be fully elucidated.

To address this critical gap, we conducted a perceptual decision-making experiment using functional MRI (fMRI) to identify the global neural architecture supporting CoM. We designed the behavioral task so that participants would produce a larger number of CoM trials than previously observed. Critically, to increase CoM events, we introduced time pressure by setting a response deadline1, and reduced motor cost by shortening the distance between target locations4. We hypothesized that the large-scale network supporting CoM extended beyond frontal neocortical regions previously associated with CoM. Specifically, the parietal and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, essential for confidence in decision-making5, might constitute key components of the large-scale network enabling the CoM behaviors.

Methods

We collected behavioral and fMRI data from 28 healthy right-handed participants, all of whom provided written informed consent in accordance with protocols approved by the University of Tokyo.

We recorded BOLD signals (2 mm isotropic voxels, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view = 212 × 212 mm, matrix size = 106 × 106, 68 slices, and multiband factor = 4) from the participants during a perceptual decision-making task with varying levels of motion strength (coherence) of the random dot motion (RDM, Fig. 1a). The participants used their right index finger to control the on-screen cursor with an MRI-compatible touchpad. They began by moving the cursor to a start position that was indicated by a circle at the bottom of the screen. After reaching the start position, a trial commenced with a jittered delay of 2 to 3.5 s. Following the delay, a beep occurred every 500 ms, for a total of five beeps. At the third beep, the RDM stimulus appeared, and the participants had used their perception of sensory stimuli (left or right) to decide whether to move the cursor from the starting position to the left or right target located at the top of the display. We encouraged participants to respond as accurately and as quickly as possible. The perceptual difficulty of the RDM was manipulated by varying motion coherence (0, 3.2, 6.4, 12.8, 25.6, and 51.2%), which represents the probability of the dots moving in one direction. Participants viewed RDM only until they moved the cursor away from the starting position or until 500 ms had elapsed since RDM onset. We instructed participants to avoid moving the cursor over the wedge positioned above the start position. We defined a participant’s initial decision corresponding to the initial displacement of the cursor (which reflected arm movement) to the left or the right side of the wedge. We labelled a trial as a CoM even if the initial movement direction decision was reversed to end the movement at the opposite target. That is, start left, end right or start right, end left. (Fig. 1b). To identify CoM timing, we first calculated the cursor movement speed and defined the moment when the horizontal velocity switched sign on the side opposite to the final target, relative to the center of the screen.

Using SPM12 (www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/), we performed several preprocessing steps on the task-related fMRI data. This processing included slice timing correction, realignment, normalization to a default template (ICBM 152) with interpolation to a 2-mm cubic space, spatial smoothing with a Gaussian filter (full-width at half-maximum of 8 mm), and high-pass temporal filtering with a cutoff of 128 s. At a single-participant level, we employed a general linear model for an event-related fMRI design that included five regressors: one regressor for RDMs, a parametric regressor reflecting RDM coherence and regressors for initial decisions in CoM trials and non-CoM trials. Additionally, we included a regressor for rule-violating trials and regressors for head motion, which served as nuisance covariates. We estimated contrasts for each participant through a fixed-effects analysis and subsequently combined data across participants in a random effects model. The statistical threshold was set at P < 0.001 at the voxel level and a family-wise error (FWE)-corrected P < 0.05 at the cluster level. To investigate the relationship between behavior and brain activity, we created 4-mm spherical regions of interest (ROIs) around the brain regions showing a statistically significant effect of CoM . We then analyzed the correlation between individual differences in CoM-related behavior and the brain activity (CoM > non-CoM) within these ROIs, using the Spearman’s rank correlation test. To account for multiple comparisons, we applied a Bonferroni correction, setting the significance level at α = 0.05/11 ROIs.

Results

We observed that the group mean rate of CoM was 19.9%. The CoM rate we observed was relatively higher than in earlier studies, likely due to the influence of time pressure on decision-making and lower motor costs. Consistent with previous research1,4, the CoM rate was higher when the participants changed an incorrect initial decision compared to changing a correct initial decision (t27 = 10.1, P < 0.001, Fig. 1c). Furthermore, we observed a correlation between the CoM rate and the time elapsed from the initial decision to the CoM response (r = -0.61, p < 0.001, Fig. 1d). This finding suggested that the delays in CoM responses might reflect individual difference in the processing of post-decision evidence, which can influence how the initial decision is revised.

The fMRI results revealed greater activation during CoM trials than non-CoM trials in the right precuneus, superior frontal gyrus (SFG), supramarginal gyrus (SMG), angular gyrus, left SMA, and bilaterally in the middle frontal cortex. Conversely, non-CoM trials exhibited greater activation in the left posterior insula, right medial frontal cortex (mPFC) and bilateral anterior lobe of the cerebellum (Pcluster-level FWE < 0.05, Fig. 2).

Additionally, brain activity of the right SMG and posterior insula was found to correlate with CoM rate (right SMG: r = -0.67, P = 0.0002; left posterior insula: r = 0.59, P = 0.0014, Fig. 2). Furthermore, the activity of the left SMA and right mPFC was correlated with reaction times for CoM (left SMA: r = 0.55, P = 0.0021; right mPFC: r = -0.55, P= 0.0031, Fig. 2). Among the regions exhibiting significant CoM effects, the right SMG, left posterior insula, left SMA and right mPFC were found to account for individual differences in the CoM behaviors and the process of decision revision.

Conclusion

By utilizing a protocol to precisely track a participant’s actions that reflect perceptual decision and revisions, our findings provide novel evidence that the frontoparietal network including the dorsolateral frontal and parietal cortices would be involved in the process of the CoM behaviors, shedding new light on the neural substrate of flexibility in decision-making. This work advances our understanding of the large-scale brain structure underlying self-correction or vacillation of our own thoughts and behavior and identifies potential target areas for non-invasive brain stimulation to address cognitive inflexibility.
