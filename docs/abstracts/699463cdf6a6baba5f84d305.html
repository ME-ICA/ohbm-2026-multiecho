Introduction

The pulvinar is an important thalamic structure with the ability to modulate cortex. In humans, the pulvinar is enlarged compared to other species. The medial pulvinar in specific shows disproportionate expansion that parallels the growth and complexity of cortex. For this reason, it is critical to understand how the human pulvinar uniquely influences cortex. Previous functional and structural mapping studies indicate that the pulvinar is organized along large-scale gradients1,2, but these studies do not capture the directional influence that brain stimulation can exert on cortex. Here, we tested how pulvinar stimulation in humans influences cortex, and whether this directional influence is organized along a small number of gradient axes. 

Methods

Nineteen patients with drug-resistant epilepsy undergoing stereotactic EEG for clinical monitoring received single-pulse electrical stimulation (Figure 1a). Stimulation was delivered within the pulvinar while brain stimulation evoked potentials (BSEPs) were recorded across the cortex (Figure 1b). To group electrodes into cortical zones of influence, we parcellated the measurement electrodes according to the Glasser HCP-MMP atlas.3 The 180 Glasser atlas areas were grouped according to 22 regions to ensure sufficient electrodes in each group and reduce sparsity (Figure 1c). The strength of influence from each pulvinar stimulation site on each of these 22 cortical regions was calculated by averaging a metric of pulvinar influence (coefficient of determination via parameterization4) across half the electrodes within a region. The other half was used for cross-validation.

This creates two matrices: 

Xtrain, Xtest ∈ ℝNxM

with N the number of stimulation pairs and M=22 regions, with cells containing not a number if the region was not sampled. To understand whether pulvinar influence on cortex clusters into different patterns, we performed a probabilistic PCA (PPCA) on the training set Xtrain

xi ≈ μ + Wzi + εi

where xi is the influence fingerprint of stimulation site i, μ is the mean, W∈ ℝNxM contains cortical loadings, zi ∈ ℝk are latent scores, and εi is Gaussian noise. Explained variance was computed on observed entries of Xtrain and Xtest  with components retained if the incremental gain in test explained variance exceeded 5%. For each retained component k, cortical loadings zik  were mapped back to the cortical surface to visualize the signed pattern of influence across cortex. The corresponding latent scores  were assigned to each stimulation site’s MNI pulvinar coordinate (Figure 1d). Weighted centroids of positive and negative scores were computed to observe if pulvinar stimulation varies across the dorsal-ventral axis, medial-lateral axis, anterior-posterior axis, or in a random fashion.

Results

Explain-variance curves delineate three main components which show that pulvinar to cortex influence can be described in a low-dimensional space (Figure 2a). For each retained component k, the cortical loadings W(:, k)  defined a signed pattern across the 22 regions. The first component had positive cortical loadings, indexing the overall influence of stimulation-evoked responses across the posterior quadrant. After accounting for this global factor, the second and fourth components expressed opposing weights across specific territories (Figure 2b). The second component contrasted dorsal parietal cortex with occipital and temporal weights, while the fourth component contrasted lateral temporal cortex with striate and extrastriate occipital regions. Mapping the corresponding latent scores onto the pulvinar revealed variation across stimulation sites (Figure 2c), with the dominant axes running roughly medial-lateral and dorsal-ventral. Together, these components indicate that pulvinar stimulation across subjects engages cortex along a global axis plus two large-scale gradients separating temporal from occipital regions and parietal from more ventral visual-temporal cortex.

Conclusion

Altogether, our results show that the influence of human pulvinar stimulation on cortex is captured by a small number of components that distinguish influence on visual/ventral-temporal, lateral temporal and parietal sites. These components form large-scale gradient-like axes across pulvinar and cortex and provide a low-dimensional system for how the expanded human pulvinar can modulate distributed cortical systems. More broadly, this framework illustrates how connectivity of the large human pulvinar allows for modulating specific cortical networks that have to be carefully mapped and considered for potential neuromodulation treatment.
