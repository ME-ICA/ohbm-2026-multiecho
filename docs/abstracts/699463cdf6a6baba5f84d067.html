Introduction

Task-based fMRI is one of the most widely used tools to study human brain function, yet its test- retest reliability remains debateable. Individual participants often exhibit substantially different task-evoked activation maps across repetitions, even when task design and acquisition protocols are held constant and no genuine changes in neural activity are expected1,2. One possible explanation for this is that task-evoked activity maps may reflect not only the underlying “true” trait-like neural activation, but also transient, state-depenent factors such as residual motion, scanner-related artifacts, and fluctuations in attention. Here, we demonstrate that task-activation maps predicted from task-free resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) may be used to isolate the stable components of task-evoked brain activity while reducing the effect of these transient factors. Hence, predicted activation maps generated by our prediction framework, that has been shown to produce accurate and specific predictions across datasets and populations3,4, may be used to separate stable trait-like components from other factors. We demonstrate that predicted task- activation maps resemble the consensus between task repetitions performed weeks or months apart (defined as the averaged task-activation map across visits), more than the actual map acquired at each of those visits. Moreover, we show that participant-level task-based identification analysis (“fingerprinting”) reveal higher accuracy when using predicted maps as identifiers compared to empirical task-activation maps. Together, these findings suggest that prediction of task-activation from rs-FC provides a promising approach for capturing stable trait-like components of task- evoked brain activity. By isolating task-relevant infromation and minimizing noise-driven variability, this approach has the potential to enhance test-retest reliability and support more robust individual-level inference.

Methods

We used data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) S1200 release to train all prediction models. A total of 740 healthy participants with complete resting-state and task-fMRI data were used for training3,4. Models were trained to predict task-evoked brain activity in 6 different cognitive domains (language, working-memory, gambling, relational, social, emotion), from task- free (resting-state) functional connectivity. Broadly, our prediction framework feature extraction includes itertative PCA for dimensionality reduction, group-ICA to yield group-level resting-state networks (RSN’s) and dual regression to yield individual-level RSN’s, fed to GLM for model training (for more details see Fig. 1 and Tik et al., 20233). The trained models were applied to two held-out test-sets: (1) HCP-retest containing data from 40 participants that completed the entire HCP scanning protocol at two timepoints, a few months apart (average of 139 days between visits). We used one exemplar task contrast from each cognitive domain: Language MATH-STORY, Working memory (WM) 2BK, Relational REL, Gambling REWARD, Social TOM, Emotion FACES-SHAPES. The data was collected and preprocessed by the HCP5. (2) In-house dataset of 40 participants that were scanned in an HCP-like protocol, using similar 3T Siemens Prisma MR scanner, using comparable acquisition parameters and HCP-style preprocessing5. These participants completed a full resting-state scan and the working-memory task from the HCP battery (2BK contrast) on 3 different visits (average interval: 12.6 days between visits 1-2; 31.2 days between visits 2-3). Prediction accuracy was quantified using Pearson’s correlation between predicted and actual activation maps, and prediction specificity was measured using the diagonality index (DI)3, defined as the mean difference between congruent (same participant) and incongruent (different participants) preditcted-acutal correlations. Statistical significance was assessed using a permutation test (1000 permutations). 

Consensus maps were computed for each participant and task as the average of both visits (HCP-retest dataset), or three visits (in-house dataset). The prediction models, one for each task contrast, were applied to data from a single visit. We then compared the resemblance of the predicted map to maps of each individual visit and to the consensus map. Resemblance to the predicted maps was evaluated by Pearson's correlations and tested for significance using a permutation test (1000 permutations). One-way ANOVA, one for each task contrast, was carried out to evaluate the differences in resemblance scores between each visit and the consensus maps, corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. Planned post-hoc tests were used to determine whether the consensus maps had significantly higher resemblance scores than any of the single visits.

To further demonstrate that the predicted maps preserve individual-specific features, and that their high similarity to consensus maps is not merely driven by predicting group-level activation, we performed an identification (“fingerprinting”) analysis. Identification was tested using (1) actual activation maps, (2) predicted maps, and (3) the RSNs used as model features. Identification was considered successful when a participant’s activation map from one visit (predicted, actual, or RSN) showed the highest correlation with the actual map from another visit of the same participant. All visit combinations were tested within the HCP-retest (two visits) and in-house (three visits) datasets.

Results

All prediction models that were trained on the outgroup of 740 HCP participants produced accurate and specific predictions of task-evoked brain activity across datasets and task contrasts. In-house data (N=40, 3 visits): WM: 𝑟 = 0.71, 𝐷𝐼 = 0.09, 𝑝 < .001.

After validating prediction success, we compared predicted activation maps to actual brain activation maps from a single visit and consensus maps, defined as the average of multiple visits. Across both datasets, predicted maps consistently showed higher similarity to the consensus maps than to any single-visit map, including the visit from which they were predicted. In the HCP-retest dataset, one-way ANOVA revealed significant differences in resemblance across visit 1, visit 2, and the consensus map for all task contrasts (Bonferroni-corrected): Language: 𝐹(2,117) = 8.64, 𝑝 < .001, WM: 𝐹(2,117) = 6.64, 𝑝 < .005, Relational: 𝐹(2,117) = 13.8, 𝑝 < .001, Gambling: 𝐹(2,117) = 19.06, 𝑝 < .001, Emotion: 𝐹(2,117) = 13.5, 𝑝 < .001, Social: 𝐹(2,117) = 9.1, 𝑝 < .001. Planned post-hoc comparisons showed that predicted maps resembled the consensus maps significantly more than either visit’s actual activation map for all six task contrasts (Fig. 2A). Similar to the HCP-retest data, one-way ANOVA conducted on the WM 2BK contrast in our in-house dataset revealed significant differences between resemblance to the single-visit maps and the three-visit consensus map: 𝐹(6,273) = 16.4, 𝑝 < .001. The planned post-hoc comparisons confirmed that predictions based on a single visit more closely matched the consensus across three visits than any single visit’s actual map (𝑝 < 0.001, Figure 2B). These results indicate that the predicted maps may capture stable trait-like task activation rather than transient factors influencing brain activity.

Next, we conducted participant identification analysis (“fingerprinting”) using actual maps, predicted maps, and RSNs (as a control). Overall, identification based on predicted task activation maps was the most accurate in both datasets. Using predicted maps from visit1 to identify participants based on actual maps from visit2 resulted in 100% identification for all six task contrasts in the HCP-retest dataset. Identification using actual visit1 maps was lower for four contrasts: Language: 97.5%, WM: 92.5%, Relational: 97.5%, Gambling:100%, Emotion: 85%, Social: 100%. Using RSNs as identifiers produced an identification rate of 89.47%, lower than predicted maps and most actual maps. In our 3-visit in-house dataset, discriminability was assessed for the WM 2BK contrast. Predicted and actual maps were each correlated with actual maps across the three visit pairs (visit1-visit2, visit2-visit3, visit1-visit3), and the analysis was repeated using RSNs. Predicted WM 2BK maps achieved 100% identification in all three pairs. Actual maps yielded identification rates of 95%, 95%, and 100%, respectively. RSNs yielded identification rates of 77.5%, 80%, and 87.5%, respectively.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that task-derived brain activation maps, predicted from task-free rs-FC measures using our generalizable prediction framework yield accurate and individual-specific estimates of task-evoked brain activity. Predicted task-activation maps more closely resembled the consensus across multiple task repetitions, acquired weeks to months apart, than any single-visit activation map. This pattern was consistent across datasets, task contrasts, and repetition counts (two or three). Therefore, it is highly indicated that prediction of task-evoked brain activity from rs-FC not only enables obtaining vast information on brain-behavior relationships without any tasks actually being performed, but also has the potential to capture the stable, trait-like components of task activation while overlooking transient, task-irrelevant variability.

A potential concern is that high prediction accuracy could reflect group-level activation patterns rather than individual-specific information. However, we have demonstrated high prediction specificity across all test sets and contrasts (as assessed by the diagonality index), and fingerprinting analyses showed that predicted maps achieved 100% identification accuracy across all task contrasts and datasets, exceeding identification based on actual task maps or RSNs. These findings confirm that predicted activation maps retain rich individual-level information rather than reflecting shared group variance alone.

Together, these results suggest that predicting task activation from rs-FC offers a promising avenue for enhancing sensitivity to trait-like neural activations and improving the test-retest reliability of task-fMRI. This aligns with recent efforts in precision neuroimaging emphasizing the value of large, high-quality datas data that relies on multiple repetitions high quality acquisition. Our framework may leverage such datasets for model training and generalizes effectively to smaller or independent samples3.

By harnessing models trained on large cohorts, predictions of task activation from rs-FC provide insight into key brain regions engaged by specific tasks while preserving individual variability. Given the relatively short acquisition time and minimal compliance demands of resting-state scans,this approach holds promise for generating trait-like activation maps in diverse clinical populations and for studying meaningful differences in task-evoked activity while minimizing noise and transient fluctuations. In conclusion, predicting task activation from rs-FC appears to be a robust method for isolating stable components of task-evoked brain activity while retaining, and potentially enhancing, individual differences, thereby supporting more reliable and generalizable task-fMRI research.
