Introduction

Prenatal opioid exposure, often concomitant with exposure to other substances, has been linked to neuroanatomical alterations from infancy to adolescence, but less is known about the lifelong impact. In this longitudinal study, individuals with opioid and poly-drug exposure and matched controls have been followed for more than three decades with repeated MRI, cognitive and clinical assessments.

78 exposed children and 58 unexposed controls were recruited at pre- or neonatal stage ~3 decades ago (Moe and Slinning, 2001). Most of the prenatally drug-exposed children grew up with foster or adoptive parents who were specially selected to provide care for infants at risk. 

Previous studies in this cohort have revealed various neuroanatomical differences at different ages. The initial MRI assessment performed during childhood (age 9-13 years) has disclosed differences in volumetry, cortical thickness (Walhovd et al., 2007) as well as fractional anisotropy (Walhovd et al., 2010). When this cohort was assessed again as young adults (age 17-22 years), differences in cortical area and thickness were observed (Nygaard et al., 2018). 

In this work we extend the investigation into adulthood (age 27-33) with the goal to examine whether prenatal exposure-associated neuroanatomical differences persist. We further explore how attrition may influence observed neuroanatomical patterns and how it affects the interpretation of long-term outcomes.

Methods

When the participants were assessed as young adults, 44 exposed (21 male; 19.0 ± 1.45 years) and 46 controls (31 male; 17.9 ± 0.34 years) were included. At follow-up, 17 exposed (6 male; 30.4 ± 1.73 years) and 32 controls (21 male; 28.8 ± 0.45 years) returned, with 27 exposed and 14 controls lost to follow-up.

Participants completed structural MRI, cognitive, and clinical testing as youth (mean age 18.4 ± 1.19 years) and again approximately ten years later as adults (mean age 29.5 ± 1.33 years). T1w scans were collected at both timepoints: on a 3T Siemens Skyra (1.0 mm isometric) within the previous wave and on a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma (0.8 mm isometric) within the recent wave.

The acquired images were processed using FreeSurfer’s (v7.1.1) segmentation tool “recon-all”. For participants with two timepoints, the “-long” flag was used to generate a subject-specific template for longitudinal processing. The resulting segmentations were processed using “mri_glmfit” to perform the General Linear Model (GLM) analysis of the surfaces. Participants’ age, sex and birth weight were included as covariates. The standard fsaverage template was used for registration. The results were tested against an empirical null hypothesis, accounting for multiple comparisons across the cortical surface using Monte-Carlo simulations. We performed the following comparisons of the cortical area (CA) and cortical thickness (CT):

  1. Adult cross-sectional comparison:
    1. Exposed (returning; n = 17) vs. Controls (returning; n = 32)
  2. Youth cross-sectional comparisons among participants who returned for follow-up:
    1. Exposed – returning (n = 17) vs. Controls – returning (n = 32)
  3. Youth cross-sectional comparisons for attrition evaluation:
    1. Exposed – non-returning (n = 27) vs. Controls – returning (n = 32)
    2. Exposed – non-returning (n = 27) vs. Exposed – returning (n = 17)

Results

None of the differences in CA found in youth/young adulthood ~10 yrs ago survived multiple comparison corrections in returning participants at both timepoints. Lower CT was observed in portions of the left occipital lobe of returning exposed participants at youth (cluster-wise p < 0.05), but not in adulthood. 

Furthermore, we examined participants who only completed the youth session. Relative to the same 32 controls who returned for the follow-up, this withdrawn subgroup showed lower CA, again with modest CT differences (p < 0.05). Differences in CA, but not in CT, were observed when comparing returning and non-returning participants in youth/young adulthood (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1). Cognitive and clinical data will help clarify the characteristics of the participants who withdrew from the study within the last 10 years.

Conclusion

The latest wave of data collection did not reveal statistically significant neuroanatomical differences between the exposed and control group in adulthood. However, there was a notable attrition in the exposed group – while 69.6% individuals from the control group took part at both timepoints, only 38.6% did so in the exposed group. This pattern suggests a strong selection effect, in which the exposed participants who remained in the study likely represent a neuroanatomically more typical subgroup.

Importantly, this attrition allowed us to study the withdrawn exposed subgroup. These individuals showed widespread differences in CA when compared both to the control group and the returning exposed group. CA is mostly formed prenatally and in early childhood (Huang et al., 2022), these findings are consistent with long-standing neurodevelopmental differences in the exposed group. Notably, performing the comparison within the exposed group (returning vs non-returning) evades the environmental differences that are present between the exposed and control groups.

Taken together, these results indicate that prenatal exposure-associated neuroanatomical alterations may persist through adulthood, but selective dropout may mask these differences in adult returnees.
