Introduction

Investigating the structure-function coupling (SFC) is of primary importance to understand how the relatively static structural connectivity (SC) constrains the complex spatiotemporal patterns of brain activity, ultimately shaping cognition and behavior. Previous studies on functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have shown that the brain SFC follows the sensory-transmodal gradient, with stronger correspondence in sensory cortices and weaker in transmodal cortices1. Yet, in contrast to fMRI, there is a limited understanding of the relationship between electrophysiological signals and the underlying brain structural connectome, as well as of the convergence-divergence in the SFC between fMRI and electrophysiological recordings.

    In recent years, the introduction of graph signal processing (GSP) methods to neuroscience, in particular, graph harmonic analysis, has opened new avenues for investigating the SFC2. The decomposition of neural activity with respect to low and high connectome harmonics allows the quantification, for each brain region, of the SFC as the structural decoupling index (SDI), denoting the extent to which the signal is (un)constrained by the SC2. 

In the present study, we analyzed the SFC in the rich spectral dynamics of magnetoencephalography (MEG) data and compared it with the SFC in fMRI. To do so, we implemented the graph harmonic decomposition using structural, hemodynamic, and electrophysiological data from N = 75 healthy participants. 

Our findings reveal pairwise differences across frequencies that align with spatial variations along large-scale gradients, including the ventral–dorsal, anterior–posterior, and sensorimotor–association axes. Finally, the SDI comparison between MEG frequencies and fMRI demonstrates greater similarity between the alpha frequency band and fMRI than between other frequencies and fMRI.

Methods

Diffusion-weighted imaging, resting-state MEG, and resting-state fMRI were recorded from 75 subjects (45 males, 29 females, one unreported gender) with a mean age of 31.89 years (range: 18-62). 

T1-weighted MRI, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and 15-minute eyes-open resting state (RS) fMRI scans were obtained with a 3 Tesla MRI scanner. DWI data preprocessing included denoising and motion correction. For tractography construction, the principal diffusion direction was obtained from 20,000,000 seed points. 

Subject-level structural connectomes (SCs) were derived from individual diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) data, with edges representing the number of reconstructed white matter streamlines normalized by the volumes of the connected regions defined by the Schaefer atlas. For subsequent analyses, a group-level SC was obtained by averaging the individual subject-level connectomes. 

15-minute eyes-open resting-state MEG data were recorded with a 306-channel MEG (Megin Oy, Helsinki). Data preprocessing and source reconstruction were performed with the MNE-python software (https://mne.tools/stable/index.html). Source reconstruction was performed using minimum-norm estimation (MNE) and dynamic statistical parametric maps (dSPM). Source vertices were collapsed into 200 parcels of the Schaefer atlas. Broadband parcel time series were downsampled to 250 Hz, then filtered into narrowband time series by convolution with 20 Morlet wavelets at center frequencies spanning 4-40 Hz in log-linear space. Narrowband parcel-level amplitude envelopes, computed as the magnitude of the Morlet-derived complex timeseries, were z-scored across time for each frequency. 

The fMRI measurements were acquired with 3mm isotropic resolution, TR = 1.25 seconds, and TE = 30ms. Functional time courses were obtained after preprocessing with fMRIprep, which included slice-time correction, resampling onto native space, co-registration to the T1w reference, and nuisance regression (DVARS, white matter, and CSF). Component-based noise correction (CompCor) and high-pass filtering were also applied. After preprocessing and denoising, subject-level BOLD time series for 200 cortical brain regions from the Schaefer atlas were obtained and z-scored across time.

To quantify the regional SFC, we implemented the harmonic decomposition approach and the structural decoupling index (SDI) [2] (Figure 1). This metric quantifies the (de)coupling between the regional activity and the underlying SC. Regions with SDI > 1 exhibit greater energy in high-frequency than in low-frequency harmonics, indicating sharper signal variations relative to their topological neighbors, and are therefore considered more liberal, or “decoupled” from the underlying structure. In contrast, regions with SDI < 1 are dominated by low-frequency harmonics and are considered more “coupled” to the SC. 

For interpretability, for each subject, the 20 MEG SDIs were averaged into five canonical frequency bands corresponding to theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), low beta (13–20 Hz), high beta (21–30 Hz), and gamma (31–40 Hz) bands. We examined differences in SFC across MEG frequencies and fMRI at the whole-brain and regional level. To do so, we compared, across frequency bands and modalities, the average SDI across regions or the region-specific SDI, using paired t-tests, followed by false discovery rate (FDR) correction (α = 0.05). 

To quantify the relationship between regional SDI differences across MEG frequencies and cortical gradients, we correlated the regional t-statistics with their coordinates along four large-scale brain axes: medial–lateral, ventral–dorsal, anterior–posterior, and the sensorimotor–association axis. We assessed the statistical significance of these correlations by comparing them against null distributions generated from surrogate MEG signals with a disrupted SFC. 

To assess the SDI correspondence across modalities, we computed the correlation between the fMRI and MEG SDIs.

Results

The spatial patterns of SDI values denoting coupling (blue) or decoupling (red) were spread across the cerebral cortex and highly consistent across frequency bands (Figure 2A). In fMRI, we found that negative SDI values, corresponding to stronger coupling, in regions of the visual and somatomotor networks. Conversely, positive SDI values indicative of decoupling were primarily observed in control and ventral attention network regions. The default mode, dorsal attention, and limbic networks appeared halfway between coupling and decoupling.

The whole-brain SDI showed its lowest peak, corresponding to higher global coupling, in fMRI, followed by the alpha, low-beta, theta, high-beta, and gamma bands (Figure 2B). 

When quantifying pairwise differences across frequencies using FDR-corrected paired t-tests, we observed a striking distribution of regional t-statistics, reminiscent of large-scale cortical gradients (Figure 2C). The correlation analysis confirmed that the spatial patterns of SDI differences across frequency bands were significantly associated with these gradients (Figure 2D). 

To determine whether these correlations were driven by the spatial distribution of SDI within a single band rather than by the pairwise contrast, we correlated the group-level SDI maps for each frequency with the four axes and assessed significance using the signal surrogates (Figure 2D, columns on the right of matrices). We found no significant correlation between SDI maps and the ventral-dorsal axis. SDI patterns in the alpha and gamma bands were significantly correlated with the anterior-posterior axis (r = 0.28 and r = 0.16, respectively). Moreover, the alpha- and low-beta band SDI maps correlated with the sensorimotor-association axis (r = 0.37 and r = 0.28, respectively). Although we found significant correlations between the frequency-dependent SDI patterns and large-scale axes, their strength was clearly lower than that of the t-maps. 

Finally, to assess the correspondence between SDIs across modalities, we computed the correlation between the fMRI and MEG SDIs. Using group-averaged SDIs, the highest correlation with fMRI was found in the alpha band (r = 0.47), followed by low-beta (r = 0.35), theta (r = 0.30), high-beta (r = 0.25), and gamma (r = 0.18). Correlations decreased but followed the same order when computing the average of subject-level correlations: alpha (r = 0.26), then low-beta (r = 0.20), theta (r = 0.17), high-beta (r = 0.15), and gamma (r = 0.10).

Conclusion

In the present study, using graph harmonic decomposition, we investigated the SFC in MEG and fMRI in terms of the smoothness of hemodynamic or electrophysiological brain activity relative to the (SC). To quantify the SFC, we used the structural decoupling index (SDI)2, a regional metric that quantifies whether brain signals (in the present work, either BOLD activity or amplitude envelopes) change progressively (coupled) or sharply (decoupled) across structurally connected brain regions. 

Although the majority of SFC investigations using harmonic decomposition have been carried out with fMRI data, recent studies using electrophysiological recordings have demonstrated the importance of connectome harmonics in the organization of fast neural activity3. In accordance with and complementary to these investigations, our results support the idea that the connectome harmonics are the fundamental building blocks of neural activity at different spatio-temporal scales. Our main finding is that the SFC in the rich spectrotemporal patterns of MEG data is organized along large-scale brain gradients. Furthermore, we showed that, among frequencies, the relationship between alpha-band oscillations and the SC is the most similar to that observed between hemodynamic and the SC.

When examining the regional level SFC, we found a highly similar SDI pattern across MEG frequency bands, corroborating previous results using the GSP on electrophysiological data3. Interestingly, when comparing the SDI across frequency bands, we found that the relative dominance of specific frequencies changes systematically along the anterior–posterior, ventral-medial, and sensorimotor-association axes. That is, although all frequencies share a similar global pattern of the SFC distribution along the cortex, their relative differences follow large-scale anatomical and functional brain gradients. This is intriguing in line with recent investigations on MEG showing evidence that dominant peak frequency, traveling waves, and cortical timescales follow large-scale gradients4. 

The second aim of this work was to investigate overlaps and differences in the SFC between hemodynamic and electrophysiological data. Previous studies comparing SFC between fMRI and MEG showed a stronger relationship between functional connectivity (FC) matrices derived from frequency-dependent MEG signals and the SC than between fMRI FC and the SC5. Our results show a higher relationship (more coupling) between fMRI and SC than between MEG and SC. Methodological differences could explain this discrepancy. We measured SFC based on the smoothness of the signal across topological neighbors rather than calculating the correlation between structural and functional connectivity matrices. Yet, it remains an open question whether the SC differentially shapes regional activity compared to pairwise functional connectivity.

The MEG SDI in the alpha band showed the strongest correlation with the fMRI SDI. This suggests that both BOLD activity and MEG amplitude in the alpha band may be similarly influenced by the underlying structural network, consistent with previous investigations showing a higher correlation between fMRI and alpha-MEG FC than at other frequencies. Importantly, the low overall correlation values in our results indicate that, although there are similarities in the SFC between fMRI and MEG signals, a large portion of these signals arises from independent mechanisms, which is expected given the different nature of the brain signals (hemodynamic vs. neural activity). 

In summary, we employed graph signal processing to investigate the SFC, utilizing graph harmonic analysis in MEG and fMRI data recorded from the same participants. Our results highlight the organization of the SFC in MEG along large-scale cortical gradients. Furthermore, we show a particular trimodal correspondence between oscillations in the alpha band, BOLD activity, and the structural connectome.
