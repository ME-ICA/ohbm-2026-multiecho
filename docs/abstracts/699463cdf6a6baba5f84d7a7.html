Introduction

Dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) analysis of functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) data has emerged as a powerful framework for characterizing the time-varying network disruptions underlying Parkinson's disease (PD)1. However, the rapid expansion of this field has introduced substantial methodological heterogeneity, creating a fragmented landscape that complicates knowledge synthesis and clinical translation. To address this, we performed a systematic review to map the conceptual and methodological diversity of dFC research in PD. Beyond a traditional static synthesis, we developed DynaPD, an open-source interactive application that allows researchers to dynamically explore study designs, analytical pipelines, and reported findings. This work aims to consolidate the diverse dFC literature, fostering a more coherent and cumulative path toward precision neurology in PD.

Methods

We followed the PRISMA guidelines for this systematic review2. A comprehensive search was performed on Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed (June 6, 2025) using search terms related to PD, fMRI, and dynamic connectivity, limited to open-access English articles.

From an initial pool of 207 articles, we identified 37 eligible studies (Figure 1). Two independent coders extracted data across four domains: Publication Information, Study Design, Methodological Details (preprocessing and analysis pipelines), and Findings (state characteristics and clinical associations). Inter-coder agreement was 98.5%, with discrepancies resolved via consensus.

To visualize this multidimensional dataset, we developed the DynaPD application using the Shiny package in R. This tool enables users to interactively filter studies by clinical focus or methodology and visualize the variability in analytical decision-making. Access to the application is available via two methods. First, users can clone the source code from our GitHub repository [https://github.com/kristantodan12/dFC-PD] that also contains the database extracted in this review and run the application locally by following the provided instructions. Alternatively, we have deployed a web-based version for immediate access at [https://individualbrainproject.shinyapps.io/dfc-pd/].

Results

Our systematic search identified 37 eligible studies from an initial pool of 207 records, aggregating data from a total of 1,954 participants with PD. The extracted dataset reveals a landscape dominated by cross-sectional designs (N=27), with only a minority of studies (N=10) employing longitudinal approaches to track disease progression or intervention effects.

Methodological Heterogeneity and Convergence: We observed substantial divergence in data acquisition and preprocessing protocols (Figure 2). Scanning parameters varied widely, with Repetition Times (TR) ranging from 2000ms to 3000ms, and scan durations frequently falling below the recommended 10-minute threshold for stable dFC estimation 3. Denoising strategies were similarly heterogeneous, particularly regarding motion regression (24-parameter vs. 6-parameter models) and global signal regression implementation. Despite this preprocessing variability, the field has converged on a distinct analytical core: the majority of studies utilized Independent Component Analysis (ICA) for network parcellation and consistently applied a sliding-window approach coupled with k-means clustering for state identification.

Empirical Findings and Clinical Associations: Regarding brain state dynamics, a consensus emerged on the existence of two primary state configurations: a sparsely connected state characterized by weak connections, and a strongly connected state exhibiting dense functional integration. Temporal metrics, specifically dwell time, fraction time, and number of transitions, were the most frequently reported features. Our synthesis of clinical associations indicates that PD pathology is generally characterized by a rigid dynamic profile: a predominance of sparsely connected states and a reduced frequency of transitions compared to healthy controls. Higher dwell time in strongly connected states and increased transition frequency were frequently associated with better clinical outcomes, including lower MDS-UPDRS III scores and reduced severity of non-motor symptoms such as apathy. However, notable contradictions exist, with some studies reporting inverse associations in specific sub-populations (e.g., PD with dementia), highlighting the need for context-specific interpretation.

Interactive Tool: These complex relationships are fully mapped in DynaPD, our interactive application. The tool features a number of tabs visualizing methodological landscape and findings variability in PD research using dFC. We recommend to also explore DynAPD while reading the results to investigate the landscape given specified filters such as study design.

Conclusion

Our systematic review highlights that while dFC research in PD has achieved methodological convergence on specific analytical pipelines (sliding-window and k-means), the field remains fragmented by inconsistent reporting standards and limitations in data acquisition.

The Quality-Quantity Trade-off: An important barrier to progress is the prevalence of short scan durations (<10 minutes) and low temporal resolution. While current static windowing methods are standard, they impose rigid temporal scales that may obscure rapid, transient neural fluctuations relevant to PD pathology. Future acquisition protocols must prioritize extended scanning sessions and accelerated imaging (e.g., multiband fMRI) to ensure the fidelity of dynamic state estimation.

From Group-Level to Precision Neurology: The current dominance of group-level concatenation approaches risks masking individual-specific state trajectories, which are essential for precision neuroimaging4. To translate dFC biomarkers into clinical utility, such as patient-specific DBS targeting, the field must shift toward “bottom-up” approaches that identify stable states at the individual level before generalizing to the population. This transition requires not only higher quality individual data but also the adoption of longitudinal designs to disentangle state-dependent fluctuations from trait-level disease progression.

Infrastructure for Cumulative Science: Finally, the fragmentation of small, private datasets limits statistical power. We propose that Federated Learning (FL)5 offers a viable path forward, allowing the aggregation of multi-site dFC models without compromising patient privacy. Furthermore, the lack of standardized reporting hinders automated synthesis. By establishing DynaPD as a living, open-source knowledge space, we provide a scalable infrastructure that can evolve with the field, supporting the transition from ad-hoc manual reviews to Artificial Intelligence-assisted, cumulative evidence synthesis.
