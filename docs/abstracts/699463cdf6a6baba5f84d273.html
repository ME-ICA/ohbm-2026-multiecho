Introduction

With diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) and connectomics studies, stroke is increasingly seen as a network disease where a focal lesion disturbs a whole complex network and can have an impact on remote areas directly influencing both acute impairment and its recovery¹. However, because the acquisition of these very specialized images are costly and time consuming, their utilization in clinical settings at the acute phase is still very challenging. For that reason, connectomics studies in stroke patients often gather few patients and/or mainly focus at the subacute phase rather than the acute one not allowing to fully understand the acute processes supporting recovery at the stroke population level2,3. Here, using only DWI routinely acquired at the hospital admission (before day seven after stroke onset), we aimed to estimate the acute patient’s structural connectome and, using graph theory, study the association between whole brain acute connectome topology and both acute motor severity and motor recovery, in a real-world cohort of stroke patients.

Methods

One hundred-fifty patients with a first-ever unilateral stroke and an upper extremity motor deficit admitted to our center were included. Fugl-Meyer Assessment of the Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) were assessed on day 7 after stroke (as a measure of upper limb “motor severity”) and again at 3 months post-stroke. The difference in FMA-UE scores between the two timepoints is defined as “motor recovery”⁴. Lesion masks were hand-drawn on the DWI sequence acquired at the patient's admission (<7 days), registered in the MNI space and then used in BCBToolkit to compute voxel-wise probabilities of structural disconnection (Disconnectome)⁵. Using a normative tractography of 200 000 tracts, patient individual tractographies were obtained using the patient’s disconnectome and computing for each tract its maximum probability of being disconnected, the tracts were then discarded or kept according to this maximum probability (for example, a tract with a maximum disconnectome probability of 0.9 on the disconnectome map had in our algorithm a 90% percent chance to be removed). Patients' connectomes were then obtained by combining their tractography with the Freesurfer MNI cortex and subcortical’s parcellation. Each node topology was then characterized by computing its strength and clustering coefficient. Correlations between nodes graph metrics and both stroke severity and motor recovery were computed using the Spearman correlation test. Each feature was age and volume corrected by linearly regressing them out. Statistical tests were then performed on the residuals. All p values are corrected for multiple comparisons using the False Discovery Rate (FDR). The methods are resumed in the Figure.

Results

Motor severity was significantly and positively correlated with the strength of multiple nodes from the bilateral sensorimotor networks (including the paracentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, precentral gyrus, cervical/medulla junction hemisection, the cerebellum and the superior frontal gyrus which including the supplementary motor area), from the bilateral basal ganglia (including thalamus, accumbens, caudate, putamen, and pallidum) and the bilateral rostral anterior cingulate. By looking at the clustering coefficient, nodes implied in the sensori-motor network and basal ganglia were positively and significantly correlated with motor severity excepted the ipsilateral cervical/medulla junction hemisection and cerebellum for which the clustering coefficient were negatively correlated to motor severity. Motor recovery shows the implication of a broader network and a more complex local network topology as significant correlations included almost all previously cited nodes metrics but also positive correlations with the bilateral caudal anterior cingulate strength and with the clustering coefficient of nodes from the contralesional sensorimotor network.

Conclusion

By only using a stroke lesion mask derived from DWI routinely acquired at the hospital admission, we estimated stroke patients’ acute structural connectome. Using graph theory analysis, we have shown that both motor severity and recovery rely on strong connections within the bilateral sensorimotor networks, basal ganglia and cerebellum and local clustering within the ipsilesional motor network, the bilateral thalamus and caudate. Motor recovery specifically relied on the caudal anterior cingulate strength and the clustering coefficient of the contralesional sensorimotor network. This approach gave both results consistent with the actual literature (demonstrating its validity) and new insights in how acute structural connectomes are linked to motor severity and recovery.
