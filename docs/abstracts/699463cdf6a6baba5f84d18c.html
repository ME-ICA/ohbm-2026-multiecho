Introduction

Reproducibility remains a major challenge in neuroimaging. Differences across studies are often blamed on analytic variability, lack of statistical stringency or small samples, reinforcing unrealistic expectations that variability should be minimized and that single studies should yield definitive answers. In reality, variability is intrinsic to biological systems, experimental sampling, scanner environments, and analytic choices; removing it is mostly neither feasible nor scientifically justified. Studies should be performed, reported and compared in ways that recognize this; doing so will help reduce biases, increase information content and improve reproducibility.

Meta-analysis provides a principled way to synthesize evidence and characterize heterogeneity, yet commonly used approaches such as consistency rates (e.g., simple vote-counting) and coordinate-based meta-analysis (CBMA) depend on arbitrary thresholds and discard substantial information. Three methods are commonly used in image-based meta-analysis (IBMA): hierarchical modeling, Stouffer’s method, and standardized-effect approaches such as Cohen’s d or Hedges’ g. These differ markedly in how they treat effect estimates, uncertainty, and heterogeneity.

Here, we examine whether analytic variability is truly the primary cause of reproducibility difficulties, compare the strengths and limitations of different meta-analytic approaches, and evaluate which IBMA methods provide reliable inference.

Methods

Sources of fMRI variability include experimental design, acquisition, participant sampling, preprocessing, modeling, and reporting. Some components (e.g., motion or physiological noise) obscure neural signals and should be mitigated; others (biological diversity, sampling variation, analytic flexibility) contain meaningful information and should be properly modeled rather than reduced or suppressed.

We evaluated three IBMA approaches:

  1. Hierarchical modeling, which pools effect estimates and variances and jointly models between-study heterogeneity, analogous to participant-level group analysis.

  2. Stouffer’s method, which aggregates Z-statistics only, ignoring effect magnitudes and uncertainties.

  3. Hedges’ g, which standardizes effect magnitudes but does not directly yield pooled inference or heterogeneity estimates.

We constructed a pseudo-meta-analysis using a 10-participant audiovisual speech dataset[1], treating each participant as an independent study. Voxelwise effect estimates and standard errors were extracted and rescaled to mimic a group size of 26. Each IBMA approach was applied voxelwise to compare pooled effects, uncertainty propagation, and representation of heterogeneity. Hierarchical modeling was implemented through a conventional group-analysis method that shares the same analytical principles[2].

Results

Hierarchical modeling produced physiologically interpretable pooled percent-signal-change maps (Fig 1A) and voxelwise heterogeneity I² (Fig 1B), revealing spatially structured variability across studies[3]. This approach correctly propagated uncertainty and distinguished true cross-study differences from sampling noise.

Hedges’ g produced small-to-moderate standardized effects (<0.4) (Fig 1C), consistent with typical fMRI magnitudes. Although useful for quantifying effect size and informing future experimental designs, it does not provide direct statistical inference or heterogeneity estimates.

Stouffer’s method yielded inflated Z-values, often nearly double those from hierarchical modeling (Fig 1D,E,F), because it ignores differences in effect magnitude and precision across studies and cannot properly account for heterogeneity. This leads to distorted (mostly inflated) statistical evidence and overconfident inference.

Across methods, hierarchical modeling was the only approach that simultaneously captured pooled effects, uncertainty, and heterogeneity (Fig 1G). Hedges’ g provided descriptive magnitude estimates but lacked inferential strength. Stouffer’s method significantly overstated evidence and was unreliable for reproducibility evaluation.

Conclusion

Reproducibility in neuroimaging requires acknowledging variability rather than attempting to reduce or eliminate it. Biological, sampling, and analytic variation are intrinsic to scientific inquiry, and threshold-based practices that discard subthreshold information distort cumulative evidence.

Reliable reproducibility assessment requires retaining full study-level effect estimates and uncertainties. Hierarchical IBMA provides the most principled framework because it models pooled effects and heterogeneity while maintaining valid statistical inference. Stouffer’s method inflates evidence and cannot assess heterogeneity, while Hedges’ g supports magnitude comparison but not inference.

Effect-based reporting is essential. Percent-signal-change values derived via local mean scaling provide interpretable units across runs, individuals, and studies, whereas grand-mean scaling obscures such variability[4]. Open sharing of full, unthresholded statistical maps further reduces publication bias and strengthens cumulative science.

Reframing variability as informative, prioritizing effect-based reporting, and adopting hierarchical meta-analysis will substantially improve the reproducibility, interpretability, and generalizability of fMRI research.
