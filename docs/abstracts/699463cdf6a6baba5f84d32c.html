Introduction

Chronic stress increases the risk of mental and physical health issues. Mindfulness meditation and dietary management offer promising stress-reduction strategies.1,2 Mindfulness meditation typically includes two major practices: breathing and compassion meditation. Breathing meditation enhances cognitive stability by maintaining attention on the breath, whereas compassion meditation cultivates compassion through repeated phrases like “May you be happy”. The differential effects of these interventions on brain networks remain unclear, but electroencephalography (EEG) provides insights into stress-related neural activity. Applying graph-theoretical network analysis to EEG allows us to examine changes in hub regions, which play a central role in information processing. Hub nodes are essential for communication and integration of whole-brain networks. Moreover, alterations in hub architecture have been associated with neural vulnerability observed in psychiatric disorders.3 Based on these findings, we hypothesized that stress-related neural mechanisms are closely linked to changes in hub organization. In this study, we implemented four weeks of breathing meditation, compassion meditation, or dietary management, and compared their stress-reduction effects using EEG-based graph-theoretical measures. 

Methods

Sixty-five healthy volunteers (26 females, aged 19-23) with no prior mindfulness meditation experience participated and randomly assigned to the breathing meditation group (BMG, n = 23), the compassion meditation group (CMG, n = 23) or the diet management group (control group: CG, n = 19). Participants engaged in either a 4-week online meditation training program or a diet management program. In the meditation groups, participants performed daily 10-minute online training sessions using audio-guided instructions and attended weekly group meetings. The audio guides for the BMG encouraged sustained attention to one's own breath, whereas those for the CMG consisted of phrases aimed at cultivating compassion toward oneself, others, and all living beings. In contrast, participants in the CG were instructed to systematically consume the recommended daily amounts of nutrients reported to have stress-reducing effects, in addition to attending weekly group meetings. To examine the levels of mindfulness and depression, each participant filled out the psychological scales (the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ), Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) and Compassion Love Scale (CLS)) before and after the training period. 

Electroencephalography (EEG) signals were recorded from 32 scalp sites located according to the extended international 10/20 system using EEG (LiveAmp, Brain Products GmbH, Germany). EEG data were collected pre- and post-training during three sessions: pre-task rest (5 min), acute stress task (mental arithmetic task4: 10 min), and post-task rest (5 min) (Figure 1). EEG signals were sampled at 500 Hz. Connectivity (lagged coherence) was analyzed using standard low-resolution brain electromagnetic tomography (sLORETA) with thirty-six regions of interest (ROIs) were defined as network nodes, and connectivity values served as edges for graph theoretical analysis. Edges were sparsified by applying a proportional threshold of the top 30%, and a weighted brain network was constructed. To evaluate hub properties within the network, we employed degree centrality, one of the graph-theoretical metrics. Degree centrality directly reflects the number of connections that each node has with other regions and is therefore widely used as a reliable measure for identifying hub regions.5 Degree centrality was calculated for each ROI. The frequency bands were defined as theta:4–8 Hz, alpha:8–12 Hz, and beta:13–30 Hz. 

To assess effects of training, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with aligned rank transform (ART) was performed with group (BMG, CMG or CG) as the between-subjects factor and session (pre-training or post-training) as the within-subjects factor for the psychological scales. For  degree centrality, a two-way ANOVA with ART was performed for each condition, each frequency band, and each ROI with group (BMG, CMG or CG) as the between-subjects factor and session (pre-training or post-training) as the within-subjects factor. Specifically, paired post-hoc comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for nonparametric dependent samples.

Results

For the psychological scales, a two-way ANOVA with ART revealed significant interactions between group and session (FFMQ total score (F(2,62) = 6.81, p < 0.01) and CLS score (F(2,57) = 5.19, p < 0.01)), whereas no significant change was observed in the CG. Post-hoc analysis revealed that the FFMQ total score was significantly higher at post-training than at pre-training in the BMG (W = 24.0, p < 0.001) and in the CMG (W = 20.5, p < 0.01). These results indicates that mindfulness-based interventions enhanced participants’ mindfulness, consistent with their explicit focus on attentional orientation and mindful attitudes. In addition, the CLS score was significantly higher at post-training than at pre-training in the CMG (W = 25.5, p < 0.01), whereas no changes were observed in the BMG or CG. The absence of similar changes in the BMG and CG suggests that improvements in compassion are not a general outcome of mindfulness practice but are specific to interventions designed to cultivate compassion directly. 

 Furthermore, a significant main effect of session was observed for the BDI-II scores, indicating overall reductions from pre- to post-training across groups (F(1,62) = 38.3, p < 0.01). In contrast, no significant interaction between group and session was found for the BDI-II. The finding that BDI-II scores significantly decreased from pre- to post-training across all groups suggests that each intervention contributed to reductions in depression, indicating a general stress-relieving effect. 

For the EEG degree centrality, a two-way ANOVA revealed significant interactions between group and session in the inferior temporal gyrus (ITG) during pre-task rest (F(2,60) = 4.21, p = 0.019) and in the insula during post-task rest (F(2,60) = 3.25, p = 0.046) in the alpha band, and a significant interaction in the superior temporal gyrus (STG) during pre-task rest (F(2,60) = 13.1, p < 0.01) in the beta band. Post-hoc analysis revealed that in the ITG, degree centrality in the alpha band during pre-rest was significantly higher at post-training than at pre-training in the BMG (W = 40.5, p < 0.01) (Figure 2(a)). In the insula, degree centrality in the alpha band during post-task rest was significantly lower at post-training than at pre-training in the CMG (W = 120, p = 0.046) (Figure 2(b)). In the STG, degree centrality in the beta band during pre-task rest was significantly higher at post-training than at pre-training in the BMG (W = 8.5, p = 0.014) and significantly lower at post-training than at pre-training in the CMG (W = 70.5, p = 0.044) (Figure 2(c)). 

The finding that degree centrality in the alpha band in the ITG increased exclusively in the BMG during pre-task rest suggests that repeated breathing meditation may promote the stabilization of attention and the regulation of sensory processing. This, in turn, may strengthen attentional control networks during rest, leading the ITG to assume a more central role within the functional network. In contrast, the decrease in degree centrality in the alpha band in the insular during post-task rest observed in the CMG reflects reduced emotional and interoceptive responses through compassion meditation. Similarly, the increased degree centrality in the beta band in the STG observed in the BMG suggests that breathing meditation enhances sensory–attentional integration processes that support sustained monitoring of internal bodily states. In contrast, the decreased degree centrality in the beta band in STG in the CMG is likely to reflect a suppression of social and affective salience network activity induced by compassion meditation. 

Conclusion

Our results demonstrated that both meditation programs significantly improved FFMQ scores, and that the compassion meditation program additionally led to a significant increase in CLS scores. Moreover, changes in degree centrality were observed in specific brain regions: the BMG showed increased degree centrality in the ITG during pre-task rest, whereas the CMG exhibited decreased degree centrality in the insula during post-task rest. In contrast, similar changes were not observed in the dietary management program. These findings suggest that different forms of meditation may differentially influence the formation and reorganization of hub regions within the brain. In other words, meditation interventions may enhance mindfulness and compassion traits, and they may also modulate hub structures in the brain in ways that differ depending on the type of meditation.
