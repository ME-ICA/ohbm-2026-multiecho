Introduction

With daily training, memory athletes can achieve superior performance on memory tasks, such as memorizing 339 digits in 5 minutes (Fig. 1a). This is accomplished by using mnemonic techniques, particularly the Method of Loci, in which they convert abstract information into vivid scenes and placing them along a mental path, which is then retraced during recall (Fig. 1b). Understanding how such techniques can boost the brain’s memory capacity beyond 100-fold holds promise for combatting dementia and improving cognitive performance in other domains.

While we understand the techniques that memory athletes use, the brain mechanisms behind their seemingly superhuman performance remain elusive. Because each memory athlete employs individual-specific, personalized training techniques to develop their skills, it has been challenging to study them at the group level. Fortunately, precision functional mapping (PFM) with resting-state functional connectivity (FC) and task fMRI enables detailed study of individual brains1.

Methods

We repeatedly scanned the 6-time US Memory Champion in two scanning batches, in 2015 and 2021, obtaining over 400 minutes of resting-state fMRI data and over 350 minutes of task fMRI data. In 2015, he performed the Midnight Scan Club (MSC) protocol2, and we used the 10 MSC participants as controls. In 2021, he performed a tailored protocol including a series of cognitive tasks to map neural circuits underlying his elite memory performance, and two MSC participants were used as controls. Two of the tasks were so challenging that they could only be performed by the Memory Champion. We also compared the Memory Athlete functional connectivity to a large adult population consisting of 887 participants (ages 22-35 years) from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) 4, to verify the generalization of its unique functional organization.

We hypothesized that connections between brain regions utilized during training would be unusually strong even at rest following Hebbian plasticity. Thus, we capture large-scale functional connectivity difference by investigating regions reflecting increased degree centrality3 to large-scale networks in the Memory Champion’s brain. 

Regions with higher centrality in the Memory Champion compared to controls (functional connectivity >95% of the HCP participants, in more than one functional network), were annotated using automated meta-analytic term maps from Neurosynth5 (Fig. 1c&d). 

To investigate whether the Memory Champion used his brain differently than controls when memorizing, we compared task activation between the Memory Champion and controls during a working memory task when using rote memorization (Fig. 2a&b). To understand the link between his training and the specific high-centrality regions and their network, we then examined how he uses his brain during his performance of the Method of Loci using challenging memorization tasks. We analyzed task activation during the use of the Method of Loci when the Memory Champion encoded and proactively recollected the order of a full deck of cards (Fig. 2c). Finally, we analyzed his brain activity when searching and when recalling a sequence of digits within the first 10,000 digits of pi, which the Memory Champion had pre-encoded (Fig. 2d).

Results

The Memory Champion exhibited focal functional connectivity differences (i.e. high centrality regions) in retrosplenial cortex, extrastriate visual areas, dorsal frontal cortex (area 55b), and caudate, involving multiple large-scale functional networks. In the cortex, high centrality regions exhibited functional similarities, i.e their functional connectivity map showed high correlation between each other compared to random regions, suggesting that they connect to the same functional circuit. Functional annotation of the cortical regions showed two groups of high centrality regions (labelled module) processing a visual/scene and a language/semantic information (Fig. 1c). In the subcortex, the caudate showed high centrality involving uniquely strong connectivity with the brain’s memory networks (Fig. 1d).

During standard rote memorization (Fig. 2a), the champion’s task fMRI patterns were typical, with the hippocampus highly active during encoding (Fig. 2b). Interestingly, when using the Method of Loci memorization, the brain pattern was different than observed in rote memorization, showing higher activity in the hippocampus during recall than encoding (Fig. 2c) and increased striatal (including caudate) activity. Cortical regions of high centrality in the Memory Champion also showed significant activity during Method of Loci memorization practice, when contrasting the steps of recalling (searching vs finding) the location of a series of digits within the first 10,000 digits of pi (Fig. 2d).

Conclusion

Thus, championship-winning memory performance bypasses classical memory circuits use in rote memorization and instead incorporates regions typically used for navigation (caudate and hippocampus during recall) and associative learning (striatum including caudate during encoding) as well as a series of regions showing higher centrality than controls and involved in scene processing, and semantic processing. Additionally, the Memory Champion wrote: "After years of using these techniques, my images boil down to a nuance that I can’t explain in writing. It’s almost like a feeling," suggesting that the training engaged brain plasticity mechanisms that have shaped the acquisition of this elite skill. These results indicate that the brain generally acquires new skills by flexibly combining specific brain regions in a novel circuit of information processing, thereby providing further insights into the roles of these regions.
