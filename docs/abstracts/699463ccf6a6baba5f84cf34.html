Introduction

Mental imagery refers to the internal generation of sensory experiences in the absence of external input and plays a central role in perception and action (Pearson et al., 2015). While visual and motor imagery have been extensively studied, tactile imagery remains comparatively underexplored, despite its close coupling to the body and sensorimotor cortex. Understanding how internally generated tactile experiences are shaped by bodily signals is therefore critical for embodied models of perception and cognition. 

Here, we combined EEG with simultaneous cardiac and respiratory recordings to investigate whether sensorimotor oscillatory activity during tactile imagery depends on respiratory and cardiac phase at task initiation. We specifically examined phase-dependent modulation of mu- and beta-band ERD and tested whether imagery onset preferentially aligns with particular physiological phases. By characterizing how bodily rhythms interact with internally generated tactile representations, this study aims to elucidate the interoceptive constraints shaping somatosensory imagery.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-six healthy right-handed adults (18–35 years; 62% female) participated after providing written informed consent. All procedures were approved by the Leipzig University ethics committee. All participants completed the experiment and were included in the analyses.

Experimental design and task

Participants performed a cued tactile imagery task involving the left hand, targeting either the index (D2) or little finger (D5). Each experimental block consisted of a stimulation mini-block followed by an imagery mini-block. During stimulation trials, tactile stimuli were delivered to the cued finger, whereas in control trials no stimulation was applied despite visually matched cues. During imagery trials, participants were instructed to internally re-experience the tactile sensation associated with the cue, without overt movement, while maintaining central fixation. At the end of each block, participants provided a subjective rating of imagery vividness.

Physiological recordings

EEG was recorded using a 64-channel NeurOne system (sampling rate: 1000 Hz). Cardiac activity was recorded using a bipolar ECG configuration, and respiration was measured using a chest belt. EEG, ECG, and respiration signals were recorded simultaneously to ensure precise temporal synchronization across modalities.

Cardiac and respiratory phase extraction

Cardiac phase was derived from the ECG signal by detecting R-peaks and T-wave offsets. Systole was defined as the interval from the R-peak to the T-wave offset, and diastole as the interval from the T-wave offset to the subsequent R-peak. Respiratory phase was extracted from the respiration signal and divided into 14 equal bins spanning the full breathing cycle.

Early expiration was defined as bins 1–4, corresponding to the transition from late inspiration to early expiration, based on prior work linking expiration to voluntary action and imagery initiation. For each trial, cardiac and respiratory phase values were sampled at cue onset, which marked the initiation of task-related processing. 

EEG preprocessing

EEG data were average-referenced and band-pass filtered between 1 and 45 Hz. Data were epoched from −4 to +7 s relative to cue onset. Independent component analysis was applied to remove ocular and muscle artifacts. To isolate induced oscillatory activity, condition-specific event-related potentials were subtracted from single trials prior to time–frequency analysis.

Time–frequency analysis and ERD calculation

Time–frequency representations were computed using Morlet wavelets across frequencies from 1 to 40 Hz. Spectral power was baseline-corrected relative to −1.5 to −0.5 s before cue onset. ERD was quantified as the percentage change in power relative to baseline, with negative values indicating power suppression.

ERD was analyzed in the mu (8–12 Hz) and beta bands, subdivided into low beta (14–20 Hz) and high beta (20–28 Hz). ERD magnitude was quantified over a −3 to +6 s window relative to cue onset. To account for physiological power fluctuations unrelated to imagery, ERD values from control trials were subtracted from active imagery trials.

Statistical analysis

Coupling between the physiological phase and the cue onset was assessed using circular statistics. Non-uniformity of respiratory and cardiac phase distributions at cue onset was tested using the Hodges–Ajne test. Statistical significance was evaluated using permutation-based surrogate distributions (500 permutations), defining chance-level phase–cue coupling. Phase analyses were performed at the group level using concatenated trial-wise phase values.

Differences in imagery-related ERD between physiological phase conditions (early expiration vs other respiratory phases; diastole vs systole) were assessed using within-subject cluster-based permutation tests (500 permutations), controlling for multiple comparisons across time. All analyses were conducted at the group level using nonparametric inference.

Results

Respiratory and cardiac phase coupling with cue onset

Respiratory phase was significantly time-locked to task cue onset, which occurred 3 s after the onset of the initial fixation period. Figure a illustrates the distribution of cue onsets across the respiratory cycle using polar histograms, where 0° (bin 1) corresponds to the onset of inspiration and 359° (bin 14) to the end of expiration.  Across all task conditions, cue onsets were non-uniformly distributed and occurred more frequently during early expiration. Importantly, participants were not informed about the exact timing of cue onset relative to fixation onset, suggesting an implicit alignment of task initiation with the early expiratory phase. In contrast, cue onset showed a weaker coupling to cardiac phase. As shown in Figure b, cue onsets were preferentially aligned with the middle-to-late portion of the cardiac cycle (0° = R-peak; 359° = subsequent R-peak). Although this pattern resembled cardiac phase preferences reported for trials exhibiting stronger ERD in motor imagery (Lai et al., 2025), the effect size was substantially smaller than that observed for respiration.

Sensorimotor ERD during tactile imagery

Tactile imagery elicited robust event-related desynchronization over sensorimotor cortex, with stronger suppression over the contralateral hemisphere relative to the ipsilateral side. Mu-band (8–12 Hz) ERD was the most pronounced and sustained across the imagery period, whereas low-beta (14–20 Hz) ERD was weaker than mu but stronger than high-beta (20–28 Hz) ERD. This spatial and spectral pattern is consistent with previous tactile imagery studies. To isolate imagery-specific oscillatory dynamics, spectral power changes in control trials were subtracted from active imagery trials, accounting for respiration- and heartbeat-related power fluctuations present during rest.

Respiratory phase modulation of imagery-related ERD

We next examined whether the preferential alignment of cue onset with early expiration was associated with changes in imagery-related neural dynamics. Trials in which the cue onset occurred during early expiration (bins 1–4) exhibited weaker contralateral ERD across mu and beta bands compared to trials occurring during other respiratory phases. Notably, ERD lateralization patterns differed between early-expiration trials and other trials across all frequency bands. Time-resolved analyses at the contralateral sensorimotor electrode (C4) revealed significant differences in ERD time courses between early-expiration trials and other respiratory phase trials, as assessed using within-subject nonparametric cluster-based permutation tests (500 permutations). Significant time windows are indicated in Figure by orange bars. The observation of weaker contralateral ERD during early-expiration trials may therefore reflect reduced neural recruitment within sensorimotor cortex to support equivalent imagery demands. 

Cardiac phase effects on imagery-related ERD

Cardiac phase at cue onset exerted a comparatively weak influence on imagery-related ERD. Trials aligned with diastole showed slightly stronger mu- and low-beta-band ERD than trials aligned with systole, whereas effects in the high-beta band were minimal. Accordingly, time-resolved analyses did not reveal significant differences in ERD dynamics between systolic and diastolic trials. This may reflect the short duration of the cardiac cycle relative to the sustained imagery period (4 s), limiting its impact on prolonged sensorimotor oscillatory activity.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that tactile mental imagery is systematically related to ongoing bodily rhythms, with respiration exerting a stronger influence on sensorimotor cortical dynamics than cardiac activity. Despite fixed external cue timing, task initiation was preferentially aligned with early expiration, indicating that the respiratory phase provides a temporal scaffold for initiating internally generated tactile representations.

Tactile imagery reliably engaged contralateral sensorimotor cortex, characterized by sustained mu-band ERD and weaker beta-band suppression. Critically, imagery trials initiated during early expiration exhibited altered ERD magnitude and lateralization compared to trials initiated during other respiratory phases, highlighting respiration-dependent modulation of imagery-related neural dynamics. In contrast, cardiac phase effects on ERD were comparatively small and did not yield robust time-resolved differences.

Together, these findings identify respiration as a possible interoceptive factor shaping tactile imagery-related sensorimotor activity and support embodied models in which internally generated sensory experiences are constrained by ongoing physiological rhythms.

Future analyses will examine associations between imagery vividness ratings and ERD magnitude, phase-dependent lateralization of ERD across respiratory and cardiac cycles, circular statistics of trial-wise ERD, and the influence of breathing and heart rate variability on sensorimotor oscillatory dynamics.
