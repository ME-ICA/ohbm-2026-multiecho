Introduction

A very preterm birth is associated with an increased risk of language delays later in life. The final trimester of pregnancy is a critical period for brain development, with the developing brain being shaped by early sensory experiences. Speech perception differs between the intrauterine space and the outside environment. In the womb, language is low-pass filtered and the fetus can only perceive the prosodic features of its native language. After birth, in an unfiltered environment, also the learning of fine-grained phonotactic regularities (i.e., the combinations of phonemes in a given language) is possible. Very preterm infants are exposed to the full frequency range of language earlier in life than term-born infants, but it is still unclear whether their immature brains are capable of processing fast phonotactic information. In this study, we investigate whether an early language intervention affects language processing in very preterm infants. We focus on brain processing mechanisms of language by measuring the vascular responses to pseudowords corresponding to native and non-native linguistic rules at three months of term-equivalent age.

Methods

In total, fifty-one newborn infants born before the 32nd week of gestation (range: 24 + 6 to 31 + 6 weeks) were recruited for this study. All infants met the following inclusion criteria: raised with German as their native language, no contact to Romance languages or Slavic languages, normal auditory evaluation measured by otoacoustic emissions, normal language development in both parents and no clinical signs of neurologic disorders. 

We randomly assigned the infants into either the intervention group (n=27) or the control group (n=24). The infants in the intervention group received an additional hour of language stimulation in the form of German-language children's audiobooks played through a loudspeaker twice a day. The intervention started as soon as the parents provided written consent and no later than one week after the infant's birth and stopped once the 35-week gestation period was completed. The timing of the intervention was chosen so as not to interfere with parental presence. The control group received standard care without additional auditory exposure.

We assessed neural processing associated with the ability to discriminate between native (German) and non-native prosody/stress pattern (French) and phonotactics (Slovak) in both groups at three months of term-equivalent age. Therefore, the infants took part in an experiment involving 200 acoustically presented pseudowords, which differed in terms of their linguistic properties. The topographical localisation of the underlying brain activations was measured by functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS).

Results

Preliminary fNIRS results revealed a widespread increased activation for non-native compared to native phonotactics for the control group while the intervention group showed the reversed pattern (i.e., a larger activation for native versus non-native phonotactics). 

We found no significant differences in brain activation between native and non-native stress patterns.

Conclusion

Our data suggest that very preterm born infants have already achieved an important step in language learning by three months of corrected age. The results showed that very preterm infants in both groups could distinguish between native and non-native phonotactic rules. However, different discrimination patterns were observed between the intervention and control groups.

While infants in the intervention group showed greater activation in response to native phonotactics compared to non-native rules, infants in the control group showed greater activation predominantly in response to stimuli with non-native phonotactics. 

An orientation towards native rules can be interpreted as a more mature neural commitment. To better understand the impact of additional language exposure on our cohort of very preterm infants, we will also track their behavioral language outcomes at 24 months of term-equivalent age and in future analyses. 

A better understanding of the effects of early language exposure on very preterm infants could optimize the auditory environment in neonatal intensive care units, potentially reducing the risk of language deficits.
