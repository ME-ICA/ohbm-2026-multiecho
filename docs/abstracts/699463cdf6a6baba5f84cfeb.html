Introduction

Distracted eating has been linked to higher food intake, unhealthier food preferences, and a greater risk of overweight and obesity in adolescents1. These findings indicate that external visual stimulation systematically alters eating behavior. While behavioral studies suggest that attentional load can influence taste-related processing2, the neural mechanisms underlying this effect remain insufficiently understood. In particular, it is unclear how visual distraction affects taste perception and the functional connectivity (FC) within brain networks involved in gustatory processing and attentional control3,4.

The aim of this study was to examine how visual distraction alters FC between key taste- and attention-related regions, including the anterior insula, thalamus, anterior cingulate cortex (AC), and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). A second aim was to assess whether the influence of distraction on FC differs as a function of taste type (sweet vs umami) or taste intensity.

Methods

Thirty-eight volunteers completed a taste-video task during a 3T functional MRI (fMRI) scan. Participants were shown two distracting level videos during their scanning session. High distracting videos consisted of fast and visually dense action scenes, silent films, roller-coaster and cartoon videos. Low-distraction videos consisted of basic food clips that matched the tastant presented at that moment. During each video, participants were being delivered sweet (sucrose) and umami (monosodium glutamate) taste solutions in two concentration levels using a gustatometer. FC analysis was performed with predefined regions-of-interest (ROIs) covering salience, dorsal attention, default mode networks, as well as taste related regions. ROI-to-ROI connectivity was computed using the standard CONN settings with Family Wise Error (FWE) correction5.

Results

Visual distraction affected functional connectivity across several taste-related and attention-related regions. Overall, distraction was associated with reduced connectivity between the right inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and multiple regions involved in taste processing, including the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), anterior insula, anterior cingulate cortex (AC), and the left IFG. Reduced coupling was also observed between the right thalamus and both the AC and left anterior insula. In contrast, increased connectivity emerged within frontal control regions, most notably between the right IFG and right frontal eye field (FEF), as well as between the left OFC and AC.

Within the high-intensity condition, distraction showed a comparable pattern. Connectivity between the right IFG and the left IFG, left OFC, and left anterior insula was reduced, while increased coupling was observed between the right IFG and right FEF and between the left OFC and right anterior insula. In the low-intensity condition, distraction effects were more restricted and primarily involved interactions between taste and attentional regions, with reduced connectivity between the right anterior insula and bilateral FEF, as well as decreased coupling between the AC and right thalamus.

Taste-specific analyses indicated a stronger modulation for sweet compared to umami. During sweet trials, distraction was associated with reduced connectivity between the right IFG and both the left OFC and anterior insula. Additional decreases were observed between the left FEF and right anterior insula and between the right thalamus and both the AC and right anterior insula. An increase in connectivity was observed only between the medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC) and the right anterior insula. In contrast, during umami trials, distraction led to reduced connectivity between the left OFC and right IFG.

The interaction between taste and distraction revealed a decrease in connectivity between the right anterior insula and the left FEF, indicating a taste-dependent modulation of insula–attention coupling.

Conclusion

Distraction reduced FC within the taste-salience pathway, especially between insular, OFC, and AC regions. FC increased during distraction, mainly between frontal attention areas. Effects were more pronounced for sweet tastes. These findings show that distraction shifts neural resources away from taste processing and toward attentional control, which helps explain changes in taste perception when eating while watching visual media. This provides a neural explanation for reduced taste awareness and altered flavour perception during eating in visually engaging environments.
