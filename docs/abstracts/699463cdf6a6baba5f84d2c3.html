Introduction

Neurocognitive systems are shaped by ongoing experience, adapting structurally and functionally to the environment and skills individuals engage with. Bilingualism is an example of such experience-based plasticity, as managing multiple languages entails different cognitive demands and involves diverse language related factors, such as proficiency or frequency of use. However, research on bilingualism has often reduced this phenomenon to categorical contrasts between “monolinguals” and “bilinguals,” overlooking the dimensionality of multilingual experience.

The Adaptive Control Hypothesis (ACH)1 proposes that different interactional contexts impose demands to which individuals need to adjust. To capture this fluctuating language demands, it has been proposed2 the use of language entropy, a measure of the uncertainty bilinguals have to face in their daily interactions, which may engage domain-general control systems. For instance, previous research has shown that functional connectivity patterns in networks supporting language and executive control relates to language entropy. 3 

Yet, little research has examined how this measure relates to structural adaptations. A feature sensitive to experience modulation is cortical thickness, whose changes across bilingual experience has been previously described.4 

Therefore, we aimed to explore the relationship between language entropy and cortical thickness in a multilingual sample.

Methods

Dataset included the scans of 168 healthy right-handed participants (mean age = 22.16 ± 2.68; 101 female) who completed an adapted version of the Language and Social Background Questionnaire. This questionnaire included items recording self-rated proficiency as well as the use percentage for each language in a specific context (home, school, work, with family, etc.). We calculated language entropy, derived from the proportion of use of each language in each context, with higher values indicating more balanced multilingual use and thus greater linguistic complexity and uncertainty. The global entropy score was computed as the average of the entropy estimates from each individual context in adulthood. To account for an additional multilingual factor, we also calculated an average self-rated proficiency score across all languages known. 

T1-weighted structural MRI data were collected using a 3T MRI scanner and preprocessing was performed using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12) using the default parameters. Surface-based morphometric measures were then calculated, applying spatially smoothing Gaussian kernel (12 mm FWHM). Statistical model included global language entropy and averaged proficiency and nuisance effects were controlled for sex, age, and fluid intelligence (measured with matrix reasoning test). Statistical inference used threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) with family-wise error correction (p < .05, two-tailed) and 10,000 permutations. Surface regions were labeled using the Desikan–Killiany (DK40) atlas.

Results

No significant associations were found for the proficiency measure. However, language entropy was positively associated with cortical thickness in several clusters extending from frontal to occipital regions in the right hemisphere (Figure 1).

Five significant clusters emerged. The largest cluster was located in posterior temporoparietal regions (MNI peak at 61, -28, 31; TFCE = 310811.69; pFWE = 0.019), including the supramarginal, inferior parietal, and lateral occipital cortices, with additional contributions from superior, middle and inferior temporal areas. A second large cluster was primarily situated in the frontal cortex (MNI peak at 40, 43, 17; TFCE = 31009.77; pFWE = 0.018), encompassing the lateral orbitofrontal, superior and rostral middle frontal cortex, pars triangularis, and pars orbitalis regions. Medial frontoparietal structures contributed to a third cluster (MNI peak at 11, –38, 55; TFCE= 28475.51; pFWE = 0.022), involving the paracentral lobule, posterior cingulate cortex, and precuneus.

A smaller cluster on frontal cortex (MNI peak at 23, 14, 48; TFCE = 27578.22; pFWE = 0.023), that comprises the superior frontal cortex along with the rostral and caudal middle frontal gyri, was identified. The last cluster was mainly situated in the precentral gyrus (MNI peak at 12, –21, 75; TFCE = 27403.92; pFWE = 0.024), with additional involvement of the paracentral gyrus and superior frontal region.

Conclusion

The finding suggests that exposure to greater uncertainty in communicative contexts is associated with increased cortical thickness in regions related to cognitive processing. This aligns with the ACH framework, which posits that neural systems adapt to the control demands imposed by communicative contexts.

Several of the zones identified, like right frontal and temporoparietal regions and posterior cingulate cortex, have previously been linked to inhibition5 and bottom-up attentional processes¹, supporting that engaging in dynamic language contexts recruits cognitive control networks. Additionally, the observed right lateralization pattern may suggest that language entropy engages mechanisms associated more with cognitive control than with language-specific processing.
