Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a clinically heterogenous disease manifesting a variety of motor and non-motor symptoms, including psychiatric symptoms such as hallucinations. Hallucinations in PD are common, affecting around 50 % of patients, and they range from minor, including illusions, presence and passage hallucinations, to structured mostly visual hallucinations. Patients may experience a single form or any combination of different hallucination types, with substantial variability in their severity and frequency. While minor hallucinations usually emerge early in the disease, sometimes even preceding motor symptoms1, structured hallucinations appear in later stages2. Both categories of hallucinations are associated with more rapid cognitive decline3. As minor hallucinations are typically considered a precursor to structured hallucinations, it is crucial to understand their neural basis. Notably, patients who experience a greater variety of minor hallucination types are more likely to develop structured visual hallucinations, other psychiatric symptoms, and more rapid cognitive decline4. Along with the fact that hallucinations are highly subjective experiences, this underscores the importance of studying individual variability of the neural underpinnings of hallucinations. Here, we investigate how individual variability in functional connectivity (FC) profiles relates to the heterogeneity of minor hallucinations in PD. Using two fMRI datasets of PD patients without hallucinations (nH) and those with minor hallucinations (MH), we identify large-scale functional network coupling associated with individual variability in hallucinations and their severity. We further validate the results in a cohort of PD patients with both minor and structured hallucinations (SMH) and in an additional dataset including healthy controls (HC) and PD patients with structured visual hallucinations (VH).

Methods

Our main analyses were conducted on two datasets. The discovery dataset (total N = 32; MH n = 18; nH n = 14) was used to identify pairs of networks that exhibited higher individual variability in the MH group. The validation dataset (total N = 55) included three patient groups: MH (n = 19), nH (n = 19), and SMH (n = 17) for further validation. In the validation dataset, we tested whether individual variability in the identified network pairs was associated with severity of hallucinations, and we further evaluated and validated the FC-based individual variability in the patient groups. In both datasets, all patients underwent 8 min of resting-state fMRI. Additionally, in the validation dataset patients completed the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS). An additional dataset including HC (N = 31) and VH (N = 48) was used to further assess the network-pair variability trends. All data were preprocessed with the same pipeline and parcellated into 278 regions, which were then assigned to large-scale functional networks with additional subcortical and cerebellar areas as in ref.5. Whole-brain FC was estimated using Pearson’s r. 

To evaluate individual variability in FC, we performed a variation of an inter-subject representational similarity analysis (IS-RSA), in which we compared patterns of FC in each network pair between each pair of individuals using a correlation distance, obtaining a representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM) in each network pair (Fig. 1). Finally, in each network pair we calculated the mean of the RDM and regressed out the effects of the network pair size. In the discovery dataset, to enable comparison between the patient groups and to evaluate the stability of the group differences, we repeatedly subsampled the MH group to 14 subjects and performed the IS-RSA in each subsample. To find network pairs that were significantly more variable in the MH group, we finally compared the RDM means in the nH group with the distributions of means of the subsampled data in the MH group in each network pair. Using the validation dataset, we then tested whether individual variability in FC patterns in the identified network pairs was associated with individual variability in the hallucination subscore of the SAPS, which was calculated as the Euclidean distance between each pair of subjects. We quantified this relationship by Spearman’s ρ and performed a Mantel test to evaluate statistical significance. This analysis was first performed in the MH group, and then the result was validated in the SMH group. To further explore which edges contributed the most to this relationship, we ran the same analysis in the MH group, iteratively excluding an edge at a time and evaluating the changes in Spearman’s ρ.

We further validated the trends in the FC-based individual variability by repeatedly subsampling each group to 14 to match the size of the discovery dataset and comparing the distributions of the MH and SMH groups to the mean of the nH group. Finally, we compared the mean individual variability in the network pair of the various PD subgroups with HC.

Results

The discovery dataset revealed nine network pairs which were significantly more variable in the MH group (Fig. 2a). After testing in the validation set in the MH group, the coupling between visual (VN) and subcortical (SBC) networks was significantly associated with the SAPS hallucination subscore (ρ = 0.41; pFDR = 0.031; Fig. 2b). This association was also strong in the SMH group (ρ = 0.22, pMantel = 0.075), further supporting the relationship between individual variability in VN-SBC coupling and hallucination severity. An exploratory visualization of the top 2 % of edges with the highest contribution to the association suggested that while the relevant nodes in the VN were widely spread across the network, in the SBC they seemed constrained to only a few nodes in the hippocampus, brainstem and basal ganglia (Fig. 2c). The trend of higher individual variability in VN-SBC coupling in the MH group was also observed in the validation dataset in both MH and SMH groups as well as in the additional VH group, all of which showed higher variability compared to the HC group (Fig. 2d), providing further converging evidence.

Conclusion

This study gathers evidence from 3 independent datasets (total patients N = 135) suggesting that VN-SBC coupling exhibits higher individual variability in PD patients with hallucinations than in those without, and this variability is associated with hallucination severity. These findings provide further insights into neural mechanisms and individual differences underlying hallucinations in PD and help build the groundwork for more personalized clinical approaches.
