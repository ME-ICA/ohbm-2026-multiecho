Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) involves widespread structural and functional alterations in brain networks, which can be captured non-invasively using multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and connectomics, enabling simultaneous assessment of white-matter integrity and spontaneous functional activity. By constructing whole-brain connectivity maps, connectomics provides tools to analyze network topology and derive candidate biomarkers that may complement more invasive diagnostic approaches. Large multicenter datasets are essential for generalizable biomarkers but introduce substantial variability across scanners, protocols, and sites, which can obscure disease-related effects and bias machine-learning models. Moreover, analyzing single modalities in isolation risks overlooking how structural damage and functional disruption interact across the AD continuum. To address these challenges, this study combines statistical harmonization with multilayer network modeling to jointly represent structural and functional connectivity, with the goal of providing a unified view of network reorganization and identifying sensitive, interpretable biomarkers for AD classification.

Methods

Data were obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)1 and included N=354 participants. The analysis comprised cognitively normal (CN) amyloid-negative controls (N=190), while the mild cognitive impairment (MCI, N=105) and AD (N=59) groups were restricted to amyloid-positive individuals to ensure etiological homogeneity and isolate brain changes specific to the AD pathological continuum. Structural T1-weighted MRI, diffusion MRI (dMRI), and resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) underwent standardized preprocessing pipelines, and cortical and subcortical regions were defined using the Schaefer-400 atlas for the cortex and the Tian-S1 atlas for subcortical regions to ensure a common parcellation across modalities. For each subject, structural connectivity (SC) was estimated as weighted streamline counts between regions, and functional connectivity (FC) as Fisher z-transformed Pearson correlations between regional rs-fMRI time series. To mitigate acquisition-related variability while preserving biological effects, SC and FC edge weights were harmonized using ComBat and CovBat,2 with batch labels defined by scanner vendor, model, and protocol, and covariates including age, sex, and diagnosis. Harmonized SC and FC matrices were then used to build multilayer (ML) networks3 with intra-layer edges encoding single-modality connectivity, and inter-layer links connecting each region across modalities (Figure 1). Subject-level global and nodal graph measures were extracted from single-layer and ML networks.4 Two classification tasks were then defined, contrasting CN subjects against each pathological group (CN vs. MCI and CN vs. AD) to specifically evaluate the framework's sensitivity to deviations from healthy connectivity at different stages of the disease trajectory. To perform these classifications, graph measures were first dimensionality-reduced via partial least squares (PLS) and then used to train multiple supervised models. Performance was assessed using balanced accuracy in a nested cross-validation scheme, while PLS coefficients were analyzed to derive region-level importance scores.

Results

Harmonization consistently improved classification performance, with CovBat generally yielding the largest gains relative to no harmonization and to standard ComBat, as described in Figure 2.a. Across all pairwise contrasts, ML connectivity features systematically matched or outperformed single-modality SC and FC representations, indicating that jointly modeling modalities captures additional disease-relevant information. For the CN  vs. AD  comparison, ML features achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.895 ± 0.042 after CovBat, compared with 0.787 ± 0.061 without harmonization, representing the largest observed gain. In prodromal stage contrasts, improvements were more modest but robust, with balanced accuracy increasing after harmonization and peaking for ML networks. Region-level importance maps showed that SC and FC highlighted partially distinct sets of discriminative nodes, with FC emphasizing somatomotor, default-mode, control and dorsal-attention regions, and SC additionally involving subcortical areas. In the ML framework, these patterns converged, revealing additional areas, particularly hippocampal, thalamic, posterior midline, and somatomotor regions, that were not fully captured in single-layer analyses and that reflected a progressively more widespread and multimodal network disruption as AD advanced (Figure 2.b).

Conclusion

Combining connectivity harmonization with a ML network framework yields robust, non-invasive classifiers along the AD continuum, with particularly strong performance for dementia-stage contrasts and clear, though smaller, gains in prodromal stage. Beyond improving accuracy, the ML approach provides interpretable importance maps that reveal convergent vulnerability in hippocampal, thalamic, and default-mode regions which are only partially detected when structural and functional modalities are considered separately. These harmonized multimodal metrics therefore emerge as promising candidates to complement, and in selected scenarios potentially reduce reliance on, invasive biomarkers. Nonetheless, the current findings are derived from an imbalanced sample within a single multicenter initiative, underscoring the need for replication in larger, more diverse, and better balanced cohorts before clinical translation. In that context, such network-based biomarkers could support earlier, stratified characterization of individuals at risk for AD and aid in the design and monitoring of targeted interventions.
