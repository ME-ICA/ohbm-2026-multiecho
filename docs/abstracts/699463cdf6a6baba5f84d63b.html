Introduction

Long-duration space flight exposes humans to extreme environmental stressors, most notably microgravity, but also space radiation, isolation, circadian disruption, and impaired sleep.¹ In response, the brain adapts its structure and function to these novel demands through neuroplasticity.2 Such adaptations may support performance in space, yet they may also introduce challenges as astronauts transition back to Earth. As upcoming missions target longer stays on the Moon and Mars, understanding how the brain adjusts to and recovers from these environments is critical for ensuring astronaut health and mission success.

Neuroimaging studies have revealed widespread structural remodeling following long-duration spaceflight, including redistribution of cerebrospinal fluid, upward brain shift, expansion of the ventricles, grey matter volume, and white matter microstructural alterations.3 Functional MRI investigations also show altered brain activity and connectivity, such as decreases in intrinsic connectivity within vestibular and sensorimotor regions, and persistent changes in areas like the posterior cingulate cortex, thalamus, and angular gyrus in response to the altered sensorimotor environment and conflicting sensory inputs in microgravity.4

Despite growing evidence, structural and functional changes have largely been examined separately, leaving their interdependence insufficiently understood. A key question remains: how do structural alterations constrain functional organization during adaptation to spaceflight? Addressing this gap is essential for a comprehensive understanding of neuroplasticity in space. Here, we adopt a network-based perspective to examine how long-duration spaceflight alters structural and functional connectivity and their coupling. Using graph theory metrics and the graph signal processing framework,5 we assess changes in brain network topology and the coupling of functional signals with the underlying structural architecture.

Methods

Participants. Twenty male cosmonauts (age = 44 ± 5.5 years) who completed long-duration missions to the ISS (212.8 ± 77.8 days) were included. Resting-state fMRI, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and structural MRI were acquired before launch (pre-flight) and shortly after return (post-flight; 9.35±2.28 days). The relevant ethical committees approved all procedures, and written informed consent was obtained.

Preprocessing. T1-weighted data were bias-corrected and processed using FreeSurfer’s recon-all. DWI preprocessing was performed in MRtrix3 (denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, motion and distortion correction, bias field correction). Diffusion data were registered to structural images with gradient reorientation. Response functions were estimated using the Dhollander algorithm, and fiber orientation distributions (FODs) were computed using multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution, followed by log-domain intensity normalization. Whole-brain tractograms (10 million streamlines per session) were generated using anatomically constrained tractography and second-order integration over FODs, with streamline weights optimized to reduce reconstruction bias. The Schaefer-100 atlas was registered to native space, and structural connectivity (SC) matrices were built by summing streamline weights between each regional pair. Resting-state fMRI preprocessing used an in-house pipeline based on SPM12. After slice-timing and motion correction, data were registered to T1 images and spatially smoothed (6 mm FWHM). Temporal denoising was performed with CONN (GLM-based nuisance regression including motion parameters and derivatives, WM/CSF PCA components, linear trends, and outlier frames with FD > 3 mm or GS z-score > 3). Data were band-pass filtered (0.008–0.09 Hz). The Schaefer-100 atlas was applied to extract mean BOLD signals (Figure 1A).

Structural Network Analysis. Mass-univariate edge-wise comparisons were performed to detect localized SC alterations after spaceflight. Whole-brain and nodal graph metrics (global efficiency, characteristic path length, transitivity, modularity, clustering coefficient, betweenness, and closeness centrality) were computed across densities 0.05–0.5 (steps: 0.025). The area under the curve across densities was used as the metric value. 

Functional Network Analysis. Functional connectivity (FC) matrices were computed using Pearson correlations and Fisher-z transformed. Edge-wise analyses assessed connectivity changes post-flight. The same graph-theoretical metrics as above were estimated across FC network densities 0.25–0.8 (steps: 0.025). Area under the curve values represented each metric.

Structure-Function Coupling. We quantified the degree of alignment between functional activity and underlying structural architecture using the Structural Decoupling Index (SDI)5 within a graph signal processing framework. Functional signals were projected onto the structural connectivity normalized Laplacian eigenmodes; SDI values captured the balance between structurally aligned and structure-independent fluctuations.

Statistical Analysis. Linear mixed-effects models were used for all comparisons, with time (pre- vs. post-flight) as a fixed effect and subjects as random effects. Where applicable, multiple comparisons were controlled using FDR correction (α = 0.05).

Results

SC reorganization after spaceflight reflects sensory reweighting and reduced global integration. The SC analysis revealed a heterogeneous pattern of post-flight alterations across large-scale brain networks. Edgewise testing showed decreases in SC weights linking mainly the sensorimotor network to the executive control network and other cortical systems. In parallel, we also observed increases in SC weights connecting primarily visual and salience networks to the rest of the brain (Figure 1B). At the global topological level, characteristic path length increased significantly (β = 27.96, t = 2.95, p = 0.003), indicating reduced global integration (Figure 1B). Complementing this, a localized increase in clustering coefficient was detected in the visual network (β = 0.003, t = 3.49, p = 0.048), pointing to strengthened local visual subnetworks (Figure 1B). 

FC alterations highlight reduced sensorimotor integration and increased default mode network coupling after spaceflight. The FC analysis revealed a network-specific pattern of post-flight reorganization, highlighting distinct functional adjustments to microgravity. Edgewise comparisons showed a significant decrease in FC within the somatomotor network regions, as well as reduced coupling between somatomotor areas and both the visual and dorsal attention (DAN) networks. In contrast, we observed increased FC within the default mode network (DMN) and strengthened connectivity between the DMN and other large-scale networks (Figure 1C). Regarding the graph-theoretical metrics, no significant alterations were detected in global or nodal graph-theoretical metrics.

Spaceflight selectively alters regional structure–function coupling in attention and default mode networks. The SDI analysis revealed distinct patterns of regional decoupling and coupling after spaceflight. Two regions within the dorsal attention network exhibited significant increases in SDI, indicating greater structure–function decoupling post-flight. Specifically, SDI increased in the Schaefer region R68 (DAN_post2; β = 0.142, t = 3.200, p = 0.0458) and R73 (DAN_FEF1; β = 0.186, t = 3.515, p = 0.0220). In contrast, SDI decreased significantly in the Schaefer region R90 (DMN_par1; β = –0.193, t = –3.567, p = 0.0220), indicating stronger structure–function coupling in a default mode network region (Figure 1D). 

Conclusion

Integrating structural connectivity, functional connectivity, and structure–function dependency analyses in a network-based framework reveals a coherent, multimodal pattern of neuroplastic adaptation associated with spaceflight and re-adaptation to Earth’s gravity. 

SC results showed reduced connectivity in sensorimotor and control networks, consistent with reduced reliance on proprioceptive and motor planning pathways due to microgravity. In addition, increased connectivity of the visual and salience networks suggests enhanced structural support for visual processing and multisensory integration in response to diminished vestibular and somatosensory reliability in microgravity. In a network view, increased characteristic path length indicates a shift toward more segregated information processing strategies after spaceflight. This was accompanied by a localized increase in visual-network clustering, suggesting reinforcement of local visual processing circuits. Together, these findings suggest that spaceflight induces a reorganization of structural connectivity characterized by downregulation of sensorimotor pathways and upregulation of visual and salience circuits, consistent with sensory reweighting and adaptive recalibration in the altered gravitational environment.

Regarding the FC analysis, we observed decreased FC within the somatomotor network and between somatomotor, visual, and dorsal attention networks, consistent with disrupted sensorimotor integration and visuomotor coordination upon return to gravity. At the same time, FC increased within and from the default mode network, pointing to heightened internally oriented processing or compensatory recruitment of higher-order integrative systems during early re-adaptation to gravity. Together, these findings point to a functional shift characterized by reduced somatomotor integration and increased DMN coupling, reflecting disrupted sensorimotor processing and altered cognitive dynamics during the transition back to Earth’s gravitational environment.

Structural decoupling index analysis results further clarified the reorganization of structure–function relationships: dorsal attention regions showed increased SDI, reflecting greater structure–function decoupling and heightened functional flexibility in attentional control circuits. These increases suggest that dorsal attention regions become functionally more flexible and less constrained by their underlying white-matter architecture, possibly reflecting increased demands for visuospatial updating, sensorimotor conflict resolution, and top-down attentional control during spaceflight and early re-adaptation to gravity. Further, a default-mode region showed decreased SDI, indicating stronger structure–function coupling. This result suggests that the default-mode processing becomes increasingly grounded in its structural connectivity, aligning with the observed FC increases within the DMN. Together, SDI analysis results highlight network-specific recalibration of structure–function relationships, with greater functional flexibility in attention systems and greater structural anchoring in a DMN region following spaceflight.

Together, these converging findings show that spaceflight induces a coordinated redistribution of neural resources characterized by down-regulation of sensorimotor pathways, strengthening of visual and salience processing systems, increased functional reliance on the DMN, and flexible decoupling within attention networks. This pattern aligns with a broader framework of sensory reweighting, wherein visual, attentional, and interoceptive systems compensate for reduced vestibular and proprioceptive reliability in microgravity. Taken as a whole, the analysis of structural and functional networks and their relationships describes a brain that is both reconfiguring its structural backbone and adapting its functional dynamics to meet the challenges of reorientation and motor recalibration upon return to Earth.
