Introduction

Effective connectivity (EC) studies have revealed a hierarchical organization of information flow across the cortex, with primary sensory and motor areas driving activity toward higher-order association regions1. A similar pattern is observed in structure-function coupling (SFC), where primary areas show stronger alignment between structural and functional connectivity than association areas2. This convergence raises a fundamental question: to what extent can the brain’s structural architecture shape the directional interactions observed in EC? 

Traditionally, SFC has been studied as the correlation between structural and functional connectivity matrices2. However, a graph signal processing perspective provides a more mechanistic view: functional activity in a region depends on its underlying structural connectivity, independent of functional correlations. To capture this dependency, the structural-decoupling index (SDI) can be used, which inversely quantifies how strongly BOLD signals are constrained by anatomy3.  

By comparing SDI with EC, our aim is to assess whether the directional interactions of cortical regions are influenced by the extent to which its BOLD signal depends on underlying structural connectivity, providing a direct and interpretable link between anatomy and the directional flow of brain activity. 

Methods

A total of 1,163 healthy middle-to-older-aged adults from the Barcelona Brain Health Initiative4 and the Barcelona Brain Stimularion Lab5 underwent MRI on a 3T Siemens Prisma with a 32-channel head coil, including T1- and T2-weighted structural imaging (0.8 mm isotropic), 10-min resting-state fMRI (TR = 800 ms, 2 mm isotropic, 750 volumes), and multi-shell DWI (99 directions; b ≈ 5/1500/3000 s/mm²; 1.5 mm isotropic)4. rs-fMRI preprocessing included discarding the first 10 volumes to ensure magnetization equilibrium, field inhomogeneity correction, motion realignment, co-registration to T1 space, regression of motion parameters, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals, and low-frequency drifts, band-pass filtering (0.04–0.07 Hz), interpolation of volumes with framewise displacement >0.5 mm (scrubbing), and exclusion of subjects with >50% interpolated volumes. DWI data were corrected for eddy currents and susceptibility distortions and processed using multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution and tractography (MRtrix3). 

Cortical and subcortical parcellation used the HCP MMP 360 atlas plus 14 subcortical ROIs (7 per hemisphere from FreeSurfer aseg), yielding 374 ROIs. Structural connectivity (SC) matrices were constructed as the number of streamlines between ROIs normalized by ROI size. BOLD time series were extracted by averaging voxel signals within each ROI. 

Within a graph signal processing framework, a consensus SC Laplacian was decomposed into network harmonics. BOLD signals were projected onto these harmonics to compute the SDI for each ROI, defined as the ratio of energy in low-frequency (aligned) versus high-frequency (decoupled) harmonics using the 50% cumulative energy cutoff3. 

EC was estimated from individual BOLD time series using normalized directed transfer entropy (NDTE), a measure of directed information flow between brain regions, yielding incoming and outgoing flow, and the balance between them (net NDTE), per ROI1. This method is based on an information-theoretical measure quantifying the influence of the past activity of one brain region on the future activity of another, using a fixed time lag (of 10 volumes in this study). NDTE allows bidirectional analysis and is normalized to enable comparisons across regions. 

For each ROI, SDI was correlated with net NDTE (outgoing minus incoming), total incoming and total outgoing, to assess how alignment to the structural network modulates directed information flow across the brain, providing a direct measure of the influence of structural architecture on effective connectivity. Bonferroni correction was applied. 

Results

The final sample included 857 subjects aged from 40 to 82 (median: 55.26), of whom 460 were women (53.68%).  

SDI and NDTE maps exhibited spatial patterns consistent with established gradients, with primary regions showing higher net incoming flow and lower SDI values, and higher-order association areas showing the opposite profile.  

ROI-wise correlations between SDI and NDTE revealed substantial regional variability. For incoming flow, SDI and NDTE showed a widespread negative association across cortex and subcortex. No association was found in primary sensory areas, the thalamus, the angular gyri, the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, the fusiform cortex, and the entorhinal cortex. For outgoing flow, a similarly broad negative association emerged. Regions showing no association included the ventromedial prefrontal and medial orbitofrontal cortices, anterior and inferior temporal regions, the fusiform cortex, the amygdalae, and sparse patches within the cuneus. Across both measures, regions with significant negative associations indicate higher directed information flow when the functional signal is less dependent on the structural connectome (i.e., lower SDI). The absence of association in specific regions may indicate that their directed interactions rely on mechanisms that are less constrained by structural–functional alignment. 

Regarding net NDTE—the difference between outgoing and incoming flow—negative SDI–NDTE associations tended to appear in driver regions (outgoing > incoming), whereas positive associations appeared in receiver regions (incoming > outgoing). Regions with no association included the thalamus (a receiver) and territories without a clearly defined driving/receiving bias (outgoing ≈ incoming). This pattern suggests that greater structural independence of the BOLD signal (lower SDI) is associated with a stronger “driver” role and a weaker “receiver” role, respectively, in directed information flow.

Conclusion

We conclude that the hierarchical functional organization of the cortex is influenced by structural connectivity. Information flow is generally enhanced when a region’s BOLD signal is less constrained by structural connections (i.e., more flexible), which in turn shapes its functional role in the cortical hierarchy, determining whether it acts primarily as a driver or a receiver of information. Notably, regions without a strong driver or receiver bias are influenced in both directions, so increased independence does not change their functional role. In contrast, regions at the extremes of the hierarchy show directional associations only in one flow—either outgoing or incoming—reinforcing their role as drivers or receivers, respectively.
