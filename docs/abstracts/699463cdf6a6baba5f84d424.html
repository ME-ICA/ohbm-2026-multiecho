Introduction

Alexithymia is widely recognized as the inability to identify and express emotions, a construct consisting of four cognitive traits: difficulty in identifying feelings, describing feelings to others, externally oriented thinking, and limited imaginative capacity. Several studies have linked alexithymia to cognitive functioning, observing greater alexithymia scores associated with poorer cognitive abilities [1]. Very few investigations have studied alexithymia in Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), showing that alexithymia scores were elevated in AD patients [2]. Reduced volumes of key regions of the Salience Network (SN; insula, amygdala, orbital frontal cortex, striatum) have been found in people with high levels of alexithymia [3]. Despite alexithymia is also considered as a form of reduced insight into personal emotion, studies specifically focusing on awareness are lacking. In the present study, we evaluated alexithymia and the awareness of emotional deficits in patients with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) and AD, and tested the involvement of SN in the insight into personal emotion.

Methods

Fifty-seven patients (mean age ± standard deviation, 75.3±6.8 years) with MCI and AD dementia underwent fMRI and neuropsychological assessment including the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20) [4]. The latter was administered to both patients and caregiver’s and discrepancy score between the patient’s and the caregiver’s judgments was estimated as a measure of reduced insight into emotional self-awareness. Descriptive statistics of demographic, neuropsychological and clinical measures were calculated. Functional data were acquired with a 3T scanner and an EPI sequence (TR=2000 ms; echo time, TE, 28ms; flip angle 89°; field of view 192mm; 180 volumes; 3x3x3.5 mm voxels; acquisition time 6:04min). fMRI preprocessing and analysis were performed using FSL tools. After having applied Independent Component Analysis using MELODIC, functional data were correlated with TAS-20 discrepancy sub-scores (difficulty in identifying emotion, DIF, difficulty in describing feelings, DDF, externally oriented feelings, EOT) within the SN. Age, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores, and grey matter volumes were included as regressors of no interest in all the GLMs. Cluster-based thresholding was used to control for whole brain voxel-wise multiple comparisons, and FWE-corrected significance threshold of p<0.05 was applied to the suprathreshold clusters. 

Results

TAS-20 patient’s total score showed that 7 patients were alexithymic (total score >60), 18 had possible alexithymia (scores between 51 and 60), 32 were not alexithymic (total score <51). On average, patient DIF score was 12.9±5.2, DDF was 12.4±3.9, EOT was 20.3±4.4, whereas caregiver’s scores were as follows: DIF = 11.8±5.1; DDF = 12.2±4.0; EOT = 22.6±5.2. The discrepancy between caregiver’s and patient’s scores was also calculated for DIF (-1.1±6.1), DDF (-0.2±4.6) and EOT (2.3±5.4) subscale and total score (1.0±14.1). On neural level, DIF discrepancy score negatively correlated with intrinsic functional connectivity within the SN in the right posterior insula, bilateral thalamus, bilateral putamen, right caudate nucleus, irrespective of cognitive impairment stage, age, and gray matter volume (Figure 1).

Conclusion

Our behavioral results support previous findings about alexithymia in AD patients, and suggest that it could be associated to lower emotional self-awareness. Reduced insight into emotional identification deficits in MCI and AD is associated with decreased intrinsic functional connectivity within key SN nodes, including the posterior insula and basal ganglia. These regions anchor the interoceptive system that transforms bodily signals into conscious feeling states. Their reduced functional integration may therefore constitute the neural mechanism linking neurodegeneration to alexithymia and impaired emotional self-awareness.
