Introduction

Neurons are very active and so is the vasculature. A close relationship between neurons and vascular changes has enabled hemodynamic imaging studies of human brain function, especially in studies where subjects are actively performing a task [1-5]. However, this relationship can be variable during a imaging experiments and even more so during resting-state studies where no overt task or stimulus is delivered [5,6]. This motivates the notion that differences in neuro-vascular responses may result from variations in modulatory input and/or differences in the participating neuronal population [7]. 

To examine differences in neuro-vascular responses, we acquired excitatory and inhibitory neuronal activity data as well as vascular changes from the brains of awake head-fixed rodents. We calculated hemodynamic response functions from short data segments across the different brain regions to compile a library of HRFs. This assumption relaxes time-invariant requirements from linear models by allowing the possibility of different HRFs from the same data. Principal component analysis was then used to summarize and describe these different HRFs. Spatial location, temporal trajectories and previous history were examined.

Methods

Transgenic mice expressing the calcium sensor GCaMP in excitatory neurons (Thy1-GCaMP6s, n=4) or inhibitory neurons (Gad2-GCaMP6f, n=4) were used in this study. All mice underwent a surgery to affix a head-bar and cranial window for widefield optical imaging (Fig. 1A). Three narrow-band light sources were used for imaging over wavelengths centered at 470nm (to excite GCaMP), 570nm (sensitive to hemoglobin light absorption) and 620nm (sensitive to deoxy-hemoglobin light absorption). Images of the visible brain were acquired using a cooled-CCD camera at 30 Hz and each frame synchronized to one light source at a time in sequence, for an effective frame rate of 10Hz per light source. A long-pass filter was placed along the camera’s light path (>500nm) to capture GCaMP fluorescence from the 470nm light illumination (Fig. 1A). Awake imaging sessions lasted 600 seconds and at least three sessions were acquired from each mouse. These were conducted without evoked stimulation (resting-state) or during brief sporadic whisker stimulation, which consisted of 1 sec air puff delivered to the contralateral whisker pad every 30-36 sec. Analyses consisted of 20 circular ROIs (200µm in diameter) distributed over tissue regions within the cranial window excluding large surface vessels, and time series were extracted and normalized by their mode value. The modified Beer-Lambert Law was used to extract oxy-hemoglobin (HbO) time series from each ROI. HRFs were then calculated over 25 sec periods every 5 sec using a three gamma function model and the GCaMP fluorescence time series as input and HbO time series as output.

Results

HRF fits using excitatory and inhibitory neuron activity of all the data yielded overall explained variances (R2) of 0.47 ± 0.22 and 0.60 ± 0.23, compared to linear HRF fits of 0.29 ± 0.14 and 0.52 ± 0.29, respectively. The fraction of time series segments with R2 > 0.25 was 0.81± 0.16 vs. 0.90 ± 0.11 for excitatory and inhibitory time series, respectively. Brief whisker stimulation increased R2 over resting-state by less than 5%. The data are reported as mean ± standard deviation computed per animal. The excitatory and inhibitory HRFs show an average biphasic temporal behavior with distinct differences in amplitude and peak times. PCA analyses show that these shapes are relatively stable across experiments and animals with most salient changes to their amplitude. PCA analysis were then used to establish trends or trajectories in the HRF temporal evolution, and inverse relationships to the HRF amplitude and residual HRF energy after 20sec from previous time bins were observed. Experiments tracking pupil diameter, animal movement and respirations are currently underway.

Conclusion

We found that relaxing linearity constraints by estimating many HRFs from the same experimental data improved the explained variance of the data for excitatory neuron activity, but not as much for inhibitory neuron activity, suggesting that inhibitory neurons are a more reliable predictor of vascular changes. The variability in HRFs was similar for both models.
