Introduction

Inferring causal relationships between brain regions remains challenging, particularly under nonlinear neural dynamics. Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM) offers a principled generative framework for estimating causal architectures, with two prominent Bayesian structure learning methods: Bayesian Model Comparison (BMC) and Parametric Empirical Bayes with Bayesian Model Reduction (PEB+BMR).¹ BMC inverts each candidate model separately using Variational Laplace, providing precise model evidence estimates at high computational cost.² In contrast, Parametric Empirical Bayes with Bayesian Model Reduction inverts a full model once and analytically derives reduced models by shrinking prior variances of selected parameters toward zero, enabling efficient exploration of large model spaces.³ 

However, this efficiency relies on the assumption that altering a connection parameter has a predictable effect on the remaining parameters. In nonlinear neural mass models, parameters interact in complex ways; removing one can unpredictably alter estimated parameters. Since model evidence is computed from these parameter estimates, BMR's evidence calculations could become unreliable. Whether this theoretical limitation matters in practice remains unclear, as no study has systematically compared both methods under nonlinear dynamics. This gap is particularly relevant for M/EEG analyses, where DCM employs nonlinear neural mass models capturing regional population dynamics through coupled differential equations.⁴ We addressed this methodological question by comparing Bayesian Model Comparison and Parametric Empirical Bayes with Bayesian Model Reduction on OPMEG data from an auditory mismatch negativity paradigm5, modeled using nonlinear neural mass models. Our evaluation aimed to clarify when each inference scheme reliably estimates the underlying causal structure generating observed M/EEG responses. 

Methods

We analysed OPMEG data from a mismatch negativity (MMN) paradigm, where participants heard repeated tone sequences interrupted by occasional frequency changes. These unexpected 'deviant' tones trigger a characteristic neural response across auditory and frontal regions, reflecting hierarchical prediction error processing.⁵

The MMN paradigm provides an excellent test case for examining nonlinear model behaviour, as removing hierarchical levels nonlinearly alters activity patterns across all remaining regions.

We developed three models testing different hierarchical processing hypotheses based on the validated MMN network.⁵ Model 1 featured full hierarchical connectivity between primary auditory cortex (A1) and superior temporal gyrus (STG) bilaterally, with right STG additionally connected to right inferior frontal gyrus (rIFG). Model 2 excluded the rIFG-STG connections while maintaining all other pathways. Model 3 retained only minimal connectivity limited to A1 self-connections. Each cortical region was represented using a modified Jansen-Rit neural mass model comprising three interconnected subpopulations: pyramidal cells, excitatory interneurons, and inhibitory interneurons.⁴

To simulate typical study power, we generated data for 20 synthetic participants per model by fitting a DCM to our template subject using Variational Laplace, then adding Gaussian noise to model parameters, external inputs, and time series.² This yielded 60 synthetic datasets across the three architectures.

We compared two inference approaches. For Bayesian Model Comparison, we inverted each of the three candidate models separately for each synthetic subject using Variational Laplace, which iteratively optimises an approximate posterior distribution to maximise model evidence.² We then summed individual free energies at the group level and computed posterior model probabilities via softmax normalisation. For Parametric Empirical Bayes with Bayesian Model Reduction, we inverted only the full model (Model 1) for each subject, then used Parametric Empirical Bayes to pool parameter estimates at the group level.³ From this group-level posterior, Bayesian Model Reduction analytically derived the evidence for reduced models by applying tighter priors to selected parameters, effectively removing connections without refitting.¹

We constructed 3×3 confusion matrices pairing each model with data generated from all three architectures, where diagonal entries indicate successful recovery of the generative model.

To examine robustness, we varied the expected signal-to-noise ratio parameter (hE) from 2 to 6, where higher values assume greater measurement precision and allow parameters to deviate more freely from their priors. We also conducted progressive pruning analyses, gradually tightening priors on specific modulatory parameters and estimating free energy with both Variational Laplace and Bayesian Model Reduction at each step, allowing us to track how free energy evolved as connections were incrementally removed.

Results

The three model architectures produced distinct evoked response patterns. Model 1, with full hierarchical connectivity, generated the most complex waveform involving all regions. Model 2, lacking rIFG-rSTG connections, showed reduced frontal contributions. Model 3, retaining only A1 self-connections, produced the simplest response. All three architectures yielded model fits that closely matched observed OPMEG responses for both standard and deviant tones.

Confusion matrix analysis revealed complementary failure modes for the two methods. Bayesian Model Comparison assigned maximum posterior probability (p=1) to Models 1 and 2 when fit to their respective time series, but incorrectly identified Model 2 as optimal for Model 3 data. Conversely, Parametric Empirical Bayes with Bayesian Model Reduction correctly identified Models 1 and 3 but misattributed Model 2 data to Model 1. This asymmetry suggests that distinguishing architectures differing by only a few connections poses a greater challenge than identifying large-scale architectural differences.

Signal-to-noise ratio manipulation revealed divergent performance trajectories. Bayesian Model Comparison demonstrated perfect model-recovery accuracy (100%) at hE=2–3, correctly identifying the generating architecture for all synthetic datasets, but declined sharply to 67% for hE≥4. Parametric Empirical Bayes with Bayesian Model Reduction began at 35% accuracy (hE=2), rising steadily to 67% at hE≥4. Despite divergent paths, both methods converged at higher signal-to-noise values.

Progressive pruning analysis isolated the origin of each method's model-selection failure. For the Parametric Empirical Bayes with Bayesian Model Reduction failure case (pruning rSTG-rIFG connections), Variational Free Energy increased steadily while Reduced Free Energy declined after initially rising, indicating that the quadratic approximation failed to track the true free energy landscape under nonlinear parameter interactions. For the Bayesian Model Comparison failure case (pruning A1-STG connections bilaterally), Variational Free Energy showed a sharp initial drop with wide confidence intervals before gradual decline, while Reduced Free Energy increased steadily. This suggests that under conditions of large architectural differences, Bayesian Model Reduction's analytical formulation correctly favours simpler models, whereas Variational Laplace may become trapped in local minima that favour unnecessary complexity. Conversely, for subtle connectivity differences, Bayesian Model Reduction's quadratic approximation might oversimplify while Variational Laplace might be more sensitive and precise."

Conclusion

Our systematic comparison of Bayesian Model Comparison and Parametric Empirical Bayes with Bayesian Model Reduction reveals complementary strengths and limitations for Bayesian structure learning in nonlinear Dynamic Causal Models. Bayesian Model Comparison achieved higher model-recovery accuracy at low signal-to-noise ratios and for architectures differing by only a few connections, whereas Parametric Empirical Bayes with Bayesian Model Reduction performed best when model architectures differed substantially but struggled to distinguish subtle connectivity differences.These findings have direct methodological implications. The quadratic free-energy approximation underlying Bayesian Model Reduction, while computationally efficient, can, as we have shown misrepresent the nonlinear parameter interactions inherent to neural mass models. Our progressive pruning analysis demonstrated that analytically derived free energy diverged systematically from values obtained through full variational re-inversion, particularly when pruning involved few connections. In these cases, Bayesian Model Reduction failed to track the true curvature of the free energy landscape as parameters were removed.Conversely, Variational Laplace re-optimises the full posterior at each model configuration, allowing it to navigate the rugged free energy landscape created by nonlinear parameter interactions. This flexibility enables more accurate discrimination between subtly different architectures. However, this same sensitivity can become a liability: under conditions of large architectural differences, Variational Laplace may become trapped in local minima that favour unnecessarily complex models. In contrast, Bayesian Model Reduction's analytical formulation smooths over local irregularities, providing more stable evidence estimates when comparing substantially different architectures.For practitioners modelling M/EEG data with Dynamic Causal Modeling, we recommend a two-stage approach: first use Bayesian Model Reduction for rapid, computationally efficient screening to identify candidate architectures that differ substantially. Then confirm fine-grained distinctions, particularly those involving only a few connections, with full Bayesian Model Comparison. This strategy leverages the efficiency of Bayesian Model Reduction for coarse model-space exploration while reserving the accuracy of Bayesian Model Comparison for critical architectural decisions. Future research should formally delineate the boundary conditions under which the quadratic approximation remains reliable, potentially enabling adaptive selection between inference schemes based on model-space characteristics and signal quality.
