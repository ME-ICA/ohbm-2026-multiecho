Introduction

Head-down tilt bed rest (HDT) serves as a ground-based analog to investigate the effects of spaceflight-induced cephalad fluid shifts on the human body and to test potential countermeasures. A 6° head-down position recreates the fluid redistribution toward the head and reduces hydrostatic pressure gradients. Previous HDT studies have reported brain structural changes that mirror those observed following long-duration spaceflight, including upward brain shift, cerebrospinal fluid redistribution, ventricular expansion, and perivascular space (PVS) enlargement.1,2 These findings establish HDT as a valid model for studying microgravity-induced brain changes and evaluating interventions to mitigate them.

However, regional cortical grey matter responses to bed rest remain poorly characterized. While global volumetric changes have been reported, the spatial pattern and magnitude of cortical alterations across different brain regions are not well documented. Furthermore, the effect of proposed countermeasures, such as exercise and artificial gravity, in modulating these cortical changes is unknown. In this work, we investigated regional cortical volume changes induced by 60 days of 6° HDT, and assessed whether exercise alone or combined with artificial gravity could modify the cortical response to HDT.

Using longitudinal T1-weighted MRI, we characterized region-specific cortical responses to bed rest and countermeasures across five timepoints spanning before, during and shortly after 60 days of HDT in a cohort of 24 healthy male volunteers. By investigating the trajectory of cortical volumes, we aim to contribute to the understanding of regional vulnerability to cephalad fluid shifts and countermeasure efficacy on brain structure.

Methods

Twenty-four healthy male volunteers (age: 29.46±5.69) participated in the 60-day HDT study "BRACE" at MEDES Space Clinic in Toulouse, France, between April 2023 and May 2024.3 The study was approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Ile-de-France VI and by the French Drug Agency. Participants were randomly assigned to three groups: controls who received no countermeasure, the exercise group (Ex) who biked in a supine ergometer, and the exercise combined with artificial gravity group (ExAG) who biked while being exposed to artificial gravity in a short-arm human centrifuge. Participants in the Ex and ExAG groups exercised for 30 minutes on 49 of the 60 HDT days. 

Participants were scanned at five timepoints: one 11 days before HDT, during baseline data collection (BDC-11); two during HDT, at days 28 and 58 (HDT+28 and HDT+58); and two during recovery, at days 3 and 12 (R+3 and R+12). T1-weighted MRIs were acquired on a 3T Philips Achieva dStream scanner with TE=4.2ms, TR=9ms, flip angle=8°, matrix=320×320, 225 sagittal slices, isotropic voxel size=0.8mm.

T1-weighted MR images were processed using the CAT124 longitudinal segmentation pipeline, integrated within SPM25, using MATLAB Runtime R2023b. This pipeline included the estimation of Region of interest (ROI) volumes using the Neuromorphometrics atlas. In this analysis, we included 98 ROIs corresponding to the cerebral gray matter cortex.

To investigate HDT effects, we employed linear mixed-effects models with each ROI volume as the dependent variable, timepoint as a categorical fixed effect, and random intercepts per subjects. For each ROI we computed the F-stat and p-value for the timepoint term, then corrected for multiple testing using false discovery rate (FDR) on pooled p-values. For ROIs showing significant timepoint effects (p < 0.05 after FDR), post-hoc testing was done with Dunnett's method comparing baseline against the other four timepoints, followed by FDR correction across ROIs and comparisons. 

To investigate countermeasure effects, we introduced the interaction between timepoint and countermeasure group in the model and tested this interaction across all ROIs. For ROIs with significant interactions (p < 0.05 after FDR correction), we compared bed rest effects between groups using pairwise contrasts and then applied FDR across all time and group comparisons.

Results

HDT effects: from 98 analyzed cortical ROIs, 80 showed significant volume changes across timepoints, indicating widespread cortical responses to bed rest. Post-hoc comparisons against baseline revealed bidirectional effects during and shortly after HDT, exhibiting a dorsal-ventral gradient, with superior regions increasing and inferior regions decreasing. Results of comparisons for each timepoint against baseline are shown in Figure 1 (panel A). Volume increases during bed rest occurred predominantly in superior posterior and medial regions, including bilateral precuneus, supplementary motor cortex, superior parietal lobule, precentral and postcentral gyrus medial segments, along with left superior and middle occipital gyri, left supramarginal gyrus, left frontal pole, left posterior cingulate gyrus, and right angular gyrus. Volume decreases were observed in ventral anterior and temporal regions including bilateral medial frontal cortex, planum polare, frontal operculum, and occipital fusiform gyrus, plus right orbital inferior frontal gyrus, left fusiform gyrus, left anterior insula, and left temporal pole. Notably, bilateral posterior cingulate gyrus showed volume increases during bed rest but decreases during recovery relative to baseline. It is also noteworthy that effect sizes for volume decreases in fusiform lingual regions were more pronounced during recovery than during HDT.

Countermeasure effects: Three of the 98 ROIs exhibited significant countermeasure group and time interaction (p-value < 0.05, FDR-corrected). These regions are the left frontal operculum, the right superior parietal lobule, and the right subcallosal area. Post-hoc analyses revealed that the ExAG group showed significant differences in bed rest effect compared to Control and Ex groups. Changes for ExAG differed significantly from Controls in the left frontal operculum with larger volume decrease at HDT+28 and R+3, in the right subcallosal area with increased volume instead of decreasing at HDT+58, and in the right superior parietal lobule with larger volume increase at HDT+28, HDT+58, and R+3. ExAG differed significantly from Exercise in the left frontal operculum with larger volume decrease at HDT+58 and R+3. We did not detect significant differences between Ex group and Controls after FDR correction. The estimated changes per group and timepoint in these three ROIs are shown in Figure 1 (panel B). 

Conclusion

Sixty days of head-down tilt bed rest induced widespread cortical volume alterations, with most changes persisting, although reduced in magnitude, 12 days post-bed rest. The bidirectional nature of these effects (increases and decreases across different regions) suggests differential regional responses to cephalad fluid shift, cerebrospinal fluid redistribution, and intracranial pressure, and could potentially involve neuroplastic adaptation. The spatial distribution of cortical volume changes as response to HDT shows a dorsal-ventral gradient, with superior regions demonstrating increases and inferior regions showing decreases. The involvement of sensorimotor regions (supplementary motor cortex, precentral/postcentral medial segments) may reflect combined effects of fluid redistribution and reduced motor activity during bed rest.

We identified three cortical regions where combined exercise and artificial gravity countermeasures produced distinct effects compared to control or exercise alone. The right superior parietal lobule showed the most differential countermeasure response, suggesting this region may be particularly responsive to gravitational loading. The left frontal operculum demonstrated effects emerging by HDT+28 and persisting into recovery, while the right subcallosal area showed significant effects only at HDT+58, indicating heterogeneous temporal dynamics across regions. Notably, in these three ROIs, the ExAG group showed greater volume changes than the other two groups, suggesting that artificial gravity exacerbates rather than mitigates these regional responses. The absence of significant differences between Ex and Control groups suggests that artificial gravity, not exercise, drives these differential cortical effects.

Our statistical analyses provide robust evidence given multiple methodological strengths. First, the longitudinal design with five timepoints enabled the characterization of effects during and after HDT while accounting for within-subject correlation. The two-stage FDR correction prioritized robust, replicable findings over detecting subtle effects.

These results provide a regional characterization of cortical vulnerability to cephalad fluid shifts and demonstrate that artificial gravity produces distinct responses in specific brain regions. The exacerbation of volume changes by combined countermeasures in sensorimotor and spatial processing regions warrants further investigation to determine whether these reflect adaptive neuroplastic mechanisms or maladaptive responses. Future research should examine the functional consequences of these structural changes and investigate longer recovery periods to assess persistence of HDT and countermeasure effects.
