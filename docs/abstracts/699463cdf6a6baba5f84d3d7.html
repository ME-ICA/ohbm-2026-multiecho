Introduction

Quantitative MRI (qMRI) offers biophysically-grounded measures of brain tissue composition, providing information that complements conventional contrast-weighted imaging. For application of qMRI in neurological disorders such as multiple sclerosis (MS),  fast acquisition, robust and interpretable outputs, and standardized post-processing are required. Multi-parameter mapping (MPM) enables simultaneous generation of magnetization transfer saturation (MTsat), proton density (PD), longitudinal relaxation rate (R1), and effective transverse relaxation rate (R2*) maps.1 However, interpretation of these maps may be confounded by aging, sex, and age-related white matter (WM) lesions, making reliable reference data and robust calibration essential.

 Here, we applied a fast (7 minute) 3T MPM protocol with 1.6 mm isotropic resolution to a large healthy cohort and patients with MS. Our goals were: (1) to generate reference values for MPM metrics across major brain tissue classes; (2) to evaluate how age influences these measures; and (3) to implement and assess optimized PD calibration strategies that mitigate bias introduced by WM lesions or diffuse pathology. This framework aims to advance standardized qMRI usage in MS and other neuroinflammatory diseases.

Methods

We analyzed MPM data from 77 healthy adults (20-75 y) and 27 patients with MS. All imaging was performed on a 3T Siemens Prisma system using the clinically optimized 1.6-mm isotropic multi-parameter mapping (MPM) protocol described by Cooper et al.2 The protocol consists of three multi-echo 3D FLASH acquisitions with proton-density-, T1-, and MTsat-weighting. Acquisition parameters were: field of view 224×256 mm² with 112 partitions, TR = 18 ms / flip angles = 4° and 25° for PDw and T1w images, and TR = 37 ms / flip angle = 6° for MT-weighted data. MT-weighting was achieved using a 10 ms Gaussian off-resonance pulse (500° nominal flip, 1.2 kHz offset). All sequences used six echoes (2.46–14.78 ms) and identical readout bandwidth (470 Hz/pixel). GRAPPA acceleration (R=2) and 6/8 partial Fourier were applied to maintain a total MPM scan time of ~7 minutes. A vendor-supplied spin-echo/stimulated-echo B1+ mapping sequence (2 minutes) was acquired with 4-mm isotropic resolution to correct spatial transmit-field bias, following the optimized correction strategy reported by Cooper et al. that improves PD and R1 stability. Quantitative MTsat, PD, R1, and R2* maps were reconstructed with the hMRI toolbox after Gibbs-ringing removal and bias-field correction using unified segmentation.3 White matter lesions in MS and age-related T2-hyperintensities in controls were manually segmented on co-registered FLAIR images. Cortical, deep-grey-matter and WM masks were derived using FastSurfer.

Given that WM-based scaling is unreliable when lesions or diffuse WM changes are present, we evaluated two alternatives: calibration using lesion-filled WM masks and a cerebrospinal-fluid (CSF)-based approach using eroded lateral-ventricle masks restricted to voxels with T1 > 4 s. To evaluate age effects on qMRI parameters, we modelled MTsat, PD, R1, and R2* as a function of age using linear, quadratic, and cubic regression models. Model selection was based on likelihood-ratio tests and inspection of residuals, allowing for detection of non-linear trajectories. Sex and intracranial volume (ICV; estimated via SPM segmentation) were included as covariates. For each tissue class (cerebral WM, cortical gray matter [GM], deep GM, WM hyperintensities), mean parameter values were extracted and entered into regression models; significance was assessed using two-tailed tests with α = 0.05 and false-discovery-rate correction across contrasts and regions. Group differences between healthy WM, normal-appearing WM (NAWM), and WM lesions were examined using mixed-effects models with participant as a random factor.

Results

Results from this study were published previously 4.Quantitative maps derived from the clinical 1.6-mm MPM protocol showed distinct parameter distributions across WM, cortical GM, and deep GM. In healthy participants, WM exhibited low PD (70% SD 1.3%), high MTsat (1.55%, SD 0.05%), and high R1 (0.91 s-1, SD 0.03 s-1), whereas cortical GM showed higher PD (79%, SD 1.1%), lower MTsat (85%, SD 0.02%), and lower R1 (0.62 s-1, SD 0.01 s-1). Deep GM nuclei showed the anticipated pattern of higher MTsat and R2* and lower PD in the pallidum and putamen compared with the caudate and thalamus, consistent with iron- and myelin-related contrast.

Age effects were found across multiple tissue classes. In WM, MTsat and R1 decreased with age (best-fitting quadratic models, p < .01), with declines becoming steeper after approximately 45 years. PD increased modestly with age (p < .05), indicating reduced microstructural integrity and increased water content. Cortical GM showed similar but more attenuated age relationships: MTsat and R1 decreased with age (p < .05), whereas PD changes were minimal. Deep GM demonstrated region-specific patterns: R2* increased with age in iron-rich nuclei (p < .01), while thalamic MTsat and R1 showed small but significant age-related reductions (p < .05). For most structures, non-linear models outperformed linear fits (likelihood-ratio tests, p < .05), consistent with previously described non-linear aging trajectories in qMRI metrics.

The three PD calibration approaches differed markedly in scaling stability. Standard WM-based calibration yielded variable scaling factors and biased PD values in participants with WM lesions: in MS, lesional PD was underestimated by >3–5% when WM was used as the reference. Using lesion-filled WM masks reduced this bias but did not eliminate between-subject variability. CSF-based calibration produced the most stable scaling factors (coefficient of variation <2%) and harmonized PD values across normal-appearing WM and healthy WM. After CSF calibration, PD in normal-appearing WM remained slightly elevated relative to healthy WM, but lesion-related PD elevations remained clearly detectable. Paradoxically, WM-based PD calibration showed a negative correlation with age, whereas this association was absent with CSF-based calibration.

 All four qMRI parameters showed consistent contrasts between lesions and healthy WM. MS lesions showed decreased MTsat (p < 0.001), reduced R1 (p < 0.001), and substantially elevated PD (p < 0.001). Normal-appearing WM displayed more subtle abnormalities in MTsat and PD (both p < .05), indicating diffuse microstructural changes beyond focal lesions. Age-related WM hyperintensities in healthy controls showed increases in PD and modest decreases in MTsat and R1, but these changes were substantially smaller than those observed in MS lesions (all p < .01).

 Regional analyses confirmed expected sensitivity differences across tissue classes. WM exhibited the lowest within-region variability, whereas cortical GM showed larger dispersion across participants, consistent with known partial-volume susceptibility of cortical measurements. Deep GM demonstrated highly reproducible R1 and R2* values, strongly supporting their utility for assessing iron- and myelin-related alterations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a short 1.6-mm MPM protocol yields stable quantitative maps with sufficient tissue specificity to capture both age-related variation and MS-related abnormalities. The reference values derived for WM, cortical GM, deep GM, and WM hyperintensities provide a normative baseline suitable for clinical research applications, particularly in conditions such as MS where diffuse and focal tissue damage need to be distinguished.

Our results highlight that qMRI parameters show characteristic tissue-dependent profiles and reveal quantifiable deviations associated with MS. Non-linear age trajectories observed for MTsat, R1, and R2* emphasize the need to incorporate age-adjusted models when interpreting qMRI values, as linear approximations may obscure important biological variability. The observed increases in PD and declines in MTsat and R1 with aging further underscore the value of modeling expected normative change to avoid misattributing physiological trends to disease.

Importantly, CSF-based PD calibration outperformed WM-based methods. CSF normalization produced the lowest scaling variability and avoided calibration bias introduced by WM pathology, which is especially relevant in MS where neither lesional nor normal-appearing WM represent a reliable reference. Moreover, the paradoxical age effects seen with WM-based calibration were not observed when PD was calibrated to CSF.This calibration strategy therefore enhances the interpretability and comparability of PD measurements across heterogeneous patient populations and aligns with broader efforts toward harmonized qMRI pipelines.

The combination of high measurement stability, sensitivity to microstructural variation, and compatibility with routine clinical scanning workflows supports the use of this MPM protocol in longitudinal and multi-site MS studies. The clear separation of parameter profiles among healthy WM, normal-appearing WM, WM hyperintensities, and MS lesions indicates strong potential for monitoring disease burden, characterizing lesion evolution, and evaluating treatment effects. The normative values and calibration strategy provided here will facilitate harmonized qMRI analyses and improve reproducibility in future multi-site studies.
