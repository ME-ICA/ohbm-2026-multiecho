Introduction

Delineating the human primary auditory cortex (A1) in vivo remains an active area of research [1]. Although A1 can be defined cytoarchitectonically and its laminar signature is documented across species, identifying its borders along Heschl’s gyrus (HG) in humans remains difficult due to the subtlety of the laminar transitions [2]. Previous MRI studies have attempted to characterize the laminar structure of the auditory core using 0.8 mm iso. T1-w imaging at 3T [3] and 0.6 mm iso. T1- and T2*-w imaging at 7T [4]. However, despite achieving submillimeter resolution, these studies (I) did not provide clear, anatomical visualizations of the auditory core comparable to histological data, and (II) performed cortical depth analyses without using the equivolume principle [5]. While some argue that the differences between equivolume and equidistant depth metrics are minimal at standard resolutions [6], these differences become substantial at higher resolutions [7].

Recent advances in ultra–high-resolution 7T imaging have pushed voxel sizes from 0.6 mm down to 0.35 mm iso. [8, 9]. These T2*-weighted acquisitions provide a new opportunity to evaluate whether mesoscopic MRI can reveal laminar features previously detectable in post-mortem histology in individual brains and to assess the practical importance of equivolume versus equidistant cortical depth metrics. This progress raises three central questions:

1. Do new, previously inaccessible laminar features become detectable in vivo at 0.35 mm iso. resolution compared to 0.6 mm iso. (a 5-fold reduction in voxel volume)?

2. How strongly do equivolume and equidistant depth metrics differ at mesoscopic scale, and what is their impact on the A1 layer profiles?

3. If the differences between depth metrics are substantial, and if 0.35 mm data carry far more laminar information than 0.6 mm, how trustworthy are cortical layer analyses from earlier, lower-resolution studies that did not use equivolume layerification? 

Methods

We acquired data from 9 participants using the T2*-weighted mesoscopic imaging protocol described in [8] including the 90 degree rotation in the phase encoding direction that is used to mitigate the blood motion artifacts. However, we made a few modifications to increase the field-of-view to include auditory cortices and key subcortical auditory nuclei including the inferior colliculi and medial geniculate bodies. To achieve this extended coverage without lengthening the scan time, we increased the acceleration factor 3D to 3. After quality control, the resulting images were comparable in overall quality to those reported in [8].

Preprocessing was run through Python scripting, following the pipeline established in [8]. All intermediate outputs were visually quality controlled to ensure consistency with the original processing workflow. After preprocessing we used the T2*-weighted echo mean image for each participant for further analyses. HG were identified based on gross anatomical landmarks [CITE Heynckes2022], and T2*-weighted images were manually segmented following the step established in [8] and quality controlled by two experts (E.J.A., O.F.G).

For further cortical analyses, we used LayNii software [7]. Specifically, we placed geodesic cortical cylinders (radius: 15 mm) centered on the midpoint of each HG to isolate the regions of interest while respecting local cortical geometry using LN2_MULTILATERATE program. We computed normalized cortical depth metrics with equivolume and equidistance options using LN2_LAYERS. Surface parameterization (UV mapping) within each cylindrical ROI was performed (Figure 1 E-G) using LN2_MULTILATERATE, and vessel suppression was applied to mitigate the influence of intracortical vessels, using a pancake shaped median filter (LN2_UVD_FILTER -radius 3 -height 0.1). To mitigate the residual intensity inhomogeneities, we first estimated laminar background intensity using a cork shaped median filter (LN2_UVD_FILTER -radius 3 -height 2) and divided the previously computed pancake filter result with the cork filter result. This effectively yielded a normalized T2*w data with optimal contrast to see the laminar differences (Figure 1 I-K). Each cortical cylinder was then parcellated into geodesic hexagonal cells (defined by inscribed 2 mm radius circles using LN2_HEXBIN). These hexagonal parcels served as the spatial units from which cortical layer profiles are extracted.

Results

(E-H) Hexbin sampling framework: Geodesic hexagonal elements illustrating the spatial sampling used to extract layer profiles across each gyrus. (I-L) Equidistant layering results: Layer profiles for all hexbins computed using equidistant cortical depth, showing substantial variation across cortical depth. (M-P) Equivolume layering results: The same profiles are recomputed using the equivolume metric, revealing markedly reduced variation across cortical depth and a more stable pattern across hexbins.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight the critical importance of using an equivolume cortical depth metric when analyzing laminar profiles in and around HG. When equidistant layering is used (a common choice in earlier submillimeter studies) systematic geometric distortions arise: the natural thickening of the deep cortical layers at gyral crowns can be misinterpreted as biologically meaningful laminar boundaries. This is particularly problematic in the auditory core, where A1 is expected to show increased myelination in the deeper layers, which manifests as darker T2*-weighted signal. Without equivolume correction, these curvature-driven changes can be mistaken as markers of A1, creating a plausible but misleading appearance of laminar specialization.

By explicitly comparing equivolume and equidistant metrics at 0.35 mm resolution, we show that a substantial portion of the laminar variation observed with equidistant layering disappears once curvature is properly accounted for. Importantly, this relationship is not linear: the mapping between equidistant and equivolume depth depends on local cortical geometry and therefore cannot be corrected by linear regression approaches sometimes used to “de-curve” layer profiles [3]. Only a nonlinear adjustment grounded in the true intracortical volume distribution resolves these confounds.

These results raise important questions about the interpretability of earlier laminar studies of the auditory cortex that relied on equidistant depth metrics [3, 4]. Some of the boundaries previously interpreted as areal transitions may, in fact, reflect the geometric effects described by Bok decades ago rather than genuine cytoarchitectonic features [9].

Finally, our 0.35 mm processing pipeline, combining high-resolution T2*-weighted imaging with equivolume layering and advanced geodesic filters, reveals promising and anatomically plausible laminar patterns around HG, including features consistent with the expected structure of the auditory core. However, we have intentionally not quantified these patterns fully at this stage. A rigorous quantification of A1’s laminar signature will only be meaningful once we establish which depth metrics can be trusted and how much earlier results may have been shaped by geometric artifacts. The present work provides a necessary step toward that goal, underscoring that accurate laminar inference in vivo depends as much on choosing the right depth metric as on acquiring the right resolution.
