Introduction

Resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) functional connectivity (FC) is a popular technique for studying how brain regions are intrinsically organized. FC networks are inferred to be functionally connected and are thought to involve the synchrony of neuronal populations involved in a common function (Meyer-Baese, Jaeger, & Keilholz, 2025). Yet to what degree rsfMRI FC reflects underlying neuronal activity, and how this varies as a function of behavioral state, is still poorly understood. We fill this gap by identifying recurring co-activation patterns (CAPs) from neuronal and hemodynamic signals. To this end, we are using a rapid voltage sensor JEDI-1P (Lu et al., 2023) to look at how functional resting-state networks manifest across temporal scales. Unlike traditional calcium sensors, JEDI-1P a voltage sensor that allows you to track both slow subthreshold membrane dynamics and fast gamma band oscillations.  We look at frequencies spanning infraslow (0.05-0.1 Hz) to faster gamma band (30-60 Hz) signaling in the dorsal cortex of awake head-fixed mice. These signals are then compared to a simultaneously acquired readout of cerebral blood volume from the red reference mCherry fluorescent signal. How well each neuronal CAP predicts hemodynamic CAP activity is assessed by calculating the mutual information between the two as a function of different behavioral states. 

Methods

Five EMX1-Cre pups expressing pan-cortical membrane-bound green JEDI-1P, a voltage sensor, and the cytosolic red-emitting reference fluorescent protein (mCherry) were imaged (Lu et al., 2023). The imaging set-up uses two CMOS high-speed imaging cameras (MiCAM ULTIMA. SciMedia) (Fig1A). Preprocessing included image alignment, background subtraction, photobleaching correction, hemodynamic correction, and alignment to the Allen atlas. The full dataset consists of 5 sessions acquired per mouse, with a single session consisting of 10 40.96-second-long trials sampled at 200FPS. Behavioral state was monitored simultaneously using two cameras (Fig1B). The mouse’s behavioral state was broken up into four different conditions: short rest (rest < 2s), prolonged rest (rest > 2s), short movement (movement <2s), and prolonged movement (movement >2s) (Fig1C). This was determined using a conservative estimate of whether the animal is resting based on the average activity from 3 different ROIs extracted from the camera data (Fig1B). 

We used a frame-wise clustering approach to identify reoccurring co-activation patterns (CAPs) to represent different  spatial network configurations that are present in the spontaneous neural and hemodynamic data (Liu, Zhang, Chang, & Duyn, 2018). This was done on a per-session basis for different bandpass-filtered versions of the JEDI signal. This includes infraslow, slow, delta, all the way to gamma band ranges. The slow <1Hz mCherry signal represents our hemodynamic activity. Data for a given frequency band was concatenated across trials for a given session. CAPs for each session were then identified by running k-means on the z-scored session-level data (ran for 500 iterations, with 15 initializations, using KMeans function from Scikit-learn in Python). This clustering was done on a per-session basis for clusters ranging in size from k=2 – 26 (Fig1C). For each K, we calculated the gain in explained variance with increasing cluster number. The ideal number of K clusters for each session was determined by identifying the elbow point, where the gain in explained variance levels off. This was done by finding the point of maximum curvature by identifying the peak of the second derivative. Mutual information between different JEDI bands and the hemodynamic signals was calculated by looking at the joint probabilities of CAP state assignments while taking into account the known 1.5-second time-lag between the two signals. This was done separately with data from the 4 different behavioral states.

Results

An illustration of 5 identified CAPs for a single session is shown (Fig2A). A defining characteristic of most CAPs across frequency bands is that they are configured into assemblies of regional substrates that encompass previously characterized wide-field resting state networks. An example is the motor resting state network that is present in CAPs #18 and #6 for slow and delta bands, respectively. Several CAPs within a frequency band also configure into state and anti-state pairs characterized by opposing patterns of functional co-activation, such as CAP #3 and #6 for delta band (Fig1A). Each original frame can then be assigned to a given CAP (Fig1B). The number of transitions increases with increasing frequency, which in turn results in a decreased probability of remaining in the same state as a function of increasing frequency. To assess how much predictive power each of the individual JEDI frequency bands had when it came to predicting the hemodynamic data mutual information (MI) was calculated for each of the four behavioral states (Fig2C). Across behavioral states broadband, and low-frequency voltage signals (infraslow-delta) are consistently informative of the hemodynamic signal. No single frequency band dominates, however, with predictive information being broadly distributed. The different behavioral states modulate overall predictability more than the individual frequency bands. The movement states generally show higher MI than rest states.

Conclusion

Results indicate that session-level CAPs effectively summarize dominant network structure. Mutual information analyses further showed that voltage–hemodynamic dependence remains broadband and not strongly frequency-specific. Hemodynamic activity reflects a state-dependent integration of neuronal signals across frequency bands rather than being selectively driven by one band. Movement states strengths neurovascular coupling globally, while lower frequency and broadband voltage activity provide robust predictability across conditions.
