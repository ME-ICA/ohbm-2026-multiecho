Introduction

Soaking samples in gadolinium (Gd)-based contrast agents (GBCAs) has been widely used in ex vivo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). By shortening the longitudinal relaxation time of the tissue, GBCAs enhance signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) per unit time; however, prior studies report mixed effects of Gd on diffusion MRI (dMRI) metrics. In multimodal workflows that include dMRI and subsequent optical imaging, the effects of GBCAs on fiber architecture preservation and downstream optical readouts remain insufficiently characterized. 

Polarization-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PS-OCT) provides micrometer-scale sensitivity to myelinated fiber organization and has been increasingly applied to volumetric mapping of human brain tissue and white-matter architecture.1 While using GBCAs as optical clearing agents to improve OCT visibility in skin has been explored,2 and PS-OCT has been used to monitor polarization-state changes induced by optical clearing procedures, Gd-specific effects on PS-OCT in neural tissue have not been systematically evaluated. In dMRI, Gd concentrations ≥ 5 mM can alter apparent diffusion coefficients without substantially affecting fractional anisotropy (FA),3 while spatially heterogeneous tissue penetration can lead to region-dependent changes in relaxation, diffusivity, and model-derived metrics.4 Such effects may confound dMRI interpretation, which is especially important in the case of the brainstem, one of the most structurally dense and least studied regions of the human brain. Here, we evaluated how two concentrations of GBCA (gadobutrol, 3 and 5 mM) affect diffusion metrics and fiber tract reconstructions in two human hemi-brainstems scanned with 9.4T MRI. We also assessed whether Gd exposure alters 3D axis orientation estimation and true birefringence in PS-OCT.

Methods

Two 10% formalin-fixed right-hemisected human brainstem samples (58 yo/F, 70 yo/M), stored long-term in paraformaldehyde-lysine-periodate (PLP) at 4°C, were included in this study. Figure 1 shows the general workflow. In preparation for MRI, the samples were sealed in plastic filled with Fomblin– a chemically inert, proton-free, perfluoropolyether. We acquired MRI prior to Gd-immersion (baseline), then the samples were unpacked and washed of Fomblin using Dawn detergent and water. Samples were then immersed in either a 3 mM or 5 mM concentration of gadobutrol solution for 14 days. Post-immersion MRI scans were subsequently acquired. All images were collected on a 9.4 T Bruker preclinical scanner and temperature was monitored using an optical fiber sensor.

MRI relaxometry included estimation of T2 using a multi-echo spin-echo (MSE) sequence and estimation of T1 using a Rapid Acquisition with Relaxation Enhancement (RARE) sequence, both acquired at the 300 µm isotropic resolution. Measured T₁ values were used to determine brainstem-specific Ernst angles for setting the excitation flip angle in subsequent dMRI acquisitions; dMRI was acquired using a 3D pulsed-gradient spin-echo sequence. In total, four dMRI datasets were collected: baseline and post-immersion for each brainstem, all at 350 µm isotropic. Baseline scans of the 5 mM sample were acquired with 96 directions at b = 5000 s/mm2 (TR/TE = 440/20 ms; 71.4 h). The 3 mM sample’s baseline scans were acquired with 96 directions at b = 4250 s/mm² (TR/TE = 440/18 ms; 72.8 h). Scans were acquired post-immersion with 128 directions and matched baseline b-values for the 5 mM (TR/TE = 95/17 ms; 55.7 h) and 3 mM brainstems (TR/TE = 230/17 ms; 55.7 h), respectively. 

Following post-immersion MRI of the 5 mM hemi-brainstem, the sample was unpacked, and the caudal part of the medulla was blocked for PS-OCT. In preparation for optical imaging, the tissue was immersed in PBS for 72 h and then index-matched in glycerol in increments of 20% concentration until reaching 100%. The tissue block was then agarose-embedded and index-matched for 21 days in 100% glycerol. Imaging was performed on a custom 3D-axis PS-OCT system operating at 1300 nm, with 6 × 6 µm2 in-plane and 3.5 µm axial resolution. The intensity, retardance, and in-plane optic axis orientation were recovered from the recorded spectral interference data from the two polarization channels. The 3D intensity and retardance volumetric data were then processed into en face by averaging over 150 µm below the tissue surface, while the circular mean was used for en face orientation calculation. Retardance and orientation were subsequently used to calculate the through-plane orientation and 3D axis.

MRI data were processed using an in-house MATLAB (2023b, MathWorks) script to implement the following processing steps. MSE and RARE data were denoised using Marchenko-Pastur Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Quantitative relaxometry was generated using weighted linear least squares estimation. For spatial profiling, the specimen’s long and short axes were defined using PCA of the masked volume. T₁ and T₂ values were sampled along each axis and binned into 60 equally spaced bins spanning the central 98% of the specimen extent. Bin-averaged T₁ and T₂ profiles sampled along the axes were compared across conditions using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across spatial bins, with p ≤ 0.05 considered significant. Diffusion data were corrected for Gibbs ringing artifacts and eddy current distortions. Mean (MD), axial (AD), and radial (RD) diffusivity and FA were estimated using the Diffusion Tensor Imaging model. Isotropic diffusion fraction (ISO) was estimated from the single q-shell data using the Generalized Q-Sampling Imaging (GQI) model in DSI Studio (Chen, 2022). Fiber tracking was then performed and visually assessed.

Results

Mean T1 decreased from 602.7 ms (pre) to 78.0 ms (post) in the 3 mM specimen and from 774.7 ms (pre) to 64.6 ms (post) in the 5 mM specimen. Following a 30-day washout in PBS, mean T1 of the 5 mM specimen partially recovered to 367.2 ms, still substantially shorter than pre-immersion values. T2 exhibited smaller changes, decreasing from 35.7 ms (pre) to 23.7 ms (post) in the 3 mM specimen and from 46.8 ms (pre) to 28.6 ms (post) in the 5 mM specimen, with partial recovery to 33.8 ms following washout.

Line profiles (Figure 2a) of T1 and T2 along the specimen-intrinsic long and short axes showed similar spatial trends prior to immersion, reflecting baseline tissue heterogeneity. Following Gd exposure, profiles largely preserved this spatial structure but exhibited position-dependent shifts, consistent with a smooth penetration gradient rather than abrupt regional differences. Paired comparisons of bin-averaged profiles demonstrated significant reductions in T1 after immersion along both axes in both specimens (3 mM: long axis p = 7 × 10-12, short axis p = 5 × 10-10, 5 mM: long axis p = 9 × 10-10, short axis p = 9 × 10-13). T2 profiles likewise showed significant post-immersion reductions (3 mM: long axis p = 1 × 10-10, short axis p = 9 × 10-10; 5 mM: long axis p = 5 × 10-10, short axis p = 6 × 10-11). In the 5 mM specimen, washout significantly attenuated the magnitude of these position-dependent shifts for both T1 and T2 (T1: p = 7 × 10-12 and 9 × 10-13; T2: p = 1 × 10-2 and 7 × 10-12 for long and short axes, respectively), although profiles did not fully return to pre-immersion values.

Across both specimens, dMRI metrics (Figure 2b) exhibited consistent pre–post changes. In the 5 mM specimen, MD increased from 0.188 to 0.247 µm2/ms (+31.2%), accompanied by increases in AD (0.264 to 0.319 µm²/ms; +20.7%) and RD (0.150 to 0.211 µm2/ms; +40.4%), while FA decreased from 0.451 to 0.283 (−37.1%). In the 3 mM specimen, similar but larger relative changes were observed, with MD increasing from 0.149 to 0.216 µm2/ms (+44.9%), AD from 0.211 to 0.294 µm2/ms (+39.1%), RD from 0.118 to 0.177 µm2/ms (+50.1%), and FA decreasing from 0.447 to 0.341 (−23.7%). The isotropic diffusion fraction increased in both specimens, with a larger relative increase in the 3 mM specimen (+53.1%) than in the 5 mM specimen (+20.4%).

Bin-averaged diffusion profiles along the specimen-intrinsic long and short axes demonstrated significant pre–post differences for FA, MD, AD, and RD in both specimens (all p ≤ 10-8). ISO also increased significantly in the 3 mM specimen along both axes (long axis p = 10-3, short axis p = 2 × 10-11), whereas ISO changes in the 5 mM specimen were axis varying.

PS-OCT data quality after Gd immersion was evaluated with respect to imaging depth over which usable signal can be obtained as well as optical characteristics of polarization (orientation and retardance).5 As shown in the PS-OCT imaging pipeline of Figure 1, the imaging depth reached approximately 700 µm, which is comparable to, and in some cases greater than, that observed without Gd treatment. The orientation map appears to capture local fiber directions, and the retardance map shows clear birefringence contrast associated with fibrous structures. These observations indicate that the polarization properties of the tissue were well preserved following Gd-immersion. Further quantitative validation will be necessary to confirm these qualitative observations.

Conclusion

Relaxometry revealed dose-dependent Gd uptake with partial reversibility, while dMRI demonstrated systematic increases in diffusivity and reductions in anisotropy following Gd immersion. Spatial profiling showed statistically significant but smooth position-dependent modulations consistent with diffusion-driven transport.

Spatial heterogeneity has critical implications for interpreting diffusion metrics. Changes in the isotropic diffusion fraction were position- and, in some cases, axis-dependent, consistent with nonuniform but gradual Gd penetration and spatially varying relaxation effects. Regions with shorter T1, corresponding to higher local Gd concentration, exhibited higher SNR, and improved tractography continuity. However, these improvements do not necessarily reflect underlying microstructural differences. Without accounting for spatial variations in T1 and T2, and regional contrasts in FA, MD, or tractography, outcomes could be misinterpreted as biological heterogeneity rather than relaxation-driven effects. In both specimens, RD increased more than AD, suggesting direction-dependent sensitivity of diffusion estimates under altered relaxation and signal-weighting conditions rather than uniform scaling.

Temperature changes during scanning were modest and unlikely to account for the observed diffusion effects. Sample temperature increased from 24.0°C and plateaued at 25.5°C within the first 10 minutes of acquisition. With diffusivity increasing by ~2.4% per °C, a 1.5°C temperature rise would correspond to an approximate 3.6% increase. This effect is small compared to the magnitude and spatial variability of diffusion changes observed following Gd immersion.

Our findings suggest that Gd soaking alters not only global relaxation times but also the spatial distribution of signal weighting in ways that directly influence dMRI metrics and tractography. Integrating geometry-based modeling of penetration and spatially resolved relaxometry into postmortem dMRI protocols is therefore important for accurate interpretation and reproducibility. More broadly, these results reflect the need to characterize contrast agent distribution when using Gd to enhance ex vivo MRI, particularly in studies seeking to draw microstructural inferences from diffusion measurements.

The PS-OCT results from the 5 mM Gd-soaked specimen motivate further investigation. The preservation of polarization properties and potential improvement in depth penetration suggest that controlled comparisons between Gd-soaked and non-soaked tissue may be informative. Given the challenges of comparing birefringence across subcortical structures with diverse tissue properties, future work could focus on ex vivo human cortical tissue blocks soaked at varying Gd concentrations. An additional direction would be to characterize the duration of PBS washout required for Gd-induced optical and MR properties to return toward baseline.

By quantifying how Gd immersion modulates relaxation, diffusion metrics, and optical polarization properties, this study demonstrates that Gd immersion can alter ex vivo dMRI while preserving tissue polarization properties relevant for downstream optical imaging. Meanwhile, the observed position-dependent relaxation and diffusion effects highlight the need for spatially resolved characterization of contrast agent distribution to avoid misinterpretation of microstructural metrics. These results provide practical guidance for multimodal imaging pipelines, such as those developed within the NIH BRAIN CONNECTS Consortium, where MRI contrast enhancement must be balanced against quantitative fidelity and cross-modal consistency.
