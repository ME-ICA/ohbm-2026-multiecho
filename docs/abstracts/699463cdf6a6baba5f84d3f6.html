Introduction

Language syntax is the ability to process sentences of varying complexity based on rules and dependency mechanisms across words. For instance, the sentences “The writer takes the paper” and “The poet admires the writer” can be combined into a single more complex sentence with an object relative clause: “The writer [that the poet admires] takes the paper”. Analogously, action syntax is the ability to perform goal-directed motor actions composed of more or less complex operative sequences. For example, moving a peg involves dependencies between the different elements of the operational chain (reach, grasp, lift, shift, place, release). The syntactic complexity can be increased by performing the same action sequence while using a tool (e.g. pliers). The sequence remains the same, but it becomes embedded within the use of a tool, like a relative clause nested within a main clause.

Several findings suggest the existence of a domain-general syntax, in contrast to a syntax specific to each domain.1,2 First, brain regions in the basal ganglia show similar patterns of activity between linguistic and motor modalities of syntax. Second, a 30-minute motor training session using pliers can significantly improve performance in a linguistic syntax comprehension task. This learning transfer was observed bidirectionally. 

These findings have been established in adults; however, the development of a domain-general syntax in children remains unknown. The present fMRI study aimed to identify the (possibly overlapping) neural networks underlying language and motor syntax in children and early adolescents. Before adolescence, both syntax and fine motor control are developed although still being refined.3,4 Finding areas of common activity, notably in the basal ganglia like in adults,2,5 would speak in favor of an early development of a domain-general syntax. This investigation is important to improve our understanding of syntax learning and disorders for future remediation strategies.

Methods

Thirty neurotypical participants aged from 9 to 13 years old (mean: 11 ± 1.3 years old, 13 girls) were included. The experiment consisted of two sessions conducted on separate days. The first session aimed at familiarizing participants with the language and motor tasks as well as the MRI environment. They practiced performing the tasks inside a mock scanner to reduce stress during the subsequent session. During the second session (MRI), we acquired anatomical images (10 minutes), followed by functional images while participants participants performed the language task (25 minutes) and the motor task (35 minutes), with break outside the scanner between the two tasks.

Imaging was performed on a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) scanner using a 64-channel head and neck coil. Anatomical imaging consisted of high-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (1 mm isotropic resolution, TR/TI/TE=2300/925/2,98 ms, GRAPPA acceleration = 3, readout bandwidth = 240 Hz/pix) and T2-weighted scans with SPACE sequence (1 mm isotropic resolution, TR/TE= 3200/408 ms, CAIPIRINHA acceleration = 2x2z1, readout bandwidth = 723 Hz/pix). Functional imaging comprised four language runs and six motor runs with 2D-EPI multiband (2 mm isotropic resolution, TR/TE = 1360/30 ms, 68 slices, multiband acceleration factor = 4, readout bandwidth = 2272 Hz/pix). 

Language task:

We used a two-alternative forced-choice sentence comprehension task. In each trial, participants were presented with two successive sentences: the first one was either syntactically complex or not, the second was an affirmation that correctly (or not) summarized the first sentence. Participants had to decide whether the affirmation correctly matched the first sentence. They read the sentences presented on a screen via a mirror. They responded with two fingers (left hand) on a two-button response box (true/false). 

Eight French sentences were declined in two syntactic types: simple Coordinated Clauses (CC) and complex Object-Relative Clauses (ORC). Each sentence was paired with four affirmations (two correct, two incorrect). Correct responses to affirmations relied on correct comprehension of the syntactic structure of the previously presented sentence. For example, following the sentence “The writer admires the poet and takes the paper”, the affirmation could be either “The writer admires the poet”, “The writer takes the paper”, “The poet admires the writer”, or “The poet takes the paper”. After participants’ response, the screen went blank until the next trial began.

In total, participants completed 64 trials (32 CC and 32 ORC), distributed across four runs.

Motor task:

We used colored plastic pegs inserted at the center of a PVC board. The board included a hand-rest area, as well as colored LEDs. The objective was to match the visual pattern of the LEDs by moving the pegs sequentially using the right hand (see Figure). The holes and hand-rest area were equipped with sensors to record the timing of all events. Through a double-mirror system, participants could see their right hand and the board positioned around their hips.

We manipulated motor syntactic complexity along two dimensions: (1) sequence complexity (1 vs. 3 movements to achieve the objective) and (2) effector (bare hand vs. tool use). Compared to 1-movement, a 3-movement trial was expected to impose higher constraints on motor planning, due to the syntactic dependencies between successive peg movements (e.g., to match the LEDs pattern, participants had to move the blue peg first, then place the red peg in the freed hole, and finally move the green peg). Moreover, tool-use trials were expected to impose higher syntactic complexity than bare-hand trials.

In total, participants completed 96 trials, half of which involved 3-movement sequences. These trials were distributed across six runs, alternating between bare-hand and tool-use blocks.

Results

Neuroimaging data were preprocessed using the fMRIPrep pipeline version 23.2.1 and normalized to the MNI152NLin2009cAsym template space. Functional images were then spatially smoothed with a 4 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel.

Data were modeled at each voxel using the general linear model in SPM at the subject level and contrast of parameters estimates were then tested at the group level. We included nuisance regressors derived from fMRIPrep including anatomical CompCor components (CSF & white matter tissues), the full set of motion parameters (Friston-24), and spike regressors for motion outliers, corresponding to volumes exceeding 0.9 mm of framewise displacement. Due to more than 20% motion outlier scans, one subject was excluded from the motor task analyses, and another subject was excluded from both the motor and language task analyses. Statistical maps were thresholded at p<.005 (uncorrected) at the voxel level followed by FDR correction (p<.05) at the cluster level.

Language task:

We modeled the sentence-encoding phase (first sentence) and the affirmation-response phase (second sentence) with separate regressors. Our contrast of interest was the comparison between complex (ORC) and simple (CC) sentences during the encoding phase (syntax processing) for correct responses only.

As expected, we observed a significant cluster in the left inferior frontal gyrus, pars opercularis (l-IFG, 164 voxels, pFWE = 0.002; qFDR < 0.001), and a cluster in the left caudate nucleus (54 voxels, qFDR = 0.063).  Additional clusters showed significant differences in the left superior temporal gyrus/ sulcus, precuneus, cerebellum, supplementary motor area, bilateral precentral/ middle frontal gyrus, and right superior parietal lobule.

Motor task:

We modeled three phases with separate regressors: reach-to-grasp, peg movements, and return to rest.

We first looked at the effect of sequence complexity, contrasting 3-movement trials with 1-movement trials during the reach-to-grasp phase, independently of the effector used.

As expected, we observed a cluster in the l-IFG (pars triangularis; 112 voxels pFWE < 0.001; qFDR < 0.001), one in the left caudate (60 voxels pFWE = 0.009; qFDR = 0.001) and one in the right caudate (79 voxels pFWE = 0.001; qFDR < 0.001). We observed additional clusters in the left middle frontal gyrus, precuneus, temporal pole, cerebellum, anterior insula, orbitofrontal cortex and posterior temporal cortex.

To assess the effect of tool-use, we contrasted tool trials with hand trials during the reach-to-grasp phase, independently of sequence complexity. 

We observed activation in the left putamen (42 voxels, pFWE = 0.041; qFDR = 0.024). No significant activation was found in the inferior frontal cortex nor in the right basal ganglia. We also observed a large cluster around the left somatosensory cortex.

Overlap:

Overlaying the thresholded maps for each of the three contrasts of interest revealed close activations in several regions but no overlap. Restricting our analyses to the l-IFG and basal ganglia, we observed a small area of overlap in the maps thresholded with p<0.005 (uncorrected) in left caudate and upper part of the IFG pars opercularis. 

Conclusion

Our results show that processing complex syntax or performing complex actions engages brain areas similar to those described in adults2,5. Thus, although these abilities are still maturing in late childhood and early adolescence, they rely on the same brain regions as in adults. 

Our findings also support the idea that linguistic syntax and motor syntax recruit similar brain regions. This co-localization was observed for linguistic syntax and motor sequence complexity in the left inferior frontal gyrus and the left caudate nucleus: these regions showed higher activity when processing syntactically complex sentences compared to simple sentences, as well as higher activity when executing complex compared to simple sequences of action. However, the recruitment of the left inferior frontal gyrus in the complex language and motor conditions corresponded to spatially distinct subregions: linguistic syntax engaged the pars opercularis, whereas motor syntax engaged the pars triangularis. 

The overlap between complex linguistic syntax and tool-use reported in adults was not strongly evident in children. This may indicate a late development of a domain-general syntax system. It could also indicate anatomical interindividual variability across subjects, which is known to be higher in children than in adults. Further analyses constrained by individual functional neuroanatomy and with multivoxel patterns will be conducted.

Altogether, these data provide new insight into the developing brain correlate of supra-domain syntax representation in children and early adolescents.
