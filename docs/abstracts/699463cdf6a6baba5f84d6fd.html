Introduction

Introduction: Presence hallucinations (PH), experienced as non-veridical sensations of someone being around you, are minor hallucinations predictive of development of psychosis, dementia and earlier home placement in Parkinson’s disease (PD)1. The presences are commonly reported as those of familiar others2, however characteristics related to its perceived identity in relation to patients’ clinical, and neuropsychological state and its neural mechanisms have not been investigated. PH has been proposed as stemming from somatomotor conflict between one’s action and received sensory consequence, as demonstrated by a successful robotic induction protocol utilising spatiotemporal conflict of signals between one’s hand movement and the corresponding feedback on the back3. However, this does not fully account for PH phenomenology, with presences predominantly described as distinct anthropomorphic social agents, frequently experienced as well-known close others, rather than a misrepresented self. In this study of functional and neural correlates of PH in PD, we examined PH phenomenology and its potentially distinct relationship to patients’ disease progression, cognitive and motor functions and resting-state functional connectivity between six brain regions: bilateral inferior frontal gyri (IFG), ventral medial premotor cortices (vPMC) and posterior medial temporal gyri (pMTG), encompassing posterior temporal sulci (pSTS). These regions were shown to be involved in the experience of presences and comprise the PH network4, while also being involved in identity perception and fundamental social processing, such as sensitivity to biological motion and theory of mind. 

Methods

Methods: From a sample of 77 PD patients, we recruited 27 non-demented patients with PH, (NiPH=12; 5 women, NuPH=15; 5 women) and investigated their phenomenology of presences, noted from semi-structured interviews. Based on their reports, we categorised them into either the identified PH (iPH-PD) group which can recognize who this presence is from their personal experience or semantic memory (e.g. “sister”) or unidentified PH (uPH-PD) group, which cannot recognize their presence (e.g., “just someone there”). We compared the two groups on their clinical variables (e.g., age, disease duration, dopaminergic medication), disease progression (Hoehn & Yahr stage), motor and daily-life impairment (MDS-UPDRS), cognitive performance (PD-CRS, with fronto-subcortical and posterior cortical subscales), presence of other hallucination modalities, mood (HADS with anxiety and depression subscales), psychiatric state (SAPS), using linear or ordinal regression models. All patients also performed a robotic somatomotor PH-inducing task via spatiotemporal conflicts between voluntary hand movements and tactile feedback delivered to the back (see Figure 1C)4. Three stimulation delays (0, 250 and 500 ms) were applied in a randomised protocol, with patients reporting if they felt a presence on each trial (analysed using logistic regression mixed-effects models). Resting-state fMRI was acquired in an MRI-compatible subset of patients (iPH-PD n=10; uPH-PD n=12). We also performed an ROI-to-ROI analysis of the brain’s resting-state functional connectivity (rsfMRI: NiPH=10, NuPH=12) of the six nodes of the PH-network between groups, using a mixed-effects model and FDR-correction for multiple comparisons. 

Results

Results: Phenomenological analysis of 46 reports of distinct PHs in our sample of 27 PD patients, revealed that among iPH, (1) 73.3% were perceived as first-degree close others (e.g. partner or sibling, p<0.001). The two groups did not differ significantly in their age (p=0.639), disease duration (p=0.986), levodopa medication (p=0.766), mood (anxiety: p=0.662, depression: p=0.437) or delusions (p=0.503). However, compared with uPH-PD, iPH-PD patients had (2) a significantly more advanced disease stage (Hoehn & Yahr, p=0.036), (3) greater impairment of daily-life motor activities (UPDRS-II, p=0.028) and (4) reported visual illusions more than uPH-PD patients (91.7% vs. 53.3%; p=0.043). The groups did not differ in global cognitive performance (PD-CRS total: p=0.513), on posterior cortical (p=0.356), or fronto-subcortical functions (p=0.567). While replicating past results of higher probability of robot-induced PH at higher delays (main effect: p<0.001), the groups sensitivity was comparable (group main effect: p=0.835) and there was no interaction between group and the delay (p=0.984). Resting-state fMRI revealed (5) significantly reduced functional connectivity between the right IFG and right pMTG (p-FDR=0.020) in iPH-PD compared to uPH-PD (see Figure 1). Across both patient groups, this lower right IFG–pMTG connectivity was associated with poorer posterior cortical cognitive performance (Spearman ρ=0.45, p=0.037). 

Conclusion

Conclusions: Our findings distinguish iPH from uPH phenomenologically, behaviourally, and neurally and are compatible with a more advanced form of PD in iPH-PD vs. uPH-PD. Furthermore, iPH-PD patients also report greater daily-life motor impairment, and more visual illusions compared to uPH-PD patients, despite their comparable cognitive performance and equivalent sensitivity to experimentally induced PH. This preserved sensitivity supports a shared self-related somatomotor origin of PH in both groups. However, given that the groups do not differ in age, dopaminergic medication, disease duration, mood, or delusions, their impairment difference cannot fully be accounted for by these clinical and neuropsychological variables. Distinguishing PH phenomenology is therefore clinically relevant, as patients experiencing iPH display impairments more akin to impairments observed in PD patients with structured hallucinations. 

At the neural level, compared to uPH-PD, the iPH-PH patients exhibit selective fronto-temporal disconnection between right IFG and pMTG regions of the PH network, which have been implicated in self-monitoring, executive control, and person-related identity processing, particularly coding for the first degree of closeness5. The association between this disconnection and posterior cognitive dysfunction is compatible with greater involvement of posterior temporal systems in iPH-PD. These findings further support the view that iPH constitutes a more structured form of minor hallucination, potentially intermediate between unidentified PH and structured visual hallucinations. Finally, analysing reports of specific PHs, we found that when presences can be identified, they are predominantly of our significant others of the first-degree, indicating that processes of familiarity are insufficient to generate such overrepresentation of close others. Other brain regions beyond the PH network, related to social processing are likely involved, and should be further explored in future studies.
