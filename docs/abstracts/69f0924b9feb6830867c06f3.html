Introduction

The spinal cord is critical to motor, sensory, autonomic function, and increasingly implicated in neurological disease and human health, yet there have been limited studies of the spinal cord using large-scale population data. Neuroimaging advances have enabled non-invasive characterization of spinal cord anatomy and functions, revealing changes associated with neurological diseases, injury, and biological factors1. Genetically, genotype-specific spinal cord damage has been reported in spinocerebellar ataxias2. These studies demonstrate the potential role of genetic variability in shaping spinal cord structure. However, while genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified hundreds of loci associated with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) derived brain phenotypes, the genetics of the spinal cord remain largely unexplored. This gap reflects a major limitation in our understanding of the genetic basis of the full central nervous system and its relationship to health and disease. A key challenge has been the lack of reliable high-throughput methods to process spinal cord MRI at scale, due to sensitivity of spinal cord imaging to anatomical variability, neck curvature, partial volume effects, motion, and distortion artefacts, making fully automated segmentation, labelling, and quality control difficult, especially in large scale datasets.

Methods

We performed large-scale MRI-based phenotyping of the upper cervical spinal cord (C1–C3) using T1-weighted brain MRI scans from over 40,000 UK Biobank participants (Fig. 1). A fully automated deep learning–based pipeline, optimised for upper cervical spinal cord segmentation and vertebral level labelling and robust to pathological variation, was applied to derive quantitative spinal cord phenotypes1,3. These included mean cross-sectional area (CSA), eccentricity, and anterior–posterior (AP) and right–left (RL) diameters at the C1, C2, and C3 vertebral levels.

After applying rigorous quality control and validation on these quantitative traits, GWAS was conducted to characterise their genetic architecture and estimate heritability. Phenotypic and genetic correlation analyses were performed to assess shared variation between spinal cord traits and cortical, subcortical, and brainstem volumes. Associations between spinal cord imaging phenotypes and a broad range of systemic health outcomes were then evaluated. Polygenic risk scores (PRS) derived from spinal cord GWAS summary statistics were applied in independent samples to validate disease associations identified.

Results

This study presents the first GWAS of spinal cord morphology and the largest effort to date in extracting spinal cord shape metrics from MRI data. We identified We identified 179 unique genetic loci influencing morphology and observed sex-specific associations. Our findings indicated that the spinal cord sizes have significant phenotypic and genetic overlap with brainstem and subcortical volumes including the ventral diencephalon, pallidum, and the thalamus. Leveraging health-related outcomes, we provide evidence that spinal cord structure was associated with a wide range of neurological, metabolic, and systemic conditions, such as multiple sclerosis, neuropathies, diabetes, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, highlighting its importance as the conduit of nerve signals and its multifactorial vulnerability (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

Our findings establish spinal cord as a heritable, biologically informative, and clinically relevant structure. They extend imaging genetics research beyond the brain, revealing shared and distinct genetic influences throughout the central nervous system. The results highlight the potential of spinal cord derived measures as promising new biomarkers for risk in ongoing and future clinical studies across the spectrum of human disease.
