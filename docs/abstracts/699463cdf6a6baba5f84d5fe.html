Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by widely reported abnormalities in brain network connectivity. In recent years, advances in dynamic functional connectivity analysis have revealed that connectivity alterations in ASD exhibit distinct patterns across different connectivity states. 

Against this backdrop, dynamic phase synchronization analysis provides a powerful framework for investigating brain function abnormalities in ASD.  It leverages neural oscillation phase to assess instantaneous, nonlinear coupling between networks with high temporal resolution. To effectively characterize dynamic phase synchronization in high-dimensional neuroimaging data, Leading Eigenvector Dynamics Analysis (LEiDA)1 extracts the leading eigenvector from each time point's phase synchronization matrix, representing the dominant global pattern, and then clustering these eigenvectors to identify several recurring brain states. By focusing on the leading eigenvectors, LEiDA reduces data dimensionality and mitigates noise interference, while enhancing the reliability and computational efficiency of subsequent clustering analysis. However, the inherent sign ambiguity problem in eigenvalue decomposition can lead to inconsistencies in eigenvector orientation, thereby compromising the accuracy of clustering results. 

To address this issue, this study proposes an improved approach termed SC-LEiDA, which integrates spectral clustering into the traditional LEiDA framework. Building on this methodological advancement, the present research applies SC-LEiDA to analyze dynamic phase synchronization in ASD, aiming to systematically investigate abnormalities in different phase synchronization states. This work seeks to provide new insights into the neurodynamic mechanisms underlying ASD.

Methods

Data: We analyzed resting-state fMRI data of 264 male subjects (132 ASD, 132 healthy controls (CON)) from 7 sites of the ABIDE II dataset,2 with full IQ higher than 80 and age/IQ matching within each site. There were 184 subjects (92 ASD, 92 CON) having records of Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS) T scores.

SC-LEiDA: The flowchart of SC-LEiDA is showing in Fig.1. After extracting time courses using a 400-node cortical atlas,3 the multivariate empirical mode decomposition (MEMD)4 was used to decompose time courses into five intrinsic mode functions (IMFs), each of which represents a specific frequency band, as shown in the gray box. The SC-LEiDA was applied to the IMF2 (0.059 ± 0.005Hz), IMF3 (0.031 ± 0.004Hz), IMF4 (0.017 ± 0.002Hz) and IMF5 (0.011 ± 0.002Hz), as the frequency ranges of these IMFs fall within the conventional bandwidth used for computing functional connectivity. Then, using Hilbert Transform (HT), the phase of each time point was calculated, and the phase synchronization between any two nodes was defined as the cosine of their relative phase, as shown in the green box. After getting the phase synchronization matrix of each time point, the eigenvalue decomposition was applied to them and computed the leading eigenvector of each time point. Then, as shown in the blue box, the spectral clustering was used to compute the cluster indices of time points. The spectral clustering is grounded in graph theory, where clustering is reformulated as a graph partitioning problem through the construction of a similarity graph across time points. In this study, we adopt the spectral clustering algorithm proposed by Ng et al.5 The distance between two time points in the similarity graph is defined as the absolute value of the Pearson correlation coefficient between their corresponding leading eigenvectors. This definition effectively circumvents distance bias caused by sign ambiguity in the eigenvectors. Subsequently, the degree matrix and the normalized graph Laplacian are computed from the similarity graph. Following eigenvalue decomposition of the normalized graph Laplacian, the eigenvectors associated with the smallest eigenvalues reveal the underlying substructure within the similarity graph. Finally, these eigenvectors are submitted to k-means clustering to assign each time point a cluster index. In this study, the definition of each phase synchronization state was based on the cluster indices and the original phase synchronization matrices, as shown in the red box. The leading eigenvector of the average phase synchronization matrix within a cluster (i.e., a phase synchronization state), was used to represent this state.

Statistical analysis: After calculating the average phase synchronization matrix for each state within individual subjects, the network-based statistic (NBS) method was used to compare phase synchronization strength between the ASD and CON groups. NBS can identify subnetworks that exhibit significant group differences. To further assess the relationship between phase synchronization and social deficits, partial least squares correlation (PLSC) analysis was applied to the subnetworks derived from the NBS results. Permutation tests (5,000 iterations) were employed to assess the significance of the PLSR model. Bootstrap resampling (5,000 iterations) was used to evaluate the significance of phase synchronization between each pair of nodes.

Results

We found four phase synchronization states that were stable among all the IMFs, see spatial maps of them in Fig. 2(a). The four phase synchronization states identified across various IMFs exhibit similar spatial distributions, so only the results for IMF2 are presented here. State 1 is characterized by phase synchronization between the visual network (Vis) and the dorsal attention network (DAN), coupled with anti-phase synchronization between the default mode network (DMN) and these two networks. State 2 exhibited anti-phase synchronization between functionally unimodal regions (somatomotor network (SM) and Vis) and transmodal regions (executive control network (ECN), DMN and limbic network (Lim)). State 3 was characterized by anti-phase synchronization between task-positive networks (ECN and dorsal attention network (DAN)) and the task-negative network (DMN). State 4 demonstrated anti-phase synchronization within unimodal regions, specifically between the SM and Vis networks.

Combining NBS and PLSC analyses, we identified patterns of aberrant phase synchronization in specific states that were associated with the severity of social deficits in ASD, as illustrated in Fig. 2 (b)-(f). The colored maps represent the spatial distribution for each state. In the connection graphs, red and blue connections denote significantly higher and lower phase synchronization in the ASD, respectively. Only synchronizations that survived significance testing in the PLSC bootstrap analysis are displayed. The scatter plots depict the correlation between the first pair of latent variables from the PLSC analysis, where u1 is the latent variable for phase synchronization strength, v1 is the latent variable for SRS scores, r is their correlation coefficient, and p is the significance level derived from permutation testing.

 In State 2 of IMF2, people with ASD demonstrated increased phase synchronization between unimodal and transmodal regions, particularly between the Vis and prefrontal areas, while exhibiting decreased synchronization within unimodal regions and between unimodal regions and the salience network (SN) or DAN (Fig. 2b). For State 3 of IMF2, people with ASD showed reduced synchronization between Vis and both the SN and DAN (Fig. 2c). In State 1 of IMF4, decreased synchronization was observed within the SM and between SM and adjacent higher-order functional areas, including the angular gyrus and temporal regions of the DMN (Fig. 2d). State 3 of IMF4 was characterized by enhanced synchronization related to the DAN in ASD, specifically between DAN and the insula, ECN, and SM (Fig. 2e). Finally, in State 3 of IMF5, people with ASD exhibited decreased synchronization between the auditory area of SM and the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), between the primary motor cortex and the insula, and between the orbitofrontal cortex and temporal regions as well as the PCC (Fig. 2f).

Conclusion

This study optimized the LEiDA algorithm based on spectral clustering and identified four distinct phase synchronization states with similar spatial distributions across subdivided frequency bands, suggesting scale invariance in brain network connectivity. Utilizing NBS and PLSC analyses, state-specific aberrant phase synchronization patterns were detected in individuals with ASD.

State 2 reflects functional segregation between unimodal and transmodal regions. In the ASD group, they exhibited reduced segregation between unimodal and transmodal areas on the state2 of IMF2, indicating potential disruption in the hierarchical processing of information from primary to higher-order regions.

Both State 1 and State 3 reflect functional antagonism during internal versus external information processing. Notably, phase synchronization abnormalities in individuals with ASD were predominantly observed in sensory (SM, Vis) and attentional (SN, DAN) functional areas, suggesting disrupted attentional mechanisms.
