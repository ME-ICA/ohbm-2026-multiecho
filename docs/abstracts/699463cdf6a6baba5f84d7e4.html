Introduction

Modern analytic frameworks move beyond static connectivity, using measures of dynamical information flow across brain networks to capture disruptions in mental illness and to identify targets for neuromodulatory interventions such as noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS).

Yet, such higher-order metrics are often derived from intrinsic activity; i.e., resting-state fMRI data. By contrast, tasks constrain brain function toward specific functional domains. When combined with concurrent NIBS, task-based neuroimaging moves beyond association: it establishes causal relationships between neural function and behaviour, offering a window into therapeutic mechanisms.

If the clinical efficacy of NIBS depends on its ability to modify behaviour via targeted changes in brain function, stimulation should be personalized to the neural feature most predictive of that behaviour. While higher-order metrics grounded in information theory and fluid dynamics capture information integration and propagation across the brain, it is unclear whether they outperform network connectivity metrics in explaining trial-by-trial variations in task performance. Therefore, the first objective of this study is to test whether network-based or higher-order dynamical metrics better predict task performance. 

Achieving this objective also completes the first step in a framework utilizing whole-brain models to identify the region that, when stimulated, most effectively shifts the neural metric towards improved task performance^1. 

Here we present the pilot data establishing the feasibility for a sham-controlled, within-subject study testing whether personalised, neuroimaging-informed transcranial interferential stimulation (TIS) more effectively modulates both brain function and behaviour vs generic or sham stimulation. These methods and pilot results are in submission as a registered report; data collection will begin in January 2026.

Methods

Across three experimental sessions, participants will receive sham, personalized, and generic TIS while performing the Iowa Gambling Task (IGT) during fMRI acquisition. The IGT captures trait-like mechanisms of learning and decision-making under uncertainty. IGT task performance is disrupted across multiple conditions, including addiction, depression, and Parkinson’s disease.

During the first visit, participants undergo sham stimulation, providing task-fMRI data used to identify personalized TIS targets. We will employ our previously validated pipeline for personalized whole-brain modeling using Virtual Brain Twins (VBTs)^1. This approach unfolds in three steps: (1) identify neural metrics that best predict task performance; (2) construct individualized whole-brain models whose parameters are optimized to reproduce those neural metrics; and (3) systematically perturb the whole-brain models. The perturbation parameters that shift the target metric furthest toward improved task performance is the personalised TIS target. As part of the first step, we will compare whether network-based vs higher-order information-theoretic metrics better predict task performance.

FMRI preprocessing utilises fmriprep and AFNI’s 3dTProject to perform registration, regression of 25 confounds (framewise displacement and motion parameters and temporal and spatial derivatives), smoothing (8mm Gaussian kernel FWHM), and filtering (0.01-0.20 Hz). 

Cortical regions are defined by registering the 200, 400, and 600-region Schaefer atlas from standard to subject space. Subcortical regions are defined anatomically via FreeSurfer. Timeseries are computed as the mean across all voxels in a region. 

Network-based measures are computed from phase-based instantaneous functional connectivity (iFC). The pairwise cosine phase differences are computed from Hilbert transformed timeseries. Mean coherence between regions in 7 canonical networks (e.g., default mode and control networks) are computed in the iFCs, yielding dynamical, trial-wise network metrics.

Higher-order metrics capturing the directionality and complexity of information propagation across hierarchical cortical gradients are computed as in^2. 

Finally, random forest regression assesses the relationship between task performance and both network-based and higher-order metrics. These results answer our first research question;  whether higher-order dynamical markers provide superior prediction of task performance compared to network coherence.

In step two of identifying the personalised targets for TIS, each participant’s structural and functional data instantiate their whole-brain model. Regions are represented as Stuart-Landau oscillators operating near Hopf bifurcation point. Model outputs are optimised to match the empirically derived target neural metric by iteratively tuning model connectivity. 

In step three, the effects of TIS on each region is simulated by iterative perturbation of the bifurcation parameter. The region where “stimulation” maximally enhances the target neural metric is a subject’s personalised TIS target. 

Then, the electrode configuration to deliver TIS to the target region is determined using finite-element electric field modeling on individual head models. 

Participants return for two more visits, and receive personalized and generic TIS in counterbalanced order. The left nucleus accumbens (NAc) serves as the generic target, given its well-established role in reward-guided decision-making and demonstrated susceptibility to TIS modulation. The target amplitude (4 mA) and beat frequency (130 Hz) follows reports showing that striatal high-gamma TIS is well tolerated and can modulate both neural activity and decision behavior.

We collected pilot data in 7 participants (2 visits each) to test the feasibility of the task-MRI setup, set up the computational pipelines, assess task performance reliability with concurrent fMRI collection, and delivery of TI in the MRI scanner without artifact.

Results

We collected pilot data to test the feasibility of three aspects of the study: (1) feasibility of the task-MRI setup, (2) task performance reliability with concurrent fMRI collection, and (3) delivery of TI in the MRI scanner without artifact. 

Pilot data to establish the feasibility of task-MRI, set up the computational pipelines, and assess task performance reliability was collected in 7 participants across two separate visits. 

After performing the IGT in the scanner on their first visit, participants were asked to complete a Participant Debrief Questionnaire, which gathered feedback on how engaging they found the task (scale from 0, not at all, to 10, very engaged), the consistency of their concentration during the task (not consistent at all, somewhat inconsistent, neutral, mostly consistent, very consistent), and clarity of task instruction (yes or no). Responses show the task was engaging (range 8-10), that concentration was sufficient (range mostly consistent to vary consistent), and task instructions were clear (all but one responded yes, with the “no” reporting that after a few trials they felt they understood the task). These responses support the feasibility of scaling the task fMRI setup from a pilot to full cohort. 

Analyses of task data suggest task performance is reliable across sessions. A paired T-test found no significant difference in neither TP earned on visit 1 and visit 2 (p=0.27, df=6, t=1.23, sd=21) nor the mean EvU per visit (p=0.47, df=6, t=-0.78, sd=±970). We examined the stability of EvU distributions across visits using Jensen-Shannon divergence, a symmetric measure of distributional difference. Statistical significance was assessed via permutation testing (10,000 permutations), which tests whether the observed divergence exceeds that expected under the null hypothesis of no systematic difference between visits. The EvU distribution remained stable across visits for 6 out of 7 subjects (all p>0.05), with only one subject showing significant distributional change (p=0.03). 

To assess whether the TI-MRI setup or administering TIS in the scanner introduced artifacts, we acquired structural and functional images of a saline-filled phantom with and without placement of the external coupling block (ECB). The ECB was placed on the right side of the phantom, corresponding to the intended position in human participants. Structural and functional sequences matched those planned for the in vivo study. Three additional fMRI runs were acquired while delivering TIS at the target current intensity (4 mA per channel) and carrier frequencies (9000 and 9130 Hz).

Visual inspection of the phantom images with and without the ECB did not reveal noticeable artifacts in either the structural or functional data. To quantify potential ECB- and stimulation-related effects, temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR) was computed for three predefined “points” (right, left, and centre of the phantom). For each point, a binary mask was generated by inflating a 5 mm-radius sphere around the visually selected centroid coordinate. TSNR was defined as the mean signal within non-zero voxels divided by the temporal standard deviation within the mask (mean and standard deviation estimated with fslstats).

Across locations, introduction of the ECB was associated with small reductions in tSNR, typically below 10% of the tSNR obtained without the ECB. The only deviation from this pattern was observed in the centre point during TIS, where tSNR increased by approximately 12–14% relative to the fMRI acquisition without the ECB. However, these differences are consistent with modest spatially local signal fluctuations rather than widespread degradation of data quality. We also note that the tSNR in the left point during the T1-weighted scan was nearly twice that of the right point. However, the change in tSNR between scans with and without the ECB was minimal at both points, indicating that this asymmetry is unlikely to reflect ECB-related artefacts.

Conclusion

This pilot phase demonstrates the technical and methodological feasibility of an integrative framework linking individualized computational modeling, task-based fMRI, and personalized TIS. The results validate the reliability of our task design, confirm stable behavioral performance across repeated sessions, and show that the concurrent delivery of TIS within the MRI environment can be achieved without introducing substantial imaging artifacts. Together, these findings provide the essential foundation for scaling to the full within-subject, sham-controlled study beginning in 2026.

By combining hierarchical metrics of dynamical information flow with established network-based measures, the study advances a data-driven approach for identifying and modulating neural features predictive of behavior. This is achieved by utilising personalised whole-brain modeling, which offers a principled route to determine stimulation targets most likely to enhance task performance. In doing so, the framework directly addresses one of the central challenges of noninvasive brain stimulation: its typically weak and heterogeneous effects across individuals. Such variability likely arises from differences in anatomy, network organization, and neurophysiological state, all of which shape how electric fields interact with brain circuits. Our approach accounts for such intersubject variability by tailoring stimulation to the neural metric with the strongest association to an individual’s task performance. 

Importantly, the pilot outcomes support that task-based paradigms can be effectively paired with noninvasive neuromodulation inside the scanner, providing both control and interpretability unattainable in resting-state paradigms. As data collection proceeds, the study will directly test whether stimulation informed by individualized neural metrics yields superior modulation of brain function and task performance as compared to generic, one-size-fits all targeting. Success in this next phase would establish a generalizable pipeline for precision neuromodulation, with stimulation parameters guided by model-derived, subject-specific brain structure and function.
