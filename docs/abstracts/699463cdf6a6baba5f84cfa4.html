Introduction

Virtual reality (VR) applications have gained increasing popularity as tools for treating cognitive deficits, such as attentional disorders and hemispatial neglect, by providing more naturalistic testing environments^1. These approaches are based on the assumption that VR presentations provide a sufficiently realistic rendering of 3D space to activate automatic cognitive processes related to size and distance perception, such as distinguishing between peripersonal space (PPS = near grasping space) and extrapersonal space (EPS = distant out-of-reach)^2. However, few studies have systematically examined how specific VR features, such as stereoscopic presentation, influence neural processes related to spatial encoding in naturalistic environments. Additionally, studies investigating the neural correlates of PPS/EPS often fail to account for the object’s absolute retinal size. The present study investigates the neural mechanisms underlying PPS and EPS encoding in VR across different binocular presentation modes while controlling for retinal size.

Methods

Forty-four healthy participants completed a visual discrimination task – judging whether objects (cups or mugs) appeared upright or inverted – while undergoing fMRI. The virtual environment was displayed in stereoscopic 3D or monoscopic 2D through MR-compatible video goggles. Objects were positioned in either PPS or EPS and varied in size to control for potential effects of retinal coverage. Functional images were preprocessed using a standard pipeline including field-map correction to compensate for B0 distortions induced by the MR goggles, and analyzed using a general linear model.

Results

Objects presented in PPS elicited stronger activation in medial and dorsal visual areas as well as the intraparietal sulcus (IPS; see Figure 2a). In contrast, objects in EPS preferentially recruited ventral occipital regions (2b). These effects remained robust after controlling for absolute retinal size (2f). Smaller objects induced stronger responses in attention-related frontal regions compared with larger objects (2d). Stereoscopic 3D presentation additionally enhanced activation within the IPS, further reinforcing the PPS-related network (2e).

Conclusion

Our results show that VR reliably differentiates PPS and EPS processing, independently of an object’s retinal size. Overall, objects in PPS engage dorsal-stream regions, particularly the IPS, which plays a key role in representing grasp-related affordances^3, whereas objects in EPS are mainly processed in early ventral visual areas^4. Notably, stereoscopic presentation further boosted IPS activity, suggesting an additive effect on PPS-related processing. These findings indicate that stereoscopic VR presentation might have an additive effect on PPS processing which could be beneficial for therapeutic applications treating deficits in spatial attention in immersive 3D environments.
