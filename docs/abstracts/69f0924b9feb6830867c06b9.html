Introduction

Preterm birth, defined as birth before 37 weeks of gestation, is associated with altered brain development, impacting neural and cognitive outcomes.1 The claustrum is a poorly understood small forebrain region that has extraordinarily widely distributed connections in the brain2 and is associated with attention and executive control.3 Due to both its widespread connectivity and link with subplate neuron development, the claustrum may be particularly vulnerable to disruptions caused by preterm birth. While previous research demonstrated that premature birth is indeed associated with claustrum structure alterations in both neonates and adults,4-5 the impact of prematurity on the claustrum’s connectivity across development remains unclear. Plasticity of the claustrum’s connectivity may be impacted by social influences during early development, but has rarely been studied.

In this study, we investigated the structural connectivity of the claustrum to the cortex using in-vivo diffusion-weighted MRI in two independent cohorts representing distinct developmental stages: (1) preterm and term-born neonates from the Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP), and (2) very preterm/very low birthweight (VP/VLBW) adults and term-born controls from the Bavarian Longitudinal Study (BLS). Our goals were as follows: first, to test for group differences in claustrum-cortex connectivity between preterm-born and full-term individuals at those two developmental stages. Second, to test whether early environmental and social conditions impact connectivity, and third, to test whether altered connectivity is linked to cognitive outcomes - early cognition at 18 months as well as full-scale IQ and attention in adulthood.

Methods

We conducted a diffusion-weighted MRI study with two cohorts, representing separate stages of development: first, 72 preterm-born neonates (scanned at term-equivalent age; mean gestation 31.9±3.34) and 72 term-born newborn infants (mean gestation 39.85±1.22) from the publicly available Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) dataset; second, 65 very preterm/very low birthweight-born (VP/VLBW) adults (mean gestation 30.20±1.95) and 81 full-term controls (mean gestation 39.70±0.99), aged 26 years, from the Bavarian Longitudinal Study (BLS). The dHCP cohort was matched to the BLS cohort for gestational age (within the term-born and preterm-born subgroups, respectively) and gender. Diffusion-weighted images (DWI) were co-registered with T1-weighted images (BLS cohort) or T2-weighted images (dHCP cohort) and preprocessed using the PreQual pipeline, which included MP-PCA denoising, inter-scan normalization, and generation of a synthetic undistorted b0 for distortion correction. The bilateral claustra were segmented in the structural images using a deep learning-based tool developed in-house (Figure 1, Panel A). Probabilistic tractography as implemented in the FSL toolbox was used to assess the structural connectivity from the claustrum to primary cortices (sensory, motor, visual, and auditory), associative cortices (prefrontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital), hippocampus, and anterior and posterior cingulate cortices (Figure 1, Panel B). Connection probability (CP) was then calculated for each ROI and separately for the right and left hemispheres (11 ROIs x 2 sides = 22 in total) as the number of streamlines reaching a given target region (i.e., the waytotal) divided by the total number of streamlines seeded from the claustrum. Outcome measures were harmonized for site/scanner differences prior to analysis using neuroCombat. 

Early social and economic environment (assessed in both cohorts) and pregnancy-related conditions (assessed in the BLS cohort only) were assessed using standardized metrics derived from structured interviews and clinical records conducted during pregnancy, at birth, or at five months postpartum. Socioeconomic status (SES) was available in both cohorts, while additional measures - parent-infant relationship index (PIRI), psychosocial stress index (PSI), and optimality pregnancy score, were available only in the adult (BLS) cohort.

In adults, general cognitive functioning and attention/executive performance were assessed using the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, 3rd edition (Full-Scale IQ), and the Attention Network Test (ANT), respectively. In the neonatal dHCP cohort, cognitive development at 18 months was assessed using the cognition composite score of the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (Bayley-III). 

ANCOVAs were performed to compare claustrum structural connectivity across all target regions of interest (ROIs) between preterm (PT) and full-term (FT) groups in each cohort, controlling for age and sex. Cohen’s d was then extracted to assess the effect size. Finally, general linear models (GLMs) were used within the preterm-born groups to test the relationship between tractography-derived connectivity measures and birth-related metrics (gestational age and birthweight), social and environmental factors (SES, PIRI, PSI, optimality pregnancy), as well as cognition at 18 months of age for the neonatal (dHCP) cohort or general cognition and attention (FS-IQ, ANT) for the adult (BLS) cohort, always controlling for age and sex. Statistical significance was evaluated after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction (q = 0.05).

Results

Preterm birth was associated with altered claustrum-cortical connectivity in both adults and neonates. In the adult (BLS) cohort, VP/VLBW participants showed significantly reduced connection probability (CP) to occipital and parietal associative cortices and the posterior cingulate cortex, alongside increased CP to prefrontal and temporal associative regions. The neonates (dHCP cohort) showed a broader pattern, less concentrated in associative cortices, with significantly reduced CP to parietal, prefrontal, and temporal associative cortices, posterior cingulate, as well as primary visual and sensory primary cortices, with only the primary auditory cortex showing higher CP in preterm infants. See Figure 1, Panel C, for effect sizes across all ROIs and both cohorts. 

Environmental, social, or pregnancy factors did not seem to contribute to these patterns. Socioeconomic status of parents at birth showed no association with CP either in neonates or in adults. Measures of parent-infant relationship, parental stress, and pregnancy factors (PIRI, PSI, and optimality pregnancy) also did not show any robust associations with connectivity metrics.

Claustrum connectivity showed a relationship with cognitive functioning at both developmental stages. In preterm-born adults, higher CP to the right primary motor cortex was significantly associated with better performance in the ANT orienting task (GLM: ß = 11.76, p = 0.000058, pFDR = 0.0013; Figure 2, Panel B), with a similar trend for the right primary sensory cortex that did not survive FDR correction (GLM: ß = 6.66, p = 0.030), while we observed no associations with general IQ. In preterm-born neonates, CP at birth to parietal associative cortex and hippocampus predicted cognitive performance at 18 months (GLM: ß = 109.22, p = 0.0027, pFDR = 0.043 and ß = 4736.77, p = 0.0039, pFDR = 0.043, respectively; Figure 2, Panel A), with additional uncorrected relationships in occipital associative, anterior cingulate, and primary sensory cortices.

Conclusion

Our findings, based on two independent cohorts representing distinct stages of development, demonstrate (1) that preterm birth is associated with altered structural connectivity of the claustrum across cortical regions and developmental stages. While differences in connectivity to certain target ROIs were observed in both the neonatal and the adult cohort (such as to the prefrontal associative cortex), structural connectivity to other regions was altered only in neonates or only in adults. This pattern supports a model in which early changes in claustrum connectivity as a potential consequence of premature birth are not stable over time, but instead undergo changes and adaptations during further maturation. An actual longitudinal study design would be an important next step to follow these trajectories within the same individuals across their life span.

(2) We found no evidence that early-life environmental and social factors, or parental stress, explain perinatal or adult structural connectivity of the claustrum, indicating that the developmental trajectory of cortical claustrum connectivity may be more a consequence of intrinsic maturation processes rather than these environmental factors we examined.  

(3) Importantly, altered claustrum connectivity was linked to cognitive functioning in both cohorts: neonatal CP predicted cognitive performance at 18 months, and adult CP was specifically related to attention, as measured by the ANT task's orienting subscore, but not to general cognition, as measured by FS-IQ. 

Together, our findings from this study position the claustrum as a structure sensitive to premature birth, whose connectivity alterations change with age, and have measurable functional consequences in both infancy and adulthood.
