Introduction

Time perception is a fundamental cognitive function that enables humans to estimate, discriminate, and reproduce temporal intervals. Understanding the neural mechanisms underlying temporal cognition requires examination of both behavioral performance and associated brain dynamics. In the present study, we investigated human discrimination of time intervals in the supra-second domain using a pairwise comparison paradigm [1] combined with electroencephalography (EEG) recordings.

The central aim of this research is to identify the neural oscillatory signatures of time perception as measured by EEG.

Forty healthy volunteers (16 men, 24 women; age range 18.8–48.4 years, mean 28.4 ± 8.0 years) participated in two experimental sessions separated by approximately one month (median interval: 34.5 days, range: 23–84 days). This test-retest design was adopted to evaluate the reproducibility and stability of behavioral and electrophysiological measures over an extended period, as well as to assess possible effects of varied task instructions.

During each session, participants performed 72 trials of a temporal duration discrimination (TDD) task while EEG was recorded. In each trial, two time intervals marked by visual stimuli were presented sequentially, separated by a brief pause (1.6s). Participants indicated which interval was longer or shorter by pressing designated response buttons. To minimize motor artifacts in EEG recordings, responses were delayed until a prompt appeared.

Methods

Experimental Procedure

Participants were seated in a dimly lit room in a comfortable chair with head-back support. EEG electrodes were attached, and participants faced a 24" LCD monitor (Eizo FlexScan EV2436W) positioned at viewing distance. Two push-buttons mounted on the left and right sides of the seat served as response devices. Participants were instructed to sit quietly, minimize eye-blinking, and avoid timing strategies such as subvocal counting or foot tapping.

Each session began with three warm-up trials, followed by 72 experimental trials. In each trial, two time intervals marked by a visual stimulus were presented successively, separated by a 1.6 s inter-stimulus interval (ISI). Participants indicated which interval was longer or shorter by pressing the left-hand or right-hand button for the 1st or 2nd stimulus, respectively. To minimize motor artifacts in EEG recordings, responses were delayed until prompted. No performance feedback was provided. Task instructions ("longer" vs. "shorter") varied factorially between sessions, with ten subjects assigned to each of the four instruction combinations.

Apparatus and Stimuli

The temporal duration discrimination (TDD) task was implemented on a HP Elite 7500 computer running Scientific Linux 7.4. Stimulus presentation and response registration were controlled by a proprietary C program based on the SDL2 graphics library, which also sent synchronization signals to the EEG recording system. The duration carrier stimulus was a light gray square (55 × 55 mm, visual angle 1.1° × 1.1°) displayed on a black background. A constant-sum design was employed: nine stimulus classes (k = −4 to +4) were defined, where s₁,ₖ = 4.8 − 0.4k and s₂,ₖ = 4.8 + 0.4k represent durations of the first and second stimuli, respectively. The sum s₁,ₖ + s₂,ₖ remained constant at 9.6 s, while the difference s₂,ₖ − s₁,ₖ varied from −3.2 s to +3.2 s in 0.8 s steps, covering duration ratios from 1:2 to 2:1. Each stimulus class was presented eight times in a randomized order (identical across all subjects and sessions), totaling 72 trials per session.

EEG Recordings and Preprocessing

EEG was recorded from 36 scalp locations (International 10–10 System subset) using a BioSDA09 digital amplifier (M&I Ltd., Prague) at 1000 Hz sampling rate, and 200 Hz low-pass filter. Artifact-contaminated epochs were manually rejected, and data were decimated to 500 Hz. Independent component analysis (ICA) was applied, and components reflecting blinks, saccades, heartbeat, or muscle activity were removed based on visual inspection of spectra, variance, localization, and time courses. All electrodes were re-referenced to the average.

Phase-Amplitude Coupling Analysis

Complex Morlet wavelets (4 cycles) extracted band-limited phases (4–28 Hz, 2 Hz steps) and amplitudes (24–64 Hz, 2 Hz steps). Phase-amplitude coupling (PAC) was calculated using mean vector length and normalized to surrogate distributions (1000 iterations) to obtain PACz values. Two frequency-of-interest (FOI) windows were identified: 10 Hz phase × 24 Hz amplitude and 26 Hz phase × 58 Hz amplitude (±2 Hz).

Band Limited Power Fluctuation Analysis

To investigate task-related EEG oscillatory dynamics, complex Morlet wavelets (4 cycles) were applied to extract band-limited power. The signal envelope was computed from the wavelet transformation, and power series were averaged across trials. For each participant and electrode, power was z-normalized relative to baseline (−400 to −100 ms before stimulus 1 onset) to assess EEG power increases and decreases above the level of intrinsic variance.

Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was computed to assess the stability of EEG markers across sessions [2]. Linear mixed models [3] were implemented to analyze the relationship between task-related EEG markers and participants' timing precision and accuracy during the task.

Results

The EEG signatures demonstrated a high level of inter-session stability. The most prominent changes were observed in the theta, alpha, and beta bands, while gamma-band oscillations showed only a small, non-significant increase after the first stimulus. EEG activity exhibited similar changes following the onset of both the first and second stimuli, with the largest difference observed in the beta-band activity, which showed a greater decrease after the second stimulus. The inter-stimulus interval revealed qualitatively different activation patterns compared with those during stimulus presentation, particularly in the theta and alpha bands. During this interval, alpha activity increased relative to baseline, and induced theta activity appeared in different regions compared with stimulus presentation.

Theta Oscillations

The theta activity increased both during the stimulus presentation and inter-stimulus interval compared to baseline.The highest increase in theta amplitude was observed at 200ms after the stimulus onset in frontal, fronto-central, and occipital regions during the stimulus presentation. Contrary, midline frontal and central regions exhibited an increase in theta activity during the inter-stimulus interval at time from 100ms to 500ms after the first stimulus offset.

Alpha Oscillations

Alpha activity decreased during stimulus presentation but increased during the inter-stimulus interval compared to baseline. The largest decrease in alpha activity was observed around 500 ms after stimulus onset, particularly in the occipital region. In contrast, the parietal regions showed increased alpha activity during the inter-stimulus interval, beginning approximately 800 ms after the offset of the first stimulus and lasting until the onset of the second stimulus.

Beta Oscillations

Beta desynchronization was observed during both the stimulus presentation and the inter-stimulus interval compared with baseline. The most pronounced decrease in beta amplitude occurred around 400 ms after stimulus onset, particularly during the second stimulus presentation in the parietal and fronto-central regions.

Two local maxima of phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) were observed in the comodulogram: one between the alpha and beta bands and another between the beta and high-gamma bands. PAC increased during the task compared with baseline, while the averaged task-related PAC remained highly stable across sessions.

Alpha-Beta coupling

Specific phase–amplitude coupling (PAC) was observed in the parietal regions during the task. In contrast, the PAC maxima were located more occipitally during the baseline and the inter-stimulus interval.

Linear model of precission

Although the coefficient map spans the entire time–frequency window during stimulus presentation, the highest positive values are located in the alpha band. This highlights the importance of alpha desynchronization in predicting timing precision, with greater desynchronization associated with lower precision.

Linear model of accuracy

The coefficient map indicates the strongest relationship between PAC and timing accuracy around the PAC maxima observed during the task. Alpha–beta PAC showed a negative relationship with timing accuracy, predominantly in the parietal regions. However, in frontal areas, higher PAC was associated with lower accuracy, whereas in parietal areas, higher PAC corresponded to higher accuracy. 

Conclusion

This study identified robust and reproducible EEG oscillatory signatures of time perception in the supra-second range. Task-related neural dynamics were characterized by distinct patterns of theta, alpha, and beta oscillations, with specific temporal and spatial profiles during stimulus presentation and the inter-stimulus interval. Theta activity increased in frontal and occipital regions during stimulus processing, while parietal alpha activity emergded during the inter-stimulus interval. Amount of alpha band desynchronization shown relation to the precission of the subjects. This observation can be related to the inhibitory function of the alpha rythm.  Participants capable of inhibiting the visual stimulus demonstrated superior timing precision.

We demonstrated that cross-frequency coupling plays a functional role in time perception. Alpha-beta phase-amplitude coupling in parietal regions shown to be a possible time perception mechanism, showing region-specific relationships with timing accuracy: higher coupling in parietal areas was associated with better timing accuracy. Frontal coupling showed the opposite pattern. 

Critically, all identified EEG markers exhibited high test-retest reliability across sessions separated by one month, as confirmed by intraclass correlation analyses.  Alpha desynchronization emerged as the strongest predictor of timing precision, while alpha-beta coupling predicted accuracy, underscoring the distinct neural mechanisms underlying these two aspects of temporal performance.
