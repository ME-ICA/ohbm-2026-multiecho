Introduction

Inhibitory control constitutes a core executive function enabling the suppression of impulsive responses. ^1 Traditional EEG analyses typically investigate this mechanism by isolating local oscillatory power (e.g., midfrontal theta) or pairwise connectivity (e.g., coherence). However, these approaches often fail to capture the holistic nature of cognitive control: how distributed cortical nodes synthesize diverse oscillatory dynamics into a unified, functional whole.

To address this, we applied Integrated Information Theory (IIT) to quantify the causal "irreducibility" of brain networks—the extent to which a system integrates information beyond the sum of its independent parts.^2,3 A key novelty of our approach lies in defining the network state not by a single frequency, but by the simultaneous activity across five canonical bands (delta through gamma). This allows the metric Phi (Φ) to capture the dynamic integration of multi-frequency information within functional networks. We hypothesized that effective inhibition depends on this capacity for high-level spectral integration and investigated its relationship with autonomic regulation via heart rate variability (HRV). ^4

Methods

Participants and Experimental Protocol

Fourteen healthy right-handed adults were recruited from the Instituto S. Anna (8 females: 35 ± 12 years; 6 males: 44 ± 9 years). Inclusion criteria comprised right-handedness, normal or corrected vision, and absence of dementia, DSM-5 depression, chronic medical or cardiovascular disease, and psychoactive or vasoactive medication use. Participants abstained from caffeine and tobacco for ≥4 h before testing. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Regione Calabria (No. 172) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Testing took place in a sound-attenuated (<35 dB), dimly lit (~3 lux) room. Participants were seated 70 cm from a 24-inch monitor and completed a modified Go/No-Go task derived from the Rule Shift Cards paradigm. The session included a 5-min eyes-open resting baseline, followed by two 6-min task blocks separated by a 5-min break. Visual stimuli were red, white, or chess-patterned squares (12 × 12 cm), presented for 500 ms with a 1,500 ms inter-stimulus interval. In Task 1 (low complexity), responses were required to white squares. In Task 2 (high complexity), responses were required only for two consecutive identical stimuli, engaging working memory and inhibitory control.

Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

Electroencephalography(EEG) and electrocardiography(ECG) signals were acquired at 256 Hz using a NeXus-32 system (Mind Media). EEG was recorded from 21 Ag/AgCl electrodes referenced to a common reference (impedances <5 kΩ), band-pass filtered offline (1–45 Hz), with additional channels for EOG and ECG. ECG data were processed in Kubios HRV (v3.5); R-peaks were automatically detected and manually corrected. Frequency-domain HRV indices were estimated using Welch’s method (150-s windows, 50% overlap), extracting LF (0.04–0.15 Hz) and HF (0.15–0.50 Hz) components.

EEG preprocessing was conducted in EEGLAB (v2022.0) using visual inspection and independent component analysis to remove artifacts, followed by re-referencing to the average reference.

Source Reconstruction and Network Definition

Artifact-free EEG data were projected to source space using Brainstorm with an OpenMEEG BEM solver and a three-layer ICBM152 head model. Cortical activity was estimated using sLORETA (~15,000 dipoles), smoothed with a 3-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel, and parcellated into 11 canonical functional networks based on the Gordon et al. atlas. Voxelwise time series were extracted within each network.

Time–Frequency Analysis and State Construction

Source time series were decomposed using five-cycle Morlet wavelets across 45 logarithmically spaced frequencies (1–45 Hz). Power was averaged within networks and grouped into delta, theta, alpha, beta, and low-gamma bands. Band-limited power was segmented into non-overlapping 60-ms windows and binarized using an adaptive threshold (μ_PSD + 0.5σ_PSD). Binary values across bands defined five-node network states (32 configurations).

Transition Matrices, Φ Computation, and Statistics

State sequences were used to estimate 32 × 32 transition probability matrices, converted to state-by-node TPMs under IIT 3.0 assumptions using PyPhi (v3). Maximally integrated information (Φ_max) was computed per state and averaged across time, weighted by state probability, yielding Φ per network, participant, and condition.

Non-parametric statistics (p ≤ 0.05, uncorrected) were applied. Participants were split into Good and Poor Performance groups via median Task 2 error rate. Within-condition effects were assessed using Wilcoxon tests; between-group differences used Mann–Whitney U tests. Associations with HRV and errors were evaluated using Spearman’s ρ and Kendall’s τ. Logistic regression predicted performance group from age, sex, and network-specific Φ, with repeated stratified cross-validation reporting entropy-based R², AUC, and generalized R².

Results

Failure of Network-Level Integration in Poor Performers

 Integrated information (Φ) differentiated behavioral performance primarily under conditions of high cognitive load. When all participants were considered together, Φ did not significantly change between Baseline, Task 1, and Task 2 in any network, indicating that simple task engagement alone was not sufficient to modulate network-level integration. However, a clear state- and group-dependent pattern emerged when performance groups were analyzed separately.

In the Poor Performance (PP) group, transitioning from the simple detection task (Task 1) to the more demanding rule-based matching task (Task 2) was accompanied by a significant reduction of Φ in the Visual (Z = −2.19, p = 0.031, r = 0.86), Dorsolateral Attentional (DLA; Z = −2.19, p = 0.031, r = 0.86), and Retrosplenial (RS; Z = −2.37, p = 0.015, r = 0.63) networks. By contrast, the Good Performance (GP) group preserved relatively stable Φ across phases, suggesting that effective performers maintain integrated multi-frequency network states when task complexity increases.

Between-group comparisons further revealed a robust dissociation during the high-load condition. In Task 2, Φ was significantly lower in the PP than in the GP group in the Visual (Z = −2.88, p = 0.001, r = 0.77), DLA (Z = −2.17, p = 0.01, r = 0.58), RS (Z = −1.91, p = 0.03, r = 0.51), Auditory (Z = −1.66, p = 0.048, r = 0.44) and Sensorimotor Hand (smH; Z = −2.40, p = 0.005, r = 0.64) networks. These findings indicate that poor performance is not merely due to reduced activation in isolated regions, but rather to a breakdown in the capacity of specific cortical systems to sustain a causally integrated, broadband state.

Behavioral Correlates of Φ

 Error rates in Task 2 showed strong negative correlations with Φ in the DLA (ρ = −0.70, p = 0.005), Visual (ρ = −0.70, p = 0.005) and Sensorimotor Hand (ρ = −0.66, p = 0.01) networks. Thus, participants who failed to integrate multi-frequency information within attentional, visual, and motor systems committed more errors, supporting the view that inhibitory success depends on the maintenance of highly integrated network dynamics rather than on local power alone.

Neurovisceral Integration (HRV–EEG Coupling) 

Heart rate variability (HRV) showed systematic associations with Φ, consistent with a neurovisceral model of cognitive control. At baseline, Low-Frequency (LF) HRV positively correlated with Φ in Auditory (ρ = 0.72, p = 0.004), Cingulo-Opercular, Default Mode, DLA, and Fronto-Parietal networks (ρ up to 0.71), suggesting that sympathetic-driven arousal supports a globally integrated cortical state prior to task engagement. In Task 1, High-Frequency (HF) HRV negatively correlated with Φ in the Visual and DLA networks (ρ = −0.55 to −0.68), indicating that initial task engagement may rely on a relative dominance of sympathetic activation to support rapid response readiness. In Task 2, HF HRV positively correlated with Φ in the Cingulate-Parietal network (ρ = 0.65, p = 0.01), suggesting that under sustained cognitive demand, parasympathetic regulation is associated with more integrated control-related processing.

Predictive Modeling of Performance

 Logistic regression analyses demonstrated that Φ in the Visual network during Task 2 is a strong predictor of behavioral performance. A model using Visual Φ as the sole regressor yielded excellent discrimination between PP and GP groups (χ² = 11.2, p = 0.0008; Nagelkerke R² = 0.73), with 86% accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Repeated stratified cross-validation (5-fold and 3-fold, each repeated 10 times) confirmed the robustness and generalizability of this model, with validation AUC ≈ 0.99 and low misclassification rates (MCR ≤ 0.08). These results support Φ as a theoretically grounded biomarker that captures variance in inhibitory performance beyond standard univariate EEG or HRV indices.

Conclusion

This study advances a network- and integration-based framework for understanding inhibitory control by applying Integrated Information Theory (IIT) to multi-frequency EEG data. Rather than focusing on isolated oscillatory bands or pairwise connectivity, we quantified Φ as an index of how much information is generated by cortical networks beyond the sum of their parts, with each network state defined by simultaneous activity across delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma bands. Within this framework, successful inhibition emerges not merely from local activation, but from the capacity of specific networks to sustain high levels of integrated, broadband information under cognitive load.

Our findings indicate that poor performance in a Go/No-Go rule-shift task is associated with a state-dependent collapse of Φ in Visual, Dorsolateral Attentional, and Retrosplenial networks when task demands increase. These networks jointly support selective processing of visual features, top-down rule broadcasting, and contextual memory integration. The reduction of Φ in the Sensorimotor Hand network, together with its correlation with error rates, further suggests that the failure to maintain integrated sensorimotor states compromises the ability to withhold or execute responses appropriately. Collectively, these results suggest that performance deficits reflect a breakdown in the synthesis of multi-frequency activity required to coordinate perception, attention, and motor control, rather than a simple reduction in regional power.

The systematic associations between HRV and Φ highlight a neurovisceral architecture in which autonomic regulation scaffolds cortical information integration. At rest, higher LF HRV predicts stronger multi-network Φ, consistent with a role of sympathetic arousal in priming integrated cortical states. Under increasing cognitive demand, the shift towards positive HF–Φ coupling within the Cingulate-Parietal network suggests that parasympathetic flexibility supports sustained rule maintenance and conflict monitoring. Thus, inhibitory control appears to depend on the dynamic coordination of central and peripheral systems, with autonomic flexibility modulating the depth and stability of cortical integration.Φ in the Visual network during the most demanding task phase accurately classified individuals as good or poor performers, with excellent cross-validated predictive accuracy. This supports Φ as a promising biomarker of executive function that is theoretically grounded in IIT yet empirically sensitive to individual variability. While the present sample is small and the analysis focused on within-network integration, the results lay the groundwork for future studies exploring inter-network Φ, larger and clinical cohorts, and direct comparisons with alternative network metrics (e.g., efficiency, modularity, or synchronization).

In summary, our findings suggest that inhibitory control depends on the ability of task-relevant networks to maintain irreducible, multi-frequency integration, supported by a flexible autonomic milieu. By treating the brain as a holistic, multi-spectral system and linking Φ to both behavior and HRV, this work provides an initial empirical bridge between IIT and the neurobiology of executive control, with potential implications for tracking cognitive decline, stress-related lapses in control, and performance in high-demand environments.
