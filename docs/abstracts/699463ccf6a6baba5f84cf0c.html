Introduction

Humans recognize objects by integrating information from multiple modalities, such as vision and touch. This crossmodal processing widely distributed and interconnected brain networks across the brain. Previous studies have shown that several brain regions, such as the posterior parietal cortex (PPC), are activated during crossmodal processing (Yang, J., et al 2021). However, it remains unclear how the brain performs crossmodal processing, particularly in the sensorimotor cortices, premotor cortex, and higher-order regions. In the present study, to identify brain regions important for crossmodal processing, we conducted a visuo‑haptic crossmodal functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) experiment. 

Methods

Ten healthy participants were asked to participate in the 3T fMRI experiment. To identify the brain function underlying crossmodal processing, we used a crossmodal delayed match-to-sample (DMS) paradigm (Kassuba, T., et al 2013). This paradigm allowed us to explore the brain network during sample stimulus encoding of each modality and then discuss how the brain network changed during the retrieval of sample features from another sensory modality relative to unimodal memory retrieval (Yang, J., et al 2021). We designed four manipulatable objects matching conditions: two unimodal (visual-to-Visual, vV; haptic-to-Haptic, hH) and two crossmodal (haptic-to-Visual, hV; visual-to-Haptic, vH) conditions. During the encoding phase (stimulus 1), participants either touched or saw a sample object, without prior knowledge of whether the subsequent matching phase (stimulus 2) would require the same or a different sensory modality. Subjects were asked to judge whether stimulus 1 and 2 were same or not in response phase (Figure 1A). To further examine category-dependent processing, the objects were divided into two categories: tools and non-tools, based on the hypothesis that object representations in the brain are organized by category (Caramazza, A. & Shelton, J., 1998). The non-tool category included vegetables and fruit. All object stimuli were manipulable man-made hand-sized toys which were made of plastic so that we can use in MR environment (Figure 1B). To help participents recognize toy tools as real tools, we allowed them to play with the toys before the fMRI experiment. The fMRI data preprocessing, general linear model analysis was conducted using AFNI. The study protocol was approved by the local medical ethics committee at the Okayama University, Japan.

Results

We compared brain activity during the matching phase (stimulus 2) between the crossmodal and unimodal conditions to investigate how the modality of the encoding phase (stimulus 1) affects neural processing during the recognition of subsequent stimuli. All statistical maps were thresholded at p = 0.05, and clusters smaller than 100 voxels were excluded (Figure 1C). Compared with the haptic unimodal condition, the visual-to-haptic condition enhanced activity in the left areas 3b and 1 and bilateral area 2 of the primary somatosensory cortex (S1), as well as the left primary motor cortex (M1), left premotor cortex, left supplementary motor area (SMA), and bilateral cingulate cortex. Incontrast, for the haptic-to-visual (hV) condition, no regions showed significant activation differences relative to the visual unimodal condition under the same statistical threshold (Figure 1D). 

Conclusion

In the present study, we used 3T fMRI to investigate neural mechanisms underlying crossmodal processing. Unlike previous research, our results indicate that the primary sensorimotor cortex (PSC) plays a critical role in crossmodal object recognition. In the crossmodal condition following haptic stimulation, the left area 3b, 1 and left M1 were strongly activated compared to the haptic unimodal condition. Furthermore, area 2 and the cingulate cortex, which are considered higher-order regions, were bilaterally activated. On the other hand, in the crossmodal condition following visual stimulation, V1 was slightly activated compared to the visual unimodal condition. We also identified category-dependent differences between tool and non-tool objects. Together, these findings provide new insights into the neural mechanisms supporting crossmodal object recognition and tool use, and represent an important step toward understanding the dynamic interaction between sensory and motor systems during object recognition.
