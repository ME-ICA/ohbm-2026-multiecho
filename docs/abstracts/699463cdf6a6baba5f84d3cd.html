Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a disorder of gut-brain interaction characterized by abdominal pain and altered bowel habits, frequently comorbid with anxiety disorders, depression, chronic fatigue, and fibromyalgia1. Psychosocial stress is a key factor in the development of IBS and its comorbidities, and leads to symptom exacerbation2. Altered brain activity in response to stress and at rest in IBS vs. healthy controls has been documented3,4. However, little is known about how brain networks dynamically reorganize during stress recovery in IBS, or how mental and functional somatic comorbidities influence these dynamics. Here, we aimed to investigate the alterations of static and dynamic functional connectivity (FC) after psychosocial stress in IBS patients with and without comorbidities.

Methods

Participants, study design and data collection

As part of the European multicenter DISCOVERIE-project, healthy controls (HC) and IBS patients were recruited from three centers: KU Leuven (Belgium), University of Gothenburg (Sweden), and Maastricht University (The Netherlands). All the IBS patients met the ROME IV criteria and were categorized as comorbid or non-comorbid based on the diagnostic criteria for depression and anxiety disorders (DSM-5), chronic fatigue syndrome (IOM), or fibromyalgia (ACR).

All participants underwent T1-weighted structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning followed by the Montreal Imaging Stress Test (MIST), which is a functional MRI stress task that reliably induces psychosocial stress by combining mental arithmetic under time pressure with unpredictable negative feedback and evaluative threat5. Visual analogue scales were used to measure negative affect during the MIST. After the MIST, 500 frames of post-stress resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI, TR = 1s) scanning recorded resting-state brain activity during stress recovery. 

Data analysis

Self-reported negative affect ratings during the MIST were analyzed with a linear mixed model. We tested group effects (HC vs. non-comorbid vs. comorbid IBS) and group×time interactions. fMRI and T1-weighted images were preprocessed with fMRIPrep 20.2.628. Further smoothing (6mm Gaussian kernel), bandpass filtering (0.008-0.09 Hz), and covariates regression were conducted in rs-fMRIs in CONN 22.v2407. Next, we performed both static FC and dynamic independent component (ICA) analyses based on denoised timeseries extracted from 20 bilateral (40 regions in total) predefined regions of interest (ROIs, Figure 1B). Static FC strength was represented by correlation coefficients between ROIs. Second-level general linear models were conducted for all connections to test the group effect on static FCs. Dynamic independent component analysis (ICA) was performed to model time-varying FC between the 40 ROIs. Connectivity fluctuations were estimated using a generalized psychophysiological interaction (gPPI) model with 10 temporal modulation factors, refined using dual regression with a 30-s Hanning kernel. FastICA was applied to the group-level modulation matrices to obtain 10 independent FC patterns, and subject-specific gPPI back-projection generated individual FC pattern timeseries, indicating the temporal engagement of each FC pattern throughout the scan (e.g., Figure 1G). Dynamic FC characteristics (average engagement, temporal frequency, and variability) were compared between (1) IBS and HC, (2) comorbid and non-comorbid IBS. All statistical analyses controlled for recruiting center.

Results

The negative affect analysis included 35 non-comorbid, 41 comorbid IBS patients, and 56 HC. We found that negative affect ratings significantly increased over time (linear: p < 0.001, quadratic: p = 0.015), suggesting successful stress induction. IBS patients reported higher negative affect ratings than HC already before the MIST (F1,249 = 9.49, p = 0.002). The significantly higher negative affect was driven by IBS patients with comorbidities (HC vs. non-comorbid IBS, ptukey = 0.843; HC vs. comorbid IBS, ptukey = 0.017, Figure 1A). 

Rs-fMRI data from 39 non-comorbid IBS, 53 comorbid IBS, and 60 HC were included in static FC and dynamic ICA analysis. In static FC analysis, IBS showed increased FC between the amygdala and dorsal medial prefrontal cortex, as well as the caudate, alongside decreased FCs within frontoparietal, and the default mode networks (DMN), and decreased FC between dorsal medial prefrontal cortex and midbrain (connection level p < 0.05, cluster level qFDR < 0.05, Figure 1C) compared to HC, indicating dysregulated top-down fronto-parietal control. No significant comorbidity group effect on static FC was found.

Based on the individual FC pattern engagement timeseries, we found that IBS patients exhibited significantly reduced average engagement and temporal variability of latent FC pattern 3 after the MIST (t149 < -2.87, qFDR < 0.03, Figure 1G), characterized by positive FCs between V1/V3 and anterior mid-cingulate cortex, posterior insula, and sensorimotor areas, and negative FCs between caudate and posterior insula, together with sensorimotor areas (Figure 1D). This result suggests less active and inflexible exteroceptive-interoceptive integration during stress recovery in IBS. Additionally, we found enhanced average engagement of latent FC pattern 9 in IBS than HC (t149 = 3.52, qFDR < 0.01, Figure 1H), defined by positive FCs between sensorimotor network and DMN, which also negatively connected with anterior insula (Figure 1E), indicating heightened self-referential processing with reduced integration of interoceptive salience information. Moreover, within the IBS patient group, comorbid IBS patients showed lower temporal variability in latent FC pattern 10 than non-comorbid patients (t89 = -3.28, qFDR = 0.015, Figure 1I). This FC pattern reflected an integrative pattern linking visual, interoceptive/sensorimotor, DMN, and limbic systems, and their cross-network connections (Figure 1F), suggesting diminished network integrity and cross-network interactions in the presence of comorbidities.

Conclusion

We investigated the neural stress dynamics in IBS patients and the impact of mental and functional somatic comorbidities. Our results may indicate three disruptions in brain dynamics during stress recovery in IBS: (1) disrupted fronto-parietal top-down regulation; (2) sustained engagement of self-focused attention to bodily sensations, and (3) reduced integration of bodily state and affective information. These findings highlight the stress-recovery dynamics as a feature of IBS brain dysfunction and suggest that comorbidities further impair brain communication flexibility. Since rs-fMRI was collected only post-stress, the extent to which FC alterations are stress-induced versus trait-like remains unclear. Further studies, including baseline rs-fMRI,  are needed to separate state from trait effects.
