Introduction

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a common neurodevelopmental condition defined by developmentally inappropriate hyperactivity-impulsivity and/or inattention symptoms. The clinical course of ADHD is highly variable: some children maintain ADHD symptoms into adolescence, while others show marked improvement leading to remission or subclinical symptoms. Although dysregulation of the catecholaminergic system has long been linked to ADHD, converging evidence also implicates glutamate, the brain’s primary excitatory transmitter1. Findings on glutamate levels measured with proton Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (MRS) in ADHD have been inconsistent, due partly to a reliance on cross-sectional designs, heterogeneity of participants’ age, and technical challenges1. 

In this study, we examine longitudinal changes in medial prefrontal cortex glutamate in youth whose childhood ADHD either persisted or remitted in adolescence or adulthood, using a glutamate-specific MRS sequence. We further explore possible associations between altered glutamate in ADHD and altered functional connectivity. Specifically, we investigate whether altered glutamate relates to differences in functional connectivity, motivated by models conceptualizing ADHD as a disorder of atypical network interactions—particularly a loss of the usual counterbalance between the default mode network (DMN) and attention or cognitive control networks2. Because the medial prefrontal cortex is a key node of the DMN, we test whether glutamate levels in this region track with DMN connectivity patterns measured using resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) from the same participants.

Methods

We selected 175 participants with glutamate MRS data from an accelerated longitudinal cohort study at the National Institute of Health (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT01721720) with institutional review board approval and informed consent/assent. Persistent ADHD was defined by continued diagnostic status at final follow-up; remitting ADHD was defined by loss of diagnostic status after study entry. A typically developing comparison group free of psychiatric diagnoses served as the “Never Affected” group. 

Glutamate was quantified using 3T J-point resolved proton MRS sequence sensitive to glutamate and allowing separation from glutamine3, which is the inactive form of glutamate (voxel size: 2x2x2cm3; repetition time (TR): 2000ms; 32 echo times (TE); 35–221 ms; 5.3 min). A midline voxel was placed in the medial prefrontal cortex at the level of the anterior cingulate (Fig. 1), guided by the MP-RAGE T1-weighted image (1x1x1 mm3 voxel; TR: 8 ms; TE: 3 ms; inversion time: 900 ms; 5 min). Metabolites were quantified using a fully automated program3,4 with partial volume correction based on Freesurfer T1w segmentation (Fig. 1). Spectra were excluded for signal-to-noise ratio < 5, full width at half maximum > 0.1 ppm, concentration of metabolites outside three standard deviations of the mean, and incorrect voxel placement or corrupted anatomical sequences. 

During the same session, two rs-fMRI runs were acquired while participants viewed a fixation cross (3.4x3.4x2.8 mm3 resolution; TR: 2500 ms; TE: 27 ms; 120 volumes per run; 10 min total). Preprocessing followed the 36-parameter plus despiking pipeline described in Norman et al.5 implemented via fMRIPrep and XCP software. Region of interest (ROI) time series were extracted using the Schaefer atlas (mapped to Yeo’s seven networks) plus predefined subcortical ROIs. ROI-to-ROI correlation matrices were computed after Fisher r-to-z transformation. A-priori network metrics included within DMN connectivity and connectivity between the DMN and the dorsal attention, salience, central executive, and subcortical networks. rs-fMRI datasets with mean framewise displacement > 0.3 mm or without 10 minutes of rs-fMRI data were excluded. 

We used linear mixed models to (1) investigate developmental change in glutamate, using age, sex, outcome groups, and age-by-outcome interaction as fixed effects and participant as a random effect; and (2) investigate associations between glutamate and DMN rs-fMRI connectivity, modeling glutamate concentration, outcome groups, age, and glutamate-by-group interaction as fixed effects. Bonferroni correction for the five rs-fMRI metrics set the significance at p=0.05/5=0.01.

Results

After quality control, 241 spectra from 161 participants (aged 5.3 to 20.4 years at baseline) were retained: 69 with persistent ADHD (29 with follow-up scans), 20 with remitting ADHD (9 with follow-up scans) and 72 never affected (34 with follow-up scans). Groups did not differ in demographic variables, age at scan, or MRS quality.

A significant group difference emerged in the developmental trajectory of glutamate (F(2,76)=3.89, p=0.02; see Fig. 2A). Persistent ADHD showed an age-related increase in glutamate, contrasting with the age-related decrease in the never affected group (Persistent ADHD*Age: ßstd=0.32, 95% CI [0.04, 0.60], p=0.026). The remitted ADHD group also showed an age-related decrease in glutamate that differed from the persistent ADHD group (Remitted ADHD*Age: ßstd=-0.57, 95% CI [-1.08, -0.05], p=0.032) but not the never affected group (Remitted ADHD*Age: ßstd=-0.24, 95% CI [-0.76, 0.27], p=0.35). For completeness, we examined other metabolites: glutamine, total creatine, and total choline showed no developmental group differences. Total N-acetyl aspartate did differ by group (F(2,76)=3.91, p=0.02), with a steeper increase in persistent ADHD compared with the never affected and remitting groups

For the combined glutamate MRS and rs-fMRI datasets (104 participants, 141 observations), only the persistent ADHD and never affected groups were analyzed due to small remitting ADHD sample size. Glutamate–connectivity relationships differed significantly between groups for DMN–subcortical connectivity (ßstd=0.54, 95% CI [0.14, 0.94], p=0.009; see Fig.2B). Higher medial prefrontal glutamate in persistent ADHD was associated with increased DMN–subcortical connectivity (ρ=0.3, p=0.04), but not in the never affected group (ρ=-0.2, p=0.08). Follow-up analyses identified the caudate as the specific subcortical region driving this association (βstd=0.70, 95%CI [0.31, 1.09], p=9x10-4). 

All findings remained after controlling for IQ, psychostimulant use, and comorbidities.

Conclusion

This study provides the first longitudinal evidence that developmental changes in glutamate differ according to ADHD course. Youth with persistent ADHD show an atypical age-related increase in medial prefrontal glutamate, whereas remitting ADHD resembles never-affected development. Persistent ADHD is further characterized by an altered relationship between medial prefrontal glutamate and connectivity between the DMN and subcortical structures, particularly the caudate, highlighting a potential neurochemical component of disrupted network interactions long associated with ADHD.

These findings help reconcile inconsistent cross-sectional MRS results, which may reflect age-dependent differences in glutamatergic maturation. Since not all participants contributed post-MRS clinical follow-ups, our results should be interpreted as mechanistic rather than prognostic. Overall, the data support a model in which remission reflects normalization or compensation in neurodevelopment, whereas persistence reflects continued or progressive alterations.
