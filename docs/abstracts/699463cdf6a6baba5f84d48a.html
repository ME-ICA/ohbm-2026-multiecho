Introduction

Motion sickness (MS) is a common condition characterized by symptoms such as nausea and discomfort. Previous neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies have shown that susceptibility to MS is closely associated with the activation of right posterior parietal cortex, a key region involved in multisensory integration and self-motion perception. However,direct causal evidence through targeted neural modulation remains limited.

This study investigates whether posterior parietal alpha-band neurofeedback (NFB) can modulate visually induced motion sickness (VIMS). The goal is to determine whether such training can alleviate MS symptoms and induce expected cortical changes based on electroencephalogram (EEG).

Methods

Participants with high susceptibility to MS were recruited and randomly assigned to a real-feedback (Real) or sham-feedback (Sham) group. All participants completed a NFB training task. The Real group received NFB training designed to up-regulate alpha-band power over the posterior parietal cortex, whereas the Sham group received noncontingent feedback. Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) scores were collected before and after the intervention to assess symptom, and Fast Motion Sickness (FMS) ratings were obtained repeatedly during the task to track the temporal evolution of subjective discomfort. Concurrent EEG recordings were used to examine topographical differences in alpha-band power.

Results

Group differences in SSQ total scores showed a trend favoring the Real group and reach statistical significance (p > 0.05). Besides, FMS ratings revealed clearly divergent trajectories between groups: the Real group exhibited a slower increase in FMS scores compared with the Sham group, particularly in the later stages of the task, indicating that NFB effectively suppressed the real-time accumulation of MS symptoms. EEG topographies further demonstrated group-dependent modulations of posterior parietal alpha power that were consistent with the training target.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that NFB training targeting posterior parietal alpha-band activity can effectively modulate the dynamic progression of MS and attenuate symptom escalation. Converging evidence from SSQ and FMS ratings supports the efficacy of the approach. The study provides novel neurophysiological evidence and empirical support for developing non-pharmacological interventions for MS.
