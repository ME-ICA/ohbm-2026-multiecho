Introduction

Sleep disturbances and fatigue are common and highly debilitating symptoms in people with Multiple Sclerosis (pwMS), affecting up to 60% and 75% of patients, respectively. On a behavioral level, sleep and fatigue are conceptually related and fatigue occurs more frequently in those patients reporting poor sleep.

On a neuronal level, the thalamus seems to be involved in sleep disturbances due to its role in sleep-wake control1. When it comes to pwMS, however, literature on the relationship between brain structures and sleep quality is scarce, while relations with fatigue have been shown in several studies2. Furthermore, there is preliminary evidence of differential involvement of thalamic sub-nuclei in terms of fatigue, which should be further explored in the context of sleep.

Therefore, we aimed to investigate 1) possible similarities of thalamic involvement in sleep quality and in fatigue in pwMS and 2) whether different thalamic nuclei play a differential role in sleep and fatigue in pwMS.

Methods

We collected data from 99 pwMS (mean age 40.3 (SD: 10.1) years; median Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score 1.5 (range: 0-7), median disease duration 11.0 (range: 0-32) years; 56.6% female, 92.9% relapsing-remitting MS) between February 2024 and August 2025. In addition, 52 healthy controls (HC; mean age 36.4 (SD: 12.0) years, 57.7% female) were assessed within the same time interval. Sleep quality was evaluated with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and poor sleep quality was indicated by a total PSQI-score >5. Fatigue was assessed by the Fatigue Scale for Motor and Cognition (FSMC), with total scores >52 indicating moderate-to-severe fatigue. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences relevant to this study were 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE and 3D FLAIR. Lesions were segmented on FLAIR images with the Lesion Segmentation Toolbox (SPM 12; lesion prediction algorithm). Binary lesion masks were created (binarization threshold=0.25) and individual lesion load extracted. Lesion-filling on 3D T1 images was performed using the FSL lesion-filling toolbox, after which normalized brain volumes were estimated using SIENAX. 

Thalamus as well as the main nuclei groups (anterior, medial, ventral, posterior) were segmented using the automated HIPS-THOMAS pipeline3, Figure 1. Besides lesion load, all volumetric data were normalized for intracranial volume using the v-scaling factor derived from SIENAX. T-tests were used to compare pwMS and HC in terms of self-reported sleep quality and fatigue. ANCOVAs corrected for age and sex were conducted to compare pwMS and HC in terms of brain volumetric data. To investigate the association between brain volumes and symptoms, we conducted spearman correlations for lesion load and partial correlations for all other volumes, corrected for age and sex. All p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons (Benjamini-Hochberg correction).

Results

In this cohort, 43.4% of pwMS (n=43) reported poor sleep quality and 49.5% (n=49) reported at least moderate fatigue. Poor sleep quality was associated with higher fatigue in pwMS (r=0.405, p<0.001). The average sleep quality was significantly lower in pwMS (PSQI: M=6.04, SD=3.67) than in HC (M=4.75; SD=2.96; t(149)=2.19, p=0.015). Similarly, average fatigue-levels were higher in pwMS (FSMC: M=53.60; SD=19.32) than in HC (M=39.38, SD=14.98; t(149)=4.62, p<0.001). 

Whole-brain, grey matter and white matter volumes were lower in pwMS compared to HC (all p<0.001). Higher fatigue levels were correlated with lower whole brain volume (r=-0.272, p=0.009), grey matter volume (r=-0.378, p=0.004), and higher lesion load (r=0.269, p=0.009) in pwMS. No correlations were observed regarding sleep quality.

Whole thalamic volume as well as volumes of all nuclei groups were lower in pwMS compared to HC (all p<0.001). In pwMS, higher fatigue-values correlated with lower volumes of ventral thalamus (r=-0.264, p=0.026), medial thalamus (r=-0.236, p=0.027), posterior thalamus (r=-0.251, p=0.026), but not anterior thalamus (p>0.05). No statistically significant correlations were observed regarding sleep quality. No significant correlations were observed in HC.

Conclusion

About half of the patients included in this cohort reported poor sleep quality and/or moderate-to-severe fatigue. While both concepts were moderately correlated, we observed associations between lesion load, global and thalamic atrophy and fatigue, but no associations with sleep quality. These associations were observed in medial, ventral, and posterior parts, but not in anterior parts of the thalamus. Together, these findings indicate that while inflammatory and neurodegenerative damage contributes to fatigue, sleep quality likely depends more on other factors beyond structural neurological alterations.
