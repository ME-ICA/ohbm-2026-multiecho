Introduction

Developmental dyslexia (DD) is characterised by struggles in phonological representations of words. Phonological processing is strongly left-lateralized (in left temporoparietal areas), and research reports a reduced or disrupted left-hemisphere (LH) activation in DD. To compensate for this reduced LH engagement, children with DD increasingly recruit preserved right-hemisphere (RH) systems. But this RH activation is only beneficial if information is successfully transferred to the LH via interhemispheric (IH) connectivity through the corpus callosum. Studies have reported a weaker callosal integration seen in DD,  signifying that weak connectivity prevents the preserved RH from capably supporting the dysfunctional LH phonological network.^1 We hypothesise that, by boosting RH function to improve/restore weak LH functioning in DD through enhanced IH, hemispheric dominance will be rebalanced to support stronger phonological and reading skills in children with and without reading difficulties. Our goal is to test the feasibility of this approach in typically developing children, with future work extending the intervention to DD. We will design and implement a child-friendly EEG neurofeedback (NF) intervention to boost IH during a phonological processing task, to improve reading outcomes.^2 The NF pipeline targets the latency difference (ΔLat=N1_RH−N1_LH) between LH and RH N1/N100 auditory ERP during passive dichotic listening (DL). Previous work suggests that ΔLat reflects the efficiency of IH transfer via corpus callosum and is linked to hemispheric rebalance. However, developing child-friendly real-time NF systems that accurately extract and modulate neural signals from noisy paediatric EEG is technically difficult. Here, we present the development and validation of an EEG-based NF pipeline designed to target N100 ΔLat during DL, using existing EEG data from typically developing children to optimise single-trial N100 detection and signal preprocessing parameters.

Methods

We analysed an existing EEG dataset from 43 typically developing Spanish–Basque bilingual children (age 7–12 years). Children performed a passive syllabic dichotic listening (DL) task, in which they simultaneously presented two competing phonological stimuli (syllables) to both ears while watching a silent movie. EEG was recorded from a 64-channel sponge-based passive electrodes (Brain Products R-Net) arranged according to the international 10–10 system and referenced online to Cz electrode. Signals were sampled at 1000 Hz, with electrode impedances kept below 100 kΩ. Auditory stimuli were delivered to both ears via headphones.

Because these recordings were collected in a hard paediatric setting and were generally noisy, we selected the 11 children with the highest data quality (largest proportion of artifact-free trials) for this methodological analysis. This subset provides a baseline proof of concept for our NF protocol, and we will later use the same DL paradigm to train typical and dyslexic children using NF. Here, we investigated whether N100 is clearly visible in both hemispheres and whether single-trial N100 latency can be extracted robustly enough to serve as a feedback signal.

Preprocessing followed the pipeline planned for real-time NF. Continuous EEG was band-pass filtered 1–30 Hz and re-referenced to the average.^3 After that we applied Artifact Subspace Reconstruction (ASR; cutoff = 20) to attenuate transient high-variance artifacts, which is followed by Independent Component Analysis with ICLabel to identify non-brain components.^4 For each hemisphere we defined a region of interest (ROI) which includes fronto-central, temporal and centro-parietal electrodes and computed a Global ERP by averaging all artifact-reduced trials and channels in that ROI. Each independent component was back-projected, and its ERP in the 0.1–0.4 s post-stimulus window was correlated with the corresponding Global ERP. Components with correlation < 0.3 were removed. Single trials whose local ERPs correlated < 0.3 with the Global ERP were also discarded as outliers.

To obtain a single optimised waveform per trial and hemisphere, we compared two spatial filters:

(1) Simple averaging of all channels in the left or right ROI (transparent ERP-style baseline), and 

(2) Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA), which finds a weighted linear combination of channels that maximises correlation between single-trial signals and the template ERP, thereby increasing N100 signal-to-noise.^5

For each trial, hemisphere and spatial filter, we then estimated N100 latency. Within an a priori N100 region of interest (0.1–0.4 s), we located the most negative deflection and defined two half-amplitude points at 50 % of the peak amplitude on the rising and falling slopes. The mean of these two times was taken as the trial-wise N100 latency. This waveform-based approach is less sensitive to noise and sampling jitter than a single-sample peak and is therefore better suited for use as a continuous NF feedback variable.

Results

The analyses were restricted to the 11 children with the highest proportion of artifact-free data. For each child and hemisphere, artifact-reduced trials were averaged to obtain Global ERPs that served as templates for quality control and latency estimation.

Group ERPs. Both hemispheres showed a clear auditory N100 to syllables. Across children, mean N100 tended to be larger in absolute amplitude over the left than the right hemisphere, consistent with the theory that left-lateralised early speech processing, but this difference did not reach statistical significance at the group level. When we compare spatial filters with ERPs derived from CCA filters, the weights were smoother and showed more consistent LH–RH relationships than those based on simple averaging, reflecting a improved effective signal-to-noise without altering the basic waveform (Fig. 1A, averaging; Fig. 1B, CCA).

Single-trial quality and stability across participants. To quantify single-trial quality each trial was correlated with its hemisphere-specific Global ERP in the 0.1–0.4 s window. Across participants, the resulting distributions confirmed that a clear N100 ERP can be recovered at the single-trial level, and CCA can systematically shift correlations upward and reduced their spread comparative to simple averaging. This demonstrates that spatial optimisation materially improves the fidelity of the N100 signal (Fig. 1C, averaging; Fig. 1D, CCA).

Stability as a function of the number of trials. To visualise how single-trial quality accumulates with more data, we performed a complementary stability analysis in one illustrative participant. For each trial count (1–50), n single trials were averaged and correlated with the Global ERP using both types of spatial filters. With the averaging filter (Fig. 1E), left-hemisphere subset ERPs showed higher correlations than the right hemisphere at almost every trial count and approached high correlations more rapidly. With the CCA filter (Fig. 1F), correlations were overall higher and the left–right difference was much smaller, with both hemispheres converging towards similarly high values by ~40–50 trials. The same qualitative pattern was observed across participants (not shown).

Trial-wise N100 latencies. When the half-amplitude procedure was applied, it gave robust latency estimates for both hemispheres and for both spatial filters in the same participant.  Latencies exhibited substantial trial-to-trial variability along with systematic LH–RH timing differences. Importantly, CCA-based latencies fluctuated less from trial to trial than averaging-based latencies, consistent with improved stability (Fig. 1G, averaging; Fig. 1H, CCA). These findings indicate that left and right N100 latencies—and their difference ΔLat—can be estimated with sufficient reliability to serve as core feedback variables in the planned N100-based interhemispheric neurofeedback protocol.

Conclusion

This methodological study shows that our N100 pipeline can deliver hemisphere-specific, single-trial latency estimates that are suitable for use as participant (also pediatric) feedback signals for real-time neurofeedback. Trial-wise N100 track their Global ERPs closely, and the half-amplitude latency measure provides stable timing estimates that are appropriate for use as a continuous feedback variable.

Across analyses, CCA offered clear benefits over simple averaging, yielding higher single-trial correlations, smoother ERPs and narrower latency distributions. The stability curves further showed that, with the averaging filter, the left hemisphere reached high similarity to the Global ERP with fewer trials and maintained higher correlations than the right, whereas CCA raised correlations for both hemispheres and largely reduced this left–right gap. Thus, spatial optimisation is not just a technical detail but meaningfully improves the quality, balance and reliability of the signal we intend to use in feedback, while averaging remains a useful transparent baseline.

Together with the reliable estimation of left and right N100 latencies, these findings support the feasibility of using ΔLat as a neurofeedback-relevant metric for targeting interhemispheric timing, which is particularly relevant in dyslexia where LH phonological networks are often disrupted.

A key limitation of this study is that the current dataset is quite noisy and it was not acquired under NF-optimised conditions, making our estimates conservative but raising questions about generalisability to cleaner, NF-style recordings. So our future and a critical step will be to acquire a new dataset specifically tailored for an online neurofeedback (e.g., fewer channels focused on NF-relevant ROIs, shorter structured blocks, task designed for robust feedback updates) and apply exactly the same pipeline. This will allow us to test the robustness of our NF protocol and quantify gains in single-trial N100 and ΔLat reliability, along with refine the minimum trial counts needed for stable feedback, and move towards a child-friendly N100-based interhemispheric neurofeedback protocol.
