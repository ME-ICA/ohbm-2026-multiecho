Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) is an established therapy for alleviating the motor symptoms of Parkinson’s disease (PD) (1). Stimulation frequency is a key determinant of therapeutic response: low-frequency stimulation (LFS; 60–80 Hz) frequently improves gait and axial symptoms (2), whereas high-frequency stimulation (HFS; >100 Hz) is more effective for tremor suppression (3). When patients do not respond adequately to the standard clinical frequency (130 Hz), programming typically proceeds by optimizing stimulation at either LFS or HFS depending on their predominant symptoms.

Although frequency-specific behavioural effects are well recognized, it remains unclear how stimulation frequency shapes the broader cortical and subcortical networks engaged by therapeutic DBS. Notably, the neural effects of clinically used therapeutic HFS above 130 Hz, particularly 180 Hz, have not been previously mapped. Our prior work established a characteristic whole-brain fMRI activation signature associated with optimal stimulation at 130 Hz (4), providing a benchmark for evaluating how alternative frequencies reorganize these networks.

The present study systematically evaluates how stimulation frequency influences DBS-evoked brain activation across several levels of comparison. We first examine frequency effects independent of clinical optimization by comparing all HFS and all LFS stimulation runs across participants. We then assess frequency effects under clinically optimal conditions by contrasting patients optimized on HFS with those optimized on LFS. Finally, we determine how alternative optimal frequencies relate to the known canonical activation pattern by comparing the established 130 Hz optimal cohort to the optimal HFS and optimal LFS groups separately. Together, these analyses characterize how both stimulation frequency and clinical optimization shape the distributed neural networks modulated by STN-DBS.

Methods

Participants and Study Design
 Eleven individuals with STN-DBS (age = 62.3 ± 6.86; 4 females) who were clinically optimized on either HFS (n = 7) or LFS (n = 4) were prospectively recruited. Optimal stimulation frequency was defined as the clinically selected setting that produced the best symptom control during routine programming (5). Each participant completed two fMRI runs during bilateral DBS delivered in a block design (30 s ON / 30 s OFF; 6.5 minutes total): one at 80 Hz (LFS) and one at 180 Hz (HFS). Stimulation amplitude was adjusted to equate total electrical energy delivered (TEED) across frequencies. Preprocessing and first-level modeling followed procedures described previously (4). TEED was included as a covariate of no interest in all analyses involving HFS and LFS.
 For comparisons involving the canonical 130 Hz stimulation pattern, we additionally used a previously published cohort of 34 patients who were stimulated unilaterally (left-side) at their clinically optimized 130 Hz settings (4).

Group-Level Analyses
To characterize the effects of stimulation frequency independent of clinical optimization, all HFS runs were compared with all LFS runs using a flexible factorial model in SPM with subject included as a random factor to account for repeated measures and TEED as a covariate. To isolate frequency effects under clinically effective conditions, the optimal HFS and optimal LFS runs were compared using a two-sample t-test. Finally, to determine how alternative optimal frequencies diverge from the canonical whole-brain pattern defined at 130 Hz, we compared the optimal HFS group and the optimal LFS group separately to the established 130 Hz cohort. Because the established cohort was stimulated unilaterally, these analyses were restricted to the left hemisphere using an MNI152 hemispheric mask. All group-level statistical tests were performed in SPM using a voxel-wise threshold of p < .001 uncorrected. 

Results

Comparison of HFS and LFS revealed clear frequency-dependent differences in whole-brain activation. HFS produced greater activation in the sensorimotor cortex, supplementary motor areas, cerebellum, and thalamic regions consistent with the vicinity of the VIM (p < .001 uncorrected; Figure 2A), indicating stronger engagement of cerebello–thalamo–cortical circuits.

Optimization status further modulated these patterns. Among clinically optimized patients, optimal HFS elicited greater activation in the left precentral gyrus, right superior occipital cortex, bilateral occipital pole, right precuneus, left inferior occipital cortex, right lingual gyrus, and left subcallosal cortex. In contrast, optimal LFS produced relatively stronger activation in the left postcentral and right precentral gyri (p < .001 uncorrected; Figure 2B), suggesting heightened engagement of sensorimotor regions associated with proprioceptive and axial motor control.

Comparisons with the established 130 Hz cohort further demonstrated that stimulation frequency shapes cortical and subcortical recruitment. Relative to optimal HFS, the established cohort exhibited greater activation in the left postcentral gyrus, cerebellum, and thalamus (p < .001 uncorrected; Figure 2C), consistent with modulated engagement of sensorimotor and cerebello–thalamo–cortical pathways at different frequencies. When compared with optimal LFS, the established cohort showed increased activation in the left superior frontal gyrus, cuneal cortex, lingual gyrus, lateral occipital cortex, and posterior fusiform gyrus (p < .001 uncorrected; Figure 2D), reflecting reduced occipital and posterior cortical recruitment under LFS relative to the standard stimulation pattern.

Conclusion

Across all contrasts, the present study shows that stimulation frequency has a strong influence on the cortical activation patterns associated with therapeutic STN-DBS. By comparing HFS and LFS, evaluating frequency-specific optimal stimulation, and benchmarking both against an established 130 Hz optimal map, we show that frequency selection and clinical optimization each shape how distributed cortical regions participate in DBS-modulated networks. These findings indicate that therapeutic benefit is not tied to a single activation pattern. Instead, optimal outcomes emerge when stimulation parameters align with the neural circuits that support a patient’s dominant symptoms.

The comparison between HFS and LFS shows that stimulation frequency alone produces widespread differences in cortical and subcortical engagement, regardless of optimization. The greater activation observed with HFS in sensorimotor cortex, supplementary motor regions, cerebellum, and thalamic areas near the VIM suggests that higher frequencies recruit cerebello–thalamo–cortical pathways involved in motor control. This aligns with the observation that HFS more effectively suppresses tremor because it engages circuits involved in oscillatory motor output. LFS produces a distinct activation profile that may reflect greater involvement of proprioceptive, postural, or gait-related networks. These findings support the view that frequency is not simply a tuning parameter but a determinant of which large-scale motor circuits are engaged by DBS.

The comparison of optimal HFS and LFS further supports a clear frequency-dependent dissociation. Optimal HFS recruited visuomotor and parietal–occipital regions that support tremor control and movement precision. This pattern parallels Boutet et al4., who reported similar motor-network recruitment during optimal stimulation. Both the established optimal map and optimal HFS produced stronger occipital activation than LFS, suggesting that LFS engages these regions less and may recruit fewer whole-brain networks. HFS also more strongly engaged the precuneus and subcallosal cortex, indicating broader involvement of networks linked to sensory integration and internal state regulation.

Comparisons with the established 130 Hz cohort show that optimal outcomes can arise from distinct but related network configurations. Relative to optimal HFS at 180 Hz, the 130 Hz cohort showed stronger visuomotor and cerebello–thalamo–cortical recruitment, suggesting that although both frequencies suppress tremor, they may do so by emphasizing different components of the tremor-control network. The comparison with optimal LFS responders revealed a different effect. LFS was associated with reduced recruitment of occipital and higher-order associative regions relative to 130 Hz. Although gait relies on visual and visuospatial processing, the diminished BOLD response may reflect a more selective pattern of engagement during LFS.

Together, these findings illustrate that DBS frequency modulates cortical networks in clinically meaningful and symptom-specific ways. Although exploratory and limited by sample size, this work suggests that individualized frequency selection may enhance therapeutic benefit by directing stimulation toward the neural circuitry best aligned with a patient’s symptoms. Future studies should determine whether these frequency-dependent activation patterns can guide personalized programming or predict clinical responsiveness.
