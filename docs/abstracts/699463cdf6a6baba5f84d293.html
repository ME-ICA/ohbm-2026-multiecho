Introduction

The wiring architecture of human brain networks is conventionally viewed as an optimal evolutionary compromise, balancing the pressure to minimize costly physical connections (wiring cost) with the necessity of achieving efficient information transfer (communication efficiency) [1, 2, 3]. This trade-off is typically assessed assuming routing communication dynamics, where information travels only via the shortest path [1, 2]. However, mounting evidence suggests that neural signaling operates through more decentralized, non-shortest-path mechanisms, such as propagation (information cascades over all pathways, decaying with distance) or diffusion (random walker dynamics) [4]. 

This raises a fundamental question: Is the human brain's connectivity truly optimal for communication when evaluated under more biologically plausible signaling dynamics? Furthermore, does an optimal communication structure naturally yield the best computational capacity and reliability? 

We address these questions by combining game theory, communication models, generative brain network models, and neuromorphic computing. Game theory enables us to formally express the cost-benefit trade-off as a local and distributed optimization problem, where brain regions act as self-interested ‘players’ negotiating connections without global network knowledge. We implement these interactions via a generative network model, where regions connect only if the benefit of improved signaling outweighs the wiring cost. Then, we use neuromorphic computing to test the computational properties of networks that result from different trade-offs.

Methods

We introduce a novel game-theoretic generative network model to establish a normative benchmark for network structures with optimal cost-communication trade-off. The model starts with a spatially embedded set of 100 cortical regions derived from the Human Connectome Project. In an iterative, local, and competitive process, random subsets of regions “play” by evaluating potential connection changes. Each region independently calculates its payoff, defined as a weighted balance between maximizing communication capacity and minimizing wiring cost. Note that regions do not consider their impact on other nodes, nor do they care about the cost-efficiency of the network as a whole. This process continues until a stable Nash equilibrium is reached where no new connection enhances communication beyond its associated cost. 

Wiring cost was quantified as the sum of Euclidean distances for established links. Communication capacity was estimated using three dynamics: 1. Routing (shortest path length), 2. Diffusion (random walker-based effective distance), and 3. Propagation (broadcasting along all pathways with longer pathways contributing proportionally less). Essentially, these models can be arranged along a spectrum of solutions to minimizing the energetic cost of parallel signaling, with routing representing the most and diffusion the least expensive parallel communication. 

A trade-off parameter was fitted for each dynamic to ensure the resulting synthetic networks matched the empirical network density (20%). It is crucial to note that the goal of this framework is not to replicate the brain’s connectivity pattern but to create networks where individual nodes achieve an optimal balance between wiring cost and signaling efficiency, conditioned on the underlying communication dynamics. Thus, the model remains fundamentally agnostic to the brain’s connectivity pattern, relying solely on the spatial arrangement of nodes and the network’s density. Therefore, the modeling framework may not necessarily converge to brain-like connectivity patterns but to a pattern that optimizes this trade-off. The resulting networks were then used as normative references and compared with empirical data to determine whether the brain also exhibits characteristics of an optimal communication network.

Lastly, to assess computational properties, all emergent networks were incorporated as connectivity matrices in a reservoir computing framework, and their performance was evaluated on a memory capacity task. These models resemble conventional artificial neural networks, particularly recurrent neural networks, but differ primarily in their training approach. However, in these models the hidden layer, or ‘reservoir,’ remains unaltered, while only the readout layer is tuned to solve the task. This feature makes reservoir computing particularly suitable for measuring computational properties of different connectivity patterns [5].

Results

Firstly, we asked whether connectivity in the brain shows characteristics of an efficient communication network. We used ‘endpoint similarity,’ defined as the cosine similarity between the connection pattern of the same node in two networks (e.g., emerged from routing-based dynamics vs. the brain), to address this question. A value of one represents the highest similarity, i.e., when a region is linked exactly to all the same regions as in the target network, and no similarity results in zero. Averaging across all nodes, we found that networks constrained by all three communication dynamics better capture the empirical data compared to randomized, latticized, and fully random networks (between 0.5 and 0.6 vs. 0.2). Moreover, modeled networks also performed better than null networks in predicting individual links in the empirical brain, given two measures of accuracy (80% vs. 65%) and F1 score (between 0.5-0.6 vs. 0.2). 

Specifically, networks constrained by propagative dynamics were the most successful at generating structured long-range connectivity, resulting in architectures that were more efficient (lower average path length) and more economical (lower wiring cost) than even the empirical human brain. This is in contrast with networks generated based on diffusion dynamics that contained almost no long-range connectivity, and networks derived from routing dynamics tend to exhibit far more randomness than those of the brain. 

More interestingly, when tested for computational capacity, the human brain exhibited superior memory capacity compared to the diffusion- and routing-based, but not propagation-based networks. In other words, network configurations that emerge from propagative dynamics have a more cost-effective architecture than the human brain while having the same computational capacity. 

However, further investigation revealed a trade-off between communication and computation itself where after a point, better efficiency leads to worse computational capacity. We found that the human brain sits right at this critical edge (A). Moreover, the cost-efficient, propagation-based networks achieved their optimum by forming a centralized, high-cost, high-capacity “oligarchy” backbone, funneling all communication through them (B). In contrast, the human brain relies on a more distributed “rich club” organization. Crucially, targeted attacks demonstrated that the centralized “oligarchy” structure was fragile, leading to rapid functional breakdown and loss of advantage in communication capacity (C). The human brain’s distributed rich club organization, despite being costlier and less efficient, better resisted these attacks, ensuring higher computational reliability. 

Lastly, we found that reservoirs based on the human brain distribute their readout weights across different regions while the oligarchy is the focus of attention in model networks (D and E). This happens because the oligarchic core integrates information across peripheral regions, providing a relatively higher representational diversity. This is also the reason for their immediate collapse after targeted lesions.

Conclusion

Our findings challenge the traditional view that the human connectome is purely an optimal trade-off between wiring cost and communication efficiency. We demonstrated that local, competitive self-organization under propagative signaling dynamics naturally yields a network that is both more cost-efficient and communication-efficient than the human brain. 

However, this hyper-efficient architecture comes at the cost of computational reliability. The human brain's seemingly suboptimal wiring is probably a direct evolutionary trade-off for robustness, favoring a distributed “rich club” that resists functional failure over a centralized, yet fragile, “oligarchy.” 

This work reveals a previously overlooked and prominent design principle for cortical networks: the trade-off of both cost and efficiency for reliable computation. Our generative framework thus highlights an interplay where top-down pressures for network-level reliability govern bottom-up, local competition for connectivity.
