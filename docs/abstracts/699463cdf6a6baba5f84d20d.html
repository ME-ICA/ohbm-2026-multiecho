Introduction

Early alcohol use is a significant public health concern, as even minimal experimentation in early adolescence has been associated with increased risk for long-term negative neurodevelopmental and psychiatric outcomes. Identifying early neural predictors of when alcohol use is first initiated is therefore critically important, as it provides an opportunity to map neurocircuit markers that may inform targeted prevention efforts before alcohol use begins1.

Contemporary developmental models posit that adolescent substance use may arise from a temporal asynchrony between prefrontal and limbic neural networks, leading to limited inhibitory control capabilities or tendencies and heightened responsiveness to rewards2. However, most neuroimaging studies in this domain are cross-sectional and focus on youth who have already begun drinking, making it difficult to disentangle pre-existing neural vulnerabilities from alcohol-related neurotoxic effects. 

To address this gap, the present study used connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM), a machine-learning approach that leverages whole-brain functional connectivity to predict behavioral outcomes3. Prior CPM studies have demonstrated utility for identifying specific neural networks associated with clinically related outcomes. For example, activity patterns of specific networks during inhibitory control and reward processing tasks can predict alcohol use risk in older adolescents who have already begun drinking4. Yet it remains unknown whether activity patterns during those tasks can also predict when adolescents first begin using alcohol. 

Therefore, this study aimed to use CPM on task-based fMRI measures of inhibitory control and reward processing collected from alcohol-naïve preadolescents aged 9-10 years to predict alcohol initiation by early adolescence (age 14). We further compared the similarities and differences in the predictive networks across inhibitory control and reward processing domains.

Methods

Design

Data were drawn from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study, a multi-site longitudinal cohort following 11,875 youth from baseline (i.e., at age 9-10) into young adulthood5. The current analysis used de-identified Release 5.1 data and was exempt from further ethical review. A longitudinal design was implemented in which functional connectivity during baseline fMRI tasks was used to predict alcohol initiation during the subsequent 4-year follow-up.

Participants

Analyses included participants who met the following criteria in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study: 1) were alcohol-naïve at baseline measures, i.e., no sipping or drinking reported; 2) subsequently reported initiation of alcohol use at least once on any follow-up mid-year and annual Timeline Follow-Back interview; 3) had usable baseline fMRI data with a motion threshold < 0.3mm for both neuroimaging tasks.Alcohol initiation was defined as the earliest interval at which a participant self-reported any alcohol use (sip or full drink) on mid-year and annual Timeline Follow-Back Interviews throughout the 4-year follow up period. Initiation was coded on an 8-level discrete scale reflecting 6-month intervals (1 = earliest initiation, at age 9 to 10 years old; 8 = latest initiation, at age 13 to 14 years old).

Neuroimaging Acquisition and Preprocessing

Preprocessed fMRI data were obtained from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study database. Inhibitory control was assessed using the Stop Signal Task and reward processing using the Monetary Incentive Delay Task. Full details of imaging acquisition and preprocessing procedures are described extensively elsewhere5.

Functional Connectivity

Connectivity analyses were conducted following established procedures using BioImage Suite3. Preprocessed fMRI data were spatially normalized to MNI space, which enabled direct application of the Shen 268-node atlas for time series extraction. Pairwise Pearson correlations between all node pairs were computed and Fisher z-transformed, yielding 268×268 matrices for each participant and task. Task connectivity was computed from raw task time series to preserve both intrinsic and task-evoked network interactions. Connectivity was examined at edge-, node-, and network-levels using ten canonical networks: medial frontal, frontoparietal, default mode, motor-sensory, visual A, visual B, visual association, salience, subcortical, and cerebellar.

Connectome-Based Predictive Modeling (CPM)

CPM was implemented in MATLAB R2023b following established protocols3, controlling for motion i.e., mean framewise displacement. For each fMRI task, CPM were performed in four steps: 1) significant edges associated with alcohol initiation were selected within training folds using Spearman correlations; 2) positive and negative predictive networks were constructed by summing edge strengths; 3) linear models relating network strength to initiation were trained using ten-fold cross-validation repeated 100 times; and 4) predictive accuracy was assessed via Spearman correlations between observed and predicted alcohol initiation values, with significance evaluated using 1,000-iteration permutation testing.

Network Neuroanatomy and Comparison 
 To assess contributions of individual networks, virtual lesioning was performed using leave-one-network-in procedures, wherein CPM models were retrained after remaining one canonical network and its connections with other networks. To compare predictive networks across inhibitory control and reward processing, we conducted edgewise intersection analyses to quantify the degree of overlap between the CPM-derived positive and negative networks from each task. Results

Sample Characteristics 

The final analytic sample comprised N = 330 youth (male n = 163, female n = 167, mean baseline age = 9.65 years, SD = 0.48). The average alcohol initiation score was 5.3, which indicated that alcohol initiation occurred at 12-12.5 years old on average.

Model Predictive Accuracy 

Both inhibitory control and reward processing models significantly predicted alcohol initiation after permutation testing (i.e., yielded significant associations between observed and predicted values of alcohol initiation; inhibitory control ρ = .13, p = .032; reward processing ρ = .20, p = .001).

Canonical Network Contributions

Figure 2 visualizes the network-level and post-hoc virtual lesioning results from both task models. In the inhibitory control model, compared to other canonical network connections, relatively more positive edges i.e., positive connections within the motor-sensory network, between salience and motor-sensory networks, and between default mode and medial frontal networks, positively predicted alcohol initiation (Figure 2A). Relatively more negative edges, i.e., negative connections between motor-sensory and medial frontal, default mode, and frontoparietal networks positively predicted alcohol initiation (Figure 2B). Virtual lesioning revealed that the salience network contributed to the highest standalone predictive strength (Figure 2C).

The reward processing model also identified several similar evident networks as found in the inhibitory control model. Relatively more positive edges between motor-sensory and salience networks, between motor-sensory and visual association networks, and between cerebellar and frontoparietal networks positively predicted alcohol initiation (Figure 2D). Relatively more negative predictive edges between salience and frontoparietal, between frontoparietal and motor-sensory, between medial frontal and motor-sensory network connections positively predicted alcohol initiation in early adolescence (Figure 2E). Virtual lesioning again identified the salience network as the strongest contributor to model performance in the leave-one-network-in analyses (Figure 2F).

Overlap Between Inhibitory Control and Reward Processing Predictors

Edgewise intersection analyses revealed limited overlap between task domains. 299 edges were shared across two models, which constituted 15.4% of inhibitory control edges (total edges =1940) and 15.7% of reward processing edges (total edges = 1909), indicating largely distinct predictive signatures. Network-level analyses conducted following Bioimage Suite pipeline showed that the overall shared predictive edges connections were concentrated in connections linking salience and frontoparietal networks. Nearly no cross-directional overlap emerged (e.g., negative inhibitory control edges did not overlap with positive reward processing edges and vice versa).

Conclusion

The present study provided novel evidence that large-scale functional-connectivity patterns during inhibitory control and reward processing in alcohol-naïve preadolescents could predict alcohol initiation in early adolescence. Using a cross-validated predictive modeling framework, we identified distributed network-level signatures that were significantly associated with the onset of alcohol use over a four-year developmental window. These findings highlighted the value of whole-brain predictive approaches for understanding early-emerging vulnerability to substance use3.

Both CPM models, constructed from task-based functional connectivity during inhibitory control and reward processing, achieved statistically significant predictive performance, supporting that inter-individual differences in functional brain organization within these circuits contribute to variability in the timing of adolescent alcohol initiation. Across models, the salience network consistently emerged as the strongest standalone contributor, and the major overlap between tasks involved salience-frontoparietal connections. This finding is consistent with developmental model proposing that dysregulated salience processing amplifies reward-driven impulses or reduces the efficiency of top-down control during early adolescence2.

Despite this shared involvement of salience circuitry, the inhibitory control and reward processing models showed largely distinct predictive neural signatures. As only a small proportion of edges (approximately 15% of total edges) overlapped across tasks, indicating that early alcohol initiation may still arise from partially independent neurocognitive pathways. In the inhibitory control model, predictive edges were dominated by interactions between motor-sensory, medial frontal, default mode, and frontoparietal control systems, which may indicate the increased susceptibility to early drinking when adolescents encounter opportunities for novel salient behaviors. By contrast, the reward processing model involved a broader pattern of functional interactions, spanning motor-sensory-salience, motor-sensory-visual association, cerebellar-frontoparietal, and medial frontal networks, which may indicate that during reward anticipation and valuation, early alcohol use vulnerability may arise from multiple neural systems, rather than being attributable to dysfunction in any single reward pathway.

Overall, the study suggested that the networks most predictive of early alcohol initiation involve salience-driven coordination across control, reward, and sensorimotor systems. These results raised the possibility that early interventions may benefit from targeting the regulation of salience attribution and the flexible engagement of control networks during motivationally charged contexts. More broadly, the findings illustrate the promise of large-scale longitudinal neuroimaging datasets for identifying preclinical neural vulnerabilities before substance use begins. Future work may integrate environmental, genetic, and behavioral factors into predictive models to examine how these neural signatures evolve across adolescence.
