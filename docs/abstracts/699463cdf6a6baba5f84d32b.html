Introduction

Common marmosets shared similar sensory processing pathways with humans, have gained increasing attention as animal models for investigating the neural mechanisms underlying sensory and cognitive functions. Their relatively small brains enable the recording of cortical layer–specific activity across the entire cortex, providing a more comprehensive view of whole-brain responses compared with the restricted coverage typically possible in human ultra–high-field functional magnetic resonance imaging (UHF-fMRI). Fingers, as flexible and functionally important body parts, play an important role in interactions with the external world. Although S1 is known to exhibit layer–specific activity patterns during tactile processing ^1^2, how individual fingers are represented across cortical layers has remained largely unknown. Using UHF-fMRI, we investigated layer-specific representations of the five fingers in marmosets in the primary somatosensory cortex (S1). 

Methods

An adult male common marmoset (Callithrix jacchus; aged 8 years old; body weight 340-360g) was used in the present study. All fMRI experiments were performed in a 7T/20-cm bore magnet (Bruker‐Biospin, Billerica, MA, USA). A saddle coil with an inner diameter of 86 mm was used as a transmit coil, and the MR signal was acquired from single loop coil (Takashima Seisakusho Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). On the day of the experiment, initial sedation was induced by a small dose of ketamine (20mg/kg) and isoflurane (2.5%) inhaled via a facemask. After the marmoset received cannulation of a leg vein, anesthesia was switched to a constant intravenous infusion of dexmedetomidine (5ug/kg/hr) and 0.5% isoflurane. A battle of cerebral blood volume (CBV)-weighted runs, consisting of one block-designed run and one pair of traveling-wave runs (forward and backward) started 30 minutes after injection of 20 mg/kg of 30-nm ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide (USPIO) particles (Molday ION, Biophysics Assay Laboratory, Inc., USA). CBV-weighted fMRI datasets were obtained with a single shot gradient echo (GE), echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence (FOV, 25.875 x 23 mm2; matrix, 72 x 64; slice thickness, 1 mm; voxel size, 0.36 x 0.36 x 1.0 mm3; acquisition bandwidth, 250 kHz; TE, 10 ms; TR, 2000 ms; flip angle, 60 degrees). 

Five-finger stimulation were delivered using a custom-designed marmoset finger stimulator (Fig1.a) which fixed the marmoset's hand and provided controlled mechanical stimulation to each of the five fingers ^3. Block-designed run consisted of 30 cycles of 20-second rest (off) and 30-second stimulation (on) blocks (Fig1.b). During the on periods, one of the five fingers on the marmoset's right hand was stimulated at 5 Hz. Population receptive field (pRF) mapping included both forward and backward runs, each consisting of eight cycles of off and on periods. The off period lasted 30 seconds, while during the on period (Fig1.b), all five fingers were stimulated sequentially without rest (forward run: D1 → D5; backward run: D5 → D1), with each finger stimulated for 8 seconds before switching to the next. The study protocol was approved by the experimental animal committee at Kyoto University, Japan. 

We first preprocessed fMRI data and applied general linear model (GLM) analysis for identifying the 5 fingers representations in S1 from the block-designed experiments and employed a population receptive field (pRF) model to map the somatosensory representation of the five fingers from the traveling-wave experiments. Then identified the voxels expressed peak t-value response to the finger stimulation as this finger's representational center from BLOCK run, and the center of mass voxel as the finger's representational center from pRF run. Guided by activation areas extracted from the GLM and pRF models, we then manually draw the gray-mater masks from 21st to 23rd slices. Each mask was analyzed from layer (from WM to CSF; 6 layers) and column directions (inferior to superior; 20 columns). Then we analyzed the signal change (SC) (averaged from 6s to 16s after stimulation) across the five finger stimulation conditions in the block-designed experiment. Based on the understanding that middle layers of S1 process sensory information at an early stage, we defined the voxels expressed peak SC as the finger's representational center. 

Results

We first observed robust activations corresponding to stimulation of the five fingers in S1 using both the block-designed and traveling-wave experiments. A spatial topography of 5 fingers was found (Fig1.C). Representations of D1 through D5 were arranged from inferior to superior along the central sulcus. This organization was supported across GLM, pRF, and signal-change–based analyses. However, the five finger representations were not sharply segregated. Even in the middle layer, typically associated with early sensory processing, the responses to D2 and D3 showed overlap, indicating integrated rather than isolated processing of these digits.

Conclusion

Using a newly developed mechanical stimulation device and ultra-high-field CBV-weighted fMRI, we characterized the layer-specific representations of the five fingers in the marmoset S1. Importantly, our results reveal a consistent topographic arrangement across multiple analytical approaches, providing new insights into the laminar organization of somatosensory representations in non-human primates.
