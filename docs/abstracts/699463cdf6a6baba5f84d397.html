Introduction

Classical psychedelics have regained attention in psychiatry and neuroscience due to their potential to treat brain disorders and enhance our understanding of brain processes underlying perception, cognition, and mood. These substances mainly act on the serotonin 2A receptor and produce similar subjective effects. The Entropic Brain Hypothesis proposes that psychedelics increase brain entropy, resulting in more flexible brain dynamics, which may underlie their characteristic subjective effects. However, stimulants such as d-Amphetamine and MDMA also affect perception, neurotransmission, and brain networks, but their effect on brain entropy is not well understood. Using a large, multi-trial neuroimaging dataset, we examined whether entropy measures capture specific signatures of the psychedelic state or reflect sensitive yet nonspecific responses to pharmacological perturbation.

Methods

We analysed resting-state fMRI data from 79 healthy participants in three randomised, placebo-controlled, crossover studies, comprising a total of 255 nine-minute sessions acquired on a 3T Siemens scanner. Study 1 compared placebo and LSD (N=22), Study 2 compared placebo, LSD, d-Amphetamine, and MDMA (N=25), and Study 3 compared LSD, psilocybin, and mescaline (N=32). All data were collected using consistent acquisition protocols and preprocessing pipelines. Preprocessing with the Configurable Pipeline for the Analysis of Connectomes (C-PAC) included slice-timing correction, motion correction, intensity normalisation, nuisance regression with component-based noise correction, bandpass filtering (0.01–0.1Hz), and registration to MNI space. We used the Schaefer-200 atlas and Yeo-7 network, defining 200 cortical regions across seven functional networks: visual, somatomotor, dorsal attention, ventral attention, limbic, control, and default mode. We calculated brain modularity, complexity and entropy metrics, including Lempel–Ziv complexity, meta-state complexity, normalised spatial component entropy, dynamic conditional correlation entropy, and multiscale sample entropy at short (3.6–5.4s) and long (18–27s) timescales. We used linear mixed-effects models to assess drug effects, adjusting for sex, session order, prior drug exposure, and study cohort. We used the Holm–Bonferroni correction across all outcome measures to control for multiple comparisons, with a primary focus on contrasting psychedelic drugs with the stimulant conditions.

Results

Psychedelics significantly increased meta-state complexity (β=0.07, 95% CI [0.04, 0.1], pFWER<0.01) and modularity (β=0.6, 95% CI [0.4, 0.8], pFWER<0.001) compared to stimulants, which showed numerically opposite trends. Both drug classes increased Lempel–Ziv complexity and normalised spatial component entropy relative to placebo, with no significant differences between them. Psychedelics increased multiscale sample entropy distinctly at the short-timescale in transmodal networks, including control (β=0.01, 95% CI [0.008, 0.017], pFWER<0.001), default mode (β=0.015, 95% CI [0.01, 0.02], pFWER<0.001), and dorsal (β = 0.01, 95% CI [0.007, 0.017], pFWER<0.001) and ventral attention networks (β=0.007, 95% CI [0.002, 0.011], pFWER=0.07), and at long timescales, reduced entropy in control (β=-0.03, 95% CI [-0.04, -0.02], pFWER<0.001), default mode (β=-0.03, 95% CI [-0.04, -0.01], pFWER<0.01), and ventral attention networks (β=-0.02, 95% CI [-0.03, -0.01], pFWER=0.02). Network-level normalised spatial component entropy was higher for both drug classes relative to placebo, with no difference between them. Dynamic conditional correlation entropy was also significantly increased under psychedelics across transmodal networks and between-network interactions, whereas stimulants produced more limited effects, primarily within the somatomotor network.

Conclusion

Our results provide insight into potentially psychedelic-specific effects on brain entropy, complexity, and modularity. Modularity, meta-state complexity, multiscale sample entropy, and dynamic conditional correlation entropy showed distinct effects relative to MDMA and d-Amphetamine, whereas Lempel–Ziv complexity and normalised spatial component entropy increased under both psychedelics and stimulants, reflecting general sensitivity to psychoactive substances. These findings refine the Entropic Brain Hypothesis by identifying metrics and spatial patterns that distinguish psychedelic effects from other psychoactive drugs, highlighting brain dynamics that may underlie the acute subjective experience and potentially its therapeutic effects.
