Introduction

Working memory (WM) enables short-term maintenance of information through dynamic and distributed neural processes 1. Although tactile WM has been shown to recruit both somatosensory and higher-order parietal areas 2, the temporal evolution and performance relevance of these representations remain unclear. Prior evidence suggests that WM content may transition from concrete sensory-grounded formats to more abstract parietal representations 3, yet the functional significance of such transformations in the tactile domain is not well understood. Using a well-established retro-cued tactile delayed-match-to-sample task, we aimed to characterize when and where spatial layouts of vibrotactile stimuli are represented during WM, and whether representational strength relates to behavioral performance. Building on our concept of a “tactospatial sketchpad” 2, we hypothesized an early involvement of contralateral somatosensory cortex (S1) followed by later engagement of superior parietal lobe (SPL), reflecting representational transformation across the WM delay. We further examined whether decoding accuracy in these regions predicts successful task performance, providing key evidence on the functional relevance of parietal representations for tactile WM.

Methods

Nineteen right-handed healthy adults completed two fMRI sessions while performing a tactile retro-cued delayed-match-to-sample task (Figure 1). Vibrotactile patterns were delivered to the left index finger using a 4×4 piezoelectric Braille display. On each trial, two sample stimuli were presented sequentially, followed by a retro-cue indicating which stimulus to memorize during a 6-s delay. A subsequent two-alternative forced-choice test required participants to identify the memorized pattern. 

Functional images were acquired with 1-s TR and modeled using finite impulse response (FIR) estimates. Multivoxel pattern analysis (MVPA) was performed using a whole-brain searchlight with linear SVM classifiers to decode the identity of memorized stimuli across delay-period time bins. Six pairwise classifications were averaged for each voxel. Early (bins 5–7) and late (bins 8–10) WM phases were analyzed and statistical parametric maps were thresholded at p < 0.05 FWE-corrected.

Control analyses tested whether non-memorized stimuli could be decoded, enabling dissociation of WM-specific representations from residual perceptual activity. Test–retest reliability across sessions was quantified via intraclass correlation coefficients. Performance relevance was assessed by comparing decoding accuracy between low- and high-performing participants (median split) and correlating individual decoding strength with accuracy. Main behavioral and decoding analyses have been additionally replicated using non-parametric inference.  

Results

Participants performed above chance in the demanding WM task (mean 61.8%). Initially, in the delay period (early WM), significant decoding of memorized spatial layouts emerged in contralateral S1 and right anterior SPL (Figure 2A). Subsequently, decoding became stronger and shifted toward bilateral posterior SPL, consistent with a temporal transformation of tactile WM representations (late WM; Figure 2B). While S1 decoding was less reliable across sessions, PPC decoding—especially in left SPL—showed good test–retest reliability. Critically, decoding of non-memorized stimuli did not yield significant clusters, confirming that successful classification reflected WM-specific content rather than perceptual residues. 

Performance-dependent analyses revealed significantly higher decoding accuracy in left SPL for high-performing participants (Figure 2C). Across sessions, SPL decoding accuracy positively correlated with behavioral performance, indicating that distinctiveness of SPL representations relates to successful memory retention. These findings demonstrate that tactile WM engages a dynamic network in which encoding begins in sensory cortex and maintenance transitions to higher-order parietal regions, and that behavioral success depends on the robustness of SPL-based representations.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that tactospatial WM is supported by a dynamically evolving neural network extending from early somatosensory areas to higher-level parietal regions. Early involvement of S1 likely reflects sensory-grounded encoding of tactile spatial layout, whereas later maintenance relies on bilateral posterior SPL, consistent with a hierarchical transformation from concrete to more abstract mnemonic codes. The relationship between SPL decoding and performance underscores the functional significance of parietal representations for WM maintenance and highlights individual variability in WM efficiency.

These results not only replicate but extend previous work by revealing the temporal unfolding of WM representations with higher temporal resolution and a shorter delay interval. They strengthen the conceptual link between tactile and visuospatial WM 1 and provide the foundation for the ongoing efforts in the MeMoSLAP Research Unit to enhance WM performance through non-invasive brain stimulation targeting the left SPL.
