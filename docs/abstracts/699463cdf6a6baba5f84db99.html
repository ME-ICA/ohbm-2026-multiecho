Introduction

The increasing prevalence and burden of Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases underscore the need to better understand disease aetiology, identify and target modifiable risk factors, and accelerate diagnosis and treatment. To tackle these goals, focusing on the pre-clinical stage (i.e., the early stages of protein accumulation in the brain) or the prodromal stage (i.e., prior to the onset of specific and debilitating symptoms) is key.

Epidemiological data have led to the identification of many of the validated risk factors, largely thanks to large-scale registry data that can gather patients’ clinical data over several decades. However, this fantastic data source has some limitations. To name a few, data may be heterogeneous in terms of quality and completeness, depending on the sources (e.g., primary care, hospital), and possible confounding factors, such as socioeconomic status, BMI, smoking, or drinking habits, are rarely available. Thus, using complementary data and approaches can improve confidence in the results or identify additional risk factors. For example, genetics has been used to confirm the shared genetic aetiology of Alzheimer’s disease with educational attainment and omics data such as methylation have also helped link Parkinson’s with lead and pesticide exposure. An under-explored source of data remains brain MRI, which could help draw new links between neurodegenerative disorders and medical history. Indeed, grey-matter structure is impacted in the early stages of the disease (a consequence of protein accumulation), which can help identify risk factors in the prodromal or pre-clinical stages.

We sought to leverage structural brain MRI to identify novel prodromal risk factors of Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease. We tested whether the neuroanatomical signatures of medical conditions/medications were associated with early disease stages of Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease.

Methods

We leveraged MRI data from > 36,000 adults in the UK Biobank, together with detailed disease codes (lifetime ICD-10 [International Classification of Diseases – 10th edition]) from hospital and primary care records, as well as self-reported regular medications (ATC [Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical] codes). Both levels of information are complementary as they can capture different medical events (acute and chronic). We utilised Best Linear Unbiased Predictors to learn the neuroanatomical signature associated with each ICD-10 or ATC code, which we applied to Alzheimer’s (ADNI, OASIS3) and Parkinson’s (PPMI) databases that included individuals in early disease stages (prior to diagnosis of Alzheimer’s dementia or early stages of Parkinson’s disease). We tested whether each neuroanatomical signature (over the whole brain, or at a regional level) was associated with disease risk and followed up on significant associations using a genetic approach called Mendelian Randomisation that can generate causal hypotheses about the disease aetiology.

Results

Using whole-brain neuroanatomical signatures, we identified 18 ICD-10 codes and 7 ATC codes associated with early Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s disease (Figure 1).  Most of the associations were with AD and linked medical history to an increased disease risk. Four codes corresponded to known risk factors of Alzheimer’s disease: codes relative to obesity (ICD-10 chapter: E65-E68 Obesity and other hyperalimentation), depression (ICD chapter F30-F39 Mood [affective] disorders, and corresponding ATC N06 : Psychoanaleptics) as well as the broad ICD chapter Z70-Z76 Persons encountering health services in other circumstances, which includes codes relating to physical inactivity (Z72.3 Lack of physical exercise), smoking (Z72.0 Tobacco use, Z71.6 Tobacco abuse counselling), and alcohol use (Z72.1 Alcohol use, Z71.4 Alcohol abuse counselling and surveillance). 

                  Other risk factors whose neuroanatomical signature was associated with early AD included diseases of the brain (N06 : Psychoanaleptics; F00-F09 Organic, including symptomatic, mental disorders; F30-F39 Mood [affective] disorders; G90-G99 Other disorders of the nervous system; l60-l69 Cerebrovascular diseases), cardiovascular conditions (C05: Vasoprotective drugs, I60-I69 Cerebrovascular diseases, I70-I79 Diseases of arteries, arterioles and capillaries), immune related conditions (A07 : Antidiarrheals, intestinal anti-inflammatory/anti-infective agents; D07 : Topical dermatological corticosteroids; D80-D89 Certain disorders involving the immune mechanism and to some extent R01 : Nasal preparations and S03 : Ophthalmological and otological preparations), as well as injuries (S30-S39 Injuries to the abdomen, lower back, lumbar spine and pelvis; S50-S59 Injuries to the elbow and forearm; S60-S69 Injuries to the wrist and hand; T90-T98 Sequelae of injuries, of poisoning and of other consequences of external causes).

Two ICD-10 codes were associated with a reduced risk for Parkinson's disease, including Q65-Q79 Congenital malformations and deformations of the musculoskeletal system, which was also associated with a reduced risk of AD. 

At the regional level, we further identified 93 associations with Alzheimer’s disease, and 20 with Parkinson’s disease (after Bonferroni multiple testing correction). Our results further implicated known risk factors for AD (e.g., lipid-modifying agent, mood disorders, injuries to the head or diabetes) and PD (e.g., injuries to the neck, influenza, and pneumonia) via brain regions previously implicated in the disease progression (e.g., hippocampus, amygdala, entorhinal gyrus for AD, thalamus and superior temporal gyrus for PD). We identified several novel potential risk factors related to inflammation and corticosteroids, renal failure, or conditions affecting women’s health (Alzheimer’s); digestive and epilepsy (Parkinson’s). Mendelian randomisation further identified two possible causal pathways to Alzheimer’s disease, including one linking the STX6 gene to dermatological drug use (D11) and the neuroanatomical signature of the entorhinal gyrus.    

Conclusion

This study breaks new ground in understanding the link between health events and neurodegenerative risk by leveraging brain MRI from 36,000 UK Biobank participants. Beyond confirming established factors like head injuries and diabetes, we uncovered novel, potentially modifiable risk pathways, such as the role of dermatological drugs/conditions and the STX6 gene in Alzheimer’s pathology. By integrating neuroimaging with genetic and pharmacological data, we clarify how health events shape may brain vulnerability, offering actionable insights for risk reduction. This work highlights the power of brain MRI to reveal hidden connections between health, medication, and long-term disease risk
