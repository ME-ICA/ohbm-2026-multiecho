Introduction

Visual mental imagery (VMI) is the ability to generate and maintain visual images in one's mind in the absence of direct visual signals. VMI is a fundamental aspect of human cognition and is central to understanding how our mental world is constructed, e.g.,   in spatial navigation reading comprehension ), and moral judgment (Amit and Greene, 2012). Previous neuroimaging studies using fMRI have demonstrated that mental imagery engages a distributed 

 In a typical VMI task, participants were required to imagine one of two oriented gratings before they were asked to judge the rivalry display briefly represented on the screen (Keogh et al., 2020). Moreover, studies on grating orientation VMI tasks have reported activation of the early visual cortex (Albers et al., 2013). Shared representations were evident in the low-level visual cortex between complex VMI and perception processes (Xie et al., 2020). Moreover, object VMI often displays a transmission of signals from high-level cortical areas to the low-level visual cortex following stimulus onset, indicating that early perceptual processes are reactivated in reverse order during imagery (Dijkstra et al., 2020).

Collectively, these findings suggest a nuanced relationship between imagery content and its neural substrates, as revealed by fMRI studies, yet the spatiotemporal dynamics of these processes remain largely uncharacterized. Notably, researchers also explored the specific frequency along with both the simple and complex VMI to enable a more precise examination of their underlying neural mechanisms. Among the frequency bands, the alpha band has received the most extensive attention (Dijkstra et al., 2020). This is partly because the alpha frequency band was reported as the shared band between VMI and visual perception (Xie et al., 2020). Furthermore, it has been shown to exhibit significant activity across various VMI tasks ( Hu and Yu, 2023). 

Methods

Participants

Experiment 1: In the grating VMI study, 38 healthy participants with normal visual acuity were enrolled in the study. Experiment 2: In the object VMI study, 38 healthy participants with normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity were recruited. Further details about the participants can be found in (Xie et al., 2020). 

Experimental design

Experiment 1: In the grating orientation VMI task, the imagery image set consists of gratings at 45° and 135° on a black background. The stimulus set includes auditory cues representing a 45° grating with a low tone and a 135° grating with a high tone. The auditory cue was produced by using the MATLAB function "sound" to play a basic tone (450 or 1,000 Hz). The average duration of these sound recordings was 200 ms. In the grating orientation VMI task, participants were presented with audio recordings of high and low tones and were asked to actively imagine the corresponding grating associated with the tone they had heard. After hearing the high or low tone, participants were instructed to visually imagine the corresponding grating for a duration of 6000 ms. Following the imagery period, participants were presented with two 500 ms images of grating, separated by a 100 ms interval, during which a placeholder (The placeholder was a white square with 64-pixel sides presented in the center of the screen) was displayed. Within the following 3250-5250 ms, participants were required to discern the rotational direction of the second grating relative to the first grating. Subsequently, participants rated the vividness of their mental imagery as either high or low. The placement of response options was counterbalanced across trials. The majority of trials reported high vividness in mental imagery (1: no imagery; 2: fuzzy and dim; 3: relatively clear; 4: very clear and vivid), suggesting that participants were able to form precise mental images of the gratings. Each participant completed 256 trials of the imagery task during a recording session. Experiment 2:  see detailed infromation about the experimental design of object VMI can be found in (Xie et al., 2020).

Classification of oscillatory responses

To deconstruct the neural mechanisms underlying VMI, we employed a ten-fold cross-validation approach for each subject. We trained classifiers on the training set to discriminate between different categories based on EEG data recorded during two VMI tasks. We then assessed classification accuracy on a test set. We performed classifications with distinct resolutions: channel-specific and frequency band-specific, analyzing data separately for each channel and frequency band, and time-specific and frequency band-specific, examining each frequency band at individual time points. Classification performance exceeding chance levels indicates the presence of category-specific differences at particular frequency bands, channels, or time points. This analysis allowed us to quantify: (1) which frequency bands mediate these category differences, (2) which channels are involved, and (3) the temporal dynamics with which these differences emerge.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For the classification-based analyses, we controlled the familywise error across time points using cluster size inference. All p-value maps were first thresholded at p < 0.05 (cluster-definition threshold) to define supra-threshold clusters by their temporal contiguity. These supra-threshold clusters were used to construct an empirical distribution of maximum cluster size and to estimate a threshold at 5% of the right tail of this distribution. That is, the supra-threshold clusters of the original statistic were reported as significant if their size exceeded p < 0.05. Moreover, for the classification of time-frequency resolved signals, the cluster threshold was Bonferroni-corrected for the number of frequency bands analyzed. For the correlation-based analyses, we corrected p values for multiple comparisons by FDR-correction.

Results

Multiband Spatial Analysis during Simple and Complex VMI

Previous studies investigating VMI have primarily focused on oscillatory activity in the alpha frequency band (Xie et al., 2020). This emphasis raises the question of whether VMI is also associated with oscillatory activity in other frequency bands and whether the activated brain regions differ between simple and complex VMI tasks. The question was addressed by investigating whether oscillatory waves in the different cortices correlate with the content of VMI. Frequency decomposition was conducted and spatial analyses were performed at the channel level across multiple frequency bands for simple grating orientation and complex object VMI tasks (see Methods for details). The decoding accuracy at the channel level for grating orientation and object VMI tasks were 50% and 8%, respectively. The results are as follows:

Experiment 1: The frequency bands associated with grating orientation VMI ranged from 0.5 to 13 Hz. Notably, decoding accuracy was highest in the delta band, reaching 59.8 ± 0.99%. The theta band also exhibited substantial decoding accuracy at 62.0 ± 1.03%, while the alpha band showed lower accuracy at 57.2 ± 0.86%. Significant channel localization for grating orientation VMI was identified in central electrodes (C1/2, C3/4, Cz, FC1/2, FC3/4, CP1/2, CPz), temporal electrodes (T8, TP8, T7, TP7), and occipital electrodes (O1/2, Oz, Iz) (see left panel in Figure 1B). Experiment 2: For object VMI, decoding accuracy in the delta band was 12.4 ± 0.35%, while the theta band showed 12.1 ± 0.36%. The alpha band had lower decoding accuracy at 9.7 ± 0.19%. This aligns with previous findings in object VMI, where significant signals were detected in the theta (4-8 Hz) and alpha (8-13 Hz) bands (Xie et al., 2020). However, Xie et al. (2020) primarily focused on the alpha band's role in visual perception and shared representations, without analyzing the mechanisms of VMI formation in the theta and alpha bands. In our analysis, object VMI demonstrated significant channel localization in central electrodes (C1/2, Cz, FC1/2, FCz), temporal electrodes (T7/8, TP7/8, FT7/8), occipital electrodes (O1/2, Oz), and frontal electrodes (Fp1/2, AF/3, AFz, AF4, AF8, F7/8) (see right panel in Figure 1B). 

The Patterns of Occipital Cortex during Simple and Complex VMI

To further explore the patterns of visual signals in the occipital cortex, the temporal generalization method (King and Dehaene, 2014) was employed, using signals from multiple channels of the occipital cortex during a specific time interval for training, followed by testing with signals from another time interval (see Methods for details). Systematically adopting this approach results in a temporal generalization matrix, where each row corresponds to the time at which the classifier was trained and each column to the time at which it was tested. The results are as follows:

Experiment 1: The experimental results reveal that the activity in the occipital cortex exhibited distinct patterns across frequency bands (see left panel in Figure 3). Specifically, during the grating orientation VMI task, delta band activity in the occipital cortex shows a sustained response related to the grating orientation signal (~275-495 ms), whereas theta band activity displays an oscillatory pattern (~220-495 ms). The sustained pattern, as identified by Corriveau et al. (2023), reflects signals that remain stable over time, while the oscillatory pattern, as described by Tanigawa et al. (2022), represents signals characterized by periodic dynamic fluctuations. Experiment 2: In the object VMI task, delta band activity shows a gradual change pattern corresponding to object category-related signals (~110-1090 ms), while the theta band maintains an oscillatory pattern (~110-660 ms). In addition, the duration of object VMI is longer than that of the grating orientation VMI, likely due to the more complex nature of object imagery.

Conclusion

The aim of this study is to systematically investigate the spatiotemporal mechanisms of both simple and complex VMI tasks. To this end, two VMI datasets were analyzed in the present research. Specifically, a newly collected dataset involving simple grating orientation imagery (Exp. 1) and a publicly available dataset involving complex object imagery (Exp. 2) were examined, separately. A comprehensive spatiotemporal analysis was conducted across multiple frequency bands to compare the two VMI tasks. The results revealed that VMI processes were associated with delta and theta band activity, and suggested that distinct spatiotemporal mechanisms underlie simple and complex VMI.

First, the behavioral results from the orientation-grating VMI task revealed that significantly lower difference thresholds were observed in the congruent condition compared to the incongruent condition in the perceptual task followed VMI. This finding indicates that VMI continues to exert a measurable influence on visual perception tasks even after a delay of 6,000 ms, consistent with the results reported by Kok et al. (2012). While our EEG study of two VMI tasks revealed that significant signals related to imagined content appeared within 1,500 ms (see Figure 3). This discrepancy may stem from the limited spatial resolution of EEG, which reliably captures early-stage cortical generation of VMI but cannot detect later maintenance processes. In future work, we plan to use high-spatial-resolution techniques such as fMRI to further investigate the neural mechanisms underlying both simple and complex VMI.

Additionally,  compared with simple VMI, complex VMI elicited additional activity in frontal cortical regions. The activity of the frontal cortex is classically linked to speech perception (Cope et al., 2017) and auditory working memory (Berger et al., 2019), suggesting that the richer semantic content of complex VMI recruits higher-order signal transmissions. Temporal-generalization analyses of occipital EEG signals during VMI showed that both types of VMI exhibit an oscillatory pattern in the theta band. This oscillatory pattern corresponds to a regularly changing VMI signal (Tanigawa et al., 2022). In contrast, in the delta band, occipital cortex activity during grating orientation VMI exhibits a sustained pattern, whereas during object VMI, it displays a gradual change pattern. The sustained pattern represents a stable and unchanging VMI signal (Corriveau et al., 2023). These findings have significant implications for understanding the neural basis of VMI. They suggest that the brain employs different neural strategies depending on the complexity of the visual information being processed and the frequency band of neural activity. For simpler visual tasks, sustained neural activation may be sufficient, whereas more complex imagery requires dynamic and adaptive neural processes.

Our data provide strong evidence that simple and complex VMI involve distinct neural mechanisms, particularly in the delta and theta frequency bands. In particular, our data suggest that complex VMI requires additional signal transmission between the frontal cortex and other brain regions. Moreover, our results indicate that simple and complex VMI exhibit distinct imagery patterns in the occipital cortex. However, due to the limited spatial resolution of EEG, our analysis of the differences between simple and complex VMI is confined to the early imagery generation phase. In future studies, we plan to incorporate neuroimaging techniques with higher spatial resolution to investigate differences during the VMI maintenance phase.
