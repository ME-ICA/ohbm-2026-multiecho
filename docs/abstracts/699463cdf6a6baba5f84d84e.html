Introduction

Non-invasive presurgical evaluation is crucial for delineating the epileptogenic zone (EZ) in patients with pharmaco-resistant epilepsy and for guiding the implantation of depth electrodes, such as those used in stereo-electroencephalography (SEEG).

Among non-invasive tools, magnetoencephalography (MEG) offers a unique combination of high temporal and spatial resolution^1, enabling the detection of interictal epileptic spikes. Despite its advantages, MEG is a surface measurement, capturing the magnetic fields generated by neural sources within the brain. Localizing these sources is challenging, as it requires solving an inverse problem that is fundamentally underdetermined and ill-posed. This process, known as source localization, has motivated the development of numerous mathematical approaches. However, validating these methods in a clinical setting remains difficult due to the lack of direct intracerebral recordings.

Here, we present the largest dataset of simultaneous MEG and SEEG recordings to date (N = 45), providing direct access to the regions generating epileptic activity and serving as a ground truth for validation. Our aim was to evaluate the accuracy of widely used dipolar source localization methods for epileptic spikes and to provide guidance on the reliability of MEG for mapping the irritative zone (IZ) in patients with epilepsy.

Methods

Simultaneous MEG-SEEG recordings were acquired during 10 minutes of resting-state activity in 45 patients with pharmaco-resistant epilepsy at the Marseille MEG center (Institut de Neurosciences des Systèmes, AMU, Inserm), located within the Epileptology and Cerebral Rhythmology unit.^2. MEG recordings were acquired with a 4D NeuroimagingTM 3600 whole head system, at a sampling frequency of 2034.51 Hz (248 channels). 

Interictal spikes were manually marked on MEG by an experienced epileptologist (F.B.), blinded to SEEG. A total of 1,034 spikes were identified. Within each patient, spikes with similar MEG topographies were grouped into clusters. Eleven patients without detectable interictal spikes were excluded, resulting in a final cohort of 34 patients.

Each patient’s T1-weighted MRI was coregistered with post-implantation CT scans to localize SEEG electrodes. MRIs were segmented using FreeSurfer, and MEG forward models were constructed using a boundary element method implemented in OpenMEEG.

MEG source localization was performed using two dipolar methods: moving dipole^3 and TRAP-MUSIC^4. The moving dipole method estimates, at each time point, the location and orientation of a single equivalent current dipole that best explains the measured MEG fields, allowing dipole parameters to vary dynamically over time. TRAP-MUSIC (Truncated Recursively Applied and Projected MUSIC) is an extension of the MUSIC approach. Like MUSIC, it relies on the separation of the data space into signal and noise subspaces and tests whether candidate source topographies belong to the signal subspace. 

Analyses were conducted at three levels: single-spike, cluster, and whole-trace.

At the single-spike level, a 60 ms window centered on each MEG spike was extracted, and corresponding SEEG signals were obtained. MEG source localization was performed independently for each spike and compared to SEEG. At the cluster level, spikes with similar topographies were averaged to improve signal-to-noise ratio, and localization was performed on the averaged signals. At the whole-trace level, precision–recall metrics quantified each method’s ability to recover the SEEG irritative zone. In addition to sensor-level dipole fitting, dipole fitting was applied to MEG independent components obtained from ICA decomposition.

MEG and SEEG were compared using two approaches: (i) a correlation-based approach, projecting MEG dipolar solutions into SEEG sensor space and computing correlations with SEEG activity (^5), and (ii) a distance-based approach, calculating Euclidean distances between the MEG dipole and the nearest active SEEG contact. Statistical comparisons were performed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with rank-biserial correlation as an effect size.

Results

At the single-spike level, TRAP-MUSIC demonstrated significantly lower localization error than the moving dipole approach (median error: 1.76 mm vs. 2.15 mm; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 2.99 × 10⁻¹¹). The rank-biserial correlation (RBC = −0.207) indicates a modest but consistent improvement across spikes. At the cluster level, localization accuracy was similar between methods, with median errors of 1.72 mm for TRAP-MUSIC and 1.82 mm for the moving dipole (p = 0.073, RBC = −0.221).

At the whole-recording level, performance was assessed using precision and recall within a spatial tolerance radius of 20 mm around the active SEEG channels. TRAP-MUSIC yielded higher mean precision (0.43) than both the moving dipole approach applied to MEG data (0.37) and ICA-based localization (0.36). Mean recall values were comparable between TRAP-MUSIC (0.41) and the moving dipole method on MEG (0.38), whereas ICA showed higher recall (0.53). Overall, these results point to complementary strengths across methods at the whole-recording level, with TRAP-MUSIC favoring spatial precision and ICA favoring recall, while no method consistently outperformed the others across all metrics.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of MEG source localization methods for epileptic spikes using the largest dataset of simultaneous MEG and SEEG recordings to date. At the single-spike level, TRAP-MUSIC demonstrated slightly but consistently improved localization accuracy compared to the moving dipole approach. At the cluster level, differences between methods were minimal. At the whole-recording level, performance metrics highlighted complementary strengths: TRAP-MUSIC favored spatial precision, whereas ICA-based localization yielded higher recall, with the moving dipole method showing intermediate performance.
Overall, these findings support the utility of MEG for mapping the irritative zone in patients with pharmaco-resistant epilepsy and emphasize that the choice of source localization method should be guided by the specific clinical or research objective, balancing spatial accuracy and sensitivity to epileptic activity. Further studies are needed to confirm these results across different epilepsy types.
