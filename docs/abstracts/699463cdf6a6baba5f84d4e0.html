Introduction

A major goal in cognitive neuroscience is to understand how individual differences in brain network development relate to variability in cognitive development. This is particularly salient during the transition from childhood to adolescence. Previous studies have developed cross-sectional brain–cognition models that can predict individual-level cognitive performance in children, adolescents, and young adults based on resting-state functional connectivity (FC).1-3 While valuable, these cross-sectional models reflect a single snapshot of brain–cognition relationships, neglecting dynamic changes that can only be revealed in longitudinal designs.  Here, we leverage longitudinal resting-state fMRI and cognitive data to examine how individual-level brain–cognition predictive models develop longitudinally during this critical period.

Methods

We included 2,949 unrelated ABCD participants with resting-state fMRI and seven cognitive measures at baseline (9.93 ± 0.62 years) and Year 2 (11.94 ± 0.65 years) across 22 sites. FC was estimated using a 419 × 419 matrix (400 cortical + 19 subcortical ROIs),4-5 and overall cognition was represented by the first principal component (PC1) of the seven cognitive measures. Cognitive and FC stability were assessed via baseline–Year 2 correlations, and longitudinal change was modeled using linear mixed-effects models. For the prediction analysis, we applied kernel ridge regression with a leave-3-site-cluster-out nested cross-validation across 10 site clusters (≥280 participants each), yielding 120 replications spanning all possible splits. Cross-sectionally, we used baseline FC to predict baseline cognition, and Year 2 FC to predict Year 2 cognition. For comparison, baseline FC was also used to predict Year 2 cognition. We further tested model transferability by applying baseline-trained models to Year 2 FC and cognition without additional tuning. To control for head motion, we repeated the model transfer analyses in a motion-matched subsample (n = 2,642) with comparable mean framewise displacement across time points (p = 0.18). Longitudinally, we used longitudinal FC change (delta) to predict Year 2 cognition and cognitive change across two timepoints. Prediction significance was assessed using site-wise permutation tests (1,000 permutations) with leave-3-site-cluster-out cross-validation. Model accuracies were compared using the corrected resampled t-test, with multiple comparisons controlled using FDR (q < 0.05). To interpret feature importance, we applied the Haufe transformation to derive a 419 × 419 predictive network feature (PNF) matrix for each cognitive measure. Finally, we repeated all analyses separately in males and females to explore potential sex differences.

Results

Cognitive ability at baseline and Year 2 was positively correlated, suggesting that individuals with higher baseline cognition generally maintained their cognitive advantage over their peers at Year 2 (Figure 1a; FDR q < 0.05). Although participants generally showed improved cognitive ability from baseline to Year 2, except for RAVLT, substantial individual variability in longitudinal cognitive change was observed across most cognitive measures (Figure 2b and 2c; FDR q < 0.05). Similarly, FC showed both longitudinal change and individual stability (Figure 1h–k; FDR q < 0.05), organized along the sensory–association (S-A) axis: sensory regions changed more over time and were less stable across individuals, while association regions exhibited the opposite pattern. We also observed notable individual differences in FC change (Figure 1l and 1m), especially for somatomotor and visual networks, as well as medial prefrontal cortex. Cognitive stability did not differ significantly between males and females (Figure 1d), whereas females showed slightly higher stability only for a small set of FC edges (FDR q < 0.05), particularly in association regions (Figure 1n and 1o). For longitudinal change, males exhibited greater increases than females in overall cognition (PC1; t = −2.40, FDR q < 0.05). Although RAVLT did not show a significant longitudinal change in the combined sample (Figure 1b), sex-stratified analyses uncovered opposing developmental patterns, with males improving over time and females exhibiting a slight decline (t = −4.41, FDR q < 0.05). Sex differences in longitudinal FC change were not uniform across the cortex but were distributed across multiple networks (Figure 1p and 1q; FDR q < 0.05), with females showing shallower decrease in the salience network and steeper decrease in the control network relative to males.

Focusing on the relationship between FC and cognitive performance, we found that baseline FC was a stronger predictor of future cognitive ability than baseline cognition (Figure 2a-c; FDR q <0.05). Moreover, models trained on baseline FC to predict baseline cognition performed better when applied to data at Year 2 (Figure 2d; FDR q < 0.05). These results suggest that brain–cognition relationships strengthen over time. Intriguingly, longitudinal FC change was a weaker predictor of future cognition than baseline FC, even after accounting for differences in reliability (Figure 2e; FDR q < 0.05). Furthermore, FC change provided only limited predictive value for longitudinal change in cognitive ability (Figure 2f and 2g; FDR q < 0.05). This contrasts with the robust cognitive prediction achieved with FC, revealing a prediction-level discrepancy between cross-sectional and longitudinal measures. We also identified convergent and divergent network features across cross-sectional and longitudinal models (Figure 2i–k),  uncovering a multivariate twist on Simpson’s paradox. Sex-stratified predictive analyses showed that only for baseline model, females achieved higher accuracy for Little Man Task than male after FDR correction (FDR q < 0.05). For each sex, we constructed fold-wise binary consistency matrices for each network block, indicating whether the cross-sectional and longitudinal PNFs had the same sign (consistent = 1) or opposite signs (inconsistent = 0) within that block. We then compared the average fold-level consistency between sexes across 120 cross-validation splits using corrected resampled t-tests, with FDR correction applied across network blocks. Females showed higher consistency between the visual and control networks, whereas males showed higher consistency between the somatomotor and dorsal attention networks, within the somatomotor network, and between language and subcortical networks (Figure 2l).

Conclusion

Our large-scale longitudinal analysis reveals that although functional connectome and cognition co-evolve during the transition from childhood to early adolescence, stable individual differences exert a stronger influence on future cognitive ability than short-term neural changes.
