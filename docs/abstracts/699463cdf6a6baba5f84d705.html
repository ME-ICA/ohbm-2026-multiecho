Introduction

Narcolepsy type 1 (NT1), narcolepsy type 2 (NT2) and idiopathic hypersomnia (IH) are central disorders of hypersomnolence (CDH). The neurometabolic effects of hypocretin deficiency in NT1 remain unclear, and NT2 and IH currently lack reliable biomarkers. Previous studies observed functional and structural alterations in key regions of the default mode network (DMN) of patients with CDH1,2. However, the underlying neurochemical drivers of these structural and functional alterations remain under-investigated. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy offers a unique window into these mechanisms by quantifying both metabolites related to synaptic function and markers of neuronal and glial structural integrity.

Early studies reported altered N-acetylaspartate (NAA) levels in the hypothalamus of NT1 patients, suggesting neurodegeneration3. Later work found elevated γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) levels in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)4. Gao et al. (2024) reported higher GABA/glutamate+glutamine (Glx) ratios in the mPFC of people with NT1 during N2 sleep, driven by increased GABA concentrations5. However, prior studies were often limited by small sample sizes, restricting the ability to explore associations between neurometabolite levels and clinical variables such as hypersomnia severity, age of onset, and duration of excessive daytime sleepiness. Additionally, most focused primarily on NT1 compared to well-rested healthy controls, often excluding NT2 and IH.

We therefore investigated neurometabolite levels using MEGA-PRESS spectroscopy in a well-characterized, multicenter sample of NT1, NT2, and IH patients, as well as healthy controls, who were scanned following both a well-rested night and a night of partial sleep deprivation. We focused on two DMN regions, the mPFC and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), to determine whether neurometabolite variations differentiate between groups and provide insight into the neurochemical basis of hypersomnolence.

Methods

This harmonised multicenter study was conducted at Concordia University and the Centre de recherche de l’Institut universitaire de gériatrie de Montréal (CRIUGM, Montreal, Canada), and Amsterdam University Medical Centers (Amsterdam UMC), location VUmc / Spinoza Centre for Neuroimaging (Amsterdam, the Netherlands).

Patients with CDH (NT1 n=20, NT2 n=15, IH n=27) and healthy controls underwent GABA-edited MEGA-PRESS at 3T MRI in the mPFC and PCC. To differentiate disorder-related from state-dependent effects of sleepiness, healthy control participants completed two imaging sessions: one following a habitual night of sleep (rested condition) (n=18) and another following partial sleep deprivation (n=17), restricted to four hours of total sleep opportunity.

In both sites, GABA-edited MEGA-PRESS spectroscopy was used to quantify GABA+ levels in the mPFC and PCC (TE/TR=68/2000 ms, voxel size= 30×30×30 mm³, bandwidth=2000 Hz, vector size=2048, 320 transients). Editing pulses were applied at 1.9 ppm (ON) and 7.5 ppm (OFF) with a 15-20 ms pulse duration. Unsuppressed water reference scans were acquired for eddy-current correction and absolute quantification. Voxel placement was guided by the T1-weighted images and optimized to maximize gray-matter coverage of the target region while avoiding the corpus callosum. 

Water-scaled, tissue-corrected GABA+ from the edited spectrum, glutamate + glutamine (Glx) from the off-spectrum, the ratio between GABA+ and Glx (excitatory-inhibitory ratio), and secondary metabolites related to structural and glial properties (total N-acetylaspartate (tNAA), total Choline (tCho), myo-inositol, from the off-spectrum) were analyzed using preregistered linear mixed models corrected for age, sex, site and intelligence quotient with False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction. Covariate-adjusted partial correlations were used to check for association between metabolites and clinical measures (e.g., hypersomnia severity index, Pittsburgh sleep quality index, mean sleep latency at the multiple sleep latency test, and excessive daytime sleepiness scores). Exploratory covariate-adjusted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses quantified the diagnostic discrimination of CDH subtypes using metabolites for both the mPFC and PCC.

Results

Groups did not differ in age (p=0.14), but sex distribution varied (p=0.03), with a higher proportion of female participants in the patient groups. IQ scores were slightly higher in controls (F=3.12, p=0.038). As expected, patient groups showed elevated daytime sleepiness (F=108.26, p<0.001) and hypersomnolence severity (F=100.03, p<0.001) compared with controls. Sleep quality (Pittsburgh sleep quality index; F=5.07, p=0.005) and sleep inertia (sleep inertia questionnaire; F=43.69, p<0.001) were also worse in patients. Within CDH participants, mean sleep latency differed across groups (F=5.54, p=0.008), being shortest in narcolepsy and longest in IH, while frequency of rapid entry into REM sleep periods distinguished NT1 and NT2 from IH (F=114.58, p<0.001). Age at onset and duration of excessive daytime sleepiness did not differ significantly across patient groups.

Regarding neurometabolites, no significant group differences in GABA+ or Glx survived FDR correction when comparing CDH groups with well-rested healthy controls (Fig. 1A). However, comparisons against sleep-restricted healthy controls revealed state-dependent distinctions: sleep-restricted HC showed higher PCC excitatory-inhibitory ratio than NT1, NT2, and IH (adjusted mean diff.≈1.1 to 1.2, pFDR=0.043) and lower PCC mI than NT1 and NT2 (adjusted mean diff.≈-2.1 to -2.9, pFDR≤0.050). 

Correlational analyses revealed distinct phenotype-specific associations. In the combined CDH sample, higher mPFC tNAA related to poorer sleep quality (Pittsburgh sleep quality index; r=0.38, pFDR=0.018), and higher PCC tCho was associated with longer MSLT sleep latency (r=0.58, pFDR=0.027) (Fig. 1B). Subgroup analyses showed that within NT2, lower mPFC Glx was associated with higher hypersomnolence severity (r=-0.74, pFDR≤0.037) (Fig. 2B). Conversely, within IH, PCC Glx and the excitation-inhibition ratio correlated positively with the duration of excessive daytime sleepiness (r=0.85, pFDR≤0.004) (Fig. 2D). 

Finally, exploratory ROC analyses indicated region-specific diagnostic utility: mPFC metabolites (Glx and mI) showed good discrimination between NT1 and NT2 (AUC=0.81 and 0.84, pFDR<0.002), while PCC tCho and tNAA levels demonstrated good discrimination of NT2 vs IH (AUC=0.90 and 0.89, pFDR<0.001) (Fig. 2A+C). Cross-validated AUCs were lower but preserved this diagnostic hierarchy.

Conclusion

Although no large group-level neurotransmitter abnormalities were observed relative to rested HC, subgroup analyses revealed distinct frontal (NT1/NT2) and posterior (IH) neurometabolic phenotypes. Frontal metabolites differentiated NT1 from NT2 and were associated with disease severity in NT2, whereas posterior excitatory and glial-membrane markers differentiated IH from NT2 and were linked to IH chronicity. These region-specific profiles offer biologically grounded signals that may improve objective differentiation and classification of CDH subtypes beyond existing clinical criteria.
