Introduction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) offers a vast array of acquisition modalities, which are sensitive to different contrast mechanisms and provide complementary views on biological tissue state and microstructure. However, most MRI modalities are notoriously non-specific. Cellular-level changes alter the MRI signals, which enables clinicians to localise the effect of disease. However, identifying the cellular underpinning of these signal changes remains a challenge. The problem is that MRI is simultaneously sensitive to many microstructural properties but lacks specificity to any single one.

Current efforts to map tissue microstructure with MRI use quantitative scans, which quantify MR-visible phenomena, such as the random movement of water (diffusion-weighted MRI1), tissue magnetic susceptibility (susceptibility-weighted MRI2), magnetic relaxation rates (relaxometry3), or the transfer of magnetisation between molecules (magnetisation transfer4). These phenomena can be measured from quantitative MRI, but they do not have an intrinsic biological interpretation. Linking these MR quantities to tissue biology requires biophysical models. Existing models are limited in scope: (1) they rely on simplified parametric descriptions of the complex underlying tissue; (2) they only cover a single MRI modality at a time, ignoring complementary information from other scans; and (3) they don't make use of so-called non-quantitative images.

We recently published a new Monte Carlo MR (MCMR) simulator5 that can simulate a large array of microstructural features and generate signals for arbitrary MRI modalities from the same simulated tissue substrate (Fig 1). In this abstract, we show that our simulator can be used to produce MRI signal fingerprints of tissue changes across multiple modalities (here from diffusion-weighted, magnetisation transfer, spin echo and gradient echo MRI). In the future, this approach will allow for truly multimodal in vivo microstructure mapping.

Methods

The MCMR simulator simultaneously simulates the effect of magnetisation relaxation, diffusion, exchange, magnetic susceptibility, and magnetisation transfer on the MRI signal for arbitrary MRI sequences and digital representations of the tissue geometry (Fig 1).

The simulator uses a Monte Carlo approach. It follows the random walk of individual spins, which is hindered by the user-provided tissue geometry. The spin magnetisation is updated using Bloch equations, which include the off-resonance field due to the tissue magnetic susceptibility of myelinated, infinite cylinders, or of arbitrary meshes.  At each collision with the tissue, there is also user-defined probability of spins to pass through the surface, which enables the modelling of exchange. Magnetisation transfer is implemented by allowing the spin magnetisation to transfer to a stationary “bound” pool during a collision with the tissue. The magnetisation transfer is controlled by a user-defined surface density and dwell time.

Here we model the signal in a simplified representation of partially myelinated white matter. Only axons are modelled, represented as randomly distributed parallel cylinders orthogonal to the main magnetic field. Half of the axons are myelinated. Myelinated axons are represented by an inner and outer cylinder circumscribing the myelin (g-ratio=0.7). The effect of the myelin magnetic susceptibility on the local off-resonance magnetic field is given by the hollow-fibre model6 . T1 and T2 relaxation rates are both set to 2 seconds. Magnetisation transfer is modelled for spins colliding with the cylinders with the magnetisation transfer rate set to such a value that the effective T2 due to magnetisation transfer is about 80 ms. In other words, we assume that the T2 in tissue is dominated by magnetisation transfer from the water with a component with a much shorter T2.

We calculate how the signal deviates from that predicted for this baseline configuration in 5 different scenarios. We reduce the myelin density by half by (1) halving the fraction of myelinated axons or (2) reducing the myelin thickness. We also model the effects of (3) reducing the axon density by 20%, (4) doubling the axon diameters, or (5) turning the axons to be parallel to the main magnetic field. 

Results

Figure 2 shows the resulting multimodal fingerprint illustrating how the five different scenarios of change in microstructure affect the signal for four different modalities. These results suggest that it might be possible to tell the difference between demyelination due to a reduction in the fraction of myelinated axons versus demyelination due to thinner myelin sheaths by studying the relative impact on the spin echo (i.e., T2) versus gradient echo (i.e., T2*). However, the similarity of these fingerprints with reducing axon density suggests that this combination of modalities might not be ideal to distinguish these effects. 

Axon radius and orientation have very distinctive fingerprints with axon orientation only affecting the T2* due to its effect on the off-resonance field generated by the myelin.

For ease of illustration, we limited this visualisation to just a single metric per modality (e.g., radial attenuation for diffusion-weighted MRI). Of course, a full diffusion-weighted MRI acquisition would show sensitivity to axon orientation.

Conclusion

By combining the effects of diffusion, permeability (exchange), magnetic susceptibility, and magnetisation transfer in the MCMR simulator, we allow for a more coherent and multimodal analysis of the different ways in which microstructure affects the MRI signal. The use of the example fingerprint in Figure 2 is limited by the simplified representation of the tissue geometry and the fact that we only consider a single signal (or single signal ratio) for each modality. However, it does illustrate the power of the MCMR simulator to create a multimodal fingerprint of tissue microstructural changes.
