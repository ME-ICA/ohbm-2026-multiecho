Introduction

Decoded neurofeedback (DecNef) is an fMRI technique aimed at subconsciously generating specific brain patterns in participants1. Briefly, it consists of two phases. In the decoder phase, a multivoxel pattern classifier is trained to predict the participant’s multivariate brain pattern associated with a specific cognitive-behavioral feature (the target state). In the neurofeedback phase, this decoder is used in real-time to provide visual feedback to the participant on how close their brain activity is to the target state. Participants will then try to get as close as possible to the target by changing their own thoughts, motivated by a greater monetary compensation. As a result, the target state — specific brain patterns — is generated in the participants, even without their conscious awareness of the features associated with those patterns.

Currently, it is not well understood why some individuals are better at self-regulating their brain activity during DecNef towards the target, while in other cases they fail to induce it reliably. This has been a fundamental problem since the origins of this line of research2. Our goal is to identify brain markers in the functioning and structure of the subcortex capable of explaining these individual differences in previously published DecNef studies. Defining this would help understand the underlying mechanisms of neurofeedback intervention in different cognitive operations and domains, as well as contribute to the development of the therapeutic potential of this technique in more clinical applications for the population.

Methods

We explored the performance (accuracy), functional (fMRI-BOLD), and structural (MRI-T1) data of 102 participants (41 female, 23.9 ± 3.8 years) who underwent different DecNef procedures: (i) 67 participants from the studies 1 (face preference), 2 (color-orientation association), 3 (conditioned fear reduction), and 5 (common fear reduction) published in the DecNef Collection3; (ii) 17 participants from a study of perceptual meaning4; and (iii) 28 participants from a yet-to-be-published study of confidence prediction error.

Selected DecNef studies included multiple neurofeedback sessions — with the same target state — structured in different trials. Each trial was composed of an induction period (≈6 sec, where the participants tried to self-regulate their brain activity), a feedback period (≈6 sec, where the participants were provided with feedback of how successful their previous induction was), and an inter-trial interval (≈6 sec).

Performance of each participant was defined by computing their DecNef induction accuracy; that is, the proportion of trials in which they were able to successfully induce the target state. A trial was considered successful if its neurofeedback score (the likelihood of the inducted pattern being the target state) was higher than 0.5. Induction accuracy of all participants was then orthogonalized across all studies.

Functional data was preprocessed following the default event-related pipeline in SPM12 (slice scan time correction, realignment, coregistration, spatial normalization to the MNI template, and a 6-mm spatial smoothing). General linear model of the HRF function was defined for the induction and feedback periods of each trial, using the inter-trial interval as an implicit baseline. A whole sample activation map of the induction period was obtained through voxel-wise one-sample t tests (voxel-wise P < .001, FWE cluster-corrected at P < .05). Following this, the averaged time-series during induction were extracted in each participant for all subcortical structures as defined by the Tian atlas. Hippocampal structures and the amygdalae were discarded, as they were not functionally relevant — they presented no significant group activation — thus extracting 21 left-hemisphere and 21 right-hemisphere subcortical averages. Finally, activation averages of all participants were orthogonalized across all studies.

Structural data was preprocessed following the default voxel-based morphometry pipeline in the CAT12 toolbox of SPM12 (segmentation, registration, and spatial normalization to the MNI template). Then, native gray matter volumes were extracted in each participant for all subcortical structures as defined by the Tian atlas. Similarly to the fMRI analysis, hippocampal structures and the amygdalae were discarded. Finally, gray matter volumes were orthogonalized across all studies, and adjusted for age and total intracranial volume.

Correlational analyses were then performed between DecNef induction accuracy and the structural plus functional measures. Relation between accuracy and subcortical activations was tested via multiple Pearson’s correlations (FDR-corrected for 42 tests). Relation between accuracy and subcortical volumes was tested via multiple Spearman’s correlations (FDR-corrected for 42 tests), as gray matter volumes were found to follow non-normal distributions.

Results

The whole-sample activation map during the induction period of neurofeedback trials can be found in Fig 1a. Briefly, induction bilaterally engaged the fronto-parietal network, the insula, the basal ganglia, and the thalamus.

When exploring the subcortical regions of interest, DecNef induction accuracy was found significantly correlated with a higher activation of the left posterior globus pallidus (r = .319, P = .001, FDR-corrected, see Fig 1b). Noteworthy, uncorrected direct relationships were also found with the right posterior globus pallidus (r = .278, P = .005), as well as several subdivisions of the bilateral putamen and thalamus.

Interestingly, the structural characteristics of the globus pallidus were also found correlated to induction accuracy. Higher accuracy was associated with a larger right anterior globus pallidus (ρ = .345, P < .001, FDR-corrected, see Fig 1c).

Conclusion

Our preliminary analyses focusing on subcortical nuclei revealed that higher activation of the left globus pallidus (GP) during the neurofeedback induction period is directly associated with greater average success in self-regulation. The GP is believed to play a central role in behavior selection by facilitating and inhibiting different actions — primarily in relation to outcome contingencies — through efferent inhibition of other striatal structures and the thalamus. In this context, successful self-regulation may reflect a more effective pattern-selection process mediated by this structure as part of an implicit learning mechanism, which aligns with the proposed neural mechanisms underlying DecNef5.

Importantly, induction accuracy was related not only to GP activation but also to its gray matter volume. This finding suggests that at least part of the individual variability in DecNef self-regulation reflects a trait-like predisposition rather than a purely state-dependent effect, as structural neuroplastic changes are unlikely to occur over such a short timeframe.

In conclusion, these results support the hypothesis that reinforced and implicit learning mechanisms are critical for successful DecNef training, while also highlighting the potential role of the GP in determining which individuals are more likely to benefit from this technique.
