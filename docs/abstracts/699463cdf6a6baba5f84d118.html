Introduction

Glymphatic system dysfunction has been implicated in several neurological disorders, yet its role in focal epilepsy remains poorly understood. Enlarged perivascular spaces (PVS), altered diffusion-based indices, and cognitive impairment may reflect disturbances across glymphatic compartments. The objective of this study was to utilize established Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)-derived biomarkers associated with periarterial influx (PVS Volume Fraction), interstitial fluid transport (Free Water Fraction), and perivenous efflux (Diffusion Tensor Imaging along the Perivascular Space (DTI-ALPS)) to quantify group differences, assess age-related trajectories, and model disease progression using Z-Score SuStaIn (Subtype and Stage Inference).1 Importantly, we contextualized glymphatic system alterations with other neurodegenerative markers, including cortical atrophy and cognitive decline, highlighting the temporal ordering of biomarker changes as a window into disease pathophysiology.

Methods

A retrospective cohort of 449 individuals with focal epilepsy (mean age = 36.60 ± 15.61 years; 227 male, 222 female, 269 individuals with temporal lobe epilepsy, 180 individuals with focal cortical dysplasia) and 467 healthy controls (mean age = 36.45 ± 12.04 years; 332 male, 131 female) was utilized to characterize glymphatic system alterations using structural, diffusion-based, and neuropsychological markers. All subjects had at least one T1‑weighted 3 Tesla MRI scan available; a subset also had diffusion‑weighted images (80 focal epilepsy, 105 controls) and longitudinal  data (942 observations in the focal epilepsy group, 588 observations in the control group). PVS volume fraction was estimated based on T1-weighted scans using the SHiVAi deep‑learning pipeline.2 In the subset with diffusion-weighted imaging, the Single‑Shell Free Water Elimination Tensor Model3 was used to compute free‑water fraction and derive the DTI-ALPS index 4 as a measure of interstitial fluid transport. It should be noted that in some subjects, DTI-ALPS could be computed successfully while freewater fraction could not be estimated, as reflected in the slightly differing sample sizes. Cortical thickness was calculated based on T1-weighted scans using FastSurfer5. Verbal and figural memory testing were performed as part of the diagnostic workup.

A cross-sectional dataset was constructed by taking one observation per participant. For participants with only one scan, that scan was used; for participants with multiple scans, the scan with the most complete biomarker data was selected. Using this cross-sectional dataset differences in PVS volume fraction, free water fraction and the DTI-ALPS index between patients and controls were assessed using an ordinary least squares linear regression model. PVS volume fraction, free water fraction and DTI-ALPS were used as dependent variables respectively. Sex and Age were independent variables. T-tests were performed on the regression coefficients. Using the longitudinal dataset the relationship between PVS volume fraction and age in both the focal epilepsy and the control subgroup was assessed by means of a linear mixed effects model. PVS volume fraction was again used as dependent variable, with independent variables comprising age, group, the interaction of age and group, and sex.  Due to restricted power, we did not investigate the relationship between age and DTI-ALPS or freewater fraction. The cross-sectional dataset again was used as input for the SuStaIn model. The dataset included six biomarkers: PVS volume fraction, free water fraction, DTI-ALPS index, cortical thickness, verbal memory, and figural memory. PVS volume fraction, DTI-ALPS index and cortical thickness were normalized for age and sex using an ordinary least squares model fitted to the control cohort. The data were then z-scored relative to the control cohort. Verbal and figural memory were standardized according to a normative sample of 488 healthy individuals (mean = 100, standard deviation = 10) and corrected for age. This allowed SuStaIn to infer progression patterns of glymphatic dysfunction, neurodegeneration, and cognitive impairment.

Results

Compared with healthy controls, the focal epilepsy cohort exhibited significantly (p<0.0001) higher PVS volume fraction and free‑water fraction, as well as a significantly (p<0.0001) reduced ALPS index, indicative of impaired glymphatic influx, interstitial transport, and efflux. Longitudinal analyses revealed a significant interaction between diagnosis and age for PVS volume fraction, indicating an accelerated age‑related increase in PVS burden among epilepsy patients.

SuStaIn modeling identified figural memory decline and reductions in DTI-ALPS index as the earliest alterations, followed by verbal memory deficits and increases in free‑water fraction. Cortical thinning and an increase in PVS volume fraction emerged later, around stages 9–12, suggesting the presence of a later-appearing structural and perivascular component. This temporal ordering is similar to observations in other neurodegenerative conditions, such as Alzheimer's disease and mild cognitive impairment, in which ALPS and free water markers change before PVS enlargement. No robust phenotypic subtypes were detected in the focal epilepsy cohort.

Conclusion

Focal epilepsy is associated with widespread disruption across glymphatic compartments, as evidenced by increased PVS volume fraction, increased free‑water fraction, and reduced ALPS index. Longitudinal models reveal that these alterations progress more severely in epilepsy than in healthy aging. Data‑driven staging indicates that markers of impaired interstitial fluid transport are accompanied by cognitive decline and precede cortical atrophy, whereas structural perivascular damage follows cortical atrophy. Together, these findings support a model in which glymphatic dysfunction is an intrinsic and progressive marker of neurodegeneration that has an earlier onset than cortical markers in focal epilepsy.
