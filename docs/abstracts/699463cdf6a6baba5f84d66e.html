Introduction

Working memory (WM), the ability to maintain, manipulate and update information for a short period, supports goal-directed behavior and predicts school achievement and mental-health outcomes in childhood. Across early and middle childhood, WM undergoes rapid changes accompanied by large individual differences in accuracy and speed1. Neuroimaging studies have mapped canonical WM activations in the frontoparietal cortex (bilateral middle frontal gyrus, superior parietal lobule)2 and reciprocal down-regulation of the default-mode network (DMN)3. Beyond regional activations, the directed interactions that implement control (i.e., effective connectivity) remain undercharacterized, and, to the best of our knowledge, have not been examined in children.

Developmental research has demonstrated that large-scale brain systems become more segregated and efficiently integrated during the early school years, with stronger anticorrelation between task-positive networks and the DMN, suggesting a shift from widespread, effortful control toward more selective, automatized routing. Yet pediatric WM studies have primarily emphasized regional activation or undirected connectivity, thus limiting mechanistic inferences about how age and accuracy shape information flow. To address this gap, we used Dynamic Causal Modeling4 (DCM) for functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) to estimate effective connectivity among frontoparietal network (FPN) and DMN nodes during a child-friendly visuospatial n-back task, testing whether (i) higher accuracy relates to stronger FPN drive and tighter DMN regulation, (ii) age is associated with increasing posterior-to-frontal influences consistent with automatization, and (iii) load modulates hemispheric specialization.

Methods

A total of 120 typically developing primary-school children (age range: 5–9 years; 69 females; mean age = 7.29 years, SD = 0.83) were included in this cross-sectional study, which explored the baseline data of a randomized controlled trial evaluating the effects of art interventions on development5. 

Task & behavior. Children performed a child-adapted visuospatial n-back task in the scanner with two load conditions (0 versus 2-back, yellow monster versus green monster respectively, Figure 1). Stimuli were presented in alternating blocks with a fixed trial structure: 14 blocks (7 per load), 8 trials/block, 112 trials in total, 750ms of stimulus presentation, 3s for response time, 25% match trials. Accuracy and reaction time (RT) were recorded for every trial. Behavioral data were analyzed with mixed-effects models for accuracy and reaction time, with load as a within-subject factor and age as a continuous predictor. 

MRI acquisition & preprocessing. Data were collected on a 3 Tesla Siemens Prisma Fit scanner using whole-brain T2*-weighted echo-planar image sequence for the task (TR = 1000 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel resolution = 2.5 mm isotropic, 51 slices, GRAPPA = 3, 580 volumes) and a high-resolution T1-weighted structural scan (MPRAGE: voxel resolution = 0.8 mm isotropic). Preprocessing followed standard steps in SPM (realignment, coregistration to T1, normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm full-width at half-maximum). First-level conventional general linear models specified blockwise task effects with nuisance regressors for motion (and derivatives) and mean white matter and cerebrospinal fluid tissue signals. A high-pass filter was additionally applied to remove low-frequency drifts. Group-level maps identified high-load > low-load contrast and its reverse, and guided region-of-interest (ROI) selection together with prior literature. Results were thresholded using a family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < 0.05.

Effective connectivity. We applied DCM to estimate directed coupling among eight ROIs: bilateral middle frontal gyrus (MFG), superior parietal lobule (SPL), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and precuneus (PCU). Subject-specific eigenvariates were extracted from 6-mm radius spheres centered on the group-level peak coordinates. The intrinsic context-independent (A-matrix) network specified bidirectional intra-hemispheric connections and homologous interhemispheric links, except for midline homologous connections which were excluded due to their spatial proximity potentially confounding effective estimates. Task inputs (C-matrix) entered bilaterally through the SPLs. Finally, load-dependent modulatory effects (B-matrix), that capture changes in coupling between low vs high WM load, were applied to all within-hemisphere and interhemispheric connections (excluding self-connections). Individual DCMs were inverted for each child, and group effects were estimated with Parametric Empirical Bayes (PEB), including mean connectivity and covariates for age, accuracy, and their interaction. We used Bayesian model reduction to prune weak parameters and Bayesian model averaging to obtain posterior estimates. Effects were interpreted at conventional posterior-probability thresholds (robust ≥ 0.75; strong ≥ 0.95). To clarify significant age×accuracy interactions, we ran complementary subgroup PEBs (younger vs older children, median split). 

Results

Behavior. Accuracy exceeded chance level defined at 50% in both loads conditions for the full sample (low: t = 31.47, p < 0.0001; high: t = 34.96, p < 0.0001) and within the younger/older subgroups. Accuracy demonstrated a main effect of age (𝜒 ²(1) = 5.453 p, < 0.05), load (𝜒 ²(1) =5.647, p < 0.05), and an age×load interaction (𝜒 ²(1) = 7.854, p < 0.01). RTs showed a main effect of age (𝜒 ²(1) = 4.924, p < .05), no significant effect of load, and an age×load interaction (𝜒 ²(1) = 5.98, p < .05). Older children performed faster on easy trials (larger RT reductions at low load) and were more accurate on difficult trials (greater accuracy gains at high load).

Task activation. High > low load engaged canonical frontoparietal regions (bilateral SPL, bilateral MFG), whereas low > high load showed a stronger signal in DMN-related regions (ACC, PCU/posterior cingulate cortex, hippocampi). 

Effective connectivity. Baseline (A-matrix, Figure 2, top panel): The baseline network showed strong bidirectional excitatory coupling within hemispheres and homologous interhemispheric connections. Inhibitory coupling was present bidirectionally for left ACC-SPL and unidirectionally for right ACC to SPL (unidirectional) (Figure 2, A1). Strong self-inhibition was observed across nodes. With age, posterior-to-frontal/parietal drives increased, and several frontal outputs weakened as well as right MFG self-inhibition (Figure 2, A2). Higher accuracy amplified left-hemisphere FPN coupling, increased left PCU to ACC connection, and reduced several right-frontal outputs (Figure 2, A3). Self-inhibition also decreased in left MFG, left PCU, right ACC and right PCU, indicating greater excitability in better performers. An age×accuracy negative interaction emerged for several connections (Figure 2, A4). The subgroup PEBs analysis showed, for example, that left SPL to right SPL connection tracked accuracy in younger but not older children, consistent with interhemispheric parietal coupling stabilizing with maturation.

Task modulations (B-matrix, Figure 2, bottom panel): Across loads, task modulation increased bidirectional ACC-PCU coupling and strengthened unilateral right ACC to right PCU drive (Figure 2, B1 and B2). Under low load, additional left-hemisphere routes were recruited and better performance was associated with suppressed feedback to PCU from cingulo/frontal sources (Figure 2, B4). With age and under low load, right MFG to right SPL feedback connection decreased (Figure 2, B3). Notably, left PCU to left SPL connection showed a strong age×accuracy interaction: coupling strength increased with accuracy in older, but not younger, children (Figure 2, B5).

Conclusion

Across the early school years, visuospatial WM recruits a load-sensitive FPN with reciprocal down-regulation of DMN regions. Our effective-connectivity results point to emerging specialization: performance aligns with stronger left-frontoparietal coupling and tighter suppression of the PCU node, known as a DMN hub; age, by contrast, relates to reduced reliance on frontal top-down drive and stronger posterior inputs, consistent with increasing automatization processes. As demands rise, coupling shifts toward a more right-lateralized attention configuration. Interhemispheric parietal integration and PCU–ACC control also mature, as these performance-related patterns appear to be more clearly consolidated in older children. 

In sum, children appear to transition from broadly distributed control to more efficient and selective interactions between control and default-mode systems. This profile corresponds to the developmental emergence of executive functions: the highest performers show stronger coupling within task-positive circuits and more effective suppression of task-negative nodes, while age leads to a shift toward posterior-driven, less effortful control that can be flexibly lateralized with demand. Together, these markers summarize the systems-level support for WM gains in early and middle childhood and provide concrete targets for tracking maturation over time.

Finally, because these data come from the baseline wave of a randomized trial of arts-based interventions, the next step is to test whether participation in the different intervention groups modifies these effective-connectivity signatures over time. Longitudinal follow-ups in the same children will allow us to assess whether training shifts FPN–DMN interplay, strengthens interhemispheric parietal integration, or changes hemispheric weighting with demand, and whether such neural changes track gains in accuracy, speed, and broader executive-function outcomes.
