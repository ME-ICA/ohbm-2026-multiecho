Introduction

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) are a key marker of cerebrovascular dysfunction and can carry significant consequences for both brain health and cognition, with accumulation linked to cognitive decline and an increased risk of dementia later in life 1. Although WMH have traditionally been viewed as progressive and largely permanent, emerging evidence has highlighted their dynamic nature, with some individuals showing long-term stability or even regression 2. Despite this, the heterogeneous trajectories of WMH change remain poorly understood, and the factors that differentiate progression from stability or regression are not well characterised. Advancing our understanding of these divergent trajectories, and the vascular and neuroimaging phenotypes that give rise to them, may be essential for identifying individuals at greatest risk and informing targeted interventions to preserve white-matter integrity and cognitive health in aging populations. Here, we aim to delineate distinct WMH developmental profiles and identify the vascular and brain-structural factors that drive each trajectory.

Methods

We adopted a UK Biobank sample of 4,123 healthy participants (M: 46.45%, F: 53.55%) aged between 46-80 with imaging-derived WMH data across 2 time points (years between scans - median: 2.25, range: 1.00-6.98). Baseline grey matter volume, white matter volume and anterior MD and FA were also used in the analysis. A latent factor was created, representing both FA and MD using a confirmatory factor analysis approach (all tracts loaded significantly to each latent construct p < .001). Alongside neuroimaging, data from vascular risk factors at time point 1 were also used. This includes systolic and diastolic blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking status, waist to hip ratio, BMI and diabetes status. The duration of risk factor presence was also calculated where data was available for specific risk factors - namely, number of smoking pack years, duration since hypertension diagnosis and duration since diabetes diagnosis. 

We modelled heterogeneous WMH changes using latent class finite mixture modelling, solely based on rate of WMH change (delta) between the two time points.  To evaluate whether vascular, demographic, and neuroimaging factors could predict membership of the latent WMH-development classes, we trained a supervised machine-learning classifier using a random forest. Data were split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets using stratified sampling, with balanced class sampling applied in model training to account for uneven classes sizes. Performance was evaluated using test-set accuracy and variable importance. Group differences were assessed using ANOVA or non-parametric equivalents.

Results

The three-class latent mixture model provided the best fit to the data and revealed distinct patterns of WMH change. A regression group (33%) showed small but reliable decreases in WMH volume (ΔWMH = –0.04), a rapid progressor group (5%) exhibited markedly accelerated WMH accumulation (ΔWMH = 0.89), and a stable progressor group (62%) demonstrated modest, gradual increases (ΔWMH = 0.04). Posterior probabilities were high for all classes (0.903, 0.928, 0.833, respectively), indicating clear separation and strong model-based classification.

The random forest model predicted latent class membership with 63% accuracy (no information rate: 0.57, p = .0003), with balanced accuracy remaining stable across groups (regressors: 63%, rapid progressors: 61%, stable progressors: 72%). Variable importance estimates (Mean Decrease Gini, illustrated in Figure 1a) showed that baseline WMH volume was the strongest predictor of latent class membership. Age and anterior MD were the next most influential predictors, followed by baseline grey matter volume, waist-to-hip ratio, systolic blood pressure, and smoking (pack years). Traditional vascular risk factors such as diabetes status, cholesterol, and BMI provided modest contributions. 

Underlying vascular health and baseline brain structure of the latent classes varied significantly (shown in Figure 1b). The stable progressors group were younger, had the lowest baseline WMH, stronger microstructural properties, higher grey-matter volume, and lower vascular risk across all measures (p < .05). In contrast, the rapid progressors group had the highest burden of baseline WMH, poorest microstructural properties, the least amount of baseline grey matter, and the most adverse vascular profile, including elevated BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, blood and pack years (p < .05) The regressor group had the same age characteristics as the rapid progressor group, but tended to have moderate baseline imaging characteristics and average vascular risk profiles relative to the other two classes.

Conclusion

Here, we demonstrate the heterogeneity of WMH development, highlighting 3 distinct latent trajectory classes: regressors, stable progressors and rapid progressors. Using a supervised machine-learning approach, we show that these trajectories can be predicted with meaningful accuracy from baseline neuroimaging, vascular, and demographic profiles.  Baseline WMH burden and white-matter microstructure were the strongest determinants of progression phenotype, but vascular risk factors, including waist-to-hip ratio, systolic blood pressure, and smoking pack years also played substantial, though less critical, roles. Together, these findings reveal that WMH trajectories are biologically meaningful, predictable, and tightly coupled to both cerebrovascular and structural brain health, offering a potential pathway toward earlier identification and targeted intervention for individuals at elevated risk.
