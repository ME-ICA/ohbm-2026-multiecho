Introduction

We often perceive our character, preferences, and personality as stable features, shaped throughout childhood and adolescence. However, major shifts in life circumstances can alter one’s cognitions, emotions, and patterns of behavior. Such changes should be reflected in changes in brain function, and specifically in functional brain connectivity. The functional connectome has been used to predict personality traits, psychopathology, and cognitive abilities1. As such, the connectome may serve as a viable tool for representing one’s self.

Finn and colleagues2 explored the possibility of using the functional connectome as a neural “fingerprint”, and identify an individual based on their connectivity patterns alone. Two crucial properties were demonstrated: (a) the connectome is highly stable within individuals, and (b) the connectome is sufficiently unique to identify individuals with near-perfect accuracy. However, the effect of one’s environment on their connectome, and whether this effect can be disruptive enough so that it no longer serves as a valid fingerprint, has not been explored as of yet.

Here, we examine this possibility using a longitudinal dataset of infantry soldiers whose lives undergo dramatic transitions, from the highly structured, deindividualizing conditions of basic training, through varying deployment environments, to full autonomy after discharge. A matched group of university students provides a benchmark of neural stability under relatively consistent circumstances. We tested whether extreme life transitions alter the stability or uniqueness of the connectome, whether these changes disrupt neural fingerprinting, and whether specific functional networks contribute to these effects.

Methods

Participants and Design. Fifty male infantry soldiers and fifty male university students  completed four resting-state fMRI sessions spanning approximately three years. Soldiers were scanned shortly after enlistment (TP1), after 15 months of shared basic and advanced training and their first deployment (TP2), one year into dispersed mission assignments (TP3), and roughly four months after honorable discharge (TP4). Students were scanned at comparable intervals throughout their undergraduate studies. Soldiers’ timeline provided a structured transition from intense group cohesion to varied and autonomous environments, whereas students’ environments remained comparatively stable. All participants were healthy, MRI-qualified, and free of neurological or psychiatric diagnoses, and all provided written informed consent.

MRI Acquisition and Preprocessing. Resting-state fMRI data (TR=1s; TE=30ms; 2mm isotropic; 600 volumes) were acquired on a Siemens Prisma 3T and preprocessed using the Human Connectome Project minimal pipeline3, including motion correction, nonlinear registration to MNI space, and FIX-based noise denoising. Anatomical (T1-weighted) scans were also collected for registration and quality assurance.

Connectivity and Similarity Matrices. Connectivity matrices (1000×1000) were generated for each participant at each time-point using the Schaefer 1000-node parcellation. For fingerprinting, the lower triangle of each participant’s baseline matrix (TP1) was correlated with their matrices at TP2–TP4, as well as with every other participant’s matrices at those time-points, yielding similarity matrices for each group.

Fingerprinting Success. A fingerprint was counted as successful when a participant’s TP2–TP4 matrix was most highly correlated with their own baseline matrix compared with all other participants. Group success rates reflected the proportion of successful identifications at each time-point. Missing data were handled via multiple imputation (MICE4; five imputations per dataset).

Statistical Analysis. Group-by-time interactions were evaluated via Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE). Permutation tests (1,000 label shuffles preserving group sizes) determined whether group differences in fingerprinting success exceeded chance.

Uniqueness, Stability, and Discriminability. To identify the source of observed changes, we computed the following measures. Stability: defined as the self-correlation between TP1 and later time-points. Typicality: the mean correlation with all others. Uniqueness: the inverse of typicality; lower values reflect greater inter-individual similarity. Discriminability: stability / typicality.
 Each measure was analyzed using GEE with imputation. These metrics allowed us to dissociate whether reduced fingerprinting was driven by changes within individuals (stability) or by convergence between individuals (typicality/uniqueness).

Network Contribution Analysis. To determine which networks contributed to fingerprinting, we repeated the fingerprinting procedure 126 times – once for each possible combination of the seven canonical networks. A network’s contribution was defined as the difference in mean success rate for iterations including vs. excluding that network. This approach enabled estimation of each network’s positive or negative influence on identification accuracy.

Results

Fingerprinting Success. A significant group-by-time interaction emerged (GEE, p<0.05). Students showed high, stable fingerprinting success across all time-points (≥90%), consistent with prior observations of robust connectome individuality in stable environments. Soldiers, however, showed a marked reduction at TP2: only 80% were successfully identified, significantly lower than the students’ 97% (permutation p<0.01). This reduction coincided with the height of military cohesion, when soldiers shared nearly all aspects of daily life. Fingerprinting success improved to 90% at TP3 as soldiers dispersed across bases and duties, and by TP4 exceeded that of students (93% vs. 90%; p>0.05). Split-half reliability within each time-point was 100% for both groups, confirming that reduced success was not driven by data quality or noise.

Uniqueness vs. Stability. Uniqueness, but not stability or discriminability, exhibited a strong group-by-time interaction (GEE, p<0.001). Soldiers showed a pronounced drop in uniqueness at TP2, indicating that their connectomes became more similar to one another during the period of greatest environmental homogeneity. Stability remained high in both groups throughout, suggesting that the drop in fingerprinting was driven by increased between-subject similarity rather than increased variability within individuals. By TP3 and TP4, soldiers’ uniqueness steadily increased, paralleling the diversification of their environments. Students showed consistently high uniqueness across all sessions.

Network-Level Contributions. Across all time-points and groups, the dorsal attention network (DAN) and executive control/frontoparietal control network (CON) provided the strongest positive contributions to fingerprinting accuracy, aligning with prior work implicating frontoparietal regions in individual-specific functional architecture. In contrast, the default mode network (DMN) had a negative contribution for soldiers specifically at TP2. This pattern is consistent with evidence that the DMN encodes social and psychological similarity: individuals who are close, live together, or share similar internal states tend to show convergent DMN connectivity5. Soldiers’ DMN similarity at TP2 likely contributed to their reduced fingerprinting accuracy.

Overall, these results indicate that environmental pressures that increase conformity and restrict autonomy can temporarily compress neural individuality by reducing connectome uniqueness.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study demonstrates that extreme, homogenous environmental conditions can meaningfully disrupt the capacity of the functional connectome to serve as a neural fingerprint. The primary mechanism underlying this disruption was an increase in similarity among soldiers living under highly cohesive, structured, and uniform conditions, rather than a degradation of each individual’s internal stability. As the soldiers transitioned into more varied and individualized environments, the uniqueness of their connectomes increased accordingly.

These findings highlight that shared routines, reduced autonomy, and close physical and social proximity temporarily reduce connectome uniqueness, diminishing fingerprinting accuracy. However, this effect is reversible: when individuals return to more diverse and self-directed environments, their connectome individuality re-emerges. Finally, specific functional networks play distinct roles in modulating individuality, with frontoparietal networks consistently supporting identification, and the DMN contributing to similarity under highly cohesive social conditions.

More broadly, these results challenge the assumption that the functional connectome is a fixed neural signature and suggest instead that it reflects a dynamic balance between stable intrinsic architecture and environmental pressures. They raise important questions about which environments most strongly shape neural individuality, how long such effects persist, and why some individuals show greater susceptibility to these influences. This work also has implications for future biomarker development, resilience research, and the interpretation of individual-differences analyses in neuroimaging studies. Ultimately, the findings underscore the profound and ongoing influence of our surroundings on the structure of our neural “selves.”
