Introduction

Globally, an estimated one billion children experience maltreatment. Recent reports suggest that ~36% of adults experienced emotional maltreatment and one-quarter experienced physical maltreatment during childhood1. Childhood trauma (CT) is thought to interfere with brain development during sensitive periods, increasing vulnerability to stress2. It is considered a risk factor in the development of psychopathology, accounting for ~26.2% of mood disorder cases3 and contributing to the onset of bipolar disorder (BD)4. Studies investigating the association between early trauma exposure and brain functional connectivity (FC) are limited, yet whole-brain analyses have suggested alterations primarily in the default mode (DMN) and salience network connectivity5. Characterizing trauma-type–specific effects is therefore critical for elucidating the neural mechanisms through which early adversity confers risk for mood-related psychopathology and for advancing understanding of stress vulnerability and resilience. We hypothesize that forms of abuse will be associated more positively and neglect more negatively with functional connectivity in the visuomotor, sensorimotor and salience networks (abuse) or salience and default mode networks (neglect). Moreover, evidence suggests that these associations will be exacerbated in BD relative to controls.

Methods

Twenty-six BD participants meeting the DSM-IV criteria for type-I or type II BD who were euthymic at the time of assessment, defined as Hamilton Depression Rating score <8 and Young Mania Rating Scale score <7 and 37 healthy controls (HC) underwent 3T T1-weighted and resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI). Resting-state fMRI data underwent slice-timing correction, co-registration, segmentation, normalization and smoothing (full-width-half-maximum=6mm, SPM12). Thirty-seven independent components (ICs) were extracted (GIFT v4.0.6.18), of which 20 were denoted as signal and categorized as belonging to eight established resting-state networks (RSN). Childhood trauma was assessed using the 28-question Childhood Trauma Questionnaire deriving an overall total score as well as subscale scores for physical abuse, emotional abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect. Multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was used to examine a main effect of trauma-type and for any interaction between diagnosis-group and trauma-type on intra-network functional connectivity (spatial map and power spectra) while controlling for age, sex and motion parameters. 

There were no significant differences between the BD group (n=24, mean age ±SD, 38.5±11.6) and HC (n=37, 44.3±12.1) on trauma-type scores (physical abuse U=494, p=0.38 and neglect U=383, p=0.34, emotional abuse U=381, p=0.35 and neglect U=429.5, p=0.83). Two participants were excluded due to excess motion during scanning and sexual abuse was not examined due to inadequate frequency.

Results

As hypothesised, abuse significantly predicted intra-network connectivity within the visuospatial (emotional), sensorimotor network (physical) and salience (physical and emotional) networks and while neglect was associated with salience network (emotional), the default mode connectivity was not. Moreover, in BD, the salience network connectivity was consistently differentially associated with abuse (physical IC33 T=66.98, p<0.05 and emotional IC24 T=79.58, p=0.01) and neglect (emotional IC7 T=58.75, p<0.05) as were the sensorimotor (physical abuse, average β=1.68, p<0.05, depicted in Figure 1) but not the visuospatial network, relative to controls.

Additional unanticipated main effects observed include associations between abuse and connectivity of the default mode (physical), cerebellar (emotional) and visual (physical and emotional) networks and between neglect and FC of the executive control (emotional), cerebellar (physical) visuospatial and sensorimotor (physical and emotional) networks. Additional diagnosis-by-trauma-type interactions included a differential effect of abuse in BD relative to controls on FC of the default mode (physical), visual (physical and emotional) and cerebellar (emotional) networks, and of both physical and emotional neglect in FC of the visuospatial and sensorimotor networks.

Conclusion

Altered resting-state functional connectivity was associated with specific forms of childhood trauma, with physical abuse linked to sensorimotor network alterations that were more pronounced in individuals with BD. These alterations may impair environmental awareness and emotional regulation following exposure to high levels of threat, such as physical and emotional abuse. Similarly, experiences of abuse, were mostly associated with altered connectivity of the salience network. In this regard, a novel finding was that emotional abuse was associated with alterations in the salience network, particularly involving the anterior cingulate cortex in BD. This region plays a key role in stress processing, and trauma has been associated with alterations in its morphology previously. In contrast, neglect was associated with altered dorsal and ventral attention networks (involving the visuospatial component), potentially affecting the detection of salient stimuli, sustained attention, and attentional switching. Alterations in this network were distinct in BD relative to HC, and these differences were predominantly driven by the experience of emotional and physical neglect.  

In conclusion, trauma disrupts functional connectivity with differential patterns of functional connectivity in BD, involving networks implicated in stress processing and response, environmental awareness and emotion regulation. Further work may refine the risks posed by exposure to different forms of trauma in the neural basis conferring risk for the development of mood disorders.
