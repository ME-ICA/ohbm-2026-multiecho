Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorder, yet single-marker electroencephalography (EEG) approaches often fail to capture this variability.1,2 Resting-state EEG patterns may help identify clinically meaningful subgroups, particularly when integrated with cognitive and behavioral profiles. This study aimed to define ADHD subtypes using EEG-based clustering and to examine their associations with attentional and emotional-behavioral symptoms.

Methods

We analyzed data from 19-channel eyes-closed resting quantitative EEG (qEEG) recordings of children aged 7 to 12 who were diagnosed with ADHD (n=90, age=9.21±1.62, M:F=69:21) using DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. First, relative power was extracted across the delta, theta, low alpha, high alpha, low beta, high beta, and gamma frequency band. Then, subject's EEG data underwent dimensionality reduction and was then clustered based on Ward’s method of cluster analysis.3 To evaluate clustering performance, we computed silhouette scores for cluster numbers ranging from three to five. Finally, we aimed to identify EEG features within meaningful clusters that showed significant relationships with symptom levels (Korean-Child Behavior Checklist (K-CBCL), Swanson, Nolan, and Pelham Rating Scale (SNAP)-IV), the Junior Temperament and Character Inventory (JTCI), and Comprehensive Attention Test (CAT). Spearman correlation analysis with Bonferroni correction was conducted to find robust association.

Results

Three distinct EEG clusters emerged (Figure 1). Group 1 included 19 children with ADHD (age = 9.89 ± 2.01, M:F = 16:3), group 2 included 21 children with ADHD (age = 8.88 ± 1.54, M:F = 16:5), and group 3 included 50 children with ADHD (age = 9.09 ± 1.45, M:F = 37:13). No significant differences were observed among the groups in age and gender.

(1) Group 1 showed reduced theta with increased high alpha/low beta. Increases in delta were associated with reduced attention quotient (AQ) scores for visual attention response time (RT) in CAT (r=-0.692, p=0.001) and also correlated with higher T-scores for CBCL others (r=0.730, p=0.001), while excessive high beta power was linked to reduced AQ for divided attention RT in CAT (r=-0.845, p=0.002).

(2) Group 2 presented increased delta with markedly elevated high beta/gamma and suppressed alpha. High beta correlated with improved AQ scores for interference attention RT standard deviation (r=0.795, p<0.001) yet greater withdrawn/depressed in CBCL (r=0.712, p<0.001).

(3) Group 3 showed elevated theta and reduced high alpha/low beta. Increased delta in frontal regions was associated with worse CBCL attention problems (r=0.468, p=0.001), whereas parietal gamma was related to higher AQ scores for interference attention RT (r=0.435, p=0.002).

Conclusion

The three clusters represent distinct profiles of arousal regulation and attentional control. Group 1 showed compensated high-arousal, vulnerable to performance decline if delta or excessive beta increased. Group 2 exhibited low arousal with elevated high beta. While high beta appeared to compensate for attentional deficits, its association with social withdrawal suggests a potentially anxious nature in this group. Group 3 was the classic low-arousal inattentive profile, where frontal delta correlated with symptom severity, while parietal gamma provided efficient processing compensation. These findings may illustrate utility of qEEG in identifying distinct symptom dimensions of ADHD.
