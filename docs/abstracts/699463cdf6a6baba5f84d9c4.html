Introduction

The brain development of the infant is a period of rapid and intense transformation. From the first weeks of life, the brain is exposed to an ever-changing external environment. To adapt to this, it enters a phase of spectacular growth, often referred to as the "synaptic big bang", during which the number of synapses reaches about 150% of that observed in adults [1]. This concurrent increase in cortical thickness and myelinization represent an essential step in the formation of the early brain. Despite their importance, the earliest stages of postnatal brain development remain relatively understudied, even though they are critical for subsequent brain organization. In this study, we specifically investigate how time spent outside the womb, indexed by postnatal age, influences early cortical development. 

Methods

Participants. We retrospectively analyzed data from the MYRNA (Maternal depression and Youth: Research on Neurodevelopment and behAviour) cohort. Inclusion criteria were normal birth, and an absence of antidepressant use during pregnancy.  

Neuroimaging. All infants underwent T2-weighted MRI scans. These images were preprocessed using the nf-pediatric pipeline (https://github.com/scilus/nf-pediatric), an end-to-end connectomics pipeline for pediatric (0-18y) structural MRI brain scans. The preprocessing steps included denoising, N4 bias-field correction, resampling, brain extraction, and spatial cropping. Cortical surfaces were then reconstructed for each infant using MCRIB-S and surface registered to the fsaverage6 template. Cortical thickness in each infant was then computed as the Euclidean distance between the pial and white matter surfaces.  

Statistics. We examined the association between postnatal age and cortical thickness using a surface-based linear model, as implemented in BrainStat [2]. The statistical model included postnatal age as a fixed effect and sex as a covariate. Results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.  

Results

A total of 52 term-born infants were included in the analysis (28 girls). Mean ± SD postmenstrual age was 39.4 ± 1.0 weeks at birth and 45.7 ± 2.6 weeks at scan. Mean postnatal age at scan was 6.3 ± 2.7 weeks. Regional variation in cortical thickness was observed in infants (Fig. 1A), with thinner cortex in insular, occipital, and superior parietal cortices, and thicker cortex in fronto-temporal cortices. Within the MYRNA cohort, we observed significant and widespread cross-sectional cortical thickness increases with increasing postnatal age, primarily in bilateral insular and occipital cortices (all t-values > 3.3, pFDR < 0.05; Fig. 1B). No regions showed significant decreases in cortical thickness with increasing postnatal age.   

Conclusion

These findings indicate that the first weeks of life represent a critical window for cortical development, during which time spent outside the womb contributes to rapid structural maturation of the infant brain. This work emphasizes the importance of considering postnatal age when studying early neurodevelopment and supports further longitudinal investigation into how early extra-uterine experience shapes brain organization.
