Introduction

Resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) enables the identification of brain regions exhibiting synchronous fluctuations in spontaneous BOLD signals. Extensive research has shown that the resulting patterns of synchronous activity, often referred to as resting-state networks (RSNs), are evolutionarily conserved and undergo structured dynamic reconfiguration as a function of brain state1. However, the mechanisms orchestrating the dynamic organization of RSNs remain elusive.

Neuromodulatory nuclei like those located in the basal forebrain (BF) - a major source of cortical acetylcholine and a key regulator of arousal - are strong candidates for controlling RSNs dynamics. Yet, whether and how BF output causally organizes large-scale RSNs remains unclear.

Methods

Here, we addressed this question by employing targeted manipulations of the BF in mice. To probe the effect of global BF output on RSNs dynamics, we induced pan-neuronal lesions in the BF via local expression of diphtheria toxin. To do so, we leveraged genetically modified ROSA-DTA mouse line2 (N = 16 experimental, N = 17 sham) in which expression of the diphtheria toxin is Cre-dependent. By injecting Cre-expressing viral vectors directly in the BF, we triggered selective neuronal ablation in the targeted area (Fig. 1A). 

In a subsequent set of studies, we used chemogenetics to dissect the role of distinct BF neuronal populations in controlling RSNs activity. We expressed the excitatory  DREADD hM3Dq, by means of viral injections, in the BF of either Pv-cre (N = 11 experimental, N = 9 sham) or Chat-cre (N = 9 experimental, N = 11 sham) mice, thus assessing the selective contribution of BF parvalbumin-positive (Pv+) and cholinergic (Chat+) neurons in controlling RSNs organization. 

The outcomes of all these manipulations were assessed using rsfMRI while the animals were awake and head-fixed. Scanning sessions took place after a habituation period (1 week) during which the mice became accustomed to body restraint and to the sound of the MRI, both of which can be potentially stressful.

For chemogenetic studies, a baseline period was acquired as a reference to better characterize the effects of cell type–specific activation. Additionally, both groups received the same concentration of the DREADDs ligand clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) to control for potential off-target effects of the drug3. Lastly, we probed the behavioral effect of chemogenetic manipulations by assessing the mice’s spontaneous behavior in the open field test (OFT), before and after CNO administration.

Results

Chronic BF ablation produced widespread reconfiguration of fMRI connectivity, with robustly strengthened integration within the default mode network (DMN) and a broad reduction in cortico-cortical coupling (Fig. 1B,C). In contrast, chemogenetic activation of the two principal BF neuronal populations produced a convergent increase in large-scale network integration. Both manipulations robustly enhanced cortico-cortical coupling, yielding heightened functional connectivity between the DMN and somatomotor networks (Fig. 1D,G).

Importantly, these network effects were strongly state-dependent. Under reduced arousal induced by sedation, BF perturbations yielded markedly attenuated and qualitatively distinct connectivity signatures. This underscores arousal as a key gate through which the BF regulates RSNs dynamics.

 At the behavioral level, activation of BF Pv+ neurons stabilized locomotor output over time, counteracting the natural drift toward immobility. This effect was absent following stimulation of BF Chat+ neurons, suggesting that these populations recruit distinct circuit mechanisms to shape behavior (Fig. 1F,I).

Conclusion

Together, our results provide initial evidence that the BF exerts a causal, arousal-dependent influence on RSNs, and suggest that divergent pathways within the BF, such as those modulated by Pv+ and Chat+ neurons, can converge to modulate cortico-cortical integration.
