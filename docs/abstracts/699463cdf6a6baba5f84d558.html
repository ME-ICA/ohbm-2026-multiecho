Introduction

A fundamental characterization of Alzheimer's Disease (AD) is the progressive disruption of large-scale neural integration. Electroencephalogram (EEG), with its millisecond temporal resolution, offers a critical window into these fast-changing network dynamics. While microstate analysis has provided valuable insights into brain functions, emerging theoretical frameworks suggest that neural dynamics can be understood as a continuous evolution along structured trajectories within a high-dimensional state space. Characterizing the intrinsic geometry of this landscape remains a key challenge in understanding pathological brain alterations.

To address this, we propose a novel dynamic state-space graph framework. This approach models the temporal progression of brain activity as a complex network. Specifically, we utilize the first eigenvector of short-window (0.1s) covariance matrices to estimate the instantaneous dominant topography within each window. We then construct a k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) graph, where nodes represent state feature within time windows and edges encode spatial similarity. This construction effectively maps the low-dimensional topology structure underlying the complex brain dynamics.By applying the Leiden community detection algorithm to this temporal graph, we allow metastable brain states and their transition structures to emerge naturally from the data topology. We apply this pipeline to a cohort of AD patients and healthy controls, investigating how AD pathology reshapes the organization of this dynamic state space and alters the stability of neural transitions.

Methods

Dataset

Publicly available resting-state EEG recordings were obtained from the OpenNeuro repository (dataset accession number: ds004504). The analyzed cohort consisted of 36 patients diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease (AD) and 29 healthy controls (HC). Data were acquired using a 19-channel system positioned according to the international 10--20 system. All recordings were performed under an eyes-closed resting-state condition. To facilitate computational efficiency and focus on relevant timescales, signals were downsampled to 200 Hz. We applied a band-pass filter to alpha band (7-14 Hz). This specific band was selected because it encompasses the alpha rhythm, which is the dominant generator of resting-state activity and is notably disrupted in AD pathology. The final data structure for each subject is a 19 x T matrix.

Dynamic State-Space Graph Construction

To model the temporal evolution of brain activity, we employed a sliding window approach. The filtered signals were segmented into overlapping windows of 0.1s with step size of 0.05s. For each window, we computed the spatial covariance matrix to quantify the instantaneous co-variation of electrical potentials. To extract the instantaneous dominant topography, we performed an eigendecomposition on each covariance matrix and selected the first eigenvector (corresponding to the largest eigenvalue). This vector provides a robust estimation of the principal spatial mode of the underlying source orientation, serving as a noise-resistant feature for state identification. We then constructed a dynamic state-space graph to map the intrinsic geometry of neural transitions. In this graph, each node represents the first eigenvector of a single time window. Edges were defined based on the spatial similarity between topographies, calculated using the absolute Pearson correlation coefficient. A k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) constraint was applied to ensure a sparse, topologically faithful representation of the state space manifold. The parameter k was determined adaptively as a function of the data size, ensuring a balance between global graph connectivity and the preservation of local neighborhood structures.

Community Detection and Metric Quantification

We applied the Leiden algorithm to the k-NN graph to identify metastable brain states. This modularity-optimization method allows brain states (communities) to emerge naturally from the data structure. To quantify dynamic alterations, we computed specific graph-theoretical and temporal metrics: (1) Transition Rate (TR), measuring the lability of state switching; (2) Alternation Index (AI), quantifying the probability of immediate state reversion (A - B - A) to detect pathological oscillatory loops; (3) Run Coefficient of Variation (Run CV), assessing the complexity of dwell time distributions; and (4) Modularity (Q), reflecting the distinctness of the functional brain states.

Statistical Validation and Surrogate Testing

Group differences were assessed using Welch's t-tests, with effect sizes quantified by Cohen's d. To verify that observed differences arose from genuine network pattern rather than simple spectral power differences or signal artifacts, we performed surrogate testing. We generated surrogate datasets by applying random circular time-shifts independently to each EEG channel. This procedure preserves the auto-correlation and power spectrum of individual channels but destroys the inter-channel coupling. Metrics were re-calculated on surrogate data; effects that persisted in real data but vanished or diminished in surrogate data were considered to be driven by bona fide network dynamics.

Results

• Modular Organization of the State Space

The application of the Leiden algorithm to the dynamic k-NN graphs successfully revealed a robust, non-random modular architecture across all participants. The mean Modularity (Q) was substantial for both the HC (0.73 ± 0.01) and AD (0.72 ± 0.01) groups, consistently exceeding the threshold of 0.4 typically indicative of meaningful community structure. This high baseline modularity confirms the validity of our eigenvector-based framework, demonstrating that even in the presence of pathology, the momentary dominant topographies naturally organize into distinct, metastable clusters rather than evolving along a random continuum.

• Group Differences in Dynamic Metrics

While the fundamental modular architecture was preserved, quantitative comparisons of temporal dynamics and graph topology revealed significant differences between AD patients and healthy controls.

First, regarding the global topology of the state space, the AD group exhibited a significantly lower Modularity (Q) compared to controls (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.89). This reduction, while numerically small relative to the high baseline, points to a robust functional dedifferentiation and a blurring of the boundaries between distinct brain states in the patient group. Second, regarding dynamic stability, the AD group showed a marked acceleration in state switching, evidenced by a significantly increased Transition Rate (TR) (p = 5.3 × 10−6, d = 1.25). This indicates a highly labile system where microstates fail to sustain prolonged activation, leading to a fragmented temporal profile. Third, regarding the structure of these transitions, the Alternation Index (AI) was significantly elevated in the AD group (p = 0.0028, d = 0.74). Unlike simple instability, this metric specifically captures a higher probability of immediate state reversion (A → B → A). This suggests that AD dynamics are not merely random but are characterized by a repetitive trajectory of states. Finally, the temporal complexity was markedly diminished in patients. The Run Coefficient of Variation (Run CV) was significantly reduced in the AD group (p < 0.001, d = −0.99). While healthy controls exhibited a broad, heavy-tailed distribution of dwell times (indicative of rich temporal structure), AD patients showed a narrowing of this variability, reflecting a collapse towards more regular or random temporal dynamics.

• Validation on Surrogate Data

To rigorously delineate true global effects from potential confounds related to signal properties (e.g., spectral power or single-channel artifacts), we repeated the entire analysis pipeline on shifted surrogate data. This procedure destroys the precise temporal synchronization between regions while preserving auto-correlation.

In the surrogate datasets, the significant group differences observed in the real data for Modularity (Q) and Alternation Index (AI) were effectively abolished (Q: p = 0.19, d = −0.32; AI: p = 0.63, d = −0.10). The complete disappearance of the Alternation Index effect is particularly notable, confirming that the pathological “rigidity” observed in AD is strictly a property of inter-regional network synchronization rather than local signal artifacts. Furthermore, while the Transition Rate (TR) remained statistically significant in the surrogate analysis (p = 9.3 × 10−4), the statistical strength was drastically attenuated compared to the real data, with the p-value weakening by two orders of magnitude (10−4 vs. 10−6). Similarly, the Run CV showed only marginal significance in surrogates (p = 0.028). This dissociation strongly supports the conclusion that the observed alterations in AD state dynamics are primarily driven by the disruption of large-scale network coordination.

Conclusion

The consistently high Modularity (Q > 0.7) observed across both groups serves as a fundamental validation of our dynamic graph approach. It demonstrates that the resting-state EEG state space is intrinsically organized into distinct, metastable communities rather than a continuous, unstructured flow. Crucially, the Leiden algorithm successfully recovered this structure without determining the number of states a priori. This data-driven capacity to map the natural topology of neural dynamics offers a significant advantage over rigid clustering methods (e.g., k-means), suggesting that this graph-theoretical framework warrants broader application across diverse neurophysiological datasets to uncover subtle dynamical phenotypes.

The observed group differences provide important insights into AD pathology. The reduction in Modularity in AD reflects a functional dedifferentiation of the state space, in which the boundaries between distinct brain configurations become blurred. This structural degradation likely underpins the elevated Transition Rate (TR), indicating a dynamically labile system that is unable to sustain stable neural representations. However, this instability is not merely chaotic. The elevated Alternation Index (AI) reveals a paradoxical pathological rigidity: the AD brain exhibits repetitive trajectories, becoming trapped in recurrent loops between restricted local attractors (A → B → A). Furthermore, the reduced Run CV in patients indicates a loss of temporal complexity. Whereas healthy brains display heavy-tailed dwell-time distributions—indicative of scale-free dynamics and operation near criticality—AD brains collapse toward more regular or random temporal structures, thereby limiting their capacity for flexible information integration.

Our surrogate analysis offers critical insights into the origins of these alterations. The persistence of significant differences in Transition Rate within the surrogate data suggests that basic signal properties (e.g., spectral power distribution or signal variability) inherently contribute to the apparent instability. However, the amplification of statistical significance in the real data—by approximately two orders of magnitude—indicates that genuine inter-regional phase synchronization acts as a critical multiplier of these effects. Crucially, the group differences observed for both Modularity (Q) and the Alternation Index (AI) were effectively abolished in the surrogate datasets. This dissociation is particularly compelling, as it confirms that neither the global functional dedifferentiation (reduced Q) nor the local pathological rigidity (increased AI) can be explained by local signal quality or artifacts alone. Instead, both impairments represent emergent properties of the network topology, strictly dependent on the integrity of fine-grained inter-regional phase synchronization.

Nevertheless, several limitations merit consideration. First, our analysis treated AD as a binary condition. Given that Alzheimer’s disease represents a progressive continuum, future studies using larger, longitudinally stratified datasets are required to determine whether these dynamic metrics evolve systematically across preclinical, prodromal (MCI), and dementia stages. Second, although our parameter choices (e.g., 0.1 s window length and adaptive k) were theoretically motivated, the topological properties of the reconstructed state-space graph may vary with different temporal resolutions or connectivity thresholds. Systematic sensitivity analyses with respect to window length, k-NN sparsity, and similarity measures therefore remain important avenues for further refining and validating this promising methodological framework.
