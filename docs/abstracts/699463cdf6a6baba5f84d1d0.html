Introduction

Shaped by both affiliative and competitive motives, female social relationships are highly complex. While same-sex friendships offer crucial social support, they can also present relational threats, particularly in the context of mating competition1. There is growing evidence that the social dynamics among women are influenced by hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle. Specifically, women tend to derogate potential competitors the most when it is evolutionarily advantageous, i.e. during the fertile phase of the cycle. Olfactory cues play a crucial role in intrasexual competition. In nonhuman mammals, body odor reliably signals competitive ability. According to emerging evidence, human body odors may serve similar functions. In addition, body odor not only communicates reproductive status but also shapes social perception at the neural level2. However, very little is known about how women perceive the body odors of other women.

Methods

Here, we combined functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) with behavioral measures to test how body odors from women at different reproductive states (menstruation (ME), ovulation (OV), and early pregnancy (PRG)), affect social perception in female recipients across their own menstrual cycle. 

Body odor was collected from naturally cycling women during ME and OV, and from first-trimester pregnant women. Hormonal phase was determined via self-report and Clearblue ovulation tests, and blood samples validated estradiol, progesterone, and (for pregnancy) hCG levels. Odors were presented to 33 normosmic, naturally cycling women during two fMRI sessions (menstruation and ovulation, counterbalanced). Using an air-diluting olfactometer, participants received ME, OV, PRG, and a odorless control odor while viewing neutral female faces unrelated to the odor donors. They rated attractiveness, desire for closeness, and pregnancy likelihood on each trial.

Results

Attractiveness ratings and pregnancy ratings showed no effect of odor or recipients’ cycle phase. Closeness ratings revealed an odor effect: participants kept greater distance under pregnancy odor than under ovulation or menstruation odor, and showed a trend toward closer approach when they themselves were ovulating. 

fMRI analyses showed widespread cycle-dependent odor responses: ovulating participants exhibited stronger activation to body odors than during menstruation, including limbic, insular, temporal, parietal, and subcortical regions. OV odor elicited enhanced right-lateralized responses, especially in temporal, insular, and basal ganglia structures. PRG odors were associated with more circumscribed responses, particularly in prefrontal and cingulate areas. These effects were modulated by the recipient’s cycle phase, with broader networks engaged during ovulation compared to menstruation. 

Conclusion

This study shows that women’s responses to other women’s body odors depend on both the donor’s and the perceiver’s reproductive state. Behaviorally, PRG odors increased interpersonal distance, whereas OV and ME odors did not alter attractiveness or pregnancy judgments, indicating that chemosensory cues can shape social behavior without shifting explicit evaluations. Neurally, odors produced strong cycle-dependent modulation: ovulating participants showed widespread activation to all body odors, and in particular to OV cues, across limbic, insular, temporal, and subcortical regions involved in social evaluation. PRG odors elicited more circumscribed prefrontal and cingulate responses. Together, these findings demonstrate that subtle body-odor signals shape female social cognition in a cycle-dependent manner, suggesting an adaptive mechanism through which olfactory cues guide competition, vigilance, and affiliation among conspecifics.
