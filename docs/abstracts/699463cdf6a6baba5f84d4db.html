Introduction

Forged by evolution, the human brain learns continuously through neural plasticity, dynamically adapting its structure and function. Although brain-inspired principles have shaped artificial intelligence (AI), a fundamental question remains: how does the brain organize learning at the system level? Current AI architectures, built largely on models of local synaptic change, lack the higher-order organization required for robust generalization, efficient learning, and flexible behavior.1 Deciphering the brain's system-level learning mechanisms is therefore crucial—not only to reveal how cognition emerges from neural circuits, but also to enable more capable and adaptive machine intelligence.

Learning remodels the brain across multiple scales: from synapses and specific regions to functional networks, neural dynamics, and the brain-body interface.2 This mounting evidence across scales affirms that learning is a process of systematic state change. The brain sustains allostasis, a process that maintains the body's dynamic equilibrium amid a changing environment.3 A key challenge is to understand how learning modifies this allostatic brain state—a question that demands a system-level analytical framework.

A deeper understanding of these learning mechanisms hinges on the development of ecologically valid experimental paradigms. The efficacy of cognitive training remains debated, being highly dependent on the paradigm itself.4 An optimal paradigm shall bridge the gap between constrained laboratory tasks and the complexity of real-world, long-term skill acquisition. In this study, we propose abacus-based mental calculation (AMC) learning as a model. Culturally embedded and amenable to long-term curricular integration, AMC recruits multiple cognitive domains through a visuospatial strategy. It thus offers a powerful, ecologically valid window into the acquisition of complex cognitive skills, providing a unique platform to investigate system-level learning in humans.

Methods

Participants

This longitudinal study enrolled 188 school‑aged children (6–8 years old). The training group consisted of 100 children who received two hours of weekly AMC instruction over five years, starting in first grade and concluding at the beginning of sixth grade (Fig. 1a). The control group (n = 88) had no prior abacus training and instead received supplementary instruction in standard academic subjects such as mathematics and reading. The groups were matched for sex (P = 0.448, Fig. 1d) and age (P = 0.386, Fig. 1e). A subset of 135 children underwent between one and four fMRI scans, yielding a total of 287 scans (Fig. 1b, c). Behavioral assessments of mathematical competence and executive function (EF) were administered to the participants. Mathematical evaluation included an arithmetic test, a mathematics‑related visuospatial task, and the Compare‑to‑5 task (designed to assess numerical magnitude processing). EF was measured using two established paradigms: the Go/No‑go task (inhibitory control) and the Dots task (cognitive flexibility). This longitudinal design was implemented to examine the sustained effects of AMC training on brain organization and behavior.

Dynamic Network Analysis 

In order to quantify differences in brain state between the groups, a dynamic network approach grounded in bifurcation theory was applied.5 The present theory posits that, in the vicinity of a critical or non-stationary state, a dynamical system will manifest cooperative fluctuations, characterized by (1) a pronounced increase in variance within individual system variables and (2) a substantial enhancement in the correlations between these variables. Our analysis proceeded as follows. First, we computed the standard deviation (SD) of the fMRI time series for each brain region in each group, obtaining region‑specific sets of training and control groups. Regions showing significantly greater fluctuation in the training group or in the control group were identified via independent‑samples t‑tests (one-side, P < 0.05) and formed the node set VK, where K denotes the number of such regions. Next, for these K regions, we calculated the absolute Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) for every region pair within each group. Pairwise comparisons (independent‑samples t‑tests, P < 0.05) identified edges with significantly stronger correlations in the training group, yielding the edge set EQ, where Q is the number of such pairs. Using VK as nodes and EQ as edges, we defined a dynamic‑network score to capture whole‑brain cooperative fluctuations. For each participant in the training and control groups, the score was computed as the average SD multiplied by the average PCC within the corresponding sets. Group‑level scores were obtained by averaging across participants. A higher score indicates that the brain network is closer to a non‑stationary state. Note that the score is a relative measure and is not directly comparable across different datasets.

Results

Brain Allostasis Remodeling During Abacus Learning

To test the hypothesis that learning drives systematic brain allostasis remodeling (Fig. 2a), we analyzed a longitudinal dataset of children undergoing multi-year abacus training using dynamic network analysis. We observed a significant group-by-stage interaction in whole-brain dynamic network scores (P < 0.001, Fig. 2b). While no differences existed at baseline, the training group displayed higher scores than controls early in training, followed by lower scores in middle and late stages—a pattern indicating nonlinear remodeling of functional networks with learning.

Stage-resolved network analysis (DU-15NET atlas) revealed a spatiotemporal progression of reorganization (Fig. 2c). Changes initially emerged in visual-related regions (visual network, VIS; dorsal attention network, dATN), then extended to higher-order associative networks (action network, AN; default network, DN; Fig. 2d). Dynamic connections shifted from being confined within VIS early on, to reshaping between AN and DN later in training. Nodal dynamics mirrored this shift: early training elevated volatility in VIS (measured by KFD, ALFF, and SD), whereas later stages reduced volatility in AN and DN, alongside attenuated AN–DN connectivity (all P < 0.05).

Progressive Sculpting of Functional Networks

This network reorganization aligned with a strategy-decomposition model of skill acquisition, progressing through three phases: (1) initial sensorimotor integration, (2) kinesthetically reinforced mental imagery, and (3) fully internalized mental calculation. Module allegiance analysis further delineated progressively topological changes underlying this progression. Specifically, training enhanced module recruitment in VIS while reducing it in AN, and strengthened integration between VIS and AN. Conversely, AN became less integrated with the salience/parietal memory network (SAL/PMN), whereas SAL/PMN–DN integration increased. Analyses of intra- and inter-network connections also confirmed these shifts statistically (Fig. 2e): intra-network connections strengthened early in VIS (P < 0.05) and were reduced later in AN (P < 0.01); inter-network connections between AN and VIS increased in the late stages of training (P < 0.05), while AN–SAL/PMN connectivity decreased in the early (P < 0.001) and middle (P < 0.01) stages. SAL/PMN–DN connections decreased in the late stage (P < 0.05).

Training Transfer on Cognitions

This staged network reorganization was accompanied by a corresponding cascade of cognitive transfer effects. Behavioral improvements unfolded sequentially: arithmetic gains emerged early (P < 0.001, Fig. 2f), visuospatial mathematical ability improved in the middle stage (P < 0.001, Fig. 2g), and inhibitory control was enhanced later (P = 0.018, Fig. 2h). A random forest classifier trained on post-baseline behavioral data reproduced the same order of feature importance, corroborating the sequential transfer pattern. Together, these results indicate that cognitive transfer through visuospatial mental abacus training radiates from proximal to distal cognitive domains, mirroring the progressive network sculpting observed in the brain.

Conclusion

This multi-year longitudinal study establishes that complex skill acquisition drives a staged, nonlinear reorganization of functional brain networks, providing a system-level account of how learning unfolds in the human brain. Through AMC—an ecologically valid model of skill learning—we demonstrate that brain remodeling progresses from early visual-attention engagement to later integration of higher-order associative networks. This phase-dependent neural progression mirrors a cognitive strategy-decomposition process and scaffolds a corresponding cascade of behavioral transfer, where gains emerge sequentially from arithmetic to visuospatial abilities and finally to executive control. Our findings reveal that system-level learning operates through a structured, phase-dependent reconfiguration of brain networks, enabling the expansion of cognitive capabilities from domain-specific to domain-general functions. This work provides a principled framework linking neural reorganization to behavioral outcomes, offering insights for understanding cognitive development and informing the design of more adaptive artificial learning systems.
