Introduction

Auditory verbal hallucinations (AVHs) are a critical feature in schizophrenia, affecting 70-80% of patients and being associated with distress and functional disability 1. Despite the availability of several antipsychotics, 30% of patients develop treatment-resistant AVH, highlighting the need for novel interventions 1. In healthy controls, auditory verbal imagery (AVI), i.e. the ability to mentally imagine verbal representations without external auditory stimulation, can be used as an experimental model for AVH to test innovative approaches. 

Real-time fMRI neurofeedback (rt-fMRI-NFB) has recently emerged as a promising technique that enables voluntary control over neuronal activity and can lead to behavioural changes and symptoms improvement 2. In addition, recent advances in machine learning now allow the use of multivoxel pattern analysis (MVPA) to discriminate between brain states with high sensitivity, enabling neurofeedback based on participants’ proximity to targeted neural patterns rather than activity in specific regions. MVPA-based neurofeedback has been successfully applied in healthy samples to train shifts toward or away from defined brain states 3. Moreover, previous studies have provided preliminary evidence of rt-fMRI-NFB benefits for AVH 4,5. However, no previous study has used MVPA-based fMRI neurofeedback. Thus, this study aims to test the feasibility of MVPA-based fMRI neurofeedback to train healthy participants to modulate AVI-related brain activity, used as a proxy for AVH. 

Methods

Twenty healthy participants completed four visits, including two training sessions followed by two rt-fMRI-NFB sessions (see Figure 1A). During Visit 1, participants learned two cognitive strategies to modulate brain states away from the AVI network: Subvocalization (SVA) and Meditation (MED). SVA involved repeating short sentences, with minimal articulatory movement and no overt speech, while MED consisted of a body scanning technique aimed at sequentially focusing attention on different body parts. Participants performed the two tasks in a block-design inside a custom VR environment that mimicked the MRI setting (supine posture, mirror-based display and background scanner noise) to familiarise themselves with the strategies. Visit 2 involved MRI training, where participants performed the same block-design tasks. This visit had two objectives: 1) to train participants to conduct the tasks in a real MRI setting and 2) to acquire subject-specific activation patterns to optimise the classifiers used during NFB sessions. Two linear Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifiers (AVI vs SVA; AVI vs MED) were trained on an independent sample using a functional mask comprising voxels activated during AVI, SVA, MED and the DMN.

Visits 3 and 4 consisted of two rt-fMRI-NFB sessions (Figure 1B) conducted on consecutive days. Across both visits, participants completed 12 runs (6 SVA, 6 MED) in counterbalanced order, attempting to volitionally shift their brain activity away from the AVI-state. Each run lasted 5’42’’ and included eight trials (Figure 1C) composed of 6 s rest, 30 s regulation, 1.5 s delay, and 4.5 s feedback. During regulation, participants executed the instructed strategy.

Real-time feedback was delivered intermittently at the end of each regulation block and was based on the classifier’s hyperplane distance averaged over the last 15 s of the regulation period (accounting for hemodynamic delay). Feedback was displayed as a circle whose size reflected participants’ proximity to the target (non-AVI) brain state, alongside the previous trial’s performance to allow direct comparison. After each NFB session, participants completed questionnaires assessing confidence and perceived strategy success.

Across all visits, strategy execution was monitored using EMG placed on the upper lip to detect articulatory activity during SVA, and a respiration belt to track breathing patterns during MED.

Learning was assessed by comparing rt-fMRI-NFB scores between early (runs 1–2) and late runs (runs 5–6) and post-training confidence scores were used to identify strategy-specific learners. To evaluate neural modulation, representational similarity analysis (RSA) was used to quantify correlational distances between AVI and strategy-specific brain activation patterns, comparing first and last runs, within the classifier mask.

Results

Considering the whole sample, rt-fMRI-NFB scores showed no overall change (i.e. no learning effect was observed) for the SVA strategy (t (19) = 0.06, p = .90), whereas the MED strategy showed a trend towards learning between early and late training runs (t (19) = –1.58, p = .13). Learning was positively correlated with post-training confidence, allowing the identification of strategy-specific learners (n = 8 for SVA and n = 13 for MED). Within these subgroups, MED learners showed a significant increase in rt-fMRI-NFB scores across training runs (t (12) = –3.73, p < .01) (Figure 2A). However, whilst an increase in rt-fMRI-NFB scores was seen across SVA training runs, the learning effect was non-significant (t (7) = –1.35, p = .21) (Figure 2B).

RSA revealed significant changes in neural distance between AVI and strategy-specific brain states from first to last run. For SVA, the neural distance from the AVI pattern increased (t (19) = 2.84, p = .01, mean Δ = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.02–0.16), indicating a greater separation between AVI and SVA brain states over time (Figure 2D). In contrast, MED elicited a significant decrease in distance (t (19) = –2.13, p = .047, mean Δ = –0.10, 95% CI: –0.20 to –0.00), reflecting increased similarity between AVI and MED patterns across runs (Figure 2C).

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate the feasibility of MVPA-based rt-fMRI-NFB to modulate AVI-related brain activity in healthy participants. Although learning across all participants was non-significant, individual differences in confidence were found to influence neurofeedback success, with high-confidence participants showing greater ability to utilize strategies across training runs. We observed a significant increase in rt-fMRI-NFB scores across training runs for MED, while SVA showed a positive trend that failed to reach significance possibly due to a lack of power. 

This distinction between learners and non-learners has important implications for the optimisation of training procedures and identifying predictors of learning is key to enhancing neurofeedback training efficacy. Furthermore, these findings support the notion that multiple strategies may be employed to achieve modulation of AVH-related neural activity, allowing for flexible and individualized intervention approaches.

RSA analyses revealed strategy-dependent neural modulation. SVA increased representational distance from the AVI network, reflecting the ability to move neural activity away from AVI-like patterns, whereas MED decreased distance, suggesting increased convergence with the AVI neural pattern. These results suggest that different cognitive strategies may differentially engage AVI-related networks. 

Overall, this study provides proof-of-concept for using MVPA-based neurofeedback to train the ability to modulate brain states associated with AVI. These insights will inform the design of a forthcoming multi-centre clinical trial in patients with schizophrenia and treatment-resistant AVH, guiding protocol optimisation and supporting the potential use of different cognitive strategies to modulate AVH-related networks.
