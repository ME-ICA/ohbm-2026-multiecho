Introduction

Trial averaging is a cornerstone of psychophysiological data analysis. Averaging multiple observations per participant reduces within-participant variability and is assumed to improve statistical power. However, this improvement depends on the ratio of between- to within-participant variance, a quantity that is rarely reported.

As a result, decisions about the number of trials per participant are often guided by heuristics or conventions rather than principled power calculations. Standard formula-based power analyses, such as those implemented in the pwr package in R1, generally assume a single observation per participant and therefore ignore the statistical consequences of averaging. Although simulation-based approaches can address this limitation, they are computationally expensive, require careful tailoring to the data-generating process, and are often difficult for readers and reviewers to evaluate. Overall, inadequate power estimation contributes to the ongoing replication crisis in neurosciences and should be remedied2.

Here, we present an analytical, closed-form correction for standardized effect sizes under trial averaging. Building on classical formulations of standardized mean differences (SMD)3, we derive an exact expression that quantifies how finite trial counts attenuate observable effect sizes as a function of the between–within variability ratio. This correction enables direct estimation of the number of trials required to achieve specified power for a given true effect size.

We validate the analytical predictions using simulations and demonstrate their practical implications in two independent datasets. While the framework generalizes to many contexts involving averaged observations, we show that such benchmarking is particularly critical for low signal-to-noise measures such as heartbeat-evoked potentials (HEP), and illustrate this by contrasting them with other classes of evoked responses.

Methods

Analytical derivation

We derive a closed-form expression that quantifies the attenuation of standardized effect sizes induced by trial averaging. The derivation assumes that participant-level estimates are obtained by averaging T repeated observations and that within-participant variability is homogeneous across participants. Under the standard variance decomposition, the variance of an averaged signal equals the sum of between-participant variance and within-participant variance scaled by T, yielding a multiplicative correction factor for SMD that depends solely on the between–within variability ratio (r) and T: sqrt(1 + 1 / (r · T)).

This formulation enables direct computation of observable effect sizes under finite trial counts and the minimal true effect size required to achieve a specified power threshold. The correction applies to statistical tests whose test statistic is algebraically equivalent to an SMD computed from participant-level averages, including one-sample and paired t-tests, two-sample t-tests with equal group sizes, and one-factor ANOVAs with two levels. Paired designs and within-participant contrasts are treated equivalently: when effects are expressed as change scores, the same correction applies provided that r is estimated for the resulting contrast. The assumption of equal group sizes is introduced solely to obtain a compact closed-form expression; analogous corrections can be derived for unequal group sizes or heteroscedastic variances at the cost of additional parameters. Extensions to standardized correlations and simple regression coefficients follow the same logic, with attenuation depending on the variability ratios of both predictor and outcome.

Simulation validation

We validated the closed-form power prediction using 2000 Monte Carlo simulations for a one-sample t-test (two-sided α = 0.05). Simulations spanned a range of between–within variability ratios (r = 0.01–1.0), participant sample sizes (N = 20–100), and numbers of trials per participant (T = 10–1,000), using a representative true effect size (dtrue = 0.5). For each parameter combination, analytic power was computed by converting dtrue to the observable effect size after averaging via the closed-form attenuation factor and evaluating exact noncentral-t power. Empirical power was estimated as the proportion of significant tests across simulated datasets. Agreement between analytic and Monte Carlo power was quantified using calibration and error metrics (Pearson correlation, MAE/RMSE). As paired and two-sample tests are algebraically equivalent to SMD-based tests on participant-level estimates, validation of the one-sample case suffices for all SMD-based designs.

Empirical datasets

To illustrate practical implications, we analyzed two independent datasets with concurrent electroencephalography (EEG) and electrocardiography (ECG) recordings (Dataset 1: n = 34; Dataset 2: n = 65). Dataset 1 was recorded by us and is not yet publicly available, whereas Dataset 2 is an open-access resource4. EEG data were preprocessed using standard pipelines (band-pass filtering, artifact rejection, etc.). ECG R-peaks were detected to define HEP. Stimulus-locked event-related potentials (ERP) were computed from Dataset 1.

HEP were quantified at frontocentral (FCz in Dataset 1, Fz in Dataset 2) and parietal (Pz) electrodes using canonical time windows (250–350 ms and 450–550 ms post-R-peak). Other ERP were quantified at the same electrodes: P300 amplitudes were extracted using 50-ms windows centered at 360 ms (Pz) and 378 ms (FCz), and feedback-related negativity (FRN) at FCz centered at 266 ms post-stimulus (feedback in a learning task). For each signal type and time window, variability was decomposed into within- and between-participant components to estimate r. Variance components were estimated using method-of-moments estimators (with within-participant variance summarized by the harmonic mean) and linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts.

Results

Validation results

Analytical predictions closely matched Monte Carlo power estimates across the full parameter grid, confirming the accuracy of the closed-form prediction. Predicted and simulated power showed near-identity agreement (Pearson rho = 0.999); with small absolute error (MAE = 0.005; RMSE = 0.007), 95% of absolute errors were below 0.017 and the maximum deviation was 0.025. Calibration was essentially ideal (intercept = −0.0007; slope = 1.00), indicating no systematic bias across the power range. Agreement was stable across all tested values of r, including very small ratios (r ≤ 0.02), and invariant to the choice of true effect size used for validation (0.2 < dtrue < 0.7). Additional simulations explicitly modeling trial-level variance yielded equivalent results.

Empirical variance ratios

Empirical estimates revealed pronounced differences in between–within variability ratios (r) across signal types. For stimulus-locked ERP, r values were on the order of 10-1. At frontocentral electrodes, r was 0.19 for FRN (between = 7.4μV2, within = 38.3μV2) and 0.11 for P300 (between = 5.8μV2, within = 50.7μV2). At parietal electrodes, P300 activity yielded r 0.14 (between = 5.0μV2, within = 35.1μV2). These values indicate moderate between-participant variability relative to single-trial noise, consistent with typical ERP signal-to-noise characteristics.

In contrast, HEP exhibited substantially smaller variance ratios. Across datasets, electrodes and time windows, r values for HEP ranged from approximately 0.007 to 0.013 (between = 0.27–0.39μV2, within = 29.6–37.5μV2), corresponding to an order-of-magnitude reduction relative to ERP and likely attributable to the markedly lower signal amplitudes characteristic of HEP5. This pattern was consistent across early and late time windows and recording sites. Method-of-moments and mixed-effects estimators yielded nearly identical values, and estimates were stable under resampling of trials, indicating robust variance decomposition.

Practical implications

Applying the analytical correction shows that finite trial counts systematically attenuate observable effect sizes relative to true standardized effects (dtrue), with the attenuation depending strongly on r. For higher-SNR measures (r  = 0.1), commonly used trial counts already recover a large fraction of the true effect, whereas for lower-SNR measures (r = 0.01), substantial attenuation persists even with hundreds of trials, leading to pronounced power loss unless compensated by additional trials or participants.

For medium effects (dtrue = 0.5) in paired or one-sample designs, a higher-SNR measure reaches 80% power at N = 60 with ~20 trials, whereas a lower-SNR signal requires ~200 trials at the same sample size — an order-of-magnitude increase in trials to achieve equivalent power. At N = 100, the corresponding values are ~10 versus ~50 trials, indicating a persistent five-fold penalty. For sample size, achieving comparable power for r = 0.01 at T = 20 trials requires increasing N from 60 to 200.

For large effects (dtrue = 0.8), attenuation is reduced but still requires 2–5 times more trials in the lower-SNR regime compared to higher-SNR. For small effects (dtrue = 0.2), power is primarily limited by participant numbers: trial averaging can help approach the asymptotic limit (e.g., ~80% power at N = 200), but for low-SNR signals doing so may also require large trial counts.

Two-sample designs amplify these effects: for dtrue = 0.5 at n = 100 per group, higher-SNR signals reach 80% power with ~20 trials, whereas lower-SNR signals require ~200 trials, and at smaller group sizes no feasible increase in trials can compensate for insufficient participants. Overall, reducing r from 0.1 to 0.01 incurs predictable, often order-of-magnitude increases in required trials, underscoring that trial averaging primarily serves to recover attenuated effects.

Conclusion

Accurate power estimation remains challenging in psychophysiological research. Simulation-based approaches can address these complexities but are often computationally expensive, difficult to tailor to specific designs, and hard to benchmark across studies. As a result, power considerations are frequently omitted or handled heuristically, contributing to uncertainty in study planning and to broader concerns about reproducibility. The present work introduces a simple, analytical, closed-form correction for standardized effect sizes under trial averaging. While the derivation rests on explicit assumptions, it is fast to compute, easy to implement alongside standard tools (e.g., the pwr package in R), and well-suited for routine benchmarking at both the design and post hoc stages.

The correction applies directly to a wide family of tests whose statistics are algebraically equivalent to standardized mean differences, including one-sample, paired, and two-sample comparisons, as well as simple ANOVA contrasts, and can be extended to correlations and simple regression. Even when analyses involve more complex models, the framework remains informative: by quantifying how finite trial counts attenuate participant-level effects, it provides a conservative reference point against which more elaborate power analyses can be interpreted. In this sense, the approach complements rather than replaces simulation-based methods. From a Bayesian perspective, the formulation characterizes attenuation at the likelihood scale and is therefore relevant for Bayesian power considerations.

A key practical implication is that between- and within-participant variance components are straightforward to estimate from pilot data, and even coarse estimates of their ratio are sufficient to determine whether a study operates in a high- or low-SNR regime. Routine reporting of this ratio—at least post hoc—would allow future studies to benchmark their designs against empirical distributions of signal quality and facilitate meaningful comparisons across tasks, measures, and laboratories. Over time, such reporting could enable community-level benchmarks for different psychophysiological signals.

Our results also clarify the limits of trial averaging. Increasing trial counts primarily serves to recover effects attenuated by within-participant noise; it cannot compensate indefinitely for small true effects or insufficient participant numbers. These limitations are especially pronounced for low-SNR measures and for between-group designs, which are substantially more costly than paired or within-participant comparisons. For the same effect size, variance ratio, and trial count, two-sample tests often require several-fold more participants or trials, and may be infeasible for low-SNR signals at moderate group sizes. For low-SNR measures such as HEP, additional trials yield diminishing returns, highlighting the need for careful interpretation of null results and realistic expectations about detectable effects. 

By explicitly quantifying the statistical consequences of trial averaging, the proposed framework enables principled decisions about feasible effect sizes, trial counts, and sample sizes. We will provide openly available code, notebooks, and benchmarks (at https://github.com/mariaazanova/CorrectForTrials) to facilitate adoption and encourage routine reporting of between–within variability in psychophysiological research.
