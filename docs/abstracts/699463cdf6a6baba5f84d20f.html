Introduction

Cortical convolutions are one of the most recognizable and intriguing anatomical features of the mammalian brain. Among the many folded structures it may comprise, the cortices of the cerebrum and the cerebellum are the largest ones. However, while a folded cerebral cortex is characterized by broad, irregular convolutions alternating between elevated ridges (gyri) and deep grooves (sulci), the cerebellar cortex exhibits a fine, accordion-like structure given by the dense packing of numerous, thin, predominantly parallel folia. 

Based on previous phylogenetic studies[1,2], we hypothesize that, despite their morphological differences, the mechanism underlying cortical folding in the cerebrum and the cerebellum is the same; namely, a buckling instability triggered by the tangential growth of an elastic, outer layer on top of a viscoelastic, inner layer. In the cerebrum, the outer layer corresponds to the neocortex, where the proliferation of dendritic trees from pyramidal neurons is the major force driving tangential expansion on top of the inner white matter[3]. In the cerebellum, our conjecture is that the molecular layer plays the role of the outer expanding layer, with proliferation of dendritic trees from Purkinje cells providing an analogous tangential expansion on top of the inner granular layer and white matter[2].   

To test this hypothesis, we have designed a unified computational model of cortical folding in the cerebrum and cerebellum based on this two-layer idea. This model not only reproduces several aspects of the resulting folding patterns, but also allows us to explore the effect that the initial geometry can have in regulating the timing of folding development and the resulting pattern. We use our model to explore these questions further using a series of idealised geometries with different symmetries.

Methods

As in previous models, our computational model of cortical folding considers two different layers of isotropic hyper-viscoelastic materials lying one on top of the other (see Figure 1). In cerebral simulations, the outer layer represents the neocortex, while the inner layer stands for white matter. In cerebellar simulations, the outer layer corresponds to the molecular layer and the inner one to a concatenation of the granular layer and white matter. Each layer will therefore be characterized by different mechanical properties such as the young modulus (E), the Poisson ratio (𝜈) and the plasticity constant (P). We adopt the classical assumption that volumetric growth can be modeled as a multiplicative decomposition of the deforming gradient (F) of the folding material[4], that is, F=Fe·G, where Fe is a tensor describing elastic deformation and G encodes growth deformation. As we mentioned before, our model only considers tangential growth in the outer layer, so we have G=1 for the inner layer. This means that any expansion of this internal layer is due to elastic deformation triggered by the expansion of the outer layer together with relaxation by plasticity. Elastic deformation is then translated into material stresses by means of a strain energy W(Fe;E,𝜈), that defines the elastic response of each material. In our model, we use a fixed corotational strain energy for both layers[5].

Unlike previous works, we also include growth triggered as a response to stress in our computational model. This is done by incorporating a plastic relaxation process modulated by a plasticity constant P in such a way that the larger P is, the faster a material forgets about its rest state. In our simulations, both layers are subject to this plastic relaxation, with the inner layer having a higher plasticity constant than the outer layer. Additionally, we use the Material Point Method (MPM) algorithm[5] for our simulations. The main advantage of using MPM is that it allows for a simpler implementation of self-collisions, something which is especially important for the reproduction of the thin, densely-packed folia in the cerebellum, allowing for large deformations and higher-order folding.

For our simulations, we use a series of four different idealised initial geometries with different axial symmetries and curvature profiles (see Figure 1). These idealised geometries are: the circle x2+y2=R2, with infinitely many symmetry axis and constant curvature; the ‘squircle’ xp+yp=Rp, for some p>2, with four axis of symmetry (its two diagonals and its two medians) and a higher curvature in the smooth vertices than in the middle of the sides; the ellipse x2+y2/(1-ε2)=R2, with two axis of symmetry (its major and minor axis) and a higher curvature in points further from its center; and the ovoid x2+y2/(a+bx)2=R2, for b<a≤0.5, with only one symmetry axis and a higher curvature at the pointy edge.  

Results

The ability of simulating more extensive amounts of tangential cortical expansion enabled the formation of several levels of nested folding: folds appearing initially will fold again as soon as the critical threshold for buckling is reached. This results in nested folding, reminiscent of those observed in the cerebellum (see Figure 2). Our simulations also reproduced a direct correlation between cortical thickness and wavelength of the folding pattern along the perimeter of the folded structure. In this sense, simulations with a thicker outer layer result in sparser, wider convolutions resembling gyri and sulci in the cerebral cortex, whereas thinner outer layers give rise to thinner, more densely-packed folds similar to the folia in the cerebellar cortex.

Regarding simulations with idealised initial geometries, we observed that initial symmetries were highly preserved in the final folding pattern. In general, regions of the original geometry with a smaller curvature tended to invaginate, forming the first grooves observed in the folding structure. Conversely, regions of the original geometry with a greater curvature tended to evaginate and delayed their folding into secondary or tertiary folds. In geometries like the ellipse or the ovoid, this evagination translated into an elongation of the whole structure in the direction of greater curvature, which in both cases coincided with one of the axes of symmetry.

Conclusion

Our model is able to produce folded structures that mimic both, the cerebral and cerebellar folding, hinting at a conserved mechanism that underlies the folding of both structures, and very likely also other folded brain structures, such as the hippocampus. From isotropic growth, it is able to produce variations observed in real brains, including variations in cortical thickness, as well as one or several levels of folding, suggesting that these features emerge through mechanical morphogenetic processes without the need of a specific genetic encoding of the folding pattern. Gyri in our model are consistently thicker than sulci, as can be observed in histological data. Similarly, the model reproduces conserved neuroanatomical features, such as the wavelength of the folds. In both cases, models of the cerebral and cerebellar folding, the wavelength corresponds to roughly 4.4 times the cortical thickness, as observed in real brains[1,2]. 

Moreover, our simulations with non-circular geometries suggest that different geometrical aspects of the unfolded structure, such as curvature or axial symmetry, play a major role in guiding the folding process. In particular, regions of the initial geometry that present a smaller curvature seem to be more prone to folding and therefore, primary folds tend to concentrate on those regions. On the contrary, regions of the initial geometry with a higher curvature seem to delay the appearance of these primary folds and, consequently, they are preserved with almost no alterations until later stages of the folding process. These observations hint in the direction that curvature in the initial geometry acts as a mechanical kind of positional information that can be traced all along the folding process until the resulting folding pattern.
