Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by progressive disruption of neural circuits caused by the accumulation of amyloid beta plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles1. This molecular pathophysiology alters the synaptic function way before the neuronal loss, making functional changes in neurons and circuits particularly relevant for understanding the disease mechanisms. The signature change in electrophysiology in AD patients include increased activity in the lower frequencies (2-8 Hz) and reduced activity in the mid to higher frequencies (8-30 Hz)2. However, it remains unclear whether these changes reflect a unitary mechanism that gives rise to a left shift on the neural power spectrum or multiple processes that may contribute to frequency-specific changes associated with distinct consequences of amyloid and tau pathologies. The answer to this question is a key piece of the puzzle between AD proteinopathy and functional changes in neurons and circuits. In this study, we examined frequency-specific neural synchrony measures across different spatial and temporal scales in patients with AD and specifically investigated whether increased delta-theta and decreased alpha and beta activity is independent of a global spectral frequency shift. Further, we investigated whether specific synchrony alterations show distinct temporal relationships with amyloid and tau accumulations, and global cognitive impairment.

Methods

In this study, we used high spatiotemporal resolution magnetoencephalography (MEG) to examine spectral changes in a well-characterized, biomarker-positve cohort of AD patients (n=77; age, 62.6 ± 8.9 years) and age-matched healthy controls (n=90; age, 65.5 ± 10.2 years). Using magnetoencephalography (MEG), we computed four different neural synchrony measures capturing a range of spatial and temporal scales.  Specifically: spectral power (SP) to measure local synchrony; imaginary coherence (IMCOH) to measure long-range synchrony at fast timescale; phase lag index (PLI) to measure phase-based long-range synchrony at fast timescale; and   amplitude envelope correlation (AEC) to measure long-range synchrony at slow time-scale. Each metric was examined within three frequency bands: delta–theta (2–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and beta (13–35 Hz). Using statistical models that covaried with spectral peak frequency within 13-35 Hz band (Figure 1), we examined whether frequency-specific changes are independent of a spectral frequency shift. To investigate the temporal relationship between neural synchrony changes and disease progression, we used an event-based modelling (EBM) approach3,4. Specifically, we used regional tracer uptake values from amyloid-and tau-PET, and alpha band AEC, in our cross-sectional sample and examined the temporal relationships in an EBM model using disease staging from global cognition measured from Mini Mental State Exam (MMSE) and hippocampal atrophy as pseudo-time. We also examined how frequency specific neural synchrony changes relate to MMSE and functional decline measured from clinical dementia rating (CDR).

Results

Patients with AD exhibit consistently showed increased delta–theta and decreased alpha and beta synchrony, across all 4 metrics. These frequency-specific spectral changes were also region dependent, where delta-theta increases were most pronounced in the frontal cortices and anterior cingulate regions, while alpha and beta decreases were more pronounced in the posterior temporo-parietal cortices. We also found that the spectral peak frequency (3-15Hz) is reduced in AD compared to controls in almost all brain regions. Importantly, however, frequency-specific neural synchrony changes were significantly different between AD patients and controls, even after accounting for peak frequency reduction (Figure 1), suggesting that a unitary peak shift is an unlikely contributor to the spectral changes of AD. The EBM further demonstrated distinct relationships of neural synchrony changes relative to amyloid and tau accumulations in patients with AD. Specifically, alpha band AEC followed early amyloid accumulation but preceded tau accumulations (Figure 2A). We also found that increased delta-theta and reduced alpha synchrony correlated with MMSE deficits and higher values of functional decline (CDR-sum of boxes) in AD patients (Figure 2B).

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that frequency-specific neural synchrony alterations in AD likely reflect multiple pathological processes rather than a unitary left shift of the spectral peak. Region-specific increases in delta-theta and decreases in alpha and beta oscillatory activity remained significant even after considering the spectral peak frequency, suggesting that these effects are not merely epiphenomena of generalized spectral slowing. The finding that alpha band AEC abnormalities emerge following early amyloid accumulation but precede the tau pathology supports the hypothesis that amyloid and tau have differential relationships to neuronal function. Intriguingly, reduced AEC preceding tau accumulation raises the possibility of abnormal neural function as a trigger for neocortical tau accumulation in AD. Moreover, synchrony changes across frequency bands were associated with global cognitive deficits, underscoring their functional and clinical relevance. Together our results support a model in which distinct pathological mechanism related to amyloid, tau, contribute to frequency-dependent disruptions of neural synchrony in AD. Characterizing the temporal and mechanistic specificity of these alterations may provide critical insights into how molecular pathology translates into network dysfunction and cognitive decline. Such knowledge may guide the development of targeted neuromodulation and network-based therapeutic strategies tailored to disease stage and underlying pathology.
