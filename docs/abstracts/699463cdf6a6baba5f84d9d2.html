Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disease characterised by progressive motor impairment and cognitive symptoms. The stability of PD treatment is limited by an incomplete understanding of the aberrant brain dynamics in the disease state. Much PD research has focused on analysing static features of cortical or subcortical brain activity, e.g. by computing the average power spectrum of cortical and subcortical oscillations, most notably the elevated beta-band power observed in the local field potential (LFP) recordings from the subthalamic nucleus (STN) of PD patients.1 In contrast, dynamical features, such as the signal complexity or long-range temporal correlation, remain underexplored. Such features can provide unique insights into healthy and pathological brain function at system-level.2

Methods

Here we analysed such dynamical features of neural oscillations recorded in the STN following overnight withdrawal of dopaminergic medication and after resuming treatment. We recorded the resting state LFP from the STN of 37 PD patients using implanted DBS devices (Percept, Medtronic). We used a variant of empirical mode decomposition (EMD), called improved complete ensemble empirical mode decomposition with adaptive noise (ICEEMDAN)3 to extract the first 8 oscillatory modes present in the band-pass filtered (1 - 98 Hz) LFP signals from each patient (Figure 1). EMD is an adaptive, data-driven method for spectral decomposition that has a number of advantages over simpler filter or convolution-based approaches: EMD does not require the user to pre-specify the frequency bands of interest, does not suffer from the same time-frequency precision trade-off as convolution-based approaches, and is able to decompose non-linear and non-stationary data (such as the frequency- and amplitude-modulated signals in LFP and EEG recordings) without harmonic artifacts.

As such, EMD allows a complete breakdown of the STN oscillatory modes in the time domain, and their dynamics in the medicated and unmedicated PD states. EMD was applied separately to the most-affected side (MAS) and least-affected side (LAS) for each patient. For each mode, we computed the temporally-precise instantaneous frequency (IF) and amplitude envelopes using the Hilbert transform. We summarised the spectral content of each mode by computing the mean and standard deviation (SD) of the IF timecourses. We assessed the frequency irregularity of each mode by computing its Hjorth complexity (HC). We quantified bursting activity in each mode by thresholding its amplitude envelope by the 75th percentile of the corresponding envelope from the on-medication condition, and summarised these bursts by computing the median burst magnitude (max amplitude in each burst), inter-burst-interval and duration. We also assessed the complexity of the temporal pattern of bursts by computing the normalised Lempel-Ziv complexity (LZC), which measures the number of unique sequences comprising a discrete time-series. Finally, we analysed the scale-free dynamics of the continuous (unthresholded) amplitude envelopes of each mode using detrending fluctuation analysis (DFA), which assesses long-range temporal correlations. All metrics were compared between the on- and off-medication conditions with paired two-tailed permutation t tests with 100000 permutations. P values were converted to surprisal (S) values (-log2(p)) for all tests. S values scale more intuitively than P values, as they are equal to the number of fair coin flips in a row with the same result (P = 0.05 corresponds to S ~= 4.3).

Results

The extracted oscillatory modes corresponded intuitively to the classical EEG/LFP frequency bands, but importantly varied in their peak frequency across patients and conditions, demonstrating the advantage of the data-driven EMD approach and allowing us to better characterise the pathological dynamics of neural oscillations in the STN of PD patients. Figure 2 shows a summary of the mean difference (md), Cohen's d and S values for the metrics which were consistently different in the medicated and unmedicated states. The obtained modes showed the well-known enhancement of beta-band activity in the unmedicated PD condition, as measured by the median burst magnitudes of modes in the high beta (MAS: md = 0.71 μV, d = 0.44 , S = 7.3 bits; LAS: md = 0.64 μV, d = 0.46, S = 5.8 bits). Comparing the unmedicated to the medicated condition, we also found a reduction of the mean frequency of modes in the high beta (MAS: md = -0.89 Hz, d = -0.81, S = 9.5 bits; LAS: md = -0.81 Hz, d = -0.57, S = 5.1 bits) and low beta (MAS: md = -0.25 Hz, d = -0.61, S = 5.4 bits) bands, aligning well with similar findings in pharmacological animal models of PD4. We found altered spectral dynamics in the unmedicated compared to medicated condition in various modes: decreased frequency variability of modes in the low beta (MAS: md = -0.36 Hz, d = -1.07, S = 12.7 bits; LAS: md = -0.35 Hz, d = -0.67, S = 6.8 bits) and alpha (LAS: md = -0.11 Hz, d = -0.62, S = 8.3 bits) bands, and reduced Hjorth complexity (frequency irregularity) of modes in the low beta (MAS: md = -0.02 a.u., d = -0.61, S = 6.2 bits) and alpha (MAS: md = -0.02 a.u., d = -0.46, S = 5.5 bits; LAS: md = -0.01 a.u., d = -0.50, S = 5.1 bits) bands. We also found evidence of altered bursting activity in the unmedicated state: increased burst durations of modes in the high beta (MAS: md = 0.02 s, d = 0.75, S = 8.2 bits; LAS: md = 0.02 s, d = 0.55, S = 6.0 bits) and low beta (MAS: md = 0.03 s, d = 0.54, S = 7.6 bits) bands, decreased burst durations of modes in the theta band (LAS: md = -0.04 s, d = -0.58, S = 5.1 bits), increased inter-burst-intervals of modes in the theta (LAS: md = 0.12 s, d = 0.49, S = 5.0 bits) and alpha (LAS: md = 0.15 s, d = 0.55, S = 6.4 bits) bands, increased LZC complexity of the temporal burst pattern of modes in the high beta band (MAS: md = 0.05 a.u., d = 0.68, S = 6.0 bits; LAS: md = 0.05 a.u., d = 0.56, S = 4.8 bits), and decreased LZC of modes in the alpha (LAS: md = -0.04 a.u., d = -0.58, S = 5.4 bits) and theta (LAS: md = -0.02 a.u., d = -0.82, S = 8.2 bits) bands. Finally, we found a reduced DFA exponent of modes in the alpha band (MAS: md = -0.07 a.u., d = -0.66, S = 7.6 bits).

Conclusion

This approach, focused on the dynamics of STN oscillations, allowed us to better capture the pathological changes of STN activity in PD than is possible with a purely static description of the frequency spectrum. The unmedicated state was characterised by changes in oscillatory dynamics in the beta and alpha bands. For example, modes in the beta and alpha band consistently reduced in mean frequency, their frequency irregularity (indexed by Hjorth complexity) reduced and their frequency variability reduced. The latter result was largely driven by reduced phase slips and together with the other results likely indicates excessively stable synchronisation. Analysis of bursting activity in the unmedicated state was also richly informative: the temporal pattern of bursting activity was consistently altered in beta, alpha and theta bands (as indexed by changes in LZC, median burst durations and median inter-burst intervals), and median burst magnitude was larger in the high beta band. The consistently lower DFA exponent in the unmedicated state in modes in the alpha band indicates a breakdown of long-range temporal correlations, and could reflect a move away from criticality. The aberrant neural dynamics in STN we identified are informative for the development of better therapies that provide a reduction of symptoms that is more stable over time. Dynamical signal features such as the burst timecourse complexity (indexed by Lempel-Ziv complexity), scale-free long-range temporal correlations (indexed by the DFA exponent) and frequency irregularity (indexed by the Hjorth complexity) may provide better metrics to trigger the stimulation in adaptive DBS, improving the stability of the therapeutic effect, reducing side effects, increasing the lifetime of the DBS implant and reducing the cost of treatment. In the long term, better understanding of the dynamics of distinct oscillatory modes in the STN will inform better, more personalised computational models of PD using, for example, the Digital Twin framework.5
