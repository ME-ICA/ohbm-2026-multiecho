Introduction

Chronic tinnitus, a persistent phantom auditory sensation, has been previously associated with neuroanatomical changes extending beyond auditory pathways into the limbic system. Previous research suggested a functional segregation between the tinnitus percept itself and the affective reaction to it. Tinnitus perception has been linked to changes in the midline frontal cortex, an area implicated in sensory suppression. Specifically, patients have shown reduced gray matter in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), which correlates with perceived loudness but is independent of distress. However the reliability of these early findings is compromised by small sample sizes and the use of liberal statistical thresholds that lack proper control for family-wise error or false discovery rates, and recent reports have questioned the replicability of mental health neuroimaging findings.

Methods

This study aims to provide a replication and extension of these foundational findings which appears reasonable as previous work has often been limited by modest sample sizes. By leveraging a significantly larger cohort, our primary goal is to reliably separate distinct neural correlates of tinnitus perception and related distress.

We recruited 70 patients with chronic tinnitus and 70 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. All participants underwent high-resolution T1-weighted structural MRI scans as well as assessment of hearing thresholds, tinnitus distress (TFGH), and comprehensive psychometric characteristics of mood (BDI), state/trait anxiety (STAI) and intolerance of uncertainty(UI-18). Structural brain alterations in predefined regions of interests  (ROIs: vmPFC, auditory cortex) have been investigated using Voxel-Based Morphometry (VBM) to compare gray matter differences between groups and correlate VBM with clinical and psychometric variables.

Results

Contrary to the original reports, differences in gray matter volume between patients and controls lacks replicability in any ROI. This pattern holds true regardless of how the data is stratified—whether by sex or depression. Furthermore, this lack of replicability persists across different methodological approaches to gray matter analysis.

Conclusion

This study is relevant to show a possible lack of replicability of an established effect in the tinnitus literature when adequately controlling for psychopathology. These factors highlight the criticality for prioritizing replicability within clinical neuroimaging research.
