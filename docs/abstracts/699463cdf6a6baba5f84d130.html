Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) follows a stereotyped spatial progression where early degeneration in medial temporal regions spreads into temporal, parietal, and frontal association cortex, while primary sensorimotor areas are relatively spared. Early development shows the opposite pattern, with rapid expansion and maturation in association networks and comparatively modest change in primary sensory cortex. This "first in, last out" mirroring has been hypothesized, however, no prior work has quantified the similarities or differences between early development in infancy to brain degeneration in AD. Further, cellular, metabolic, and other spatial biological features within the human cortex may explain the similarities between early-life expansion to later-life vulnerability. However, the contribution of these features to both brain development and degeneration remain poorly characterized.

In this study we use anatomical multimodal surface matching (aMSM) to help evaluate this theory. We use aMSM-derived developmental cortical surface expansion maps from Birth to Age 2 (BA2) and Age 2 to Age 3 (A2A3) and compare them to aMSM-derived neurodegeneration maps from Cognitively Normal (CN) adults, Mild Cognitive Impairment Stable (MCI-S), Mild Cognitive Impairment Converters (MCI-C), and Alzheimer's disease (AD). Our main hypothesis is that regions with the greatest early-life expansion also demonstrate the strongest alignment with multiscale cortical features that show vulnerability to later neurodegeneration. We then assess the associations between these development and degeneration maps with features in the neuromaps data base including molecular, microstructural, functional, and genetic surface maps. 

Methods

Developmental and Neurodegeneration Dataset

The infant surface-area expansion maps were derived from the Early Life Adversity, Biological Embedding and Risk for Developmental Precursors of Mental Disorders (eLABE) cohort. Analyses were restricted to the full-term children subset, who were intentionally oversampled for social disadvantage, providing a socioeconomically and medically diverse sample for estimating early postnatal cortical growth.1 There were specifically 83 infants for BA2, and 38 infants for A2A3. Infant T1/T2-weighted scans were preprocessed with ANTs, cortical surfaces were segmented with FreeSurfer, and infant surface processing and quality control followed the pipeline described by Gorham et al.2 aMSM was then applied to longitudinal surfaces to obtain vertex-wise maps of cortical surface-area expansion for BA2 and A2A3 (Fig. 1A).

The adult surface-area atrophy maps were taken from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). We used the previously published longitudinal aMSM dataset on 1.5T MRI in older adults, with 90 cognitively normal (CN), 90 stable mild cognitive impairment (MCI-S), 90 MCI converters (MCI-C), and 90 Alzheimer’s disease (AD) participants.3 T1-weighted scans were processed with FreeSurfer, and aMSM was run on baseline and follow-up surfaces to estimate vertex-wise surface-area change over approximately 2 years in each diagnostic group (Fig. 1B).

Finally, we obtained a curated set of molecular, microstructural, developmental, and functional annotation maps from the neuromaps toolbox, which provides standardized transformations and quality-controlled reference maps for cross-modal analyses.4 

Shapley Spatial Null Permutation Framework 

We construct a parcel-by-biomarker feature matrix, where each column corresponds to a biomarker surface map sampled across all parcels. We then compute a biomarker-biomarker Pearson correlation matrix from the columns, and convert it to a dissimilarity matrix. This dissimilarity matrix is clustered using Ward's hierarchical agglomerative algorithm to produce a biomarker dendrogram. The number of dendrogram clusters that maximizes silhouette score over cosine distance yields cluster groups. 

All computations are performed with the feature-feature Gram matrix, the cross-term, and the target squared L2 norm. Within each cluster, we define an adaptive ridge penalty by tying the regularization strength to the magnitude of the cluster's Gram matrix. We compute the average diagonal element of the cluster submatrix and multiply it by a global relative penalty, giving a cluster effective penalty that respects collinearity structure across clusters; the same penalty has comparable impact in all groups.

For any subset of features, we define its value as the proportion of variance in the target values explained by a ridge regression model restricted to the same subset. The ridge coefficients are obtained by solving a linear system that depends only on the submatrix and the corresponding cross-term; the residual sum of squares result is converted to a R2-like score. For a feature, its Shapley value is the Shapley-weighted average of its marginal contribution over all subsets. This approach returns feature attributions that accounts for multicollinearity and interactions within clusters.

For statistical significance, we apply the same Shapley pipeline to a set of spatially rotated Alexander-Bloch surrogates of the target. We compute the observed Shapley vector from a target, and for each spin we recompute the specific target cross-term, repeat the cluster-wise Shapley computation, and obtain a null Shapley vector. This yields a null matrix, whose rows represent empirical null distributions of Shapley values for each feature. Comparing the observed Shapley vector to its null distribution across spins produces empirical one-sided p-values for positive and negative relationships (Fig. 2A). 

Results

Developmental vs Neurodegeneration Maps

We evaluated whether early-life expansion maps in babies are spatially related to surface-area change maps in older adults. If regions that expand most in infancy later show the greatest atrophy (or most resemble CN patterns), this would suggest that the same developmental activities that shape early cortex also align with later patterns of vulnerability and resilience. 

First, we projected all developmental and adult surface-area maps onto the Gordon-333 parcellation, summarizing data within its 333 parcels and 13 functional networks.5 This provided a common network-level framework for comparing developmental expansion and neurodegenerative atrophy as seen in Fig. 1A and Fig. 1B. We correlate BA2 and A2A3 with CN, MCI-S, MCI-C, and AD surface-area change across parcels with the Alexander-Bloch spin test (1,000 nulls). For BA2 (left column), the correlation between infant expansion and later surface-area change becomes progressively more negative from CN to MCI-S, MCI-C, and AD. CN shows only a weak trend, but by AD the spin-test correlation is strongly negative (r ≈ −0.61, p ≈ 0.001) with the highest R2 (Fig. 1C). This means that parcels that expanded the most from birth to age 2 are those that lose the most surface area in older adults with MCI and AD.

On the A2A3 side (right column), the strongest relationship is with CN (r ≈ −0.36, p ≈ 0.004), while associations with MCI-S, MCI-C, and AD are weaker and not robust (Fig. 1C). This suggests that age-2-to-3 expansion mainly tracks the stable surface-area pattern seen in cognitively normal aging, rather than the regions that undergo the largest disease-related atrophy.

Biomarker Attributions 

We applied the cluster-wise Shapley framework to all developmental and neurodegeneration target maps. Biomarker maps from neuromaps were parcellated with the Gordon-333 atlas, and grouped using Ward clustering, yielding 17 feature clusters. For each cluster we fit a separate ridge-Shapley model and controlled the family-wise error rate across these 17 models by using a significance threshold of (p < 0.003). The method’s workflow can be visualized in Fig. 2A. In Fig. 2B, the heatmap summarizes signed, normalized Shapley values for all biomarker–target pairs with at least one significant effect, with boxes indicating (FWER p < 0.05 controlling for 6 tests on target maps spanning birth to AD).

Among microstructural and systems-level maps, cortical myelin (T1w/T2w), functional connectivity homology, the principal FC gradient 1, evolutionary expansion, and the S–A axis rank all showed their strongest effects for the A2A3 developmental map, with additional contributions of the FC gradient to CN and MCI-C. In contrast, we did not observe any biomarker surviving correction for the BA2 map.

The neurotransmitter receptor maps showed the most widespread effects. The 5-HT2A map was significantly associated with five target maps, where the signed Shapley weights signify only modest influence for A2A3 and intermediate effects for CN and MCI-S, but the magnitude is largest for MCI-C and AD (-1.00 and -0.92 respectively in Fig. 2B with dark blue colors). Fig. 2C illustrates the cortical surface distribution of 5-HT2A binding itself, while Fig. 2D shows how strongly 5-HT2A associates with surface-area change patterns in A2A3, CN, MCI-S, MCI-C, and AD; 5-HT2A exhibits its greatest association with cortical surface-area changes in the A2A3, MCI-C, and AD maps. 5-HT4 showed a more selective association, predominantly with MCI-C, whereas D1 and μ-opioid maps were primarily linked to A2A3. CB1 contributed most strongly to MCI-S, and the histamine H3 map showed a selective association with AD. Together, these results highlight a small set of microstructural and neurochemical maps, such as 5-HT2A, FC gradients, and cortical myelin, that consistently explain variance in normal cortical development (A2A3, CN) and disease-related surface-area change (MCI-S, MCI-C, AD).

Conclusion

Our findings support the idea that early-life cortical expansion is spatially connected to late life vulnerability to Alzheimer’s disease related atrophy. Regions showing the greatest surface-area expansion from BA2 are precisely those that exhibit the largest longitudinal surface-area loss in MCI-C and AD, whereas A2A3 expansion aligns most closely with the relatively stable pattern observed in cognitively normal aging. This dissociation suggests that very early developmental programs leave a mark on which regions (frontal, parietal, temporal) are most vulnerable to disease-related degeneration, while primary somatosensory and primary motor cortex remain the most robust and relatively spared from neurodegeneration.

By embedding developmental and neurodegenerative maps in the neuromaps framework and applying a cluster-wise Shapley approach with spatial spin nulls, we identify a restricted set of microstructural and neurochemical annotations that account for much of this shared variance. Serotonergic 5-HT2A receptor density, cortical myelin, large-scale functional connectivity gradients, and evolutionary expansion metrics emerge as dominant contributors, with 5-HT2A showing especially strong weights for MCI-C and AD. These results point to a multiscale “vulnerability axis” where the association cortex that expands substantially in infancy, is heavily myelinated, and is enriched for specific neuromodulation systems; the association cortex undergoes the greatest surface-area loss in Alzheimer’s disease.

Our work illustrates how combining parcellation-based alignment, data-driven biomarker clustering, and Shapley value attribution under spatially informed null models can provide interpretable, cluster-level explanations of disease-related cortical change. Future studies leveraging larger cohorts, additional molecular tracers, and individual longitudinal lifespan data will be important to test the generality of these patterns and to determine whether the highlighted biomarker axes can inform risk stratification or targeted interventions across the lifespan.
