Introduction

Large-scale cortical and subcortical brain dynamics can be parsimoniously understood as resulting from excitations of specific eigenmodes of their geometry1. Each eigenmode describes a spatial pattern with a characteristic wavelength and oscillation frequency, capturing the preferred ways a system can respond to inputs. Recent work shows that cortico-cortical connectivity is arranged to support the activation of these resonant structural modes: cortical regions located at the anti-nodes, points of maximal amplitude in the standing-wave pattern, are more likely to be interconnected, facilitating efficient excitation of a wide range of modes2. 

           Here, we examine whether thalamocortical connectivity shows a similar preference, and how it can be used to understand the coupled excitation of different sets of cortical eigenmodes. Thalamocortical connections provide input to almost all cortical areas; are essential for regulating sensorimotor, attentive, emotive, and cognitive functions; play a key role in facilitating long-range cortico-cortical synchronization and can produce wave-like dynamics in the cortex3. Therefore, thalamocortical connections may be concentrated in cortical areas which are ideally placed to preferentially excite specific cortical eigenmodes over others and thereby influence mode activation patterns. Specifically, we hypothesise that thalamic connections target anti-nodes of individual cortical eigenmodes, and that the coupled activation of distinct cortical modes reflects their convergent connectivity with common thalamic subregions. 

Methods

We used minimally preprocessed data from 339 unrelated subjects provided by the Human Connectome Project - Young Adult (HCP-YA). Using a template surface, we calculated the first 50 non-global geometric eigenmodes of the left cortex (Fig. 1a). We thresholded the eigenmodes, categorizing surface vertices as either anti-nodes (50% of vertices with the greatest absolute amplitude) and nodes (50% of vertices with the lowest absolute amplitude), as illustrated in Fig. 1d. 

Diffusion data were processed using a validated connectome mapping pipeline4, which used probabilistic tractography to map connections between each voxel in the left thalamus and vertices of the left cortex, with streamlines seeded from the grey-white matter boundary (contralateral connections were discarded has the vast majority of thalamocortical connections are ipsilateral; Fig. 1b). In a subset of 5 subjects, we repeated the analyses by seeding streamlines in the left thalamus only. 

We assessed whether thalamic connectivity favours cortical eigenmode nodes over anti-nodes by splitting each participant’s thalamic structural connectivity (SC) accordingly. For every mode, we computed two SC matrices: one linking each thalamic voxel to all nodal vertices, and another linking the same voxels to all anti-nodal vertices (Fig. 1c). For each mode–voxel pair, preferential connectivity was then quantified using a t-statistic comparing SC to nodes versus anti-nodes. Finally, we constructed a mode-to-mode similarity matrix by calculating the cosine similarity between these preferential-connectivity profiles. To identify groups of modes with similar thalamic connectivity profiles, we clustered the similarity matrix using k-means with a deterministic farthest-first initialization, testing k = 1–10. The optimal k was chosen using the Silhouette coefficient, Davies–Bouldin index, and Calinski–Harabasz criterion, and the resulting clusters were cross-validated with hierarchical clustering.

Results

Consistent with previous reports5, we saw stronger thalamocortical connectivity in dorsal, frontal and somatomotor regions of the cortex (Fig. 2a).  The strength of thalamocortical connections was preferentially located in either anti-nodes or nodes, with this effect being mode-specific, but not voxel-specific, which is evident by the clear delineation between columns, but not rows in Fig. 2b. These results are highly consistent between subjects (mean standard deviation of t-statistics = 0.70).

All validity metrics identified two clusters as the optimal solution (Fig. 2c). These clusters cleanly separated modes showing preferential thalamic connectivity to nodes from those favouring anti-nodes, and this division was independent of spatial frequency. Hierarchical clustering reproduced the same two-cluster structure (Fig. 2d).

            Analysis based on SC generated with tracks seeded in the left thalamus reproduced the results described above. 

Conclusion

We computed a high-resolution thalamocortical structural connectome to understand how the thalamus is connected to geometric eigenmodes of the cortex. First, we observed that the thalamus is preferentially connected to either nodes or anti-nodes of cortical modes, suggesting that thalamocortical connections are configured to preferentially excite specific cortical modes. Moreover, thalamic voxels tend to show similar preferential connectivity profiles to geometric modes, suggesting that the thalamus is relatively homogenous in its capacity to excite different cortical modes. Second, we showed that cortical modes can be separated into two stable groups based on how they are preferentially connected to the thalamus, with one cluster corresponding to modes with anti-nodes more strongly connected to the thalamus and theoretically, whose activity is more likely to be influenced by thalamic inputs. Finally, we showed that our analysis is consistent across subjects and different tractography parameters.
