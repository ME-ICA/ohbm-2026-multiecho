Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is frequently accompanied by difficulties in visuospatial attention1 that can compromise early development and everyday functioning. Prior knowledge and expectations strongly shape spatial orienting: when a target location is reliably cued, attention can be pre-allocated, whereas violations of these expectations require rapid updating of the current cognitive and sensorimotor state. The ability to update sensory-motor and cognitive states in response to changing sensory or task requirements is often reduced in ASD.2 Attenuated visual orienting may thus be further amplified by increased costs of attentional reorienting and updating.

Visuospatial orienting is supported by the dorsal attention network, which shows functional alterations in ASD and is orchestrated by rhythmic activity in the alpha and beta bands.3 Alpha activity and its hemispheric lateralization are thought to implement top-down inhibition of task-irrelevant regions and to gate sensory processing according to spatial expectations.4 Beta-band activity has been linked to the maintenance of the current cognitive set and typically decreases when unexpected events demand a change in the ongoing sensorimotor or cognitive state.5 Together, alpha and beta oscillations provide a mechanistic framework for how the brain anticipates, selects, and updates spatial information.

Here, we investigated how attention-modulating alpha- and beta-band signatures modulate visuo-spatial orienting to relatively expected versus unexpected target locations in children and adolescents with ASD compared with neurotypical peers. By combining behavioral measures with neural signatures from dorsal attention network regions, we aimed to identify whether oscillatory mechanisms that normally support efficient orienting and flexible updating are altered in ASD, and may explain alterations in behavioral performance.

Methods

We recorded 64-channel EEG from 42 autistic and 38 neurotypical (NT) participants between 10 and 17 years (ASD: M=14.33, SD=2.29; NT: M=15.28, SD=1.66). Groups were matched for IQ (ASD: M=102.10, SD=13.44; NT: M=106.26, SD=9.36). Participants performed a Posner spatial cuing task, in which two boxes were presented on the left and right side of a screen. A cue (highlighted box on left or right; duration: 50 ms) validly or invalidly cued the location of the subsequently presented target (X-shaped stimulus in left or right box; duration: 500 ms; inter-stimulus interval: 100-250 ms). Participants were asked to respond as fast and accurately as possible with a left or right button press according to target location. Cues were generally predictive, with valid cues being presented in 70% of the trials (356 trials), and invalid cues being presented in 30% of trials (156 trials), manipulating spatial expectations.

EEG preprocessing was performed in EEGLAB v2025.0.0 and customized MATLAB (R2025a) scripts. Data were downsampled to 500 Hz, line noise was removed, and noisy channels were identified using automated criteria followed by visual inspection. Signals were high-pass filtered at 0.1 Hz and re-referenced to the average. Independent component analysis (extended infomax) was computed on a 1 Hz-filtered copy after removal of gross artifacts. Components reflecting ocular, muscular, cardiac, or line-noise activity were identified using ICLabel and manual review and removed from the broadband dataset. Rejected channels were interpolated using spherical splines.

Target-locked epochs were extracted from −1.2 s to +1.3 s around target onset to include uncontaminated baseline activity and sufficient padding for time–frequency analysis. Baseline activity was defined from −500 to −200 ms relative to cue onset, and neural measures were quantified from 0–500 ms following target presentation. Automated artifact detection guided visual trial rejection. Trials were excluded if reaction times (RTs) were <200 ms, >1000 ms, incorrect, or missing.

EEG epochs were decomposed in the time–frequency domain using complex Morlet wavelets (30 logarithmically spaced frequencies, 8–60 Hz; 4–9 cycles). Power was expressed in decibels relative to trial-specific pre-cue baselines. Analyses focused on a priori occipital (PO7, PO8, PO3, PO4, O1, O2), parietal (P5, P6, P1, P2, P3, P4), and frontal (FC3, FC4, FC1, FC2, F1, F2) regions of interest (ROI), covering key nodes of the dorsal attention network. Ipsilateral and contralateral clusters were defined relative to target side. Alpha (8–12 Hz) and beta (15–29 Hz) total power, hemispheric lateralization (ipsilateral minus contralateral), and inter-trial phase coherence corrected for trial count (ITPCz) were averaged across the target interval. 

Linear mixed-effects models tested effects of group (ASD/NT) and interactions with cue validity (valid/invalid) on RT, and further interactions with ROI (occipital/frontal/parietal) on neural measures. Separate models were estimated for alpha and beta bands, and for hemispheres (ipsilateral/contralateral) where applicable. Total power, lateralization, and RT were modelled at the trial level, whereas ITPCz was analyzed at the condition level. Participant was included as a random intercept and age as a covariate. In a second step, neural predictors of RT were examined by modelling interactions between cue validity and neural measures. To reduce collinearity, neural measures were averaged across occipito-parietal regions. Continuous predictors were scaled and centered. Effect sizes are reported as standardized fixed-effect estimates (β), which can be interpreted as standard effect size. Post-hoc tests on significant interaction effects were adjusted for multiple comparison by False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction and are reported as standardized mean difference values (Δβ).

Results

Across groups, behavioral response to targets was strongly facilitated by spatial cues. Responses were markedly slower on invalid compared to valid trials (cueing benefit: ~45–52 ms; p<0.001), with a larger invalidity cost in the ASD group than in NT participants (Δβ=-0.07; p=0.002; difference in invalidity cost: ~6 ms). Overall mean RT did not differ significantly between groups.

At the neural level, oscillatory activity was strongly lateralized (Figure 1A) and cue validity robustly modulated oscillatory activity. In posterior ipsilateral sites, valid – relative to invalid – targets increased alpha and beta power in both groups, and enhanced alpha/beta lateralization and alpha/beta ITPCz (valid–invalid: all p<.001). These cue-induced modulations were systematically attenuated in ASD (see Figure 1B for distribution of subject-level observations). The validity-related increase was smaller in ASD than NT in ipsilateral alpha power (Δβ=−0.05, p=.001), in alpha lateralization (Δβ=-0.04, p=.010), and in ipsilateral alpha ITPCz (Δβ=-0.26, p=.007). During valid trials, alpha lateralization was generally weaker in ASD than NT (Δβ=0.05, p=.008). Similarly, a lower contralateral occipital beta ITPCz could be observed for ASD in valid trials (ASD–NT Δβ=−0.8, p<.001), as well as a weaker validity-related increase of beta ITPCz (Δβ=0.68, p=.002). This was accompanied by a smaller invalidity-related suppression of ipsilateral beta power (Δβ=-0.03, p=.020), and decreased ipsilateral beta power suppression during invalid trials (Δβ=-0.07, p=.030). Across validity conditions, ASD showed a reduced occipital alpha lateralization (ASD–NT Δβ=−0.06 SD, p=.012) and decreased frontal and parietal beta power suppression relative to NT (β=0.07–0.10, all p≤0.01).

Trial-wise oscillatory dynamics were systematically related to behavioral performance, including those oscillatory features that were altered in ASD. Faster responses were associated with reduced contralateral frontal alpha power (β=0.01, p=.028), and stronger occipital alpha lateralization (β=−0.02, p=.001). Specifically in invalid trials, shorter RT were associated with increased ipsilateral posterior and reduced frontal alpha power (posterior: β=−0.03, p=.004; frontal: β=0.02, p=.010). In the beta band, greater frontal (β=−0.02, p<.001) and posterior lateralization (β=−0.01, p=.044) were linked to faster responding. The strongest behavioral associations emerged for phase-based measures (Figure 1C for subject-level observations). Elevated ipsilateral frontal alpha ITPCz was associated with generally faster responses (β=−0.11, p=.033). During invalid trials, higher contralateral frontal alpha and posterior beta ITPCz (alpha: β=−0.47, p<.001; beta: β=−0.29, p=.016) and ipsilateral posterior alpha and beta ITPCz (alpha: β=−0.38, p=.001; beta: β=−0.35, p<.001) predicted shorter RTs. More pronounced associations of ITPCz and RT on invalid trials indicated that more consistent phase alignment particularly benefited performance under higher attentional demands.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings reveal altered behavioral and neural signatures of visuospatial orienting in autistic youth, which become particularly evident when spatial expectations are violated. While overall response speed was comparable between groups, autistic participants showed a selectively increased cost for invalidly cued targets, suggesting a reduced flexibility to disengage from an established attentional set and reorient toward unexpected stimuli.

At the neural level, both groups engaged typical spatial attention-related oscillatory mechanisms, but these effects were consistently weaker in ASD, showing in a reduced posterior alpha lateralization and beta phase locking as well as elevated frontal/parietal beta power. Reduced posterior alpha lateralization and diminished alpha and beta phase locking point to a less efficient neural tuning to attentional demands with regards to the lateralization of hemispheric activity according to task-relevance and the alignment of neural activity over repeated attentional situations. This pattern suggests a diminished use of predictive information for sensory–motor preparation. In addition, altered beta-band dynamics, including reduced beta suppression and elevated frontal and parietal beta power, indicate less pronounced updating of the ongoing cognitive–motor state during the task.

Importantly, the robust observations of smaller adaptations in dependence of cue validity in autism indicate a less efficient use of expectancy for neural preparation, and a decreased adaptation to new demands when a switch of brain states is needed. This is neurally reflected by the magnitude of rhythmic brain activity, the efficient lateralization of this activity to the task-relevant hemisphere as well as its phase synchronization across repeated stimulus events. Trial-wise associations between oscillatory measures and reaction times highlight the functional relevance of these neural dynamics and support their contribution to the increased behavioral cost of expectancy violations in ASD.

Together, these results suggest that autism is associated with reduced efficiency in using spatial predictions to prepare sensory–motor processing, along with diminished flexibility in updating cognitive states when predictions are violated. Concurrent alterations in posterior alpha and frontal beta activity point to a combined disruption of sensory selection and top-down control mechanisms, offering a parsimonious account of atypical attentional orienting in autism.
