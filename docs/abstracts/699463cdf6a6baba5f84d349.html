Introduction

Dopamine replacement therapies are highly effective in early Parkinson’s disease (PD), producing robust improvements in cardinal motor symptoms. However, they do not address all aspects of the disorder, and with disease progression their efficacy often wanes and becomes increasingly variable. Axial features such as gait disturbance and postural instability are particularly refractory. This evolving treatment response parallels the spread of pathology beyond the nigrostriatal dopaminergic system, which likely contributes to the emergence and persistence of later-stage, non–dopamine-responsive symptoms. 

Within this context, brainstem cholinergic pathways originating in the pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN), and their projections to the thalamus, have been strongly implicated in axial motor impairment.1,2 The PPN provides dense ascending input to intralaminar thalamic nuclei, key modulators of dopaminergic signal integration.

The PPN is also tightly coupled with the basal ganglia. Output nuclei (i.e., GPi/SNr) project to the PPN, enabling suppression or disinhibition of its activity, while the PPN provides reciprocal projections back to basal ganglia structures and the dopaminergic midbrain. Preclinical evidence suggests that PPN dysfunction can influence dopamine signalling via midbrain pathways, indicating a potential role for the PPN in shaping responsiveness to dopamine replacement therapy.3

If PPN integrity modulates dopamine-related function, several implications follow. Variability and decline in dopaminergic benefit may partly reflect disruption of dopaminergic–cholinergic interactions rather than dopaminergic loss alone, with preserved cholinergic circuitry supporting sustained responsiveness in some patients. This framework also motivates circuit-informed adjunctive interventions—such as cholinergic modulation or PPN-targeted neuromodulation—to improve axial symptoms while potentially stabilising functional responses to dopaminergic therapy.

Methods

To investigate the relationship between dopamine-induced changes to motor symptoms and PPN connectivity with thalamus and SNc, we analysed multi-shell diffusion-weighted MRI acquired in a cohort of 25 people with idiopathic Parkinson’s disease. 

Motor symptom severity was assessed using the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale motor subsection (UPDRS-III), administered both in the OFF-medication state following levodopa washout and in the ON-medication state. Sub-scores reflecting bradykinesia (obtained from the most affected side), and axial motor function were extracted. Levodopa responsiveness was quantified as the relative change in symptom severity between OFF and ON states (OFF-ON/OFF).  

Hybrid-contrast anatomical images combining T1-weighted and quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) data were generated for each participant and nonlinearly registered to diffusion space using diffeomorphic registration to modality-matched atlases. The left and right PPN, SNc and thalamus masks were registered to diffusion space using the generated transformations. 

Structural connectivity was assessed using probabilistic tractography (iFOD2) implemented in MRtrix3. Whole-brain tractograms comprising 20 million streamlines were generated using anatomically constrained tractography, incorporating a modified subcortical grey matter tissue compartment that explicitly included the PPN, thalamus and SNc. The sum of streamline weights (following SIFT2) between regions of interest (contralateral to the most affected side) were extracted to quantify PPN–SNc and PPN–thalamic connectivity.

Results

Significant correlations were observed between levodopa-induced changes in axial motor symptoms and structural connectivity between the PPN and the thalamus, as indexed by tractography-derived streamline counts. Greater PPN–thalamic connectivity was associated with larger improvements in axial symptom severity following levodopa administration (ρ = 0.47, p = 0.029). In addition, post-levodopa bradykinesia scores were significantly associated with PPN-–SNc connectivity (ρ = 0.54, p = 0.005), with higher streamline counts corresponding to a greater improvement in bradykinesia after dopamine. No significant relationships were observed between axial symptom changes and SNc-PPN connectivity (ρ = -0.07, p = 0.77), nor between bradykinesia and PPN-thalamic connectivity (ρ = 0.02, p = 0.91), a pattern consistent with a double dissociation between PPN-centred motor networks.

Conclusion

The present findings highlight a potentially important role for the PPN as a network-level modulator of motor symptom expression and dopaminergic responsiveness in Parkinson’s disease. Specifically, connectivity between the PPN and thalamus was related to levodopa-induced changes in axial motor symptoms, whereas PPN–SNc was associated with bradykinesia severity in the ON-medication state. This pattern suggests a functional dissociation, with PPN–thalamic pathways more closely linked to the dopamine-dependent modifiability of axial features, and PPN–SNc pathways more closely linked to the expression (and dopaminergic rescue) of limb bradykinesia. 

One plausible interpretation is that stronger PPN- thalamus connectivity supports levodopa-related improvements in axial symptoms by preserving ascending brainstem control over thalamic signals that interact with thalamostriatal circuits involved in gait and postural control. The PPN is a major source of ascending cholinergic input to thalamus, including intralaminar nuclei. In PD, reduced thalamic cholinergic integrity (often treated as a marker of PPN cholinergic degeneration) has been associated with falls, postural sensory deficits, and freezing of gait, consistent with a specific role for this system in axial impairment. As such, preserved PPN–thalamic connectivity could increase the likelihood that dopaminergic therapy translates into functional gains in axial control, by maintaining the cholinergic-thalamostriatal interactions needed to appropriately modulate dopaminergic signaling during gait and balance demands. 

By contrast, the association between PPN-SNc connectivity and bradykinesia is consistent with extensive evidence for direct PPN-region influence over midbrain dopamine neurons. Anatomical and physiological studies demonstrate convergent cholinergic/glutamatergic inputs from the PPN region to SNc dopamine neurons, capable of modulating their excitability and firing patterns.  This provides a biologically plausible route through which variability in PPN-SNc connecitivty could contribute to inter-individual differences in dopaminergic responsiveness for limb bradykinesia.
