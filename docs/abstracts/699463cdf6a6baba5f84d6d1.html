Introduction

Obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) is a heterogeneous and disabling condition that affects approximately 2–3% of the population. A substantial proportion of patients do not respond adequately to psychological and/or pharmacological first-line treatments, known as Treatment-Refractory OCD (TR-OCD)¹. For these patients, neurosurgical procedures like capsulotomy have traditionally been considered. These interventions target the anterior limb of the internal capsule (ALIC) to disrupt the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuits implicated in OCD pathophysiology², as it is considered one of the key white matter pathways within these circuits. These involve structures such as the thalamus, putamen, pallidum, caudate nucleus, and orbitofrontal cortex, which have shown both functional and structural differences in OCD when compared to the general population³. 

However, these surgical treatments carry risks and do not always guarantee sustained remission. Magnetic resonance–guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) has recently emerged as a less invasive alternative, enabling precise thermal ablation of the ALIC without incisions or ionizing radiation. MRgFUS has demonstrated clinical effectiveness in essential tremor and chronic pain with minimal adverse effects, and its application to TR-OCD suggests beneficial effects to modulate the pathological neural networks underlying the disorder. Early evidence supports its safety and therapeutic potential. However, questions remain regarding the durability of its effects. This clinical trial aims to assess both clinical outcomes and neural changes following MRgFUS targeting the ALIC in individuals diagnosed with TR-OCD. Particularly, we are interested in structural changes in regions of the CSTC loop (thalamus, basal ganglia and orbitofrontal cortex) before and after the intervention, aiming a better understanding of the (preliminary) anatomical mechanisms through which MRgFUS exerts its therapeutic effects.

Methods

This open-label, non-randomized phase II/III clinical trial is currently underway, and the first three TR-OCD patients have already undergone MRgFUS intervention. At baseline, all patients completed clinical and neuropsychological assessments, including the Yale–Brown Obsessive–Compulsive Scale (YBOCS). Additionally, a multimodal pre-treatment MRI protocol was acquired one month prior to the intervention using a Philips 3T scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Structural imaging included a high-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence. The same clinical, neuropsychological, and imaging protocol was repeated one month after the MRgFUS procedure to obtain post-intervention data.

The present study focuses on the data from these first three patients, with particular emphasis on longitudinal gray matter (GM) volume changes after the intervention and their relationship with clinical outcome, defined as the percentage change in YBOCS scores between time points. Image preprocessing was performed at the individual level using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12). First, a rigid-body within-subject co-registration aligned the post-intervention scan (T2) to the pre-intervention scan (T1) to ensure appropriate initial spatial correspondence. Subsequently, a pairwise longitudinal registration was applied to non-linearly align both time points for each subject. This procedure employs symmetric diffeomorphic modeling to estimate regional volumetric changes between T1 and T2 while incorporating bias-field correction to reduce intensity inhomogeneities. For each patient, this process yielded an average anatomical image derived from both time points and a Jacobian difference map encoding voxel-wise volumetric changes between T1 and T2. The average image was then segmented to isolate GM voxels, which were multiplied by the Jacobian difference map to obtain a GM-specific Jacobian map for each subject.

Based on these GM Jacobian maps, two derivative maps were generated for each patient using MATLAB: one representing GM increases (Jacobian values > 0) and another representing GM decreases (Jacobian values < 0). This separation allowed independent analysis of volumetric expansion and contraction patterns. To enable comparison across subjects and exploratory group-level analyses, Jacobian values were subsequently transformed into z-scores within each direction of change, using the average GM change for expansions and contractions separately as reference. Each subject’s z-score maps were then spatially normalized to MNI space using SPM12. Group-level GM expansion and contraction maps were created by summing the individual z-score maps across the three patients. These maps were thresholded at z ≥ 2 to highlight regions showing relatively greater GM changes while accounting for the mean change within each direction.

From the resulting thresholded maps, clusters with a minimum extent of 10 voxels were identified and converted into binary masks. These masks were used to extract subject-specific GM change values within each cluster, enabling individual-level characterization of GM alterations in regions showing consistent effects across patients. Finally, Kendall’s Tau-b correlation coefficients were computed, in a descriptive and exploratory manner, to assess the association between GM changes in each cluster and percentage Y-BOCS improvement. This approach allowed identification of brain regions in which structural changes were associated with clinical outcome following MRgFUS intervention.

Results

Regarding the psychological assessment, preliminary data from the first three patients treated with MRgFUS suggest a favorable safety profile alongside encouraging clinical outcomes. At the one-month follow-up, patient P1 exhibited a 24% reduction in YBOCS scores, whereas patients P2 and P3 showed larger reductions of 44% and 52%, respectively, both reaching clinical remission. Throughout the following sections, the change in Y-BOCS score between baseline and follow-up (T1 − T2) is referred to as clinical improvement or percentage Y-BOCS improvement.

Neuroimaging analyses focused on GM volume differences within CSTC regions (T2 − T1), followed by an exploration of their association with percentage YBOCS improvement. At the group level, GM changes were observed across several thalamic nuclei. Specifically, a GM expansion was detected in the right mediodorsal (R-MD) thalamic nucleus (z = 4.15) relative to the average GM expansion, whereas a GM contraction was identified in the left ventrolateral (L-VL) nucleus (z = 2.01). At the individual level, the patient showing the greatest clinical improvement (P3) also exhibited the largest R-MD expansion (z = 3.22), compared with P1 (z = 0.11) and P2 (z = 0.82). The exploratory association between R-MD expansion and percentage YBOCS improvement was positive (τ = 1), suggesting a potential linear trend whereby greater symptom improvement was associated with larger GM expansion in this region. In the case of the L-VL nucleus, GM contraction values were relatively similar across patients (z = 0.66, 0.47, and 0.88 for P1, P2, and P3, respectively). Nevertheless, P3, who showed the greatest clinical improvement, also presented the largest contraction relative to the average of this GM change. Accordingly, the association between L-VL contraction and percentage YBOCS improvement was positive (τ = 0.33).

Bilateral GM expansion in the caudate nucleus also emerged at the group level. At the individual level, the largest expansions were observed in P2, both in the left (z = 3.10) and right caudate (z = 2.48). P1 showed more modest expansion (left z = 0.72; right z = 1.98), while P3 exhibited minimal GM change relative to the average expansion (left z = 0.05; right z = 0.40). Overall, GM expansion in both left and right caudate nuclei showed a negative association with percentage YBOCS improvement (τ = −0.33).

For the putamen, only a left-hemisphere cluster of GM expansion survived the group-level z-threshold (z = 3.8). Individually, P1 demonstrated the greatest expansion in this region (z = 3.95), whereas P2 and P3 showed lower values (z = −0.12 and z = −0.03, respectively) relative to the average GM expansion. The exploratory association between GM expansion in this region and clinical improvement was negative (τ = −0.33). In the pallidum, a cluster of GM contraction in the right hemisphere exceeded the group threshold (z = 2.08). At the individual level, contraction magnitudes were comparable across patients (z = 0.67 for P1, 0.58 for P2, and 0.83 for P3), and the association with percentage YBOCS improvement was again negative (τ = −0.33). Finally, bilateral GM expansion clusters were identified in the medial orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). Individually, P2 and P3 showed greater GM expansion than P1. For the left OFC, z-scores were −0.02, 0.88, and 1.44 for P1, P2, and P3, respectively, while for the right OFC they were −0.05, 1.16, and 0.97. GM expansion in both OFC clusters exhibited a negative exploratory association with percentage YBOCS improvement (τ = −0.33).

It is important to note that with such a small sample, we cannot claim statistical significance or make inferential statements; hence, p-values (which ranged roughly from 0.1 to 0.6) are not reported. The statistics presented reflect descriptive tendencies and their strength in this preliminary cohort.

Conclusion

The preliminary clinical and neuroimaging findings from this ongoing phase II/III trial suggest that MRgFUS targeting the ALIC may induce meaningful symptom improvement in individuals with TR-OCD, while maintaining a favorable safety profile. All three patients exhibited reductions in Y-BOCS scores at the one-month follow-up, and two achieved clinical remission, indicating early therapeutic potential.

From a neuroimaging perspective, MRgFUS was associated with detectable GM changes within key nodes of the CSTC network. Notably, expansion in the R-MD thalamic nucleus corresponded most with symptom improvement: the patient showing the largest clinical response exhibited the greatest change in this region. This nucleus is involved in both cognitive and emotional processing, including reward devaluation, fear extinction, working memory, behavioral flexibility, and goal-directed behavior⁴. Conversely, contraction in the L-VL thalamic nucleus, primarily associated with motor control⁴, followed a similar, although weaker, pattern.

Additional GM changes were also observed in the caudate nucleus, putamen, pallidum, and medial orbitofrontal cortex, though their associations with symptom improvement were more variable. Putamen GM reductions in patients with TR-remission could be somehow related to its involvement in action selection, habit formation, and error processing, consistent with the pallidum⁵. However, changes in the caudate were heterogeneous, highlighting the need for larger samples to determine consistent patterns. Interestingly, higher GM reductions in medial OFC GM were also observed in patients achieving remission, possibly aligning with its known role in reward-guided decision-making⁵. These divergent patterns suggest that MRgFUS may modulate multiple subcomponents of the CSTC circuitry, not all of which contribute equally to clinical improvement.

However, given the extremely limited sample size, these observations remain exploratory and should not be interpreted as statistically or clinically definitive. Nonetheless, they might provide an initial framework suggesting that structural plasticity within specific thalamic and basal ganglia regions may accompany early symptom improvements following MRgFUS. As the trial continues and additional participants are included, these preliminary findings will allow for testing more precise hypotheses regarding the neurobiological mechanisms underlying treatment response, the specificity of regional changes, and the stability of these effects over time.

Importantly, longer longitudinal follow-ups with extended neuroimaging and psychological assessments will be crucial to further characterize the effects of MRgFUS, better understand its impact on CSTC circuits, and inform future therapeutic strategies for TR-OCD. Overall, these early results support the feasibility, safety, and potential efficacy of MRgFUS in severe TR-OCD and underscore the value of integrating multimodal imaging with clinical evaluation to understand how targeted neuromodulation may alter pathological brain networks.
