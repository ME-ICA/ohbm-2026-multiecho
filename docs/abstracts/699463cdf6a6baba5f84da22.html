Introduction

Schizophrenia and related psychoses show substantial clinical heterogeneity, resulting in limited development of effective interventions. Approximately 40 to 60% of individuals do not achieve symptomatic remission within the first years after onset, an illness course associated with poorer functioning and increased relapse risk1,2. This variability reflects complex, multiscale interactions of molecular, structural and functional alterations with symptom expression, yet current diagnostic and therapeutic approaches fail to capture these dynamics3. The Virtual Brain Twin (VBT) project aims to address this challenge by creating personalized, mechanistic brain models that integrate clinical, imaging, and molecular data to inform treatment decisions. Symptom-based phenotypes constitute a crucial step in this process, providing an “initial coordinate system” to parse this heterogeneity into more homogeneous disease dimensions and linking distinct symptom profiles to underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Therefore, the objective of this study is to derive robust symptom-based phenotypes in psychosis and evaluate their neurobiological underpinnings through multimodal neuroimaging-based classification.

Methods

Our clinical sample comprised 454 patients with recent-onset psychosis (ROP) or established schizophrenia from the MUNICH cohort (n=139) and the multisite PRONIA cohort (n=315 across 10 sites, mean age 27.1 ± 7.5 years, 286 males). Symptom dimensions were derived using Orthogonal Projective Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (OPNMF) applied to ratings from the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale and the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms. We systematically assessed 3, 4, and 5 factor solutions based on the rate of reconstruction-error reduction and prior literature supporting these dimensionalities4,5. OPNMF factorizes a non-negative matrix V into a component matrix W and a loading matrix H such that V = W H (Figure 1). The resulting component matrix W was then projected onto the PRONIA sample, and each patient was assigned to the factor on which they showed the highest loading in H.

To evaluate neurobiological correlates of these symptom subgroups, we classified PRONIA patients assigned to each factor against healthy controls (HC) matched by age, sex, and site (n=522, mean age 25.6 ± 6.0 years, 306 females). Neuroimaging features included gray matter volume (GMV) derived from T1-weighted MRI and fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF) from resting-state fMRI across three frequency bands, slow-3 (0.073–0.198 Hz), slow-4 (0.027–0.073 Hz), and slow-5 (0.01–0.027 Hz). GMV was corrected using dynamic standardization, and fALFF features were corrected using offset harmonization for age, sex, and site effects. Classification employed a linear support vector machine within a nested cross-validation design, 5 inner permutations by 10 outer folds. Feature preprocessing, including ranking, thresholding, minimum-redundancy-maximum-relevance selection, dimensionality reduction, and standardization, was performed strictly within training folds to prevent information leakage.

To assess generalizability, we evaluated the ability of each factor-specific classifier to differentiate patients associated with other symptom factors. Each classifier, originally trained to distinguish a specific factor group from healthy controls, was applied to patients assigned to the remaining factors. The resulting decision scores were used as continuous regressors in statistical parametric mapping (SPM) analyses, alongside covariates including age, sex, and site, and corrected for total intracranial volume (TIV). Positive and negative linear correlations were computed for each factor regressor with local brain signal (GMV or fALFF). 

Results

The 4-factor solution outperformed others, showing the highest explained variance in both the combined (89.3%) and PRONIA (88.0%) sample, as well as the strongest neuroimaging-based predictive accuracy. This solution yielded four clinically interpretable phenotypes: Avolition-Asociality, Expressive Deficits, Cognitive Disorganization, and Positive Symptoms, with a balanced distribution of patients across subgroups.
 
Classification of patients assigned to factors from matched HC using GMV and fALFF features showed highest performance with the 4-factor model and slow-3 fALFF data (F1: sensitivity (sens) = 65.22%, specificity (spec) = 64.49% , balanced accuracy (BAC)= 64.85%, area under the curve (AUC) = 0.65 (0.57-0.74), F2: sens = 63.04%, spec= 68.18%, BAC= 65.61%, AUC= 0.72 (0.62-0.81), F3: sens= 83.67%, spec=63.86%, BAC=73.76%, AUC=0.8 (0.72-0.89), F4: sens=55.17%, spec=73.97%, BAC=64.57%, AUC=0.7 (0.63-0.77).

Conclusion

We identified four interpretable symptom dimensions in ROP patients, extended beyond traditional clinical subscales. These phenotypes were differentiable from HC using multimodal neuroimaging features. Our findings support the idea that richer symptom dimensions can reveal biologically grounded subgroups in psychosis, offering a foundation for more personalized clinical approaches.
