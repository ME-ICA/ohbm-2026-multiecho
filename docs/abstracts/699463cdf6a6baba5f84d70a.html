Introduction

Upper limb dystonia (ULD) is a focal movement disorder characterized by sustained or intermittent abnormal movements or postures of the upper limb. In some cases, it can be task-specific, such as writer's cramp1. The pathophysiology of dystonia is now conceptualized as a network disorder involving distributed brain regions, including the basal ganglia and cerebellum2. Previous task-based fMRI studies have reported altered activation patterns across multiple brain regions, including the sensorimotor cortex and occipital regions3. However, the intrinsic functional architecture of resting-state networks and their relationship to clinical manifestations remain incompletely understood. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) offers a data-driven approach to characterize network-level abnormalities without task-related confounds. Independent component analysis (ICA) enables the unbiased identification of functionally coherent networks across individuals, potentially revealing network disruptions not apparent during task performance. 

We hypothesized that ULD patients would exhibit altered functional connectivity in intrinsic brain networks compared to healthy controls. Additionally, we predicted that functional metrics within disrupted networks would correlate with clinical symptom severity as measured by the Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS), potentially serving as biomarkers for disease heterogeneity. To test these hypotheses, we conducted a ulti-metric rs-fMRI analysis using temporally concatenated group ICA to identify network alterations, followed by examination of multiple functional metrics, including connectivity strength, regional homogeneity, and amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations within affected networks. The aim of this study was to examine alterations in intrinsic brain network functional connectivity in patients with ULD compared with healthy controls. 

Methods

A total of 60 participants were recruited, including 26 patients with idiopathic right upper limb dystonia (ULD) and 34 healthy controls from Hospital Israelita Albert Einstein, Universidade Federal de São Paulo and Hospital do Servidor Público Estadual de São Paulo. Symptoms severity was assessed using the Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS). All participants provided informed consent under protocols approved by the original data contributors' institutional review boards.

Functional MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner (TR = 0.9s, 400 volumes). Preprocessing was performed using RESTplus and included: signal stabilization, slice timing correction, realignment, coregistration, and spatial normalization to MNI152 space (3mm isotropic), spatial smoothing (6mm FWHM), linear detrending, nuisance regression (Friston 24-parameter model plus white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals), and temporal filtering (0.01-0.08Hz). 

Group-level ICA was performed using FSL-MELODIC, decomposing data into 20 independent components representing intrinsic brain networks. Components were spatially matched to Smith 2009 resting-state network4 templates using AFNI-3dMatch. Subject-specific spatial maps were obtained via FSL dual regression. Group differences were assessed using FSL-randomise with 10,000 nonparametric permutations (voxelwise p < 0.001), with cluster size correction applied using AFNI 3dClustSim (α=0.05).

Within each ROI, we extracted regional homogeneity (ReHo, measuring local temporal synchronization) and fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF, quantifying spontaneous activity in 0.01-0.08 Hz), alongside the dual regression Z-scores. All metrics were averaged within ROIs for each participant. Group differences (dystonia vs. controls) were assessed using independent-samples t-tests for each metric and ROI. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d. Within the patient group, relationships between brain metrics and BFMDRS scores were examined using both Pearson and Spearman correlations to account for potential non-linear associations. Significance threshold was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Dual regression and permutation testing revealed significant group differences in four clusters within two visual networks that survived the strict cluster-size correction, including one cluster in the occipital pole network (Figure 1A, right occipital pole), and three cluster in the lateral visual (Figure 1 B, right parietal operculum; Figure 1 C, right lateral occipital cortex; and Figure 1D, left lateral occipital cortex). 

Dystonia patients exhibited significantly reduced functional connectivity (Z-scores) in all four visual ROIs compared to healthy controls. These findings indicate widespread impairment of visual network connectivity in upper limb dystonia. However, within the patient group (n=26), functional connectivity strength (Z-scores) did not show significant correlations with BFMDRS symptom severity scores across all four ROIs (all p > 0.05 for both Pearson and Spearman correlations). 

In contrast, both ReHo and fALFF showed no significant group differences in any of the four ROIs (all p > 0.05). This dissociation between large-scale connectivity metrics and local activity measures suggests that dystonia primarily affects network-level integration rather than regional neural activity itself.

Despite showing no group differences, ReHo values exhibited significant negative correlations with symptom severity in the left lateral occipital cortex. The Spearman correlation was robust (ρ = -0.535, p = 0.0049), with Pearson correlation also reaching significance (r = -0.459, p = 0.018). Lower ReHo values were associated with more severe dystonic symptoms. 

 fALFF demonstrated significant negative correlations with symptom severity in two ROIs using Spearman correlation: the right parietal operculum (ρ = -0.413, p = 0.036) and the left lateral occipital cortex (ρ = -0.438, p = 0.025). Notably, all four ROIs exhibited negative correlation trends with symptom severity (Spearman ρ ranging from -0.364 to -0.438, and p ranging from 0.025 to 0.067), suggesting a consistent relationship between reduced local neural activity and increased symptom severity.

Conclusion

This resting-state fMRI study reveals that upper limb dystonia patients show reduced visual network connectivity. The functional connectivity (Z-scores) disruption appeared consistently across patients but did not correlate with symptom severity. In contrast, local activity measures (ReHo and fALFF) showed no group differences yet correlated negatively with symptoms (lower activity linked to worse symptoms).

These findings point to visual network disruption as a core feature of dystonia pathophysiology. Task-specific symptoms during writing or instrument playing require precise visual-motor coordination5. The connectivity abnormalities we found, particularly in lateral occipital regions, may impair this integration. This finding is consistent with previous reports3 of occipital hyperactivation during movement, suggesting that these activation changes represent compensatory responses to underlying connectivity deficits.

The divergent patterns between connectivity and local activity are worth noting. Network connectivity Z-scores distinguished patients from controls but showed no correlation with BFMDRS scores, suggesting they reflect disease presence rather than severity. In contrast, whilst ReHo and fALFF showed no group differences, both metrics correlated significantly with symptom severity within the patient group. Future studies should integrate connectivity metrics (Z-scores) with local activity measures (ReHo, fALFF) in multivariate analyses to better characterize individual disease profiles.

The strength of this study is that it revealed a dissociation between network-level connectivity deficits (differentiating patients from controls) and local activity alterations (correlating with symptom severity), demonstrating that these metrics capture complementary aspects of dystonia pathophysiology. However, the cross-sectional design prevents determination of whether connectivity disruption precedes or follows symptom development, and the moderate sample size limits power to detect correlations in patients. Additionally, seed-based connectivity analyses targeting the identified lateral occipital regions could elucidate their specific roles within broader sensorimotor networks and potentially identify therapeutic targets for neuromodulation.
