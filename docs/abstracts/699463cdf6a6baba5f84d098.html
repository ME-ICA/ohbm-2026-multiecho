Introduction

A multitude of EEG-derived metrics of brain activity have been linked to neurodevelopmental outcomes in children, including spectral power and functional connectivity in various frequency ranges and regions.1 Systematic exploration of multiple candidate brain metrics in relation to developmental outcome is hindered by the multiple comparisons problem using traditional statistical approaches. Here, we use the multiverse approach to project candidate EEG metrics in a multiverse space, which is a low-dimensional representation of how brain metrics produced by different neuroimaging analyses are related in terms of their variation across individuals.2 This approach enables researchers to make informed, data-driven decisions about metric selection for subsequent hypothesis testing, reducing the multiple comparisons burden while ensuring comprehensive coverage of the EEG measurement space. 

Methods

The South African Safe Passage Study prenatally recruited participants from Cape Town, South Africa to investigate the role of environmental risk factors in stillbirth and sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS).3 A subset of children were followed up at age 5 - 16 years for the Biomarkers of Neurodevelopmental Outcomes (BONO) study. Data from 281 participants in the BONO study were included in the present analyses. EEG was collected using a 19-channel Enobio system at a sampling rate of 500Hz for two conditions of 60 second dynamic videos (social/non-social) each presented twice. Data was processed in MATLAB 2020a using the miniMADE pipeline,4 as well as in-house scripts for artefact detection.5 See Figure 1A for a detailed description of the EEG preprocessing steps. To ensure stability of power and connectivity metrics, participants with insufficient data (less than 90 good epochs) for either condition were excluded from subsequent analysis (N = 48, attrition rate: 17%). Frequency and connectivity analysis were conducted using FieldTrip 20251125. Figure 1B provides a comprehensive description of the spectral power and connectivity metrics. Separate metrics were calculated on the basis of each power and connectivity measure by averaging the absolute power or connectivity values for a given selection of channels across a given range of frequencies for each condition (social and nonsocial videos). This provided 540 EEG metrics for the multiverse space.

A distance matrix of the 540 metrics was calculated using Spearman correlation where metrics that are highly positively or negatively correlated across participants have a lower distance while lower positive or negative correlations have a higher distance. Classical multidimensional scaling was applied to the distance matrix, and the 2 dimensions with the highest eigenvalues were used to plot the metrics in a 2D scatterplot, termed the multiverse space. The relationship between metrics was examined based on their relative position in the space. The association between trial number (the number of available epochs for a given condition) and each EEG metric was calculated using Spearman correlation and plotted in the space as validation using a known source of systematic individual variation affecting EEG metrics.

Results

Figure 2 shows the resulting multiverse space where each point corresponds to one EEG metric in the low-dimensional space. The points are labelled according to condition (social or nonsocial video in panel A), topographical region (global, frontal, central, parietal, occipital in panel B), frequency range (delta, theta, alpha, low-beta, high-beta, low-gamma in panel C) or type of measure (log power, relative power, PLV, wPLI, dbwPLI, AEC, oPowCorr, COH, iCOH in panel D). A shows a lack of systematic variation in condition across the multiverse space as social and nonsocial metrics are intermixed throughout the space, suggesting the individual differences in children’s EEG metrics don't depend strongly on condition differences and the condition manipulation doesn't create a distinct "signature" of individual variability. B and C show a dispersed distribution of topographies and frequencies across the space, suggesting metrics capture aspects of individual differences unrelated to regional brain activity, or brainwaves in a particular frequency range. D shows that type of measure has a clustered distribution across the space, suggesting metrics within the same measure share similar patterns of individual differences across conditions, frequency ranges and scalp topographies. This may reflect shared underlying sources of physiological or functional variability captured by these measures which examine different aspects of the EEG signal (e.g. phase or amplitude). E shows a systematic gradient in the association between trial number and EEG metrics across the space, providing validation that the space captures patterns of individual variation in EEG metrics as trial number is known to affect EEG metrics, and highlights measures which are more strongly biased by the number of available epochs. Trial number may also reflect underlying individual differences in attention span or motor activity that are captured in how metrics co-vary across these dimensions, as children who are prone to moving a lot during the EEG session will have more artefacts and fewer good epochs in their EEG data.

Conclusion

By mapping EEG metrics in a low-dimensional space based on inter-individual covariation patterns, this method reveals which analytic decisions produce redundant versus unique information about individual differences. Results showed that condition, topography, and frequency bands did not systematically structure the multiverse space, suggesting these factors don't create distinct signatures of individual variability. In contrast, measure type showed strong clustering, suggesting shared underlying sources of variability across different measures. Trial number showed a systematic gradient across the space, highlighting how data quality affects metric covariation and may reflect individual differences in attention or motor activity. Finally, behavioural measures can be used to explore the sensitivity of EEG metrics to clinical dimensions, and other neuroimaging modalities can be incorporated for multimodal characterisation of individual variability in brain metrics (e.g. combining connectivity measures from EEG and MRI). In summary, the multiverse approach addresses the multiple comparisons problem inherent in testing numerous candidate metrics for neurodevelopmental assessment.
