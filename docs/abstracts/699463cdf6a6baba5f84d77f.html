Introduction

The neural mechanisms linking environmental minerals to affective disorders remain unclear. Using the UK Biobank cohort (n = 389,984), we examined how domestic water minerals relate to mental health. We assessed calcium and magnesium separately and created a weighted quantile sum regression index—water mineral accumulative exposure (WMAEx)—to capture their combined effect. Higher WMAEx was significantly associated with more severe affective symptoms and higher prospective risk of incident affective disorders (HR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.40–1.96).

Neuroimaging analyses identified the underlying neural substrates. Mineral exposure was linked to impaired brain structural resilience, quantified by a brain structural resilience index (BSRI) that reflects gray–white matter coupling. Causal mediation analyses showed that reduced BSRI, especially within visual–emotional circuits, significantly mediated the pathway from mineral exposure to affective disorders. These neuromediating pathways were stronger in females and individuals with lower socioeconomic status.

Together, our findings reveal an environmental–neurobiological axis in which domestic water minerals emerge as a determinant of affective health, and compromised brain structural resilience constitutes a core vulnerability mechanism.

Methods

Individual minerals. We first assessed collinearity among areal Ca, Mg and CaCO₃ using partial Pearson correlations in 389,984 participants, controlling for covariates. Multiple linear regression then tested associations between each of five domestic water minerals and three mental symptoms, adjusting for age, sex, areal SES, the first 40 genetic PCs and the other four minerals. In the discovery sample (n = 308,652), Bonferroni correction across 5 minerals × 3 symptoms yielded Pcorr<3.33×10−3Pcorr<3.33×10−3.Weighted quantile sum regression. We applied WQS regression to estimate mixture effects of minerals on each symptom, using the same covariates. Participants were randomly split into a training set (40%) and validation set (60%). In the training set, WQS with maximum‑likelihood optimization estimated mineral weights. Weights (range 0–1) were obtained from 1000 bootstrap resamples and averaged. Using these weights, we built a Water Mineral Accumulative Exposure index (WMAEx) for each participant in the validation set. Multiple linear regression related WMAEx to each symptom; Bonferroni correction across 3 symptoms gave Pcorr<0.017Pcorr<0.017.

Cox proportional hazards models. Using UK Biobank longitudinal data, Cox models tested whether individual minerals and WMAEx predicted incident affective disorders. Predictors were baseline minerals or WMAEx; covariates were age, sex, areal SES and 40 genetic PCs. Continuous variables were z‑scored; proportional hazards were checked with Schoenfeld residuals. We built three models: (1) basic (covariates only); (2) WMAEx model (basic + WMAEx); (3) full model (basic + WMAEx + individual minerals). We reported hazard ratios (HRs), 95% CIs and C‑index. Bonferroni correction across 3 models yielded Pcorr<0.017Pcorr<0.017. DeLong tests (same threshold) compared C‑indices.Propensity score matching. To reduce bias from covariate imbalance between cases (n = 22,624) and controls (n = 53,928), we performed 1:1 propensity score matching. Propensity scores (probability of being a case) were estimated by logistic regression including age, sex, areal SES and 40 PCs. Matching used greedy nearest‑neighbor without replacement with caliper 0.1. Balance was assessed with absolute standardized mean difference (ASMD < 0.05). Cox models were then repeated in the matched sample.Gray and white matter structure. Multiple linear regression related WMAEx and individual minerals to 435 neuroimaging phenotypes, adjusting for age, sex, areal SES, 40 PCs, imaging site and, where appropriate, TICV, GMV/WMV, MCT and TSA. Bonferroni correction accounted for the number of minerals and phenotypes.Brain structural resilience index (BSRI). For mineral‑associated gray and white matter measures, we derived a residual‑based BSRI using normative modeling in healthy controls at 15.3‑year visit (n = XX). White‑matter phenotypes were regressed on mineral‑related gray‑matter phenotypes, controlling for covariates; residuals defined regional BSRIs, with higher values indicating better structural integrity than expected given mineral exposure. BSRIs were z‑standardized. WMAEx and individual minerals were related to BSRI using multiple linear regression with the same imaging covariates and Bonferroni correction.Causal mediation and stratified analyses. We tested whether neuroimaging phenotypes mediated associations between minerals (WMAEx or individual) and incident affective disorders, adjusting for age, sex, areal SES, 40 PCs, imaging site and imaging covariates. Nonparametric bootstrap (5000 bias‑corrected resamples) estimated indirect effects and 95% CIs; mediation was significant when the CI excluded zero. Average causal mediation effects (ACME) were reported. Moderated mediation analyses evaluated whether mediation differed by sex or areal SES (16th, 50th, 84th percentiles), using the same bootstrap procedure and reporting explained mediation effects (EME) when indirect‑effect CIs did not include zero.

Results

We included 389,984 UK Biobank participants with qualified domestic water mineral, mental symptom, and covariate data. Discovery (n = 308,632) and replication (n = 81,352) samples did not differ in demographics or covariates .Individual minerals and mental symptoms. Five domestic water mineral indices (Ca, Mg, CaCO₃, two hardness measures) were strongly correlated . Multiple linear regression related each mineral to three mental symptom traits, adjusting for age, sex, areal SES, 40 genetic PCs and the other four minerals. In discovery, Ca (β = 0.24, P=3.12×10−126P=3.12×10−126) and Mg (β = 0.07, P=1.23×10−24P=1.23×10−24) were positively associated with depressive symptoms after Bonferroni correction (P<0.05/5. Both associations replicated (Ca: β = 0.23, P=1.94×10−20P=1.94×10−20; Mg: β = 0.11, P=1.53×10−10P=1.53×10−10), indicating that each unit increase in Ca and Mg concentration corresponded to 0.24 and 0.07 SD higher depression scores.WQS regression was used to model joint Ca and Mg effects. Data were split into a training set (n = 155,994) and validation set (n = 233,990). In the training set, constrained maximum‑likelihood with 1000 bootstraps yielded WMAEx weights of 0.54 for Ca and 0.46 for Mg. In the validation set, WMAEx was positively associated with depressive symptoms (β = 0.23, P=1.55×10−249P=1.55×10−249), controlling for age, sex, areal SES, genetic PCs, and individual Ca and Mg, indicating that higher mixture exposure related to more severe symptoms.Prediction of affective disorders. Cox models in 76,552 participants (22,624 incident affective disorders; median follow‑up 15.3 years) compared three models. Adding WMAEx to covariates improved prediction (HR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.15–1.33; C‑index = 0.61). The full model (covariates + WMAEx + Ca + Mg) showed even stronger effects (WMAEx HR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.40–1.96; Ca HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.11–1.19; Mg HR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.06–1.10; C‑index = 0.63). DeLong tests showed both WMAEx and full models outperformed the basic model, and the full model outperformed WMAEx alone. After propensity score matching of cases and controls, associations remained (WMAEx HR = 1.15; full‑model HR = 1.51), underscoring complementary contributions of mixture load and individual Ca/Mg.Neuroimaging associations. In 7,044 participants with imaging, we tested links between WMAEx, Ca, Mg and 435 phenotypes (cortical CV/SCV/CT/CSA; white‑matter FA/MD), adjusting for demographic, genetic and imaging covariates. Twenty‑one associations survived Bonferroni correction, involving two cortical and 18 white‑matter measures. Higher Ca was associated with reduced brainstem gray‑matter volume (β = −0.16, P=1.82×10−8P=1.82×10−8) and with increased FA and reduced MD in the bilateral medial lemniscus, suggesting coupled gray‑matter loss and compensatory white‑matter reorganization.Causal mediation. Mediation analyses (mediation v4.5.0) identified 10 neuroimaging phenotypes—one cortical and six white‑matter tracts—that significantly mediated the effect of mineral exposure on affective‑disorder onset. For example, increased MD in the right external capsule (ACME = 0.0036, 95% CI: 0.0014–0.0062) and reduced brainstem volume (ACME = 0.0014, 95% CI: 0.0001–0.0031) mediated the harmful impact of higher Ca; increased FA in the bilateral medial lemniscus showed protective‑direction mediation.Sex‑ and SES‑stratified mediation. Moderated mediation revealed six pathways were stronger in females; e.g., the external‑capsule MD pathway was significant only in women, and medial‑lemniscus FA and lingual‑gyrus volume mediated a larger proportion of risk in females. When areal SES was used as moderator, mediation effects showed a clear gradient, being strongest in the lowest SES regions and weakest, though still significant, in the highest SES regions. These patterns indicate that women and residents of socioeconomically deprived areas bear disproportionate neurobiological burden from domestic water mineral exposure.

Conclusion

Our large population‑based study demonstrates that domestic water mineral exposure is a robust and clinically meaningful determinant of affective health. Using data from nearly 390,000 UK Biobank participants, we show that higher concentrations of calcium and magnesium in domestic water are consistently associated with more severe depressive symptoms and with an increased prospective risk of affective disorder. These associations are not limited to single minerals: a mixture‑based index of water mineral accumulative exposure (WMAEx) captures the joint burden of Ca and Mg and provides additional predictive value beyond conventional demographic and genetic risk factors. Integrating both WMAEx and individual mineral concentrations yields the best prediction of incident affective disorder, underscoring that it is the combination of overall mineral load and specific mineral components that shapes vulnerability.

By coupling this epidemiologic framework with neuroimaging, we further identify a plausible neurobiological pathway through which domestic water minerals influence mental health. Higher mineral exposure is linked to reduced gray‑matter volume and altered white‑matter microstructure in distributed circuits, particularly within brainstem and medial lemniscus pathways. These changes reflect an imbalance between gray‑matter atrophy and putatively compensatory white‑matter remodeling. Formal mediation analyses confirm that several of these structural alterations significantly transmit the effect of mineral exposure onto affective disorder risk, indicating that impaired brain structural resilience is not merely correlated with, but partially mediates, the environmental risk.

Importantly, the strength of these neuromediational pathways is not uniform across the population. Women and individuals living in socioeconomically deprived areas show disproportionately stronger mineral–brain–affect links. In females, multiple pathways—such as those involving the external capsule, medial lemniscus, and lingual gyrus—mediate a larger share of risk, suggesting heightened sensitivity of female brain structures to mineral‑related neurotoxicity or reduced capacity for adaptive reorganization. In parallel, the same neural pathways exert greater impact in low‑SES neighborhoods than in affluent ones, revealing a socioeconomic gradient whereby environmental and social disadvantages synergize to amplify affective disorder risk.

Together, these findings delineate an environmental–neurobiological axis in which domestic water minerals, long regarded primarily as benign indicators of water hardness, emerge as an under‑recognized risk factor for mood pathology. The work advances current understanding in three ways: it provides rigorous evidence that everyday mineral exposure is prospectively linked to affective disorders; it identifies brain structural resilience—as quantified by gray–white matter coupling—as a core mechanistic substrate; and it highlights sex and socioeconomic context as key modifiers of this pathway.

Our results have direct implications for mental‑health prevention and environmental policy. Monitoring and managing mineral levels in domestic water may represent a feasible lever to reduce population burden of affective disorders, particularly in high‑risk communities. At the same time, the concept of brain structural resilience offers a quantitative target for mechanistic studies and potential interventions aimed at strengthening neural reserve against environmental insults. Future work should test causal effects in other cohorts and settings, investigate underlying cellular and molecular processes, and evaluate whether modifying water composition or enhancing brain resilience can mitigate affective disorder risk.
