Introduction

Language switching requires cognitive control, neurally known for its engagement in prefrontal brain activation. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) provides a non-invasive measure of changes in cortical hemodynamic responses during cognitive tasks such as language switching. While language switching has been widely examined, it is largely unknown whether brain signals during language switching can predict second language (L2) proficiency in the Korean(L1)-English(L2) bilingual context. In this study, we investigated, for native Korean speakers, whether oxy- and deoxy- hemodynamic changes of prefrontal regions during language-switching task could be used to predict TOEIC scores, a test that measures the level of English proficiency.

Methods

Thirty-six healthy college students, all native Korean speakers learning English (7 males, 22.03±2.06 years old) were recruited. All participants had taken the TOEIC test within 2 years.

They completed two cognitive tasks designed to probe cognitive control and language-switching mechanisms: the AX Continuous Performance Task (AX-CPT) and the Bilingual Category-Matching Task (BCMT). In the AX-CPT, participants responded to specific target sequences of alphabet cues and probes (e.g., “A” followed by “X”). The task included congruent (AX) and incongruent (AY, BX, BY) trials, allowing for the dissociation of proactive and reactive control demands. In the BCMT, participants judged whether two consecutively presented words, in either L1 or L2 belonged to the same semantic category. This task independently manipulates language switching (switch vs. non-switch) and category matching (match vs. non-match), enabling the dissociation of language-switching costs from semantic interference effects.

For both tasks, accuracy, RT and response duration were recorded as behavioral measures. Simultaneously, physiological measures were collected: pupil diameter was tracked using eye-tracker, and prefrontal cortical activation was monitored using 15-channel fNIRS (OBELAB, South Korea) to capture hemodynamic responses.

A two-way ANOVA was performed to examine the effects of LANGUAGE and CATEGORY on accuracy and reaction time. Subsequently, fNIRS signals were preprocessed for motion artifacts and physiological noise. The beta values were extracted from preprocessed signals by a general linear model. Specifically, z-scored beta values were selected based on variable importance in projection (VIP) scores from a partial least squares regression model. These values were then used as input for a gradient boosting regression model (GBR) to predict TOEIC scores.

Results

Regarding RT in BCMT, there were significant main effects of CATEGORY (F(1,35)=130.679, p<.0001, η2 = 0.0867) and LANGUAGE (F(1,35)=13.757, p=.007, η2 = 0.0031). Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction revealed that the RTs were significantly faster for congruent conditions compared to incongruent trials (p<.0001), and for non-switch compared to switch trials (p=.0007). Additionally, non-switch trials were faster than switch trials within both congruent (p=.0388) and incongruent trials (p=.0004). For accuracy, there was significant main effect only for CATEGORY (F(1, 35)=102.895, p<.0001, η2=0.436), with lower accuracy in incongruent to congruent (p<.0001).

Using fNIRS features extracted from both BCMT and AX-CPT tasks, we predicted TOEIC scores employing GBR. The BCMT model showed high predictive performances for TOEIC (R2 = 0.6382, rmse=62.0093), reading comprehension (RC; R2 = 0.4569, rmse=42.2890), and listening comprehension (LC; R2 = 0.2087, rmse=39.9017). Similarly, the AX-CPT model achieved strong predictions for TOEIC (R2 = 0.2931, rmse=86.6792), and LC (R2 = 0.3256, rmse=36.8363), but showed poor performance for RC (R2 = -0.1180, rmse=70.7190).

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that both behavioral and neural responses reflect the dynamic interaction between cognitive control and L2 proficiency. Behaviorally, both incongruency and language switching facilitated faster responses, indicating enhanced cognitive efficiency through adaptive control mechanisms. At the neural level, prefrontal activation patterns differentiated between control- and language-related demands. fNIRS-based LSTM models revealed that neural signals from the language-switching task were particularly predictive of RC, which implies our task is based on visual and lexical processing components, whereas signals from cognitive control task better predicted overall TOEIC and LC scores, reflecting the domain-general attentional control engaged in auditory language processing. These results indicate that the task-specific neural dynamics reflect different aspects of second-language proficiency. Furthermore, prefrontal neural activity can serve as a reliable biomarker for assessing L2 ability and the efficiency of cognitive control during language processing.
