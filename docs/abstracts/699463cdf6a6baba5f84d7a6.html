Introduction

Adolescence is a key period of brain maturation, marked by dynamic neuroimmune and hormonal changes that may heighten vulnerability to external factors, including viral exposures. During this period, the immune system plays a major role in the refinement of neural circuits,1 potentially increasing sensitivity  to infectious exposures that can directly or indirectly influence the central nervous system. Common herpesviruses such as cytomegalovirus (CMV) and herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1) establish lifelong latency with periodic reactivations. Herpesvirus exposure has been linked to structural brain aberrations in adults with severe mental illness,2 but less is known about virus exposure and adolescent brain structure. In the present study we examined whether seropositivity for CMV, HSV-1, or cumulative viral exposure (seropositivity for 0, 1, or 2 viruses) is associated with cortical and subcortical brain structure in adolescents, and whether any observed effects differ between youth with early-onset psychosis (EOP) and healthy controls.

Methods

Our sample consisted of 34 clinically recruited adolescent patients with EOP (79% female) and 61 healthy adolescent controls (54% female), ages 12–19, recruited from the Youth-TOP study cohort in Oslo, Norway. CMV and HSV-1 IgG serostatus were determined using solid-phase immunoassays.3 In the total sample, 51.6% were seronegative for both CMV and HSV-1, 38.9% were seropositive for one of the viruses (27.3% for CMV, 11.6% for HSV-1), while 9.5% were seropositive for both CMV and HSV-1. T1-weighted MRI data was acquired from two 3T GE scanners and processed using FreeSurfer 7.4.1 for cortical metrics and SAMSEG4 for subcortical volumes. Cortical surface area, cortical thickness, and volumes of the cerebellum, hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, basal ganglia, and lateral ventricles were extracted, and harmonized with ComBat5 to adjust for scanner-related variation. Separate general linear models tested associations between (1) CMV serostatus, (2) HSV-1 serostatus, and (3) a cumulative viral-load index (from 0 to 2, computed for each participant based on the number of seropositive results) and each brain metric. Age, diagnostic group, sex, and intracranial volume were included as covariates, and a 5% false-discovery-rate correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied for each viral predictor. Sensitivity analyses re-estimated models after removing high-influence observations identified via Cook’s distance and DFBETAs.

Results

Model-fit comparisons determined whether Group × Virus interactions improved explanatory value; as none did, final inferences were based on main-effect models. Cumulative viral exposure was significantly associated with smaller total cerebellar volume (F = 6.011; p = 0.004; pFDR = 0.029; η²ₚ = 0.120). Pairwise comparisons indicated that individuals seropositive for both viruses displayed smaller cerebellar volumes than those seronegative for both viruses (p = 0.001; estimate = −9.061; SE = 2.620), and those seropositive for only one virus (p = 0.007; estimate = −7.362; SE = 2.683), whereas the difference between the single-virus and seronegative groups was not significant (p = 0.272; estimate = −1.699; SE = 1.537). The association between viral load and the cerebellum remained significant in sensitivity analyses (p = 0.001). No other brain metrics showed significant associations.

Conclusion

Cumulative exposure to common herpesviruses was associated with smaller cerebellar volume in adolescence, independent of diagnostic status, suggesting that viral burden may intersect with neurodevelopmental and immune processes during this period. Although the underlying mechanisms cannot be determined from the present data, it is plausible that the cerebellum could be particularly susceptible to neurotropic pathogens. While based on a modest sample, these results highlight the need for longitudinal studies to determine whether viral exposures are linked to developmental brain trajectories or might interact with emerging psychiatric vulnerability over time.
