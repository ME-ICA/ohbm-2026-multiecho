Introduction

Social anxiety disorder (SAD) is characterized by excessive fear and avoidance of social situations, often accompanied by cognitive impairments such as deficits in emotion recognition, attention, and executive functioning1. Although previous neuroimaging studies have identified abnormal activity in the prefrontal cortex, amygdala and so on2, the relationship between frequency-dependent intrinsic brain activity and cognitive function in SAD remains unclear. The fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF), a reliable resting-state fMRI measure, reflects spontaneous neural activity3; however, most studies have analyzed only the typical 0.01–0.08 Hz frequency band without considering frequency specificity. Emerging evidence suggests that slow-5 (0.01–0.027 Hz) and slow-4 (0.027–0.073 Hz) oscillations represent distinct neurophysiological processes4. The present study aimed to investigate frequency-specific alterations in fALFF and their associations with cognitive performance in SAD by combining resting-state fMRI with the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB)5, to identify neural markers underlying cognitive dysfunction.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were acquired from 27 patients with SAD (diagnosed according to DSM-5/DSM-5-TR) and 40 healthy controls (HCs) using a 3.0 T MRI scanner (Discovery MR750, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA) equipped with a 32-channel phased-array head coil (TR = 2500 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel size = 3.3 mm³).

Participants in both groups met the following inclusion criteria: (1) no history of brain trauma, (2) not pregnant, (3) aged 18–50 years, and (4) right-handed. Additionally, HCs were required to (1) be free of any medications, (2) have no coexisting medical conditions, and (3) score below the clinical cutoff on standardized psychological assessments, confirming the absence of psychiatric symptoms.

Preprocessing was performed using the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF 5.4; https://rfmri.org/DPARSF), including removal of the first 10 volumes, slice-timing correction, head-motion correction, normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Voxel-wise mean fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (mfALFF) maps were computed for three frequency bands: typical (0.01–0.08 Hz), slow-5 (0.01–0.027 Hz), and slow-4 (0.027–0.073 Hz). Second-level statistical analyses were conducted in SPM12, with age, sex, and medication status included as covariates (voxel-wise p < 0.001, cluster-level p < 0.05, FWE-corrected).

Cognitive performance was assessed using four subtests from the CANTAB: the Emotion Recognition Task (ERT), which evaluates emotion recognition and social cognition; the One-Touch Stockings of Cambridge (OTS), which assesses spatial planning and working memory; the Paired Associates Learning (PAL) task, which measures visual memory and learning; and the Rapid Visual Information Processing (RVP) task, which evaluates sustained attention (Figure 1).

Clinical assessments included the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale (LSAS), which assesses fear and avoidance in social situations; the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), which measures both state and trait anxiety; and the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), which evaluates depressive symptoms. These measures were used to account for overlapping anxiety and mood symptoms in the interpretation of neural and cognitive findings.

Correlations between mfALFF values, clinical scale scores (LSAS, STAI, BDI-II), and CANTAB outcomes were examined using Pearson’s or Kendall’s tau-b coefficients, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied for multiple comparisons.

Results

Patients with SAD and HCs were comparable in age and sex distribution. However, the SAD group showed substantially higher scores on the LSAS, STAI, and BDI-II, indicating elevated social anxiety, general anxiety, and depressive symptoms. Neuropsychological assessments revealed no significant group differences across most CANTAB tasks; however, patients with SAD demonstrated significantly lower performance on two outcomes of the RVP task, "Probability of Hit” and “Sensitivity (A prime)”, indicating reduced sustained attention compared with HCs.

Compared with HCs, patients with SAD exhibited significantly lower mfALFF in the bilateral postcentral gyrus (PostCG) across the typical and slow-5 bands, and lower mfALFF in the left PostCG in the slow-4 band (Figure 2a). No regions showed higher mfALFF in SAD relative to HCs. These findings indicate frequency-dependent hypoactivity in the somatosensory cortex.

Although mfALFF values were not significantly associated with clinical symptom severity, the mfALFF value in the left PostCG of patients with SAD was significantly correlated with the “median latency to first choice” on the OTS task (r = –0.649, p-FDR < 0.05) in the slow-5 band (Figure 2b). This suggests that reduced frequency-dependent activity in the sensorimotor cortex is associated with longer planning latency and decreased spatial-planning efficiency in SAD.

This discrepancy further indicates that abnormal PostCG activity may not directly reflect symptom severity. Instead, it may be more specifically linked to distinct cognitive processes, such as spatial planning and action selection, or may reflect a stable, trait-like neural marker rather than a state marker of the disorder.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between frequency-dependent alterations in mfALFF and cognitive function in patients with SAD. Our findings demonstrate that frequency-dependent abnormal mfALFF values in the PostCG may play an important role in the neural mechanisms of SAD, particularly in sensorimotor processing, emotion recognition, and executive function. Importantly, our findings suggest that cognitive deficits in patients with SAD are strongly associated with abnormal spontaneous brain activity in specific frequency bands, highlighting the potential of frequency-based biomarkers in identifying and treating cognitive dysfunction in SAD. From a clinical perspective, the findings of this study suggest that interventions targeting sensorimotor processing, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy, may be beneficial in improving symptoms of SAD. In addition, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation may be an effective therapeutic strategy for modulating PostCG activity. Future studies should explore the role of frequency-dependent alterations in the diagnosis of SAD and examine the interactions between these neurophysiological abnormalities and treatment outcomes.
