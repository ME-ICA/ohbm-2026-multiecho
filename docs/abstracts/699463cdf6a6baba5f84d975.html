Introduction

Machine learning models using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) are becoming popular - these models often rely on training data from multiple, large, and publicly available

datasets. It is necessary to harmonize these data across sites and sequences, and algorithms like

ComBat¹,² are applied to correct for these differences. This has been shown to improve model

performance and generalizability⁴. However, traditional ComBat necessitates harmonizing

all data (train, validation, test, and unseen external test sets) simultaneously, which leads to

potential data leakage and limits deployment to unseen data. We introduce Consistent Reference

External Batch (CREB) harmonization, a novel extension of ComBat that learns the prior distribution

of site effects exclusively from a designated training set. This learned prior serves as a consistent,

easily deployable reference point that employs the empirical Bayes framework to update the site

effect for any new, external unseen data. This approach enables training, validation, and test sets

to be harmonized separately, preventing data leakage, and application to unseen data. We tested CREB with train data from 2846 participants

(ages 18-97 years) across 9 studies and test data from 1113 participants (ages 18-88)

from 3 studies. We evaluated harmonization using functional connectivity and

gray matter volume. We show that CREB can harmonize the test data to the train data and

perform comparably to ComBat. CREB conducts this harmonization in a two-step

procedure that prevents leakage and is deployable to new unseen data. Finally, we tested whether

CREB similarly preserves biological variance. We found that CREB, like ComBat could preserve age associations with both

functional connectivity and gray matter volume measures. CREB provides an easily deployable,

robust harmonization method to standardize data to a common reference distribution, making it

uniquely suitable for training generalizable machine learning models.

Methods

Methods. Data: We downloaded public datasets with T1-weighted MRI and resting-state fMRI and retained healthy controls. Data were converted to BIDS and split into 9 training studies (n=2,846; ages 18–97) and 3 external test studies (n=1,113; ages 18–88).

Preprocessing/feature extraction: MRI preprocessing used Singularity images of fMRIPrep v23.2.3 and XCP-D v0.10.5; outputs were in MNI152NLin2009cAsym. FreeSurfer processing was disabled; fieldmap-less susceptibility distortion correction was used when fieldmaps were unavailable; and skull stripping was skipped when already provided. Functional preprocessing included motion correction (mcflirt), slice-timing correction only when metadata were present, distortion correction, and registration. Framewise displacement (FD) was computed and frames with FD>0.5 mm were flagged as outliers. Nuisance regression used aCompCor WM/CSF components after high-pass filtering at 0.008 Hz (retaining components explaining 50% variance) plus expanded motion regressors (derivatives + quadratic terms).

XCP-D postprocessing applied motion censoring at FD>0.5 mm (censored volumes regressed), required ≥240 s post-scrub data, used 8 mm FWHM smoothing, and band-pass filtering 0–0.15 Hz (order=2). Time series were extracted from the 4S456 atlas (456 parcels), and functional connectivity (FC) was computed as the 456×456 Pearson correlation matrix and vectorized (upper triangle).

Structural feature: Total gray-matter (GM) volume was computed from the fMRIPrep GM probability segmentation in native space using a 0.5 threshold; for longitudinal datasets only the first session was processed; no augmentation was applied to GM.

Quality control/augmentation/imputation: Runs were excluded if <3 min remained after censoring, >20% of volumes were censored, or any 1-min window had >50% censored volumes; dataset-provided QC flags (e.g., ghosting) were also applied. FC was augmented by generating 4 additional connectivity matrices per run from random contiguous chunks (≥3 min). Missing FC entries were imputed by sampling from 3,000 synthetic matrices generated from the training-set FC mean/variance.

Harmonization & statistical analysis: NeuroHarmonize/NeuroComBat³ was run by jointly harmonizing all training and testing studies while preserving age and sex. CREB uses a two-stage Learn/Apply workflow.

CREB-Learn (train only): For each feature, we fit OLS with X=[1, age, sex], compute standardized residuals using pooled variance, and store per-site sufficient statistics (mean, variance, n) plus regression coefficients and pooled variance in a portable “bundle”. A single empirical-Bayes prior over additive and scaling site effects is then estimated across all training sites and features (joint Normal–Inverse-Gamma or iterative Normal/Inverse-Gamma).

CREB-Apply (new site): Using the bundle, new-site data are residualized/standardized, posteriors are updated from the stored priors, site effects are removed (subtract estimated site mean; divide by estimated site SD), and data are reconstructed by restoring pooled variance and the biological signal. Closed-form updates were used for connectivity and iterative updates for gray-matter volume.

Evaluation: Connectivity site effects were assessed using DMN within-network connectivity, the mPFC–PCC edge, and global mean connectivity; per-edge Euclidean distance and MAE were computed for (NeuroHarmonize, CREB) and (NeuroHarmonize, Raw). Additive/scaling effects were tested with one-way ANOVA and Bartlett’s sphericity on filtered (non-augmented) test sets (one run/participant), with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR for edge-wise tests. Biological preservation was assessed with age regressions (edge-wise Pearson’s r in CamCAN; GM volume r²)

Results

Results: CREB produced harmonized FC outputs similar to NeuroHarmonize. Box-plots for mPFC–PCC connectivity and mean DMN connectivity show both methods harmonize between sites in training and external test sets, with CREB applied via a two-step learn/apply procedure that prevents leakage. Across all FC edges, the average Euclidean distance between NeuroHarmonize and CREB was 2.6 and the average MAE was 0.019 (max ≈0.08), indicating near-identical outputs.

Site separability: On external test data, raw FC showed significant site effects for mPFC–PCC, mean DMN, and global mean connectivity (F(2,813)=26.3/45.5/47.2; all p<0.001). After harmonization, neither NeuroHarmonize nor CREB showed site differences on these summaries (NH p=0.965/0.472/0.571; CREB p=0.431/0.321/0.283). Edge-wise ANOVA with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR found 89,643 of ~103k edges differed by site in raw data, versus 3 edges after NeuroHarmonize and 0 after CREB; scaling effects (Bartlett’s sphericity test + FDR) dropped from 40,566 edges (raw) to 11 (NeuroHarmonize) and 0 (CREB).

Biological preservation: In CamCAN, the strongest age-associated FC edges had raw r²=0.18 and 0.17; after NeuroHarmonize r²=0.18 and 0.16; and after CREB r²=0.18 and 0.13. Across all edges, the distribution of Pearson’s r values was highly preserved (no change in association from raw to post-harmonization). For total gray-matter volume, CREB and NeuroHarmonize harmonized similarly across sites; age–GM regression r² was 0.38 (raw), 0.45 (NeuroHarmonize), and 0.41 (CREB). GM site effects were reduced for both methods vs raw (CREB F(3,811)=7.1; NeuroHarmonize F(3,811)=10.4; raw F(3,811)=109.5; Bartlett χ²=26.2/29.0/35.0; all p<0.001). Overall, CREB matched NeuroHarmonize on similarity and site-effect removal while using a two-stage learn/apply workflow to avoid leakage.

Additional result details: Box-plots of mPFC–PCC connectivity and mean DMN connectivity in both training and external test sets show NeuroHarmonize and CREB perform similarly, each harmonizing toward its own mean while reducing between-site dispersion. Methodologically, NeuroHarmonize harmonizes train+test jointly, whereas CREB learns priors from training and applies them to each external dataset in a two-stage learn/apply workflow that prevents leakage. For context on effect magnitude, Euclidean distance and MAE were computed across all edges for both (Raw, NeuroHarmonize) and (NeuroHarmonize, CREB). 

For biological preservation in CamCAN, the most age-associated edges were Left Hemisphere Visual Network 6 × Left Hemisphere Visual Network 19 and Left Hemisphere SomatoMotor Network 5 × Left Hemisphere SomatoMotor 35 (raw r²=0.18 and 0.17). These associations were largely preserved after NeuroHarmonize (r²=0.18 and 0.16) and after CREB (r²=0.18 and 0.13). Edge-wise distributions of Pearson’s r across all connections showed minimal change from raw to either harmonized output. 

For total gray-matter volume, box-plots on training and test data showed CREB and NeuroHarmonize harmonize comparably; structural testing included 4 external sites because both sites from the Aging dataset were used. Age–GM linear fits remained similar across methods (raw r²=0.38; NeuroHarmonize r²=0.45; CREB r²=0.41), supporting preservation of biological variance and demonstrating CREB generalizes beyond FC to structural features.

Conclusion

We developed a new, two-stage harmonization method that enables harmonization of new, previously unseen sites to

a single reference point established from training data. This reference point can be easily shared within a machine

learning algorithm without sharing all of the training data - delivered as a ’bundle.’ We found that CREB was able to

harmonize unseen test data comparably to NeuroHarmonize, which had access to both train and test data. This approach

avoids data leakage between train and test sets, but allows for harmonization of unseen test data. We showed that

there were no site differences after harmonization with NeuroHarmonize or CREB, though NeuroHarmonize exhibited

small site-specific differences on a few edges. Finally, we found that associations with biological signals (in this case,

age) were preserved after harmonization with either approach. Thus, CREB is as effective as NeuroHarmonize at

removing site effects in functional connectivity and gray matter volume data. CREB’s two-stage procedure allows for

harmonization on the training data, which can then be applied to test data even those not available to the researchers.

In addition, this can be deployed in a lightweight model as the "bundle" statistics take up 12.51MB. This approach

addresses the issue of train-test data leakage during harmonizing.

Increased access to imaging due to data sharing and big data has made it possible to train machine learning models on

vast datasets. One major challenge is that imaging data is highly impacted by ’site’ effects including scanners, sequences,

and field strengths, etc. There is compelling evidence that harmonizing data prior to training models improves their

performance⁴. One major challenge here is that harmonizing train and test data jointly can lead to

data leakage⁵ and potentially inflate performance of these models. Thus, there is a need to

conduct this harmonization without leakage. We have shown that CREB is capable of conducting harmonization in a

two-step procedure that harmonizes train and test sets independently. This can be deployed in machine learning models

as a small ’bundle’ (<13MB) to easily harmonize unseen test data.

Despite its strengths, our analysis has several limitations. During preprocessing, the DLBS (TR=2s), Glia (TR=3s),

Placebo (TR=2.5s), and HCP-YA (TR=0.72s) datasets did not have slice time in their JSON side card. Given that

HCP-YA’s TR is very short, we can probably safely preprocess it with fmriprep without doing slice timing correction,

but for the other 3 dataset, doing fmriprep without slicetiming correction may be problematic. Of note, both Placebo and

Glia dataset authors sent clarifications, but they arrived after we completed preprocessing all the data. One limitation of

CREB is that a new bundle is needed for each fmriprep, xcpd, atlas choice and biological co-variate included. This

is true of other harmonization approaches as well. While producing a new bundle for each preprocessing pipeline is

expected, requiring new bundles for every user-defined combination of biological covariates is still a major limitation of

most harmonization methods. To further validate CREB’s performance in removing nuisance variance, future work

could use traveling participants data to confirm that these methods maintain consistent subject-specific features despite

variations in scanning parameters across sites.

Harmonization is critical for analyzing imaging data and for deploying generalizable machine learning models. CREB

employs similar methods to ComBat to conduct data harmonization in a two-step procedure that prevents data leakage

and is easy to deploy within machine learning models. CREB performed similarly to NeuroHarmonize both in terms of

removing site specific effects but also in preserving biological variance. CREB is publicly available and can be easily

integrated into machine learning workflows.
