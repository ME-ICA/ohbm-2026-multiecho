Introduction

Sickle Cell Disease (SCD) is a genetic blood disorder with several complications due to its β-mutation and affecting over 20 million people worldwide. In SCD, abnormal hemoglobin causes red blood cells to become rigid and sickle-shaped, resulting in chronic anemia, pain episodes, and oxygen deprivation in tissues, including the brain.1 The restricted blood flow leads to cerebrovascular dysfunction, potentially causing deterioration in gray matter volume and functionality. Over time, frequent pain episodes can develop into chronic pain (pain that lasts for more than 3 months), significantly impacting patients' health and quality of life.2 Additionally, this frequent pain may lead to central sensitization (CS), an enhancement of pain sensations resulting in severe chronic pain perception and alter brain activity and connectivity. The complex pathophysiology of SCD pain presents a major public health challenge, contributing to high healthcare use yet it remains difficult to assess clinically due to the variation of pain experiences. While CS can induce neuroplastic changes and altered activation in brain regions involved in pain perception due to heightened sensitivity, quantitative sensory testing (QST) can activate nociceptive pathways through controlled thermal stimuli application, while remaining a safe and controlled method for assessing pain sensitivity.3 Changes in gray matter alterations and neural pathways underlying CS have been unexplored in SCD and may help characterize pain phenotypes in SCD and provide deeper insights into mechanisms underlying pain variability. Thus, we hypothesize that reductions in gray matter (GM) volumes, along with altered brain activation and connectivity in key pain-processing areas, will be associated with distinct pain phenotypes and will differentiate high from low CS groups in patients with SCD. 

Methods

We recruited eleven adults (ten African Americans and one Asian), including five healthy pain-free controls and six with steady-state SCD (3 HbSS, 2 HbSC, 1 HbSβ). Mean ages were 28 ± 8.45 years for controls and 34.67 ± 14.43 years for SCD participants. MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner at the CMU-Pitt BRIDGE Center (RRlD:SCR_023356), and pain levels were measured using the Brief Pain Inventory (least/worst pain = 3 ± 2.8/7 ± 1.4). Adults with SCD were classified as low or high central sensitization (CS) based on QST z-score profiles, following Campbell et al.4 where positive z-scores indicate high CS (greater pain sensitivity) and negative z-scores indicate low CS. T1-weighted structural images (TR/TE = 3.15/1.37 ms) and functional MRI data (TR/TE = 1031/30 ms) were collected. QST was performed on the nondominant forearm using a thermal probe to assess cold/warm detection thresholds and cold/hot pain thresholds (five stimuli per condition) with safety limits to prevent injury (0–50.1 °C).

Structural images were preprocessed using CAT12/SPM12, segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid, normalized to MNI space, and smoothed with a 3 mm Gaussian kernel. Total intracranial volume was included to control for brain size, and GM differences were assessed using two-sample t-tests. Functional MRI data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep. General Linear Models in Nilearn modeled cold/warm detection and pain conditions, including confound regressors and high-pass filtering to remove low-frequency drift. Individual contrasts were entered into group-level analyses using a 20-voxel cluster threshold (p < 0.05). Brain regions showing significant activation were subsequently used as seeds for functional connectivity analyses across QST conditions (p < 0.05, FWE corrected) (Fig. 1). In addition to brain regions identified through GLM contrasts, we performed an exploratory analysis using the insula as a seed of interest to investigate its connectivity because of its established role in pain signaling.

Results

Mean values differed between controls and SCD across all QST measures. High CS participants showed significantly smaller thermal changes from baseline (32 °C) during cold pain, with mean changes of -12.39 ± 3.54 °C compared to controls (-27.48 ± 1.44 °C, p < 0.05). During hot pain, the high CS group also exhibited lower pain thresholds (8.44 ± 1.82 °C) relative to controls (15.10 ± 2.19 °C, p < 0.01) and compared to low CS participants (14.94 ± 2.81 °C vs. 8.44 ± 1.82 °C, p < 0.05). For warm detection, low CS participants (11.26 ± 5.40 °C) differed significantly from controls (2.63 ± 0.79 °C, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). 

Structural analyses revealed higher gray matter volumes in the low CS group in the left precentral, middle frontal, and superior frontal gyri (p < 0.05 FWE) (Fig. 2B-D). High CS participants showed reduced brain activation relative to the low CS group during cold pain in the left cerebellum (VIIIa) and left putamen (p<0.05) (Fig. 2E-F), and during hot pain in the right cerebellum (VIIIb) and precuneus cortex (p<0.05) (Fig. 2G-H).

Functional connectivity revealed group differences in interactions between key brain regions. During hot pain, high CS participants exhibited decreased connectivity between the right cerebellum and right precentral gyrus (p=0.008), alongside increased connectivity between the right cerebellum and right frontal pole (p<0.001) (Fig. 2J). Exploratory analyses of the left insula revealed that high CS participants had decreased connectivity with the middle temporal gyrus (p<0.001) during cold pain (Fig. 2I) and increased connectivity with the right paracingulate/left frontal pole (p=0.02) during hot pain (Fig. 2K-L). 

Conclusion

Our study is the first, to our knowledge, to characterize pain phenotypes in SCD using a combined approach of QST and MRI. Behaviorally, the high CS SCD group demonstrated heightened sensitivity across both detection and pain thresholds, suggesting an overall amplified pain response consistent with the literature.4 GM differences highlighted how cognitive control (left middle frontal and superior frontal gyri)  and sensorimotor integration (left precentral gyrus) differ between the two SCD groups, potentially explaining differences in pain processing. 

The reduced cerebellar activity in the high CS group may reflect the diminished engagement of endogenous analgesic circuits that normally protect against heightened pain processing, resulting in enhanced pain perception. The increased right cerebellum – right frontal pole connectivity suggests continued interaction with prefrontal regions supporting executive function and the top-down modulation of pain.5 Cerebellar activity, mediating pain modulation and cognition, is a promising target for identifying distinct SCD pain phenotypes. High CS insula connectivity patterns indicate disrupted pain sensory integration and compensatory mechanisms to manage heightened pain. Future work will use EEG-informed fMRI to test whether these brain activation and connectivity changes reflect real-time neural signatures of heightened CS in SCD.
