Introduction

Dense sampling designs yield sufficient data to replicate and extend established experimental paradigms at the level of individual participants. The recently released CNeuroMod-THINGS dataset^1 follows this framework, and forms a bridge between two massive initiatives: the Courtois-NeuroMod Project, which has acquired the largest dense sampling dataset collected to date—with almost 200 hours of fMRI acquisitions for each of its six participants^2—and the THINGS initiative^3, a large-scale effort to harmonize the neuroscience of object recognition by creating “THINGS” stimulus images and showing them across species and neuroimaging modalities. For CNeuroMod-THINGS, four CNeuroMod participants underwent fMRI while performing a continuous image recognition task based on the THINGS image set. Of note, previous fMRI datasets using THINGS^3 did not include repetitions of individual images, limiting research on the representation of individual stimulus properties by requiring the grouping of multiple images under semantic labels of image categories; e.g., collapsing across all images with the same category label of “acorn.” 

Here, we capitalize on the design of CNeuroMod-THINGS to evaluate deep-learning-based visual encoding models at the single image-level within participants, enabling a richer exploration of the stimulus space. We note that, unlike several other large-scale visual neuroscience datasets, all images in CNeuroMod-THINGS were shared across participants, further allowing us to examine individual variability in encoding maps across this intensively-sampled cohort.

Methods

CNeuroMod-THINGS includes a gender-balanced sample of four healthy right-handed participants (aged  39  to  49) with normal or corrected- to-normal visual acuity. For each participant, approximately 4,000 images from the THINGS database^3 were presented 3 times (i.e., for more than 12,000 images presentations), resulting in over 18 hours of fMRI. Unlike other large-scale visual neuroscience datasets, there was no smaller, designated pool of shared images ; instead, all images were presented across participants. To ensure sustained attention throughout scanning, images were presented in the context of an image-recognition task, with each image repeated once within and once across scanning sessions. Previous work^2 confirmed that all participants engaged in the task, with behavioral response rates greater than 90%. All images without a behavioral response were removed from analysis, and any image for which this quality check resulted in less than three repetitions was discarded, resulting in at least 3800 images per participant.

This provides sufficient high-quality data to fit data-hungry models at an individual participant-level such as deep-learning-based visual encoding models. To train these models, we first generated latent CLIP network embeddings of each visual stimulus, resulting in a 1024 dimensional vector for every image. CLIP was chosen as the network embedding due to its established relationship with human similarity judgements^4. 

We used GLMSingle^5 to estimate the trial-specific beta map for each image presentation; these beta maps were then averaged to create a single beta map per visual stimulus, increasing signal-to-noise for each image representation. Using the Python library Himalaya (gallantlab.org/himalaya), we then fit regularized ridge regression models using a five-fold cross-validation scheme, ensuring that low-level semantic information (i.e., the 720 category labeling, described in^3) did not leak between train and test folds.

To compare our analyses to previous work done at the category level^3, we performed an additional decoding analysis. Specifically, we used a linear support-vector classifier implemented in Scikit-Learn to predict 720 category labels (e.g., “acorn”) directly from the 1024 dimensional CLIP embedding vector for each image. 

All code implementing these analyses is publicly available at: https://github.com/emdupre/encoding-templates.

Results

Individual encoding maps for all four participants are presented in Figure 1, projected to their respective flattened cortical surface. For all participants who completed the visual localizer scans described in^2, we overlay hand-drawn visual regions-of-interest to facilitate interpretation.

We note that, for all participants, the highest R2 values (> 0.20) are found in the visual cortex, indicating that these regions are best predicted by the CLIP network embeddings of each image. However, which visual regions are best predicted show significant variability across participants; for example, sub-01 has concentrated R2 values in the Extrastriate Body Area (EBA) and the Occipital Face Area (OFA), while sub-03 has a more distributed pattern of high R2 values. We also note significant lateralization effects for sub-01 (highest R2 values in the left hemisphere) and sub-02 (highest R2 values in the right hemisphere). 

The results of our decoding analysis confirmed that category information is highly predicted by CLIP embeddings, with 97% accuracy.  

Conclusion

The design of CNeuroMod-THINGS allows us to evaluate deep-learning-based visual encoding models for semantically relevant visual stimuli from the THINGS image database. Leveraging the dense-sampling design of CNeuroMod-THINGS, we were able to train these individual models at the single image level, highlighting the potential of AI models to examine brain responses to specific visual objects in their full complexity, rather than to somewhat reductive higher-level semantic labels, which has been the focus of previous work. Our decoding analyses also confirm that the AI model embeddings we used contained sufficient detail to recreate individual semantic categories, suggesting that our results build on—rather than oppose—previous work with semantic labels.

One direction unexplored in the current work is the significant variability seen across individual encoding maps. This variability underscores how dense-sampling datasets such as Courtois-NeuroMod make it possible to investigate high-precision, individualized estimates of neural representation for established brain imaging paradigms. We believe that the development of methods that reconcile these sources of variability will be an important direction for future research.
