Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a valuable non-invasive brain stimulation technique used for brain research. One of the TMS modalities is paired-pulse TMS, in which pulses are delivered in pairs, with different intensities and controlled temporal differences. Depending on the time interval used between pulses (called the interstimulus interval (ISI)), motor inhibition or facilitation is observed, given the variation in the amplitude of the motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) obtained. TMS allows the study of cognitive-motor functions of the brain, such as the statistical learning of stochastic sequences, a topic that has been studied by the CEPID-NeuroMat group. In the context tree approach, a context is defined as the shortest sequence of past stimuli that governs the probabilistic selection of the next stimulus.The context tree model is used as a generator of stimulus sequences, and it is observed whether brain responses can be described from this paradigm with probabilistic sequences. The proposal to study learning through this method has already been carried out with auditory signals¹ and cognitive signals based on a decision-making game, called the Goalkeeper Game². The proposed objective is to investigate the brain mechanisms associated with statistical learning through interacting directly with motor circuits through different paired-pulse TMS protocols.

Methods

The TMS system used was the MagVenture X100 + MagOption, the InVesalius Navigator software for neuronavigation, and the Elfin E5 collaborative robotic arm for positioning the stimulators. The average anatomical T1 image MNI152 developed by the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) as part of the International Consortium for Brain Mapping (ICBM) project was used in this experiment for neuronavigation, since the main concern was to maintain the same target throughout the experiment rather than create a specific cortical map. Pulse application control was performed using the MagicPy library. The electromyography (EMG) system used was the Bittium NeurOne Tesla EEG System with sampling rate of 5k Hz.The resting motor threshold (rMT) was determined by the minimum intensity capable of evoking a response of 50μV peak to peak in at least 3 out 6 pulses. 

Two sequences were created: one following stochastic method of the context tree sequence generation, shown in the figure, and the other with random sequence organization. Three pulse configurations were used, represented as 0, 1 and 2: a paired-pulse protocol with an ISI of 2.5 ms, characterizing short intracortical inhibition (SICI); a 120% single pulse; and a paired-pulse protocol with an ISI of 8 ms, characterizing intracortical facilitation (ICF)³, respectively. The conditioning stimulus was applied at 80% of the rMT and the test stimulus was applied at 120% of the rMT, but with the same pulse configuration and orientation. The random sequence contained the same number of pulses for each pulse configuration (simple, inhibitory, or excitatory) as the tree sequence, but with the order randomly scrambled, both containing 300 pulses. New sequences were generated for each participant. 

The interval between trials (ITI), the time between each pulse, was selected by means of a uniform distribution between 4 and 6 s. The muscle of interest used was the abductor pollicis brevis (ABP). 

The experiment consisted of: EMG preparation and hotspot hunting, and two stimulation sessions for each sequence, each lasting 30 minutes with an interval of approximately 10-15 minutes between them. The starting order was randomized for each participant. Procedure approved by the ethics committee (CAEE: 85778925.0.0000.5407). 

EMG was processed with a high-pass filter of 20 Hz and notch filters for 60 Hz and its harmonics were used.  MEP amplitudes were computed as the peak-to-peak amplitude of the EMG-signal in the window between 15 ms and 60 ms after the TMS-pulse application. 

For direct comparison between each pulse configuration in each block, the paired Wilcoxon test was performed.To retrieve the tree from the MEP data pruning procedure starts from the admissible context tree of maximal height computed from the stimulus sequence and proceeds iteratively by testing terminal subtrees. For each terminal subtree, the distributions of MEP peak to peak values associated with all pairs of its leaves are statistically compared with Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. If no significant differences are found between any pair, the subtree is pruned, indicating that the preceding symbols, or in this case the preceding TMS pulses, do not influence the EMG distribution, and the subtree is replaced by its parent context. Conversely, if at least one comparison reveals a significant difference, the subtree is retained, reflecting a dependence on the full context. This process is repeated until all terminal subtrees have been evaluated, resulting in the final pruned context tree.

Results

This project is ongoing and will ultimately include the participation of 20 volunteers. At the present stage, the results reported here are based on data collected from 7 volunteers. Initial analyses focused on comparisons between pulse configurations across the random and tree sequence blocks. The results of the Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to compare inhibition, single and excitation pulses revealed heterogeneous response patterns across participants, with significant differences emerging in some individuals but not consistently across the group, highlighting substantial inter-individual variability. This means that, at least up to this point, it has been possible to observe differences between the pulse modalities in structured and random sequences, although without significant consistency. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied due to the exploratory nature of the analysis.

In terms of tree recovery, the peak-to-peak MEP data from one volunteer recovered the complete generating context tree (0,01,11,21,2), showing that there were changes in the distribution of single pulses, represented as 1 of the alphabet, when preceded by single pulses, paired inhibitory or excitatory. The remaining 6 volunteers presented the recovered tree only with the branches that differentiate the 3 pulse conditions (0,1,2).

Conclusion

These findings provide an early indication of the feasibility of the proposed methodology and offer initial insights that will be further investigated into how neuronal adaptation may occur in response to structured and unstructured stimulation patterns. By evaluating how the nervous system adjusts to different temporal organizations of stimuli, this approach helps to clarify the mechanisms underlying short-term and context-dependent plasticity. In particular, investigating these adaptive processes is of direct relevance to understanding the well-known variability of motor evoked potentials (MEPs), which remains a major challenge in both experimental and clinical TMS research.
