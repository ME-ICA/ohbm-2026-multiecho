Introduction

The perivascular space (PVS) constitutes a fundamental component of the glymphatic system, serving as a critical drainage conduit for the exchange of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and interstitial fluid (ISF). Impaired glymphatic function via PVS pathways has been shown to compromise amyloid-β and tau clearance and increase neurofibrillary tangle pathology in rodent models.1 In humans, converging evidence has suggested that enlarged PVS is a biomarker of local glymphatic dysfunction.2 Quantitative post-mortem analyses have revealed significant associations between PVS impairment and Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) pathology, indicating the role of glymphatic failure in neurodegeneration.3

While clinical studies have reported increased PVS burden in AD—particularly in the centrum semiovale—its specific relationship with core Aβ and tau pathologies remains unclear in vivo.4 The inadequate MRI resolution very likely accounts for such disconnection. For example, in standard clinical 3T MRI, only significantly enlarged PVS (EPVS, typically >1–2 mm) are discernible (Figure 1A). Histologically, the PVS is predominantly composed of microscopic channels that, combined with the EPVS, constitute the 'true perivascular fluid capacity.' However, the vast majority of these microscopic PVS remains hidden below the detection threshold of standard clinical imaging. The 7T ex-vivo MRI can provide a higher resolution, which helps to address this issue (Figure 1B).

In this study, we conducted a multi-scale study linking post-mortem neuropathology, high-resolution ex-vivo MRI, ante-mortem clinical MRI, and in-vivo clinical data (Figure 1C). We harmonized the inclusion criteria across cohorts to exclude cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and significant vascular risks, which isolates the specific impact of AD pathology. We hypothesize that increased local microscopic PVS burden reflects a compromised peri-vascular fluid pathway in regional white matter (WM) associated with AD pathology.

Methods

Participants and Study Design 

We here define a cross-scale validation framework utilizing three distinct cohorts (Figure 2A).

 (1) Post-mortem (ex-vivo 7T): N=11 hemisphere samples were obtained from the Zhejiang University Brain Bank. All donors underwent high-resolution 7T ex-vivo MRI.

 (2) Ante-mortem: N=12 participants from the ADNI Neuropathology dataset who underwent in-vivo MRI within 2 years prior to death. 

(3) In-vivo Clinical: N=290 participants from ADNI (198 Aβ-negative, 92 Aβ-positive) with concurrent structural MRI, AV-45 (amyloid), and AV-1451 (tau) PET. Crucially, to isolate the specific impact of AD proteinopathy, we rigorously excluded cases with Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy (CAA) in neuropathological cohorts and participants with major vascular risk factors (based on Hachinski Ischemic Score) in the clinical cohort.

Pathology and Biomarker Criteria

Neuropathology: Diagnoses followed the NIA-AA "ABC" framework, utilizing Thal phase (0-4) and Braak stage (0-6). Donors with Braak stage >0 were classified as tau-positive. Thal Phase: Assesses the hierarchical distribution of amyloid plaques, progressing from the neocortex (Phase 1) to the allocortex/limbic regions (Phase 2), diencephalon/basal ganglia (Phase 3), and brainstem/cerebellum (Phase 4). Braak Stage: Assesses the topographic progression of NFT deposition, originating in the transentorhinal region (Stages I-II), spreading to the limbic regions (e.g., hippocampus) (Stages III-IV), and finally involving the isocortex (Stages V-VI). 

In-vivo Biomarkers: Aβ positivity was defined by a global AV-45 PET SUVR >1.11 or CSF Aβ42/40 ratio <0.071. In-vivo Braak staging was inferred from regional tau-PET SUVRs using a decision-tree approach. The mean SUVR cutoffs were set as follows: >1.407 for Braak V/VI regions, >1.232 for Braak III/IV regions, and >1.135 for Braak I/II regions.

MRI Acquisition and Processing

7T ex-vivo: High-resolution 3D T2-weighted images were acquired on a 7T Siemens scanner at 0.5 mm isotropic resolution in our previous work. PVS were segmented using a Frangi filter-based method to capture fine tubular structures. 

3T in-vivo: Clinical resolution images (1 mm isotropic) were processed using a robust nnU-Net model5 trained on multi-center ADNI data (Figure 1A). 

In all datasets, White Matter Hyperintensities (WMH) were masked out to ensure PVS specificity. PVS volume fractions were quantified in six white matter lobes (Frontal, Parietal, Temporal, Occipital, Cingulate, Insula) generated via FreeSurfer (in-vivo) or atlas registration (ex-vivo), and were calculated as the ratio of regional PVS volume to the total brain volume.

Statistical Analysis

Associations between regional PVS burden and AD pathology (Thal/Braak) were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ). Group differences in the in-vivo cohort (Braak 0 vs. >0) were assessed using Welch’s t-test. To address multiple comparisons across regions, statistical significance was assessed using False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction with a threshold of q< 0.1.

Results

7T ex vivo MRI Increased Regional PVS Visibility (Figure 2B): The violin plots showed that ex vivo and in vivo datasets shared a similar regional hierarchy, which validated the anatomical consistency across different datasets. Interestingly, we notice that the distributions of PVS fractions in each WM lobe exhibited distinct patterns between 7T post-mortem and 3T clinical MRI: the 7T post-mortem MRI detected a wider range of PVS in the temporal, occipital, and cingulate WM regions, while the frontal and parietal PVS in the 7T ex vivo dataset showed a distinct bimodal distribution. This discrepancy suggests that 7T high-resolution MRI captured a dense network of "hidden PVS" that were invisible at standard clinical 3T resolution.

PVS Burden Associated with AD Pathology (Figure 2C): In the post-mortem 7T MRI dataset (N=11), the full PVS spectrum was visible, and the total PVS burden exhibited a positive correlation with Braak stage (Spearman ρ=0.61). Furthermore, the temporal lobe PVS fraction demonstrated the strongest associations with both amyloid burden (Thal phase, ρ=0.71) and tau pathology (Braak stage, ρ=0.70). However, in the ante-mortem dataset (N=12), this pattern appeared to be weaker under the lower resolution of in vivo MRI: the specific association between temporal PVS and Thal phase was no longer significant. In contrast, the correlation with Braak stage remained significant for both temporal (ρ=0.61) and occipital (ρ=0.68) PVS.

Specific Regional PVS Window in In vivo Cohorts (Figure 2D): On the basis of our ante-mortem observations, our in vivo analysis was focused on the temporal and occipital lobes within the larger ADNI cohort (N=290). In the temporal lobe, PVS fraction differences stratified by Aβ status were subtle and non-significant (Figure 2D-1). This was consistent with our ante-mortem finding that the correlation between temporal PVS and Thal phase vanished at clinical resolution. Regarding tau pathology, while temporal PVS showed no significant stratification between early (Stage 0) and late (Stage > 0) in vivo Braak stages, a weak positive correlation was found with continuous Braak staging (ρ=0.14, p=0.019, Figure 2D-3). Strikingly, the occipital lobe demonstrated greater sensitivity to tau progression. The occipital PVS fraction was significantly elevated in participants with observable tau pathology (Braak Stage > 0) compared to those at Stage 0 (p=0.03, Figure 2D-2). In addition, occipital PVS exhibited a significant positive correlation with continuous in vivo Braak staging (ρ=0.15, p=0.013, Figure 2D-3).

Conclusion

We defined a special "triple-validation" of MRI-visible PVS across ex-vivo and in-vivo cohorts. Our results yielded three primary observations. First, comparing 7T ex-vivo with 3T in-vivo imaging revealed a vast burden of "hidden PVS" falling below the detection threshold of clinical MRI. High-resolution 7T ex-vivo MRI unveiled these microscopic channels, particularly within the white matter of the temporal and occipital lobes. Second, in the post-mortem cohort, temporal lobe PVS exhibited strong positive correlations with both amyloid-beta (ρ=0.71) and tau (ρ=0.70) progression, whereas widespread PVS showed significant associations predominantly with tau progression. Third, the occipital PVS demonstrated a robust and consistent association with tau progression across all three datasets. It exhibited significant correlations with Braak stage in both post-mortem (ρ=0.57) and ante-mortem (ρ=0.68) cohorts, and successfully stratified Braak 0 from Braak >0 groups in the clinical cohort. These findings first suggest the critical role of occipital PVS in AD-related tau progression, translationally.

Spatial Specificity of PVS Burden

We demonstrated pathology-specific spatial heterogeneity of PVS burdens in AD, isolated after a rigorous exclusion of CAA. We propose that Aβ and tau drive distinct physiological alterations in the temporal and occipital lobes, respectively. In the temporal lobe, PVS burden correlates strongly with Aβ but remains invisible in 3T clinical MRI. This likely reflects "microscopic fluid stasis" rather than structural enlargement. Early Aβ deposition may impair microscopic drainage channels, increasing fluid capacity—detectable by high-resolution ex-vivo MRI—without causing the macroscopic enlargement required for 3T in-vivo detection.

Conversely, the occipital lobe may interestingly serve as a robust window for tracking tau propagation. As illustrated in Figure 2D-4, tau pathology spreads from the medial temporal lobe (Braak I-II) toward neocortical networks (Braak V-VI). Our data suggests that occipital PVS expansion captures the intermediate transition of this spreading pathway. Unlike the frontal lobe, which is confounded by vascular noise, the occipital lobe possesses a relatively pure AD association. Consequently, tau-induced neuroinflammation or gliosis in this region triggers macroscopic PVS dilation that is readily detectable in vivo, serving as a specific sentinel for the spread of tau pathology beyond the limbic system.

Bridging the Resolution Gap to Unveils the PVS

Current clinical research predominantly focuses on PVS in the centrum semiovale (CSO). While increased CSO-PVS is frequently observed in AD cohorts, it remains widely categorized as a non-specific marker of small vessel disease (CSVD) rather than a direct consequence of AD proteinopathy. Our findings challenge this vascular-centric view by demonstrating that, once confounding vascular factors are rigorously excluded, PVS burden reveals a strong, region-specific association with AD pathology in the whole WM.

We show that the "vascular hypothesis" largely stems from a resolution bias. In standard 3T MRI, the CSO is prone to visible, vascular-driven PVS enlargement, masking subtle AD-driven changes. In contrast, our multi-scale data reveal that AD-specific remodeling—particularly the Aβ-associated microscopic stasis in the temporal lobe—remains "invisible" to standard clinical sequences. This resolution gap may account for the failure of previous studies in establishing a consistent link between regional PVS and AD pathology. To capture this "true perivascular fluid capacity" and utilize PVS as a precise biomarker for AD staging, we advocate for the adoption of ultra-high-resolution 7T MRI in vivo. Shifting the focus from non-specific CSO burden to high-resolution mapping of temporal and occipital landscapes may finally unlock the potential of PVS as a specific indicator of glymphatic failure in Alzheimer’s disease.
