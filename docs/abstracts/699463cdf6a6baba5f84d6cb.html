Introduction

Mental health challenges constitute a substantial portion of the global disease burden,1 and suicide remains a leading cause of death worldwide.2 Suicidal ideation often co-occurs with depression, yet current clinical assessment tools offer limited predictive utility.3 Identifying the cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying suicidality is therefore critical for advancing risk detection and treatment strategies. Individuals with depression, anxiety, or suicidal ideation exhibit attentional biases toward emotionally salient stimuli.4,5 The emotional Stroop task probes such biases by requiring participants to make perceptual decisions while ignoring task-irrelevant emotional content, with slower responses reflecting attentional interference.

Drift diffusion modeling (DDM) is a computational approach that decomposes decision-making into interpretable components, including the speed of evidence accumulation (drift rate), the amount of evidence needed to make a decision (boundary separation), and non-decision processes such as perception or motor response time. We applied DDM to behavioral data from an emotional Stroop EEG task in individuals with depression and suicidal ideation, as well as healthy controls. We hypothesized that clinical participants would show weaker evidence accumulation for negative relative to positive words, reflecting attentional interference from negative content. Furthermore, we examined continuous symptom dimensions as predictors of DDM parameters. Neural mechanisms underlying decision processes were assessed using event-related potentials (ERPs), with the N170 component indexing early perceptual processing and the P3 component indexing subsequent decision-related processing. We tested whether these neural markers predicted evidence accumulation and if these neural-behavioral relationships were altered in depression or suicidality.

Methods

Participants (ages 18-25) were recruited at the University of Southern California under IRB-approved protocols. Following a screening interview, participants were classified into three groups based on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Suicidal Ideation Scale (SIS): control (PHQ-9 ≤ 4, SIS ≤ 16), depressed (PHQ-9 ≥ 10, SIS ≤ 16), or suicidal (PHQ-9 ≥ 10, SIS > 16). Participants completed an in-person session that included an emotional Stroop EEG task and additional assessments of depression and anxiety symptoms (PHQ-9, GAD-7). Those with clinically significant shifts in PHQ-9 scores who no longer met screening criteria were excluded from analysis.

The task comprised 480 trials where participants identified the font color (red, green, blue, or yellow) of positive (neutral/happy) and negative (sad/suicide-related) words. Words appeared for 500 ms, followed by a variable fixation cross (900-1100 ms). EEG was recorded from 64 channels at 1 kHz. Preprocessing included line-noise removal, band-pass filtering (0.5-80 Hz), ICA for manual artifact rejection, noisy channel interpolation, re-referencing to the average, epoching relative to word onset, and baseline correction (200-ms pre-stimulus interval). ERPs (N170, P3) were computed as mean amplitudes within predefined time windows and electrode clusters (Figure 1).

Trials with response times of less than 100 ms were excluded. Participants with fewer than 10 incorrect trials were excluded to ensure reliable DDM parameter estimation, yielding a final sample of 44 control, 38 depressed, and 41 suicidal participants. DDM was applied to each participant’s data using the fddm R package, with correct responses assigned to the upper boundary and incorrect responses to the lower boundary. Drift rate was estimated by word condition, while boundary separation and non-decision time were estimated per participant.

Drift rates were analyzed using linear mixed effects models with word condition, group, and their interaction as fixed effects and random intercepts for participants. Group differences in boundary separation and non-decision time were assessed with linear models. Clinical correlates were tested by substituting group with symptom scores (SIS, PHQ-9, GAD-7) in relevant subsamples (anxiety: all participants, depression: clinical groups, suicidal ideation: suicidal group). Finally, neural-behavioral coupling was assessed by modeling drift rate as a function of ERP amplitude, word condition, and group. Significant interactions were followed up with pairwise comparisons using the emmeans R function.

Results

Group-level comparisons revealed no significant differences in DDM parameters between control, depressed, and suicidal participants. However, continuous symptom analyses revealed specific alterations. Within the suicidal group, we observed a significant interaction between word condition and suicidal ideation severity on drift rate, X2(1) = 3.86, p = .049, such that higher suicidal ideation predicted reduced evidence accumulation for negative relative to positive words. Across all participants, higher anxiety severity was associated with significantly larger boundary separation (β = 0.01, SE = 0.01, p = .029). Depressive symptom severity was not associated with DDM parameters.

Neural-behavioral analyses revealed a significant main effect of N170 amplitude on drift rate, X2(1) = 14.88, p < .001, with larger (more negative) N170 amplitudes predicting stronger evidence accumulation. This was further qualified by a significant interaction between N170 amplitude, word condition, and group, X2(2) = 11.52, p = .003 (Figure 2A). In the suicidal group, larger N170 amplitudes predicted significantly reduced drift rates for negative relative to positive words (p = .023), whereas controls showed the opposite pattern (p = .019).

P3 amplitude showed a significant interaction with group on drift rate, X2(2) = 7.35, p = .025 (Figure 2B). Follow-up comparisons showed that whereas controls exhibited the expected positive association between P3 amplitude and drift rate, this relationship was significantly weaker and directionally reversed in the suicidal group (p = .038).

Conclusion

This study examined how task-irrelevant emotional content modulates perceptual decision-making and its neural correlates in depression and suicidality. Although behavioral decision-making processes did not differ by group, symptom-specific and neural-behavioral analyses revealed behavioral and neural patterns associated with suicidality and anxiety. Suicidal ideation severity was associated with selectively reduced evidence accumulation during trials containing negative words, suggesting heightened interference from negative information during perceptual decisions. In contrast, anxiety was linked to increased boundary separation, reflecting a more cautious decision strategy.

Neural-behavioral coupling further differentiated individuals with suicidal ideation from healthy controls. Across participants, larger N170 amplitudes were associated with stronger drift rates, indicating that early perceptual processing contributed to the efficiency of evidence accumulation. However, in the suicidal group only, this coupling was selectively attenuated for negative words, suggesting that early perceptual responses to negative content were less effectively integrated into downstream decision processes. Additionally, in healthy controls, larger P3 amplitudes were associated with faster evidence accumulation, consistent with established links between centroparietal positivity and decision formation. In contrast, this relationship was reversed in the suicidal group, suggesting that P3-related mechanisms supporting decision-making are disrupted in suicidality and no longer reliably index task-relevant decision processes. Together, these results indicate that suicidal ideation is characterized by altered neural mechanisms supporting perceptual decision-making, even in the absence of overt behavioral deficits.

Overall, these findings highlight the value of combining computational modeling with ERP analyses for identifying alterations in the cognitive and neural mechanisms supporting perception and decision-making. This approach may provide a sensitive framework for identifying cognitive vulnerabilities associated with suicidality and informing mechanism-based risk assessment tools.
