Introduction

Understanding how visual cortex organization changes across the lifespan may help elucidate visual performance variation within and across individuals. Previous work has demonstrated that more V1 cortical surface area is dedicated to representing the horizontal versus the vertical meridians, termed horizontal-vertical anisotropy (HVA), and more surface area to representing the lower versus the upper vertical meridian, termed vertical-meridian asymmetry (VMA)1. Moreover, these asymmetries in cortical representation of the horizontal and vertical meridians vary between children and adults2, mirroring asymmetries in visual task performance around the visual field3. Here, we investigate whether differences in cortical geometry may underlie these age-related differences in primary visual cortex organization by retrospectively applying the deepRetinotopy toolbox4 to 11,060 anatomical scans.

Methods

DeepRetinotopy toolbox: This application integrates standard neuroimaging software (FreeSurfer 7.3.2 and Connectome Workbench 1.5.0) for anatomical MRI data preprocessing and pre-trained deep-learning models for predicting topographic maps of the visual cortex, i.e., retinotopic maps, at the level of individuals. DeepRetinotopy toolbox provides an easy-to-use command-line interface that is compatible with common neuroimaging pipelines and is packaged into Docker and Singularity software containers, enabling reproducible and scalable processing.

The toolbox is modular, consisting of three main processing steps. First, it processes the input data, that is, cortical meshes reconstructed from structural MRI images (T1w) using FreeSurfer, to estimate mean curvature maps and bring them to a common surface space. Next, the pre-trained models predict individual-specific retinotopic maps from input data. These retinotopic maps are typically defined in polar coordinates, resulting in two orthogonal coordinate maps: polar angle and eccentricity. Finally, predicted maps are resampled to the native space from the anatomical data. 

Datasets: We used T1w-derived cortical meshes from the ABCD baseline cohort (n = 9,947; age 9-11) and the HCP Young Adult dataset (n = 1,113, age 22-36) to predict retinotopic maps using deepRetinotopy.

Estimating primary visual cortex surface area dedicated to representing different portions of the visual field: To investigate asymmetries in cortical representation of the horizontal and vertical meridians, we computed an asymmetry index given as the difference between the surface area of two wedge-ROIs (horizontal versus vertical meridian, and lower vertical versus upper vertical meridian) divided by the mean combined surface area. These wedge-ROIs were determined by selecting vertices in the cortical surface within visual areas 1 and 2 and a specific eccentricity range (0-6°). Polar angle maps were used to mask vertices representing the vertical meridians [upper (UVM; 90°±45°) and lower (LVM; 270°±45°)] and horizontal meridians [left (LHM; 180°±45°) and right (RHM; 0° to 45° and 360° to 315°)] (Figure 1). A second mask (V1 mask) was applied to obtain horizontal meridian representations within V1 only. Then, we estimated the cortical surface area of each of these wedge-ROIs using custom Python code, which employed the cross-product method to calculate the area of the triangles from the midthickness surface in fsnative space. The surface areas corresponding to the upper and lower vertical meridians were divided by a factor of 2 to account for the additional vertices from V2 included in our masking procedure.

Results

We first validated our predictions by replicating the previously described asymmetries in V1 surface area allocation of different portions of the visual field1. To do so, we used a subset of the HCP Young Adult dataset that contained empirical retinotopic mapping data (the HCP 7T Retinotopy dataset; n = 181). We found more cortical surface area dedicated to representing the horizontal meridian than the vertical meridian (HVA) and more surface area dedicated to representing the lower than the upper vertical meridian (VMA) in both empirically derived and predicted retinotopic maps (Figure 2a, b). Moreover, these findings generalized to a large-scale dataset, the complete HCP Young Adult dataset (Figure 2c). These results support the notion that our approach can capture the asymmetries in visual field representation in V1.

Finally, to investigate whether cortical VMA differences across age groups emerge, at least in part, due to changes in local cortical geometry, we applied deepRetinotopy to the ABCD baseline cohort and compared it with the HCP Young Adult dataset. We found group differences in cortical VMA (higher VMA in adults compared to children; Cohen’s d = 0.18, p < 0.001; Figure 2d), consistent with previous work2. Beyond previous findings, we found a small but significant difference in cortical HVA between groups (higher HVA in adults compared to children; Cohen’s d = 0.09, p < 0.01). Importantly, because our predictions derive from cortical geometry alone, our findings reveal that differences in how the visual cortex samples different meridians across age groups can arise from local changes in cortical geometry.

Conclusion

We leveraged our toolbox to investigate the biological underpinnings of visual cortex organizational differences across age groups, a question that requires sample sizes unattainable with empirical retinotopic mapping. First, we demonstrated that predicted maps capture known asymmetries in visual field sampling in V1, validating that our approach captures genuine organizational principles. Then, we applied deepRetinotopy to over 11,000 brain scans and found age-related differences in how the primary visual cortex samples the visual field, consistent with prior findings from a small sample study2. Importantly, because our predictions derive from cortical geometry alone, these findings reveal that age-related changes in cortical folding may reshape functional organization and exemplify how deepRetinotopy enables discoveries at a population scale.
