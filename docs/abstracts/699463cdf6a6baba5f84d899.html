Introduction

Mind-wandering (MW) is a ubiquitous phenomenon, engaging approximately 20-50% of waking thought1. While early research typically treated MW as a monolithic "off-task" state, contemporary perspectives in psychology and neuroscience increasingly conceptualize it as a multifaceted construct2. Under this view, MW is not a uniform mental event but a heterogeneous family of states that vary dynamically along critical phenomenological dimensions, such as intentionality, emotional valence, temporal focus, self-orientation, and meta-awareness. Crucially, emerging evidence indicates that these variations in phenomenology map differentially onto specific psychopathologies. For instance, ADHD has been linked to pervasive spontaneous (unintentional) MW 3, whereas Major Depressive Disorder is characterized by self-centered, negative, and abstract MW 4.

Despite this conceptual shift towards a multidimensional view, the neurophysiological mapping of these specific phenomenological qualities remains surprisingly undeveloped. The majority of electrophysiological research continues to rely on binary contrasts (e.g., "on-task" vs. "off-task"), effectively collapsing distinct cognitive states into a single category. This reductionist approach obscures underlying neural dynamics, as distinct subjective experiences—such as deliberate future planning versus intrusive negative rumination—likely recruit divergent oscillatory mechanisms despite sharing the same "mind-wandering" label. Consequently, the specific EEG signatures that differentiate these dimensions remain largely unknown. The present study aims to bridge this gap by moving beyond binary classifications to continuous multidimensional assessments. By also utilizing continuous scale reporting for dimensions such as valence, self-orientation, temporal focus, and confidence, we sought to isolate the specific electrophysiological signatures characterizing the diverse phenomenology of mind-wandering.

Methods

The study sample consisted of 43 healthy participants. Each participant performed the Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART), a monotonous and repetitive Go/No-Go paradigm designed to induce spontaneous cognition. Throughout the task, participants were intermittently interrupted and prompted to report their mental state at the precise moment of the interruption. They responded using continuous visual analog scales ranging from 1 to 100 across distinct phenomenological dimensions. Specifically, participants rated: (1) Focus (ranging from completely on-task to completely off-task); (2) Valence (from totally negative to totally positive content); (3) Self Orientation (from self-centered to other-centered); (4) Temporal Focus (from the past to the future, with the present as the midpoint); and (5) Confidence in their ratings. In total, each participant completed 60 such interruptions distributed across the experimental session.

Continuous EEG recordings were acquired throughout the task using a 64-channel actiCHamp system. The raw data underwent a standard preprocessing pipeline using the MNE-Python toolbox. Once the signal was cleaned, EEG-derived markers were computed for each subject and aggregated at the single-probe level using the 10-second epoch immediately preceding each interruption. For each of this epochs three distinct categories of markers were calculated: Evoked responses (P1, N1, P3a, and P3b components), Spectral power (averaged within delta, theta, alpha, and beta frequency bands), and Connectivity (measured via weighted Symbolic Mutual Information, wSMI).

To analyze the relationship between neural activity and phenomenology, we employed Linear Mixed-Effects Models (LMM), fitted independently at each EEG channel. In these models, the marker value served as the dependent variable, while the phenomenological ratings (on-/off-task, valence, time, self/other, and confidence) were entered as fixed effects covariates, with the subject modeled as a random effect. This multivariate approach allowed us to evaluate the unique contribution of each phenomenological dimension while statistically controlling for the variance explained by the others. Subsequently, to control for multiple comparisons across sensors, channel-wise LMM t-statistics were submitted to a spatial cluster-based permutation test adapted for mixed models. Spatial clusters were defined as sets of adjacent channels exceeding a cluster-forming threshold. A null distribution of the maximum cluster statistic was generated by repeatedly recomputing the statistics under the null hypothesis. Cluster-level p-values were derived by comparing observed statistics against this permutation-derived distribution. Finally, because multiple distinct markers were tested, cluster-level p-values were further corrected for family-wise error using the False Discovery Rate (FDR, Benjamini–Hochberg) method across all clusters within each marker type.

Results

Our channel-wise Linear Mixed-Effects Model (LMM) analysis, designed to disentangle the unique contribution of each phenomenological dimension, first revealed robust electrophysiological signatures distinguishing between on-task and off-task states. The most prominent marker of task focus was the P3b evoked component, which exhibited a significant positive cluster over centro-posterior electrodes; specifically, P3b amplitude was markedly higher when participants reported being focused on the task, consistent with reduced resource allocation during mind-wandering. In the spectral domain, maintaining task focus was associated with increased oscillatory power in the delta band (localized to a frontal cluster) and the beta band (localized to a right-central cluster), whereas no significant modulations were observed in functional connectivity (wSMI) for this contrast. Crucially, to dissociate the neural correlates of the state of inattention from the specific contents of spontaneous thought, subsequent analyses were restricted to confirmed mind-wandering episodes (focus rating < 50). Within this subset, distinct phenomenological qualities recruited specific oscillatory mechanisms. Emotional valence emerged as a strong modulator of neural activity: negative mind-wandering was characterized by a widespread increase in alpha power—manifesting as a significant negative cluster peaking over the right frontal hemisphere—alongside a focal increase in posterior theta power. Regarding content orientation, the shift from self-centered to other-centered thoughts was tracked by activity in the beta band, where we observed a positive cluster over midline frontal electrodes indicating higher power for socially oriented (other-centered) thoughts. Finally, the temporal dimension of mind-wandering (past vs. future) was significantly associated with modulations in the delta frequency band, revealed by a bilateral posterior cluster that differentiated between temporal loci.

Conclusion

Our findings provide critical electrophysiological evidence that mind-wandering is not a unitary neural event, but a heterogeneous state composed of distinct oscillatory profiles. First, regarding the state of mind-wandering, the robust attenuation of the P3b component confirms the perceptual decoupling hypothesis, suggesting that the brain suppresses external sensory processing to insulate internal trains of thought. This was accompanied by reduced frontal delta and central beta power, indexing a withdrawal of executive alertness and sustained attention resources. However, our results demonstrate that the "off-task" label masks significant neurophysiological variability driven by phenomenology. We show that emotional valence recruits specific regulatory mechanisms, where increased right-frontal alpha power likely reflects cortical inhibition associated with the processing of negative affect. Furthermore, we identified novel spectral markers for specific cognitive contents, including frontal beta modulations tracking social orientation and posterior delta activity acting as a signature of mental time travel. Collectively, these results challenge the reductionist binary view of spontaneous cognition. By disentangling the signatures of state from content, this study establishes a framework for identifying precise neurophenomenological biomarkers, a necessary step for understanding how specific forms of mind-wandering contribute to mental health disorders.
