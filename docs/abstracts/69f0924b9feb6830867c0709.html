Introduction

Sleep is essential for mental and physical health, as well as healthy brain and cognitive development1. Sleep is particularly crucial during adolescence, yet most adolescents get insufficient sleep2. There is thus substantial interest in identifying how reduced sleep affects the developing brain. Previous cross-sectional studies demonstrated that reduced sleep is associated with widespread changes in the brain’s intrinsic functional architecture. A critical related question concerns how to interpret functional connectivity neurosignatures of sleep duration directionally. That is, is the presence of the brain connectivity pattern identified in neurosignatures associated with reduced sleep a consequence of, or a contributor to, reduced sleep duration? The present study aims to clarify links between sleep and the developing brain during adolescence both longitudinally (across two years) and directionally (does reduced sleep cause connectivity changes or are connectivity changes the cause of reduced sleep?). Our novel approach combines the Adolescent Brain Cognitive DevelopmentSM (ABCD) Study3, a longitudinal observational study of 11,878 youth, and a second sample of 76 adult participants scanned after a typical night of sleep and after a sleep deprivation causal manipulation from the Stockholm Sleepy Brain Study4. 

Methods

We first employed a multimodal assessment of sleep coupled with multivariate predictive modeling to identify a robust, generalizable brain-wide neurosignature of reduced sleep at a single timepoint in the ABCD Study® (N=2,991). Principal component regression5 was used to predict sleep duration (captured via parent-report, self-report, and FitBit measures) from whole-brain resting-state functional connectivity (Figure 1). We control for multiple covariates including sex assigned at birth, age, age-squared, mean framewise displacement (FD), and mean FD-squared.  We next evaluated longitudinal associations in an independent subset of ABCD participants (N=1,574), quantifying the relationship between sleep duration changes and neurosignature expression over a two-year span using multilevel mixed-effects modeling.

To investigate the directionality of effects, we utilized a novel method we dub “little data informs big data.” Neuroimaging studies that scan subjects before and after causal manipulation of sleep duration are highly resource intensive and challenging to undertake, and thus they are invariably relatively modest in size. On the other hand, consortium-size studies such as ABCD are ideal for training whole-brain multivariate predictive models of sleep, as these brain-wide methods benefit greatly from large sample sizes. To gain some advantages of both approaches while illuminating the question of directionality, we “transported” our multivariate neurosignature of reduced sleep duration learned in the ABCD sample to a separate sample of 76 adults in the Stockholm Sleepy Brain Study who underwent a sleep manipulation in which they were scanned after a night of typical sleep and after a night of sleep deprivation (3 hours or less of sleep). Specifically, we calculated expression of the ABCD reduced sleep neurosignature in each participant in the Stockholm sample in each scan after a night of typical sleep and after sleep deprivation, comparing changes after typical sleep versus sleep loss.

Results

The multivariate neurosignature of reduced sleep identified in the ABCD participants showed strong out-of-sample predictive value (leave-one-site-out cross-validated r=0.31, pPERM<0.0001). Connectivity patterns showed prominent intra-network connectivity increases within the somatomotor network and decreases within the visual network associated with shorter sleep (Figure 2). Next, we found that in an independent ABCD longitudinal sample, changes in sleep duration were highly significantly associated with changes in expression of the reduced sleep duration neurosignature (standardized beta=0.10, p=0.000050). Specifically, decreased sleep was associated with increased expression of the neurosignature. In the Stockholm Sleepy Brain Study participants, the “reduced sleep” neurosignature from ABCD was significantly increased after sleep deprivation (mean=3.39, SE=0.340) compared to after a typical night of sleep (mean=2.036, SE=0.333), and the difference was highly statistically significant when controlling for sex, age group, and motion on each scan (T=3.84, p=0.00027). Finally, spatial correspondence between the ABCD reduced sleep neurosignature and the Stockholm deprivation neurosignature was highly significant (r=0.38, pPERM=0.0089) and both showed a key role of the somatomotor network.

Conclusion

These results suggest a directional relationship between sleep and the connectome, in which brain connectivity changes occur as a consequence of sleep deprivation. While the findings do not conclusively rule out bidirectionality, they strengthen evidence that reduced sleep drives these brain-wide changes. The neurosignatures also revealed a prominent association between reduced sleep/sleep deprivation and increased within-network somatomotor network connectivity in both datasets. Future research can capture additional longitudinal changes in middle to late adolescence with future planned releases of the ABCD Study. Overall, by combining the strengths of smaller, but highly controlled experimental studies and large-scale, longitudinal observational studies, this study clarifies links between sleep and the developing brain and provides novel evidence that changes in sleep produce characteristic brain functional connectivity changes across adolescence.
