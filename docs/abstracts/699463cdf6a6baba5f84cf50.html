Introduction

White matter fibres provide the structural substrate which facilitates functional brain dynamics, mediating the transmission of functional information between grey matter regions. The capacity for functional communication via a given white matter tract is dependent on a number of microstructural properties of the tract.1 Studies of structure-function coupling aim to characterise the relationship between synchronous activity patterns in distinct grey matter regions (functional connectivity) and properties of the underlying white matter fibres (structural connectivity).2 Structure-function coupling can be measured across the entire brain, by correlating structural and functional connectivity across all edges in the brain network, or regional structure-function coupling can be measured for each node by correlating the edges connected to that node.2 

This coupling has been predominantly explored using BOLD fMRI to measure functional connectivity and number of streamlines (NOS) from tractography as a measure of structural connectivity. However, NOS is dependent on tractography parameters, parcellation scale, and normalisation strategies. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) parameters, such as fractional anisotropy (FA) or radial diffusivity (RD), are sensitive to microstructure but not specific to any given white matter fibre properties. For example, FA is strongly influenced by the presence of crossing fibres. Conversely, biophysical diffusion models such as standard model imaging (SMI) provide parameters with higher sensitivity and specificity to microstructural properties. For example, axon water fraction (f) provides a measure of axon density, and perpendicular extra-axonal diffusivity (De⟂) is particularly sensitive to myelination. 

Here, we explore the relationship between structure and function, across multiple modalities and spatial scales, using DTI and SMI parameters (FA, RD, f and De⟂) as structural connectivity weights, in comparison with NOS.

Methods

MRI data consisted of n = 66 subjects (age 22–36 years, 29 males) from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) young adult cohort, including T1-weighted images, DWI data (with b-values = 0, 1000, 2000 and 3000 s mm-2 and over 90 diffusion directions per nonzero b-value), and four runs of resting-state BOLD fMRI data (acquired with TR = 720 ms and 1200 frames per run). DWI preprocessing steps applied as part of the HCP processing pipeline included removal of eddy current and motion induced distortion, gradient-nonlinearity correction, registration to T1-weighted images and resampling to 1.25 mm resolution. Resting-state fMRI preprocessing steps applied as part of the HCP preprocessing pipeline included distortion correction, motion correction, bias field correction, high-pass filtering, ICA-based denoising and artefact removal with motion regression, registration to T1-weighted images and resampling to 1.25 mm resolution. Tractography data for these subjects was obtained from a previous study which generated a probabilistic white matter atlas at four scales of parcellation.3 

The process for generating weighted connectomes for each subject is summarised in Figure 1a. We selected SMI parameters f and De⟂, and DTI parameters FA and RD as structural connectome weights. For each subject, each parameter was averaged within each white matter bundle by measuring the mean parameter along every streamline within the bundle and averaging across streamlines. The inverse of bundle-averages of De⟂ and RD were taken to provide edge weights which increased with the strength of connectivity. Structural connectomes were then averaged across subjects, giving a group-average connectome for each parameter at each of the four parcellation scales. NOS-weighted connectomes were also generated for each subject and averaged across subjects. Only grey matter regions were considered, and connections to individual thalamic nuclei were merged to give a single thalamus region per hemisphere, resulting in the following numbers of regions for each parcellation: 82, 128, 230, 460.

We generated a functional connectome for each subject at each parcellation scale by measuring the average functional signal in each region, then demeaning, low-pass filtering (0.1 Hz) and concatenating the four functional runs. Functional connectivity was measured by the Fisher z-transformed Pearson correlation between each pair of regions. This was averaged across subjects to obtain a group-average functional connectome for each parcellation scale. 

Structure-function coupling with the group-average functional connectome was measured at both the whole-brain and regional level (Figure 1b), using the Spearman correlation coefficient between structural and functional edge weights, with bundle length included as a regressor. To compare similarity in regional structure-function coupling patterns across parcellation scales, the coupling values at scales 2, 3 and 4 were z-transformed and averaged across regions belonging to the same scale 1 region. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was then measured across z-transformed coupling values to give a measure of similarity across scales for each parameter. 

We further explored structure-function coupling with stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) conduction velocity measurements. Conduction delay measurements were provided by the F-tract database.4 These were derived from cortico-cortical evoked potentials measured from intracranial SEEG recordings from 780 epilepsy patients while undergoing resective surgery of pharmaco-resistant focal epilepsies. Population-average measurements of peak delays were mapped onto each parcellation scale, using a 50 ms cut-off to increase specificity to direct connections. Only connections with at least 50 measurements from at least 3 electrode implantations were considered. These were converted to conduction velocity using the length measurements from the tractography atlas described above.3

Results

At the whole-brain level, NOS-weighted connectomes showed moderate structure-function coupling with BOLD functional connectivity for the coarser parcellations, but this coupling decreased with increasing scale (Figure 2a). For connectomes weighted by diffusion parameters from DTI and SMI, structure-function coupling with BOLD functional connectivity was weak (but significant) across scales. 

Regional structure-function coupling with BOLD functional connectivity increased with parcellation scale for all parameters (Figure 2b). Of the connectomes weighted by diffusion metrics, 1/De⟂ exhibited the strongest and most widespread coupling, with a pattern of strong positive coupling in primary sensory processing regions (visual and motor). This pattern was seen to a lesser extent with 1/RD. NOS-weighted connectomes had high structure-function coupling across widespread brain regions. 

The consistency in patterns of regional structure-function coupling across parcellation scales, as measured by ICC (95% confidence interval), was as follows: f, 0.93 (0.91, 0.95); 1/De⟂, 0.96 (0.94, 0.97); FA, 0.90 (0.86, 0.93); 1/RD, 0.95 (0.93, 0.97); NOS, 0.87 (0.81, 0.91). This was lowest for NOS, and significantly lower than 1/De⟂ and 1/RD, indicating patterns of regional structure-function coupling in NOS-weighted connectomes are dependent on spatial scale. 

Whole-brain structure-function coupling with SEEG conduction velocity was weak for all parameters, and variable across spatial scales for most parameters. Only 1/De⟂ had significant coupling with conduction velocity at all spatial scales. Regional structure-function coupling with SEEG conduction velocity was not measured due to the sparsity of the data.

Conclusion

Connectomes weighted by diffusion parameters had weak structure-function coupling with BOLD functional connectivity at the whole-brain level, but higher than previously reported for DTI parameters with length regressed.5 Despite weak whole-brain coupling, regional structure-function coupling was high in many regions and was consistent across spatial scales. Notably, 1/De⟂ had the most widespread regional structure-function coupling of the diffusion-weighted connectomes, possibly due to its sensitivity to myelination. This regional structure-functional coupling pattern was similar to previously reported characteristics of coupling following a unimodal-transmodal hierarchy.2

1/RD showed similar patterns to 1/De⟂ but with weaker coupling. This highlights the differences between RD and De⟂; while these are both somewhat sensitive to myelination, De⟂ provides higher specificity by specifically reflecting extra-axonal tortuosity and factoring out fibre orientation dispersion. Counterintuitively, f displayed negative coupling with BOLD functional connectivity. This is possibly due to an underlying collinearity between f and tract length,1 which impacts the structure-function correlation when length is included as a covariate. 

For NOS-weighted connectomes, the dependence of regional structure-function coupling on spatial scale arises because the distribution of edge weights changes as the number of regions in the parcellation changes (since it is constructed from the same number of total streamlines, segmented into different numbers of connections). This is in contrast with tractometry-weighted connectomes, which sample measurements of diffusion properties from the underlying voxel values. This demonstrates the arbitrary nature of NOS, which depends on experimental design. 

Connectomes weighted by 1/De⟂ had weak but significant coupling with both BOLD functional connectivity and SEEG conduction velocity, showing that the structural information captured by 1/De⟂ has functional relevance across temporal scales. Coupling with SEEG conduction velocity had wider confidence intervals for all parameters due to the smaller number of edges with sufficient measurements in the delay matrix. Further differences may arise due to the fact that the SEEG conduction delay measurements come from a different population, whereas the functional connectivity matrices are measured from the same population as the structural connectivity. 

This study validates different diffusion parameters as structural connectome weights with functional relevance. Future work could better explain the relationship between structural and functional connectivity by combining microstructure parameters.1
