Introduction

Rhythmic sensory and non-invasive brain stimulation can selectively modulate neural activity, yet the mechanisms underlying their frequency-specific effects remain unclear. Empirical studies show that distinct stimulation frequencies preferentially engage specific brain networks, as observed in steady-state visual evoked potentials and auditory steady-state responses, where maximal responses occur at modality-specific resonance frequencies 1,2
Growing evidence suggests that oscillatory dynamics play a central role in large-scale communication, with different frequencies supporting interactions across distinct spatial scales. Lower frequencies facilitate long-range integration, whereas higher frequencies promote more localized processing 3. This frequency-dependent organization implies that rhythmic stimulation may selectively interact with intrinsic network dynamics rather than acting solely at the stimulation site.
Connectome-based computational models provide a principled framework to investigate this hypothesis by linking structural connectivity, conduction delays, and oscillatory dynamics. Whole-brain oscillator models have shown that large-scale networks exhibit metastable dynamics, characterized by coherent subnetworks at specific frequencies determined by network structure 4. Recent work further demonstrated that a single connectome configuration can support multiple spontaneous oscillatory modes, offering distinct targets for frequency-specific entrainment 5.
 Here, we investigate whether frequency-selective entrainment can be explained as an emergent property of structural connectivity and network oscillatory dynamics. Using a connectome-based model, we represent stimulation as periodic forcing of cortical nodes and examine how stimulation frequency, target region, and global network operating point shape large-scale synchronization. We further assess how individual variability in baseline EEG spectra modulates network responsiveness to stimulation.

Methods

We implemented a whole-brain network model of phase-coupled Kuramoto oscillators constrained by human structural connectivity from the AAL90 connectome and incorporating conduction delays as described in [4]. Each node represented a cortical region whose phase dynamics evolved according to delayed interactions with anatomically connected regions, preserving the large-scale topology of the human connectome. Sensory stimulation was modeled as an external periodic force applied to individual cortical nodes representing primary sensory cortices, allowing the controlled investigation of frequency-dependent network entrainment.

Model parameters governing global coupling strength and mean conduction delay were optimized to maximize the correspondence between simulated power spectra and resting-state EEG spectra from healthy participants. Similarity between empirical and simulated spectra was quantified using the Jensen–Shannon distance, yielding EEG-informed operating points that capture inter-individual variability in baseline network dynamics. In addition to the operating point that maximized metastability, multiple EEG-derived operating points were examined to assess how baseline oscillatory structure modulates sensory entrainment.

Sensory stimulation was applied to the left Calcarine cortex to model visual input and to the left Heschl’s gyrus to model auditory input. Stimulation frequencies were selected to align with canonical sensory resonance bands identified in both empirical EEG studies and model spontaneous oscillatory modes. Visual stimulation targeted alpha-range frequencies, while auditory stimulation targeted gamma-range frequencies. For each operating point, simulations were performed independently across stimulation frequencies, enabling direct comparison of entrainment patterns across baseline network states while keeping stimulation amplitude constant.

Network entrainment was quantified using complementary spectral and phase-based criteria. A node was classified as entrained if its power at the stimulation frequency exceeded baseline levels and if its phase locking value with the driving signal exceeded a predefined threshold, indicating stable synchronization. Spatial patterns of entrainment were compared across frequencies, operating points, and sensory modalities. Model predictions were validated by comparing simulated entrainment patterns with empirical EEG steady-state responses obtained during rhythmic visual and auditory stimulation, using regional overlap and spatial similarity metrics.

Results

The model exhibited frequency-selective network entrainment in response to sensory stimulation, with entrainment patterns depending on stimulation frequency, target sensory region, and network operating point. Visual and auditory stimulation produced distinct spatial responses, reflecting modality-specific resonance properties embedded in the network structure. Across operating points, low-frequency stimulation produced widespread synchronization across distributed cortical regions, whereas higher-frequency stimulation resulted in more spatially confined entrainment centered on the stimulated sensory cortex. This inverse relationship between stimulation frequency and spatial extent of entrainment was consistently observed across all tested operating points, indicating that it arises from fundamental network properties rather than parameter specific effects.

For visual stimulation, periodic forcing of the Calcarine cortex consistently produced maximal entrainment at alpha-range frequencies. At these frequencies, entrainment propagated beyond occipital regions into parietal and temporal cortices, reflecting large-scale integration characteristic of visual steady-state responses. Higher stimulation frequencies produced more localized effects, with entrainment largely restricted to visual areas. Importantly, this frequency-selective profile was preserved across EEG-fitted operating points, demonstrating that alpha-band dominance in visual entrainment is a stable network feature. While the spatial extent of recruitment varied with baseline network state, the core visual subnetwork engaged at alpha frequencies remained consistent across operating points.

Auditory stimulation of Heschl’s gyrus produced a complementary frequency-selective profile. Gamma-range stimulation elicited the strongest and most reliable entrainment, with synchronization extending across temporal and adjacent cortical regions. Lower-frequency stimulation generated weaker and less spatially coherent responses, highlighting the specificity of auditory network resonance. Using EEG-derived operating points the model presented entrainment at the auditory subnetwork at gamma frequencies while modulating the magnitude and spatial spread of entrainment. This indicates that inter-individual variability in baseline oscillatory dynamics modulates the magnitude and spatial extent of auditory network engagement without altering the underlying frequency-selective network architecture.

Furthermore, comparisons with empirical EEG data revealed spatial correspondence between simulated and observed steady-state responses. Visual entrainment showed the strongest agreement at alpha frequencies, while auditory entrainment peaked at gamma frequencies, consistent with known sensory resonance properties. Together, these results demonstrate that the model not only captures theoretical principles of network entrainment but also is able to reproduce empirically observed patterns of frequency-specific sensory responses.

Conclusion

These results demonstrate that frequency-selective sensory entrainment emerges from the interaction between structural connectivity, oscillatory network dynamics, and stimulation frequency, without requiring modality-specific tuning beyond network architecture. The model provides a mechanistic explanation for why visual and auditory stimulation preferentially engage alpha- and gamma-band networks, respectively, and why stimulation effects extend beyond primary sensory cortices in a frequency-dependent manner.

Crucially, our findings show that baseline network state plays a key role in shaping stimulation outcomes. While each sensory modality exhibits a stable core network that is consistently entrained at its preferred frequency, the spatial extent and strength of entrainment vary across EEG-informed operating points. This suggests that individual differences in resting-state dynamics can modulate the efficacy and spread of sensory stimulation, offering a potential explanation for variability observed in empirical neuromodulation studies.

By integrating connectome structure, oscillatory dynamics, and empirical EEG constraints, this computational framework provides a principled approach for predicting sensory entrainment patterns across individuals. The ability to model how stimulation frequency interacts with intrinsic network modes supports the rational design of frequency-specific sensory and non-invasive stimulation protocols. Therefore, these results highlight the importance of considering both network architecture and baseline dynamics when interpreting steady-state responses and optimizing neuromodulation strategies, paving the way toward personalized, network-informed stimulation approaches informed by computational models.
