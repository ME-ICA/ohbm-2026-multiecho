Introduction

Dysregulation—encompassing difficulties in modulating adaptive cognitive, emotional and behavioral responses (e.g., heightened emotional reactivity, impulsivity, difficulty recovering from distress)—is common in children and adolescents with neurodevelopmental conditions (NDCs) and interfere with social adaptive behavior.1–3 The neural mechanisms linking dysregulation and social adaptive behavior remain unclear. Although behaviorally distinct, these constructs may share neural circuitry underlying their association.4 This study aimed to (1) identify brain-wide resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) correlates of dysregulation and social adaptive behavior; (2) assess the extent to which variability in dysregulation-related rs-FC can explain social adaptive behavior, and vice versa; (3) examine the overlap between dysregulation-related and social adaptive behavior-related rs-FC correlates; and (4) explore whether these rs-FC patterns differ across diagnostic groups.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed from 1,044 participants 6–to-18-years-old with autism, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), or typical development from the POND network. Dysregulation was measured using the Child Behavior Checklist Dysregulation Profile and social adaptive behavior with the Adaptive Behavior Assessment System, 2nd edition, Social domain scale. Analyses are underway using CONN (22.v2407) and SPM12. The sample was split into training and test sets. Whole-brain Functional Connectivity Multi-Voxel Pattern Analysis (FC-MVPA) tested dysregulation effects.5 Planned follow-up analyses include: 1) testing whether predictive network edges generalize to social adaptive behavior between independent sets, 2) assessing spatial overlap between dysregulation- and social adaptive behavior-related rs-FC to identify shared versus distinct substrates using non-parametric spin tests using non-parametric spin tests, and 3) confirming network localization through seed-based connectivity analyses, including diagnostic-specific effects. These steps are designed to clarify both shared and distinct neural substrates of dysregulation and social adaptive behavior across groups. 

Results

CONN-based Artifact Detection Tools (ART) excluded 17% of participants. Behavioral data were available for 563 of the 865 participants who passed ART quality control due to pending upload of missing behavioral data; this number will increase as data entry progresses by the time of presentation. Included versus excluded participants do not differ in dysregulation or social adaptive behavior (p=0.53; p=0.76), though excluded participants are younger (p<0.01), more likely male (p<0.01), and have higher rates of ADHD or autism (p<0.01). The preliminary sample includes 188 ADHD, 246 autistic, and 149 typically developing participants (172 female, 31%; mean age = 11.35, SD = 3.20). Training (n=353) and test (n=210) sets are comparable across age, sex, diagnosis, and behavioral measures (all p>0.05), supporting predictive validation. FC-MVPA in the training set identified dysregulation-related rs-FC patterns across prefrontal, parietal, temporal, visual, and limbic regions, implicating default mode, frontoparietal, salience, and sensorimotor networks (Table 1 and Figure 1). Remaining analyses will 1) test whether these network edges generalize to social adaptive behavior in the test set, 2) assess spatial overlap between dysregulation- and social adaptive behavior-related rs-FC, and 3) confirm network localization via seed-based connectivity analyses in both sets, examining diagnostic-specific differences.

Conclusion

Preliminary findings indicate that dysregulation in neurodiverse youth is linked to widespread resting-state connectivity across frontoparietal, salience, default mode, and sensorimotor networks involved also involved cognitive control and social cognition. These patterns suggest that difficulties in emotion regulation and social adaptation may arise from altered coordination among large-scale networks supporting both self-regulation and social processing.4 Ongoing analyses will test whether shared or distinct connectivity profiles underlie dysregulation and social adaptive behavior across diagnostic groups. Clarifying these brain–behavior relationships may inform transdiagnostic models of neurodevelopment and guide network-targeted interventions to improve both emotional and social functioning in youth.
