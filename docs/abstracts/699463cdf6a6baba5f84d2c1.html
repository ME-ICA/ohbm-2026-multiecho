Introduction

The consistency of fMRI  measurements upon repetition, known as test–retest reliability, is essential for the sensitivity and the validity of neuroimaging results. However, quantifying the reliability of resting-state fMRI is complex. Reliability metrics range from univariate measures (e.g., single functional connection, or edge) to multivariate approaches (e.g., the stability of whole-brain connectivity patterns, including fingerprinting)1,2. Significant efforts have been made to develop  appropriate reliability metrics1,   and to assess the impact of choices in the analysis pipelines3. Previous research has indicated poor reliability for univariate functional connectivity (FC) measures at the edge level, whereas multivariate metrics reflecting whole-brain patterns tend to demonstrate greater reliability1. Regarding the study design, extended scan durations and larger subject cohorts are often recommended, but these solutions can be impractical, especially in clinical populations. We aim to systematically investigate the impact of the number of subjects and the scan duration on the test–retest reliability of both univariate and multivariate resting-state fMRI FC metrics. Specifically, we assess the test–retest reliability of FC using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and two multivariate measures (fingerprinting2 and discriminability3) across varying scan length and numbers of subjects . Since data preprocessing pipelines have been shown to profoundly affect FC measures, we further considered two main preprocessing choices: minimal preprocessing (minimal) and further denoising (denoised).

Methods

We used minimally preprocessed resting-state fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP), WU-Minn Consortium (http://www.humanconnectome.org/). The sample consisted of 100 healthy, unrelated subjects (54 females, 46 males; ages 22–35). Each participant completed two resting-state fMRI sessions on separate days (rfMRI_REST1_LR and rfMRI_REST2_LR).  Functional images were acquired with 2 mm isotropic voxels, TR = 720 ms, multiband factor = 8, and 1,200 volumes per run for a total scan duration of 14 minutes 24 seconds.

A schematic overview of the methodological pipeline is presented in the Methods figure. 

Starting from the minimally preprocessed HCP dataset, we performed two additional preprocessing pipelines using FSL (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/): (i) minimal: high-pass temporal filtering with a cut-off frequency of 0.01 Hz to remove slow drift fluctuations and spatial smoothing using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum of 3 mm; and (ii) denoised: included all steps in the minimal pipeline plus nuisance regression of motion parameters using the Friston 24-parameter  model (6 head motion parameters, their first temporal derivatives, and the 12 corresponding squared items, totalling 24 motion regressors) and mean white matter and cerebrospinal fluid BOLD time series. 

A node-based connectome analysis was performed using the Schaefer atlas, parcellated into 400 regions4.  For each session and participant, FC matrices were generated by computing Pearson correlation coefficients between all pairs of regions, and pairwise connections were then normalised using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation. 

We systematically varied the preprocessing pipeline (minimal vs denoised), scan duration, and number of subjects. Different scan durations were obtained using temporal truncation, retaining only the initial time points to yield 3.6, 7.2, 10.8, and 14.4 minutes of data. Cohort sizes were set to 10, 20, 50, and 100 subjects. For each combination, we performed 100 iterations with subjects randomly selected in each iteration. 

We quantified the test-retest reliability across sessions using five measures. First, we used ICC in two ways: (i) edge-level ICC (between-participant reliability), computed as ICC(2,1) for each edge to reflect the consistency of each edge’s connectivity strength across sessions between participants. We then reported the average ICC within each of the seven intrinsic RSNs according to Yeo et al.4, and (ii) whole-connectome ICC (within-participant reliability), computed as ICC(2,1) by comparing vectorised FC matrices between sessions within each subject, thereby capturing test–retest reliability at the whole-connectome level. Third, we computed the Pearson correlation between within-participant vectorised FC matrices across sessions. Fourth, we calculated differential identifiability based on the functional fingerprinting approach2,  which compares correlations between connectomes and evaluates whether individual FC profiles are more similar within a subject across sessions than between different subjects; differential identifiability was defined as the difference between within-subject and between-subject similarity. Fifth, we computed discriminability3,  quantifying the extent to which within-subject similarity exceeds between-subject similarity based on distances between connectomes. 

Statistical analyses to assess the effects of the different factors (scan length, number of subjects and preprocessing pipeline) were conducted in R (R version 4.4.1, https://www.r-project.org/). A linear model was fitted, followed by ANOVA to test for main effects and interactions. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Results

The results obtained for the test–retest reliability using five node-based connectome metrics (edge-level ICC, within-participant ICC, within-participant connectivity matrix correlation, differential identifiability, and discriminability) in HCP data under varying conditions of scan length, cohort size, and preprocessing pipeline are presented in the Results figure.

Overall, the reliability values align with previous reports1,5, indicating that univariate metrics such as edge-level ICC showed limited reliability (ICC ≈ 0.3 – 0.5), depending on the RSN under study. In contrast, multivariate approaches such as differential identifiability (Idiff ≈ 20 – 30 %) and discriminability (Discr ≈ 0.8 – 1), demonstrated moderate to high reliability. 

The effects of scan duration and the number of participants on the reliability of the five metrics considered are shown for the minimal and denoised preprocessing pipelines in the first and second rows of the figure, respectively. For the denoised pipeline (second row), we found that scan duration had a statistically significant effect on all five metrics (ANOVA, p < 0.001). Specifically, reliability increased with scan duration, with the full scan length (14.4 min) yielding the highest reliability. In contrast, cohort size had a small and not significant  effect overall. Based on our findings, a sample size of 20 participants was sufficient to achieve stable test–retest reliability for edge-level ICC, with no statistically significant gains from increasing the cohort size beyond that point. For within-participant ICC, differential identifiability, and discriminability, even 10 participants yielded reliability estimates that were statistically comparable to those obtained with 100 participants. However, although the mean values for these smaller samples did not differ significantly from those of larger samples, the associated standard deviation across the 100 iterations was notably higher when fewer participants were included. This suggests that achieving reliable results with small samples may depend on the inclusion of particularly stable or “good” subjects, making such estimates sensitive to inter-individual variability. In the case of univariate measures (edge-level ICC), which are inherently less reliable, more participants were required for sufficient power to observe univariate effects. These findings are consistent with our previous results5, where we performed the same analysis on a minimally preprocessed pipeline and reported that, for connectome metrics, 12–14 minutes of scanning and a sample size of approximately 20 participants were sufficient to achieve reliable estimates (first row of the figure).

Comparing both preprocessing pipelines (minimal vs denoised) across scan duration for a fixed sample of 100 subjects (third row of the figure), we found  that scan duration had a similar effect on the five metrics across pipelines: reliability increased with scan duration. However, there was a statistically significant difference between the results of the two pipelines for all five metrics except differential identifiability. A similar pattern was observed when comparing the two pipelines across cohort size for a fixed scan duration of 14.4 minutes (fourth row of the figure). The results showed that increasing the cohort size did not increase the reliability of the metric considered; however, there was a significant difference between the two pipelines.

Conclusion

Given the increasing emphasis on reliable findings, there has been growing interest in the reliability of resting-state connectivity measures. In this study, we examined the test–retest reliability of five node-based connectome metrics (edge-level ICC, within-participant ICC, connectivity matrix correlation, differential identifiability, and discriminability) under varying conditions of scan length, number of participants, and preprocessing pipelines. Our results suggest that a scan duration of about 14 minutes and a cohort of around 20 participants are sufficient to achieve good reliability for most connectivity-based metrics. Data denoising by nuisance regression in the preprocessing pipeline increased reliability across all metrics, indicating that regressing out motion- and physiology-related variance helps improve reliability.  In summary, the choice of scanning protocol, both in terms of scan length and number of participants, should be guided by the specific goals of the study, with the aim of maximising reliability while maintaining feasibility. Importantly, denoising in the preprocessing pipeline can partially compensate for limited scan duration, although longer acquisitions still provide increased reliability.
