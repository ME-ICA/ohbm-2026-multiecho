Introduction

The Lyon Neuroscience Research Centre (CRNL), established in 2011, now comprises 23 research teams, 10 technical platforms, and over 450 staff members. The Centre is strongly committed to the principles of open science.

Informal discussions on research integrity and good laboratory practices began around 2015, initiated by a researcher’s proposal to form an internal working group. This group, initially consisting of ten members, organized its first workshop in 2017. The event featured three invited speakers and three roundtable discussions, attracting more than 100 participants.

By the end of 2017, the working group received formal recognition from the Centre’s internal board of team leaders. Since then, it has expanded to include PhD students, postdoctoral researchers, engineers, and faculty, who actively participate in monthly meetings—ten per year since 2019.

In 2019, the recruitment of two research engineers, each dedicating part of their time to promoting open science, marked the beginning of a coordinated open science policy at the Centre. This initiative, which incorporated the existing Scientific Integrity working group, led to the establishment of additional thematic groups, including “Open Access and Researcher ID,” “Electronic Laboratory Notebooks,” “Data Management Plans,” “Open Methods and Tools,” and “Citizen Science.”

The two engineers proposed adapting France’s Second National Plan for Open Science (2021–2024) into a local “Plan of the CRNL for Open Science” which was approved by the Centre’s team leader council at the end of 2022. This plan outlines four key priorities, each supported by specific objectives, actions, tools, and guidelines designed to enhance open science practices among CRNL members, in alignment with the broader open science movement. After three years of implementation, a review assessed progress and identified remaining objectives.

Methods

The present work outlines the design and implementation of a local plan to promote open science within a neurosciences research Centre. The plan was organized around four priorities:

1) increasing the visibility and traceability of scientific outputs and authors

2) supporting the management and sharing of research data and code

3) reinforcing the mastery of open science tools

4) structuring the dissemination of knowledge towards civil society

Priority #1 – Visibility of publications and publishing scientists.

All publishing researchers were required to create or update their ORCiD and idHAL identifiers, ensuring the profiles included all scientific publications produced since 2019. The systematic deposition of publications in the HAL open archive was formalized in the Centre’s internal regulations. Additionally, a charter of affiliations was developed standardize institutional signatures. The Centre and its research teams committed to updating open archive HAL collections  and implementing the French open Science Monitor1 to track scientific production since 2019, in the open science perspective. 

To capture evolving publishing practices (e.g. preprints, data papers, preregistration, open peer review), an annual questionnaire was introduced. The Centre also sought to quantify the budget allocated Article Processing Charges (APCs) by analysing expense labels in the accounting software (since 2019) and using data from the French Open Science Monitor.

Priority #2 – Data management, data sharing and code dissemination

The Centre planned to establish a dedicated server for storing source data from all protocols conducted by its members. The XNAT platform was selected to host diverse data types (e.g., MRI, EEG, MEG, PET, genetics, behavioural data). The initial server cost (€2,000) was covered by the Centre, with renewal scheduled for the end of 2026.

To promote interoperability, reuse, and long-term preservation, the Centre aimed to adopt standardized metadata management practices. Training programs on data and code management were also planned to support best practices throughout the data lifecycle.

Priority #3 – Mastery of open science tools

The Centre aimed to organize internal thematic training workshops to enhance researchers’ proficiency in open science tools, including repositories, persistent identifiers, electronic laboratory notebooks, and collaborative platforms. These workshops were to be supplemented by targeted communication and awareness-raising initiatives. Methodological support was provided for the development and implementation of data management plans, as well as for the adoption of electronic laboratory notebooks across the Centre.

Additionally, the Centre committed to integrating open science criteria and considerations into its internal governance structures and processes, including doctoral schools, commissions, committees, and recruitment procedures.

Priority #4 – Knowledge sharing with and towards civil society

The Centre planned to establish an internal database to systematically document outreach and public engagement activities undertaken by its members, such as public lectures, school visits, and participation in science festivals. A dedicated matching tool was to be developed to connect external requests for interventions, partnerships, or collaborations—from schools, associations, volunteers, industry, and other stakeholders—with the Centre’s teams, thereby enhancing visibility and fostering new collaborations with academic, civil-society, and corporate partners.

In parallel, the Centre sought to strengthen its public presence through social media and traditional media channels. This effort was supported by the development of a graphic charter and the regular updating of institutional communication materials, including presentation flyers and related media.

Results

Priority 1: Visibility of Publications and Publishing Scientists

The most significant outcome under Priority 1 was the widespread adoption of the HAL open archive by CRNL members. The Centre established dedicated HAL collections for each of its 23 research teams, with a central CRNL HAL collection aggregating all contributions. Prior to 2019, deposits in the CRNL HAL collection were limited to a small group of open access advocates. However, following the adoption of HAL by the French CNRS for individual researcher evaluations in 2019 and the CRNL’s policy of feeding HAL as part of its evaluation by the HCERES in 2024, deposit rates increased substantially. By the end of 2025, the CRNL HAL collection contained over 3,000 full-text publications and approximately 1,800 references authored or co-authored by CRNL researchers (see Fig. 1B). This collective effort has significantly enhanced the visibility of the Centre’s scientific output, with CRNL publications accumulating more than 350,000 views and 300,000 downloads by the end of 2025 (see Fig. 1C and 1D).

The open access rate of CRNL scientific publications with a Crossref DOI, measured annually, rose from 43% in 2018 to 54–57% in 2019–2020, 73–74% in 2021–2022, and 82–83% in 2023–2024.

With regard to Article Processing Charges (APCs), the Centre’s financial service identified 217 correctly labelled APC transactions between 2019 and 2025, with costs ranging from €0 to €5,300 (median: €1,950 per article). The total expenditure for these 217 APCs amounted to €440,000. While this method was reliable and precise, it was time-consuming and based on a limited subset of publications—217 out of the 3,000 manuscripts deposited in HAL during the same period.

A second approach, using data from the French Monitor of Open Access2, estimated a larger number of open access articles with APCs (between 120 and 180 articles per year). Since 2019, the estimated average APC per article for CRNL publications has increased by approximately 11% over five years (see Fig. 2A), consistent with broader trends3. The two primary open access routes—Gold and Hybrid—are illustrated in Fig. 2B (also online). Notably, the proportion of Gold open access publications at CRNL decreased from 60% to 53% between 2013 and 2023, while Hybrid open access increased from 10% to 28% over the same period (see online results for details). The Diamond open access route remained marginal for CRNL publications (<1% in 2023).

Priority 2: Management and Sharing of Data and Code

The most significant achievement under Priority 2 was the establishment of the XNAT server for storing source data from all protocols conducted at the Centre. Since its launch at the end of 2022, the server has hosted 75 studies, with datasets from over 2,000 participants occupying 2.7 TB of storage. Data management practices were further advanced through the adoption of the Brain Imaging Data Structure4 (BIDS) standard, which has been widely embraced by the Centre’s neuroimaging community. The use of BIDS-compatible applications, such as MRIQC and fMRIPrep, has become standard practice.

Priorities 3 and 4: Mastery of Open Science Tools and Knowledge Sharing with Civil Society

Key outcomes for Priorities 3 and 4 included the organization of more than 20 internal training sessions to promote and facilitate the adoption of open science tools. These sessions covered Git repositories, electronic laboratory notebooks, data management plans, open access publishing, preregistration, statistical methods, the editorial process, and research reproducibility.

The Centre also integrated open science principles into the local doctoral school (“Neuroscience and Cognition,” University Lyon 1, France), requiring the adoption of open access criteria for PhD thesis defence approval. Among the most recent initiatives is the development of an internal database to systematically document science outreach activities aimed at the general public.

Conclusion

The implementation of the “Plan of the CRNL for Open Science” was made possible through the commitment of the Centre’s directors, dedicated internal funding, and human resources allocated to open science. This policy facilitated the consolidation of individual initiatives and the establishment of thematic working groups within the Centre. Many of the actions proposed in 2022 have since been executed, while the completion of remaining objectives depends on the availability of additional financial and human resources.

Priority 1: Visibility of Publications and Publishing Scientists

Open access to CRNL publications increased significantly due to the widespread adoption of the HAL open archive. Future efforts will focus on ensuring all researchers maintain updated ORCID profiles and, where possible, deposit full texts in HAL. Another challenge was the underestimation of Article Processing Charges (APCs), due to complex financial tracking using multiple tools that was unsuitable for a large institution with more than 450 members spread across 23 research teams. This review also revealed that, despite institutional promotion of economically virtuous publishing models5, individual researchers often prioritized immediate dissemination of results—even when alternatives such as HAL (which permits post-print deposition after a six-month embargo under Article 30 of the French “Law for a Digital Republic”) were available.

Priority 2: Management and Sharing of Data and Code

The adoption of the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) standard was widespread within the Centre’s neuroimaging community. However, further efforts are required to extend the use of such standards to other communities, including bioinformatics and microscopy. A key priority remains encouraging and supporting dataset sharing, which remains rare. Additionally, the use of the Software Heritage platform for the preservation and dissemination of source code and research software was minimal, highlighting an area for future action.

Priorities 3 and 4: Mastery of Open Science Tools and Knowledge Sharing with Civil Society

A major advancement was the adoption of open access criteria for PhD thesis defense approval by the “Neuroscience and Cognition” doctoral school at the University of Lyon 1. Although regular training sessions on open science tools were organized, their adoption across the Centre remained partial. Broader integration of open science practices among research teams and scientists is anticipated. One of the most recent initiatives is the development of an internal database to systematically document public outreach activities.

This three-year review has enabled a comprehensive assessment of both completed and outstanding actions. Open science represents a sustained, long-term commitment—both at the individual and collective levels.
