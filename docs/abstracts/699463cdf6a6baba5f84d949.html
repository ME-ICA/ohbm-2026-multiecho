Introduction

Identifying biomarkers of neurodevelopment has been a longstanding objective in psychiatric and neuroscientific research. Electroencephalography (EEG) has proven to be a particularly powerful tool in this context. Several EEG features, such as power spectrum and various complexity measures, have been found to predict age.1,2 Notably, the amplitude of low-frequency (delta, theta) oscillations decreases across development, while the amplitude of high-frequency (gamma) oscillations and neural complexity increase. Despite these advances, the search for robust biomarkers continues. Task selection remains a key challenge, as multiple studies have shown that it influences which EEG biomarkers are found to best predict age.

Here, we propose a new method for characterizing neurodevelopmental trajectories. Instead of focusing on individual feature values, we quantify the distances (i.e., dissimilarities) between these features across participants in each age group, and we study how this set of distances (which we term neural feature space) reorganizes across development. By capturing additional information independent of average feature values, these distances offer a novel class of (second-order) biomarker candidates. Moreover, while feature values vary greatly across time and as a function of external stimulation, feature distances should be more robust to stimuli and experimental setup. We demonstrate the feasibility and utility of this method by characterizing the neural feature spaces of children (aged 2-18 years) and adults (aged 30-45).

Methods

We analyzed data from 195 neurotypical individuals aged 2-45 years that participated in studies at the CHU Sainte-Justine Azrieli Research Center. EEG activity was recorded using the EGI HydroCel 128-channel EEG system while participants watched a short clip from the movie Rio (2011). Data was preprocessed using the HAPPE pipeline (filtering between 1 and 80 Hz, line noise removal, bad channel rejection, wavelet-enhanced ICA and ICA with automatic component rejection) in Matlab.3 Data were then segmented into contiguous 5-second epochs, excluding segments that exceeded predefined amplitude thresholds. EEG features were extracted for each segment using MNE-Features, NeuroKit2 and FOOOF in Python. These features were: Hurst exponent, sample entropy, multi-scale entropy complexity indices (CI) for low, high and all scales, Higuchi and Katz Fractal Dimension (FD), aperiodic activity exponent and offset, and periodic power in delta, theta, alpha, beta, low gamma and high gamma frequency bands (after removing background aperiodic activity). Finally, feature values were averaged within 7 ROIs of electrodes as well as across the whole scalp, and across segments for each half of the data separately.

We computed pairwise distances between features across participants of each age group. To ensure that distances were independent of average feature magnitude and sign, we used an absolute Spearman correlation distance (1 – abs(rs)). Moreover, to ensure that distances were not biased by noise and so they could be analyzed as standard statistics, we used a “cross-validated” form of that distance. Such cross-validated distances require, in our case, that two feature values are obtained from independent parts of the data for each participant.4,5 In addition, we repeated this analysis 1,000 times while resampling participants with replacement. These bootstrap estimates allowed us both to compute the uncertainty around each distance and to obtain an empirical null distribution for statistical analyses (after removing the bootstrap mean). To visualize the feature spaces, we projected the data to two dimensions using multidimensional scaling (MDS) and computed standard errors around each coordinate by computing MDS from the bootstrap estimates of distances after Procrustes alignment to the observed feature space. To test whether distances significantly differed between groups, we first computed, for each observed distance, the difference between groups. We then computed the differences between the bootstrap distributions of both groups, we removed the mean of the resulting distributions for each feature, and we computed the maximum across features for each bootstrap estimate. We computed the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of that distribution of maxima to obtain statistical thresholds corrected for Familywise Error Rate (FWER). Finally, each observed difference of distances was compared to those thresholds to determine statistical significance.

Results

We first analyzed the complete neural feature spaces (distances between each feature in each ROI) of each group (Figure 1). Forty distances were significantly different between groups (p<.05, two-tailed, FWER-corrected). When constraining our analysis to whole-brain features, four distances were significantly different (p<.05, two-tailed, FWER-corrected). Three were larger in adults: the distance between Hurst exponent and Katz FD, the one between Hurst exponent and sample entropy, and the one between theta and low-scale CI. The distance between Delta and low-scale CI was significantly larger in children than in adults.

We also analyzed whether feature distances were similar between tasks on a subset of participants that performed an additional resting-state run with a fixation cross. Feature distances were more correlated (r=.87) between both tasks than feature values (r=.77).

Conclusion

We showed that distances between EEG features may be useful and robust biomarkers of neurodevelopment. Distances were more similar between tasks than feature values themselves, suggesting that they are reliable biomarkers that are more robust to the choice of task and stimulus. Furthermore, we found that many distances changed across development. Notably, we observed that various complexity measures were more distant to each other in middle-aged adults compared to children, possibly resulting from increased functional specialization and more multidimensional neural dynamics in adults. Moreover, the changing distances between low-frequency (theta, delta) oscillatory amplitudes and complexity measures may hint at the changing roles of these frequency bands across development. In the future, we will adapt our method to characterize neural feature spaces in neurodevelopmental populations, predict genetic conditions, and hopefully identify new avenues for investigating brain health.
