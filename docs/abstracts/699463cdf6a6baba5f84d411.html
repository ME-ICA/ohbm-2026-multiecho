Introduction

Brain digital twins (BDTs) are avatars of individual brain dynamics, constructed from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data and validated using functional MRI (fMRI). Electroencephalography (EEG), when available, can constrain model-fitting. BDTs’ core engine is a dynamic system simulator, The Virtual Brain (TVB), which constructs a computational surrogate of the brain as a network: nodes represent grey-matter regions’ neuronal activity through neuronal population models; edges are their structural connections derived from diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) data1. The activity of individual brains reproduced in-silico is then optimized on individual biological functional signals, as Blood Oxygenation Level Dependent (BOLD) signal. Recent advances in large-scale signal acquisition and biologically informed modeling have made BDTs powerful tools for simulating disease severity (e.g., in dementia, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis) and for predicting treatment effects (e.g., in clinical trials of epilepsy surgery)2. To date, BDT reconstruction relied on high-field (3T and above) DWI, making it a niche technology, accessible to settings able to afford such facilities, thus creating economic barriers. Ultra-low-field MRI (ULF-MRI, e.g. 0.064T) could be a key equalizer if it provides high quality DWI-based connectomes. Moreover, signals recorded with electroencephalography (EEG) could be used as functional ground-truth to tune BDTs. Here we propose a new framework for constructing a low-resource (LR) BDT pipeline (i.e., BDTLR) by combining these two technologies: ULF-MRI to define the BDTLR structural connectome and EEG recordings to optimize simulated activity. Our prototype is validated against a state-of-art BDT construction pipeline using 3T-DWI data, representing the first step towards broader accessibility to BDT technology.

Methods

The proposed BDTLR implementation introduces two deviations from the standard TVB pipeline (Fig.1A):

1. The estimation of a personalized structural connectivity matrix (SC) from ULF-MRI: good-quality ULF tractography has been demonstrated3, hence it can feed into BDTLR;

2. The use of EEG instead of fMRI for optimizing simulated activity: this requires a novel EEG-driven optimization that integrates empirical EEG data into the neuronal model personalization

Proof-of-principle data were acquired to demonstrate the BDTLR pipeline (Fig.1B).

Structural architecture

DWI data:

DWI data were acquired at both 3T and 0.064T. For 3T, images were denoised and corrected for motion and eddy-current distortions (FSL), and the co-registered T1-weighted scan was segmented into white matter, cortical and subcortical gray matter (GM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (MRtrix3). 

For ULF, DWI volumes were affine-registered to the b=0 reference with b-vector rotation, followed by nonlinear registration with elastic regularization to account for field-related distortions. ULF data were bias-corrected and normalized using spherical-harmonic bias fields (L=5). A T1-weighted image was super-resolved from T2-weighted data (SynthSR), segmented (Samseg), and used to derive the bias-field normalization. 

Preprocessed DWI data of 8 volunteers from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) were download for the extreme no subject-specific MRI scenario.

Structural connectivity:

A brain atlas of 124 regions was used to parcellate the brain. A 30 million streamline whole-brain anatomically constrained probabilistic tractography was executed (MRtrix3). The resulting tractogram was combined with the atlas nodes. Three SCs were generated (one for each MRI setting):

1. 3T-SC from the volunteer’s own 3T-DWI data, i.e., current state-of-the-art input for TVB.

2. ULF-SC from the volunteer’s ULF-DWI data.

3. AVG-SC computed by averaging 3T-high-resolution SC matrices from HCP data.

All BDTs followed the same construction and EEG-based optimization pipeline, differing only in the MRI SC data source, resulting in 1) a standard BDT, 2) a BDTLR and 3) a BDTno-MRI, where the virtual brain becomes a twin through the personalized EEG-based optimization only, hence assessing to which extent subject-specific tuning is achievable without individualized MRI data. SCs were normalized on their own maximum value and compared for structural similarity (Spearman correlation) and for connection distribution (Kruskal–Wallis test; p<0.05).

Functional units

EEG data:

Resting‑state EEG was acquired with a 128 channel system and pre-processed to remove acquisition artifacts (EEGLab). EEG and simulated neuronal activity were bandpass-filtered (0.5–45 Hz), and power spectral density (PSD) was computed (Welch method, sampling frequency = 250 Hz, no windows overlap)4. PSD was mapped from channel space to the same brain atlas as used for SC and sources were extracted with an inverse model for source localization (sLORETA) to be comparable with simulated activity at region-level. 

Neuronal Modeling:

Each region was modeled using a Jansen–Rit neural mass model. TVB simulates whole-brain dynamics, connecting regional models according to SC strength and integrating differential equations to reproduce neuronal population activities (Hz). Simulated activities served as a proxy for EEG signals.

Optimization

A multi-parametric and multi-objective genetic algorithm (NSGA2) tuned the global coupling (G), a scaling for long-range connectivity, and local excitatory/inhibitory time-constants (te, ti), reflecting neuronal regulation. The minimized objectives were: 1) PSD frequency-band ratio (alpha-to-(delta+theta)) and 2) mean absolute error (MAE) between EEG and simulated activity. The best parameters were selected with a utility function prioritizing the PSD compared to the error, given the clinical interpretability of PSD4. 

The entire pipeline was run on a standard desktop computer (16 CPUs, AMD Ryzen 7 5800 X).

Results

SC quantified inter-regional connectivity and defined the architecture of each BDT (Fig. 2A). Pairwise Spearman correlations between 3T‑SC, ULF‑SC and AVG‑SC were ≥ 0.8, indicating preserved global topology. Distribution analysis (Kruskal–Wallis, p<0.05) showed 3T‑SC had a broader connection spread across the entire 0–1 range, while ULF‑SC resembled AVG‑SC with fewer mid‑strength connections (0.2–0.4).This pattern suggests that high-field subject-specific data capture a wider range of connections, while ULF-SC tends to yield overall lower-weight estimates

Source reconstruction mapped EEG signals to the regional atlas used for SC, enabling direct comparison with simulated node dynamics (Fig. 2B; 2D) The pre-processed experimental EEG reports a prevalence of the alpha band (Fig. 2D – continuous arrow; alpha band range = [8-13] Hz) for each brain region, as expected, validating the EEG preprocessing pipeline that confirmed a robust alpha-band peak across regions, supporting data quality. In addition, the typical beta band peak is also captured in EEG experimental recordings (Fig. 2D- dashed arrow; beta band range = [14-25] Hz). EEG-driven optimization successfully aligned simulated neuronal activity with empirical spectral features in all BDTs (Fig. 2B). Each model reproduced the characteristic alpha-band peak (8–13 Hz) observed in EEG as well as capturing the beta band peak, although with frequencies shifted towards higher value and the peak amplitude is reduced, compared to experimental data (Fig. 2B; 2D). The output of the optimization algorithm is the Pareto front, which represent the distribution of non-dominated solutions, i.e., the optimal solutions that minimized both objectives (Fig. 2C). Here the Pareto front shows a clean monotonic trend, indicating that a convergence is reached towards reliable parameter sets (G, τₑ, τᵢ). Using PSD as the prioritizing criterion, the selected solutions achieved comparable minimization of both PSD ratio and MAE across all twins. Estimated time constants were consistent across BDTs, whereas optimal global coupling differed depending on the SC source: BDT and BDTLR required similar coupling, while BDTno-MRI required substantially higher G to reproduce the same spectral profile (ΔG: BDT–BDTLR = 1.39 a.u.; BDTno‑MRI–BDT = 3.51 a.u.; BDTno‑MRI–BDTLR = 4.91 a.u.) This indicates that individualized SC, whether derived from 3T or ULF-MRI, reduces the compensatory coupling needed for spectral alignment, whereas the no-MRI twin relies heavily on global scaling to match empirical EEG.

Simulation of BDT activity required on the order of a few minutes, while the full parameter optimization converged within approximately 5 hours, confirming that the proposed workflow does not rely on high-performance computing infrastructure.

Conclusion

The main outcome of this work is a fully integrated pipeline for constructing BDTs that can be available in every setting and not only in the ones capable of supporting high-end MRI equipment, transforming the scalability of this technology. The pipeline combines three key components: (i) preprocessing of subject-specific ULF-MRI and EEG data to define the structural architecture (i.e., SC) and functional reference (i.e., PSD) of the BDT, (ii) a simulator of brain activity relying on mesoscale models to neuronal activity (i.e., TVB), and (iii) an optimization framework that tunes regional neuronal model parameters against empirical EEG signals. The extracted parameters capture individual signatures of neuronal activity. Importantly, the simulation and optimization steps do not require high-performance computing, supporting feasibility in low-resource settings. Furthermore, ULF-MRI extends BDT applicability to individuals with implants (e.g., deep brain stimulation), highlighting that this approach equalizes access not only across economic resources but also across patient eligibility.

This study presents a prototype framework to make the BDT technology truly accessible. We demonstrated the feasibility of a BDT reconstruction pipeline using ULF-MRI, optimized with EEG. ULF-SC preserved SC topology, hence the BDTLR was able to simulate neuronal dynamics comparably to BDT. The BDTno-MRI achieved subject-like spectral performance but only after EEG-driven tuning, confirming that our optimization framework can impose functional personalization not only with ULF-MRI but potentially even without personalized MRI as input. 

Low-resource setups typically use <64-channel EEG (here we used 128), which limits subcortical coverage. Incorporating region-specific neuronal models can enhance the biological realism of simulated activity in all nodes, including those wired in closed loops with the cortex, enabling reliable optimization of cortical dynamics even with sparse electrode arrays. In this context, the new TVB pipeline that has been developed for the integration of region-specific mesoscale models would further refine the functional accuracy of BDTLR when high-density EEG is unavailable5.

In conclusion, the convergence of three emerging technologies, namely portable ULF-MRI, AI-driven image reconstruction, and biologically grounded models of neuronal firing, makes the democratization of BDT not only possible, but inevitable. Our pipeline demonstrates that BDTs can be built from affordable, portable technologies, marking a shift towards inclusive neuroscience. Extending BDT frameworks beyond high-field laboratories could redefine personalized diagnostics and therapy in the context of global health.
