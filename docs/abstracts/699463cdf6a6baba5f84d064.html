Introduction

Inflammation is a fundamental physiological axis of homeostasis that consequently can modulate brain function and behaviour 1. Indices of bodily inflammation vary substantially across individuals and is influenced by both physical and mental health status2. Acute inflammatory responses can be experimentally evoked using typhoid vaccination, with the release of circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g.  interleukin-6) peaking in the circulation four to six hours post-injection3. 

Earlier neuroimaging studies report that inflammation tightens connectivity between rostral hubs of default mode network (DMN)4 and weakens connectivity between the amygdala and ventromedial prefrontal cortex5. Additionally, affective symptoms including depression and anxiety are known to modulate intrinsic brain connectivity and correspondingly changes in brain connectivity are associated with mood deterioration induced by typhoid inflammatory challenge6, 10. However, it is unknown how individual differences in psychological factors, notably fundamental beliefs about the nature of the world, influence and interact with neural, immune and psychological effects of acute inflammatory challenge.

This neuroimaging study investigated alterations in resting-state functional connectivity following an acute inflammatory challenge induced by typhoid vaccination relative to a saline control condition. We assessed whether individual differences in depressive symptoms (Patient Health Questionnaire-9, PHQ-9)7, anxiety symptoms (Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7, GAD-7)8, and primal world beliefs (Primal world belief inventory-99, PI-99)9 predict connectivity changes across conditions. We hypothesized that connectivity within the DMN, and between the amygdala and medial prefrontal cortex, would be altered following typhoid vaccination. We further predicted that individual differences in anxiety, depression, and primal world beliefs would explain variance in these connectivity changes.

Methods

Fifteen healthy participants aged 18 to 40 years completed two MRI sessions in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover design. One session of resting-state imaging occurred five hours after saline injection (placebo control), and the other occurred five hours after typhoid vaccine injection (0.025 mg Salmonella typhi polysaccharide; inflammatory challenge). Blood serum samples were collected at baseline (09:45 ± 15 minutes) and immediately before the MRI scan (14:20 ± 15 minutes) to quantify inflammatory cytokines. Participants completed questionnaires (PHQ-9, GAD-7, PI-99) immediately after the injection during their first visit.

Participants were scanned using a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner with Human Connectome Project (HCP) Development & Aging resting-state fMRI acquisition protocols (13 minutes, TR = 800ms, multiband acceleration factor = 8)10. Prior to resting state imaging, a structural scan (T1-weighted in first session, T2-weighted in second session) and task-related fMRI datasets were collected using HCP protocols. Data (fMRI timeseries) underwent minimal HCP multi-modal pre-processing pipelines, followed by spatial normalization to the MNI152 template for statistical analysis in the CONN toolbox.

We conducted two separate region-of-interest to region-of-interest connectivity analyses. The first analysis examined changes in DMN connectivity between the placebo and vaccine conditions using the following regions of interest: Medial prefrontal cortex, left and right lateral parietal, and the posterior cingulate cortex. The second analysis investigated amygdala–prefrontal cortex connectivity using the following regions of interest: Left and right amygdala, medial prefrontal cortex, and left and right orbitofrontal cortex. All regions of interest were defined using masks from the Harvard-Oxford atlas within the CONN toolbox. Graph-theoretic measures including eccentricity, closeness centrality, and average path length were computed for both analyses. Furthermore, we tested whether psychological measures (PHQ-9, GAD-7, PI-99) predicted connectivity changes, controlling for age, sex, and injection order. 

Results

DMN connectivity analysis using graph-theoretic measures revealed significant functional alterations in the vaccine condition compared to placebo, while controlling for age, gender, and injection order. These included increased average path length (β = 0.25, T(34.23) = 34.23, p = 0.001, FDR-corrected p = 0.0026) and reduced closeness centrality (β = –0.10, T(–34.23) = –34.23, p = 0.000852, FDR-corrected p = 0.0026), indicating reduced network integration.

In the amygdala–frontal cortex connectivity analysis, the vaccine condition was associated with significant alterations compared to the placebo condition, while controlling for age, gender, and injection order. Specifically, we observed higher left amygdala eccentricity (β = 1.49, F(3,32) = 32.79, FDR-corrected p = 0.034) and higher closeness centrality (β = 0.29, FDR-corrected p = 0.010) following typhoid vaccination.

When controlling for age, sex, and injection order, both depressive symptoms (PHQ-9) and anxiety symptoms (GAD-7) significantly predicted the strength of inflammation induced changes in functional connectivity between the left lateral parietal cortex and the posterior cingulate cortex within the default mode network. Higher PHQ-9 scores were associated with increased connectivity (F(1,9) = 17.44, p = 0.0072), and higher GAD-7 scores similarly predicted greater connectivity (F(2,9) = 8.77, p = 0.0231). The relationship between PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores and amygdala–frontal cortex connectivity remained non-significant and inconclusive. Further analyses are needed, including repeating this investigation using PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores from each separate day and replicating the study with a larger sample size. Primal world belief scores derived from the PI-99 did not independently predict connectivity changes in either the DMN or amygdala–frontal cortex networks, but further analyses in a larger dataset (additional data collection ongoing) will establish the strength of this interesting individual difference in psychological stance.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that acute immune activation induced by typhoid vaccination significantly modulates functional brain network organization within the wider default mode network. Specifically, experimental inflammation reduced DMN network as indexed by graph-theoretic properties. Additionally, depressive and anxiety symptom severity predicted enhanced connectivity within core DMN hubs, suggesting that affective symptomatology may interact with inflammatory processes to shape network architecture. Induced inflammation also altered amygdala–frontal connectivity patterns as indexed by graph-theoretic properties These results extend prior work linking peripheral inflammation to brain connectivity11, 12.

Further planned analyses will examine whether health mindset, i.e., the individuals' beliefs about the malleability of their health, is altered by following an inflammatory challenge or predicts changes in connectivity. Additional psychological and behavioural factors including mood shifts, sleep quantity and perceived quality, adverse childhood experiences and resilience, will be investigated as potential moderators of neuroimmune coupling. We will further refine the analyses by examining how the inflammatory response correlates with psychological measures and brain connectivity, by analysing cytokine levels from the blood samples (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, C-reactive Protein). Furthermore, we will leverage independent component analysis and parcellation-based approaches using HCP pipelines to characterize more comprehensively the inflammation-induced changes in functional network organization. Understanding these neuroimmune mechanisms could inform the development of targeted interventions for inflammation-associated neuropsychiatric symptoms in clinical populations.
