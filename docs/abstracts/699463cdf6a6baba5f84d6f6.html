Introduction

Malformations of cortical development (MCDs) are structural anomalies of the cerebral cortex resulting from aberrant formation during early development. Interruptions to normal cortical formation, leading to MCDs, primarily arise from genetic abnormalities, chromosomal aberrations, or less frequently, environmental factors such as hypoxia or intrauterine infection.^1

Although the exact incidence is unknown, MCDs are being diagnosed more frequently due to the use of Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) in patients presenting with epilepsy and congenital neurological deficits. MCDs are thought to be the underlying cause of 25% to 40% of medication-resistant or intractable childhood epilepsy, with at least 75% of patients with MCDs ultimately developing epilepsy.^1

Given their strong association with epilepsy and the increasing detection of these conditions, obtaining reliable estimates of the prevalence of MCDs in large population cohorts is essential to guide healthcare planning and inform future research on early diagnosis and intervention. However, estimating these numbers is challenging due to heterogeneous clinical reporting and variability in how MCDs are described in radiological practice.

Building on prior work using radiology reports and text mining in imaging cohorts, we develop and validate a large-language-model–based pipeline for extracting cortical development malformations from free-text Danish radiology reports, enabling scalable phenotyping in a large national cohort.^2

We evaluate these algorithms on a small manually annotated cohort on eight rare MCDs. Finally, based on a large collection of radiological reports, we estimate the population prevalence of epileptogenic MCDs on patients suspected or diagnosed epilepsy within the Greater Copenhagen Area, Denmark.

Methods

We collect radiological reports from 22.506 patients with suspected or diagnosed epilepsy (G40 code) treated in the Copenhagen Capital Region, Denmark, from 2016 to 2023. These are randomly split into two cohorts. Cohort A (N=1.755) has been manually annotated to extract relevant findings according to various epileptogenic brain abnormalities. These reports have been selected with regular expressions in order to find matches to specific findings, i.e. “focal cortical dysplasia”, and then manually annotated by two native Danish speakers: a medical doctor and a medical student, supervised by an epilepsy neurologist. Following the same selection and annotation method of Cohort A, for validation on the large Cohort B (N=78.921), we find reports mentioning polymicrogyria (N=165). 

Specifically, eight malformations were selected on Cohort A. These are divided into two subsets, rare (focal cortical dysplasia, hamartoma, heterotopia and polymicrogyria) and extremely rare (encephalocele, hemimegalencephaly, lissencephaly, schizencephaly . After annotation, we found 28 extremely rare (ER) abnormalities and 725 rare (R) MCDs.

Given the patient-sensitive nature of our dataset, which is protected by GDPR, we opted for a locally deployed, privacy-preserving solution. For the automated extraction of MCDs we use MOSAIC, a family of compact large language models trained on extraction of mentions of abnormality from radiological reports.^3

We prompted the model with the following text and report combination:

“You are a helpful radiology assistant. Given a radiology report, classify each abnormality into a class. Output a valid JSON with each abnormality as key, and the class as value. The keys must be ['encephalocele', 'focal cortical dysplasia', 'hamartoma', 'hemimegalencephaly', 'heterotopia', 'lissencephaly', 'polymicrogyria', 'schizencephaly']. The values can be one of [-1, 1]. The values have the following interpretation: (1) the abnormality was mentioned, even with uncertainty, in the report e.g. 'A large pleural effusion', 'The cardiac contours are stable.', 'The cardiac size cannot be evaluated.';  (-1) the abnormality was not mentioned in the report, or the abnormality was negatively mentioned in the report; e.g. 'No pneumothorax.'. Text: <<< report >>>”

The prompt follows what was used to train MOSAIC, which is specialised on chest X-rays radiological reports. To increase the robustness of the classification, the predictions for each report were selected by majority voting through 3 different inference sampling seeds. Results on the population, where each unique subject might have more than one associated radiological report, are an aggregated set of labels, assuming as present an abnormality that has been positively mentioned at least once.

Cohort A was used for fine-tuning language models on a binary multilabel classification task and was split into training, validation, and test sets (63/7/30). Fine-tuning on the target task did not result in improved performance on the held-out test set. This is likely attributable to the extreme class imbalance and the very low prevalence of several target findings, which limited the model’s exposure to positive examples during training and evaluation. For extremely rare abnormalities with a single example in the training set, the available annotated data may be insufficient to meaningfully adjust model representations beyond those learned during pretraining, resulting in minimal or no performance gains from task-specific fine-tuning.

Results

Before estimating the prevalence of epileptogenic MCDs from on the large Cohort B, we evaluate the language model MOSAIC-12B on Cohort A: the model achieves an F1 score of 82 ± 8 (mean ± standard deviation) when tested on Set R, and 69 ± 16 on Set ER. Over the specific MCDs (Figure 2a), Mosaic-12B achieved consistently high specificity across all malformation subtypes; however, recall and F1-scores varied substantially. This variability was most pronounced for extremely rare malformations, where performance metrics were computed from only a small number of annotated reports (Figure 2b). On the latter, specificity was consistently high (99 ± 0.1), while recall was lower and more variable (69 ± 33). Across all abnormalities, precision was consistently lower than recall, indicating a tendency toward false-positive predictions at the report level. As some malformations are represented by only a few annotated cases, single misclassifications can disproportionately affect performance estimates, limiting the interpretability of per-entity metrics.

As validation on Cohort B, we manually annotated 165 reports mentioning polymicrogyria. On this subset, MOSAIC-12B achieved perfect precision (1.00) and specificity (1.00), with a recall of 0.94 and an F1-score of 0.97. The model correctly identified 101 of 107 positive cases, with no false positives and six false negatives.

Even if the results on Set R are promising, low precision on extremely rare abnormalities mean that prevalence estimates derived from this approach should be interpreted as entity-specific, with potential overestimation due to many false positives. Considering these limitations, we estimate the prevalence of MCDs in approximately 20,000 patients with diagnosed or suspected epilepsy (Figure 2c). The prevalence is reported for both Cohort A and Cohort B, with Cohort B annotations inferred using MOSAIC-12B. 

In Cohort B, while most MCDs match Cohort A frequency, hamartoma and heterotopia exhibit substantially higher estimated prevalence than in Cohort A, deviating from the pattern observed for other malformation types. This can be explained both by the model tendency to low precision on these MCDs, and by the post-processing report label aggregation applied at the subject level, where even a single false-positive report can disproportionately influence prevalence estimates.

Overall, these findings highlight both the strengths and limitations of large-scale, report-based phenotyping for estimating the population burden of cortical malformations. While prevalence can be estimated for many abnormalities using a locally-deployed small language model, validation on manually annotated subsets is necessary for correct interpretation of its results.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and evaluated a large-language-model–based pipeline for extracting malformations of cortical development from free-text MRI radiology reports and applied it to estimate the prevalence of epileptogenic MCDs in a large Danish epilepsy cohort. By leveraging a locally deployed, privacy-preserving model, we demonstrate that automated report-based phenotyping can be scaled to tens of thousands of patients while maintaining consistently high F1 scores and very high specificity across multiple rare malformation types. While specificity remained high across all malformations, recall varied substantially, particularly for extremely rare entities with very limited numbers of annotated examples. As a result, model performance evaluation was primarily influenced by disease frequency and data availability. 

At the population level, aggregation of report-level predictions enabled estimation of MCD prevalence in a large cohort of patients with diagnosed or suspected epilepsy. However, the combination of reduced sensitivity for extremely rare entities and lower precision than recall at the report level implies that prevalence estimates should be conditioned on the interpretation of a validation step on manually annotated reports. For certain entities, such as hamartoma and heterotopia, subject-level aggregation may lead to overestimation driven by false-positive report-level detections.

Overall, this work demonstrates the feasibility of large-scale, text-based phenotyping for studying rare neurodevelopmental brain abnormalities using routinely collected clinical data. Future work should focus on acquiring larger annotated datasets for extremely rare malformations, which will be essential to improve model calibration and ensure more accurate population-level prevalence estimates. The proposed framework provides a foundation for population-scale neuroimaging research that integrates automated text analysis with complementary data sources to better characterize rare cortical malformations.
