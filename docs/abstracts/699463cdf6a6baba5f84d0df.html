Introduction

Deep learning models applied to MRI data have shown strong predictive performance for neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders, yet understanding the features driving their decisions remains challenging. Voxel-wise interpretability methods like. Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) or Class Activation Maps (CAM) are used in neuroimaging to generate high-resolution attribution maps that highlight spatial patterns influencing a model’s prediction. However, their voxel-level nature can make it difficult to relate interpretations directly to established neuroanatomical regions, and their sensitivity to local gradients and model architecture can introduce further variability. These characteristics do not diminish their utility; instead, they motivate the use of region-level explanations aligned with conventional cortical and subcortical parcellations, as commonly adopted in model-agnostic methods such as LIME or Shapley values.

Shapley values offer a model-agnostic interpretability framework that estimates the marginal contribution of predefined features. While voxel-wise application in 3D MRI is computationally infeasible, using atlas-derived regions as “supervoxels” provides a tractable and anatomically grounded solution. Here, we propose a method to compute region-wise Shapley values for a 3D convolutional neural network trained on structural MRI by using FreeSurfer’s cortical and subcortical segmentations as supervoxels. This approach enables interpretable attribution maps that are comparable to region-based measures used in neuroimaging, such as cortical thickness and volumetric analyses. We compared region-wise Shapley values with region-aggregated relevance scores derived from LRP to assess agreement between both methods. With this, we aim to provide a complementary interpretability tool that preserves the predictive power of deep learning models while offering anatomically meaningful explanations aligned with standard MRI analysis workflows.

Methods

We trained a 3D fully convolutional neural network (CNN) using structural MRI from the OASIS-1 dataset1 to classify healthy controls and individuals with dementia, defined as a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR > 0). The dataset includes 307 healthy controls and 100 individuals with very mild to moderate Alzheimer’s disease (AD). 

The model architecture was adapted from a lightweight 3D CNN previously optimized for brain-age prediction2, replacing the regression output with a two-class softmax and training with cross-entropy loss.

Preprocessed whole-brain FreeSurfer’s recon-all outputs (brain.finalsurfs.mgz) served as input volumes. Additional preprocessing of the brain volumes was performed using the MONAI3 framework, including intensity scaling, foreground cropping, and resizing to 96×96×96 to reduce memory requirements. The model was trained in PyTorch 2.8.0 for 130 epochs with a batch size of eight, an initial learning rate of 0.01, and a decay factor of 0.3 every 30 epochs. To achieve a 70:15:15 split, we allocated 283 subjects for training, 60 for validation, and 64 for testing, maintaining proportional class balance. The best-performing checkpoint (based on validation loss) was used for evaluation. We computed balanced accuracy, precision, recall  and ROC-AUC.

To compute region-wise Shapley values, we used FreeSurfer’s Desikan–Killiany cortical parcellation and subcortical segmentations. Each anatomical label was converted into a binary mask, treated as a supervoxel feature, and passed to Python’s SHAP package4. Shapley values were estimated using the Shap Permutation explainer4 with 50 permutations to obtain stable estimates of each region’s marginal contribution.

For comparison with another explainability method, we applied Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) using the Zennit 1.0.0 package5. Voxel-wise relevance maps were generated for each subject and aggregated to the same atlas regions using the previously defined masks, yielding region-wise relevance scores directly comparable to the Shapley-based estimates. We compared the average values for both methods using dementia subjects from the test dataset which were classified with a confidence higher than 0.7 (N=9)

Results

The best-performing model achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.86 on the validation set and 0.83 on the test set, with ROC-AUC values of 0.86 and 0.90, respectively. Recall was high in both splits (validation: 0.92; test: 0.85), indicating strong sensitivity to dementia cases despite class imbalance.

Region-wise Shapley values identified bilateral cerebral white matter, bilateral cerebellar cortex, bilateral lateral ventricles, and the right superior frontal cortex as the most influential regions. In contrast, LRP highlighted bilateral cerebral white matter, the left lateral ventricle, bilateral cerebellar cortex, the brainstem, and the left thalamus as the most relevant regions. Comparison of the top 20 regions revealed partial agreement between methods (14/20; 70%), including bilateral cerebral white matter, bilateral cerebellar cortex, bilateral lateral ventricles, the right and left superior frontal cortices, and the precentral and postcentral gyri.

Shapley values uniquely emphasized the left ventral diencephalon, bilateral putamen, paracentral and fusiform cortices, and the left hippocampus, whereas LRP uniquely highlighted the brainstem, right superior and inferior parietal cortices, left cerebellar white matter, the precuneus, and the right thalamus. Together, these findings suggest that Shapley values and LRP capture distinct but complementary aspects of model decision-making. Larger samples and more robust comparison strategies beyond simple ranking may further clarify regional contributions in deep learning–based brain MRI analyses.

Conclusion

Our proposed approach enables the computation of region-wise Shapley values for deep learning models trained on structural MRI, leveraging atlas-based cortical and subcortical segmentations to produce anatomically meaningful explanations. While model training and evaluation were performed on the full cohort (N = 407), the comparison between Shapley values and Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) was conducted on a subset of high-confidence dementia predictions.

Comparison with atlas-aggregated LRP relevance scores revealed partial overlap in the most influential regions, indicating convergent but non-identical patterns of regional importance. This partial agreement highlights that Shapley values and LRP capture complementary aspects of model decision-making when applied at the regional level using a common anatomical framework.

By leveraging FreeSurfer’s widely used cortical and subcortical segmentations, which are routinely employed across MRI studies, the proposed approach produces region-level explanations that align with how neuroimaging results are commonly analyzed and interpreted. This compatibility facilitates interpretation within established neuroimaging frameworks and enables deep learning–based predictions to be directly related to familiar regional measures, such as cortical thickness and volumetric analyses.

Moreover, because Shapley values are model-agnostic, this framework supports interpretability across modeling paradigms and maintains continuity with traditional region-based machine learning approaches commonly used in neuroimaging. In contrast, gradient-based methods such as LRP or CAM are inherently tied to specific neural network architectures, underscoring the complementary nature of these explainability techniques.

Finally, the use of rank-based comparisons and a limited sample for interpretability analysis motivates future work with larger samples and more robust comparison strategies. Such extensions may further elucidate how different explainability methods characterize regional contributions in deep learning models applied to brain MRI, supporting transparent and multi-level interpretation in studies of neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders.
