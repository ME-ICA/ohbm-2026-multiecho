Introduction

Sleep disorders, including insomnia disorder, Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA), narcolepsy, and Rapid eye movement sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD), affect around 30% of the global population and lead to cognitive, emotional, and physical impairments and reduced quality of life [1]. Although sleep disorders arise from diverse etiologies, some daytime symptoms, such as dissatisfaction with sleep duration and quality, sleep fragmentation, daytime sleepiness, cognitive or mood difficulties, and emotion dysregulation, appear across diagnostic categories. This suggests that shared neural correlates may exist across sleep disorders. Recently, a transdiagnostic neuroimaging meta-analysis [2] identified consistent regional abnormalities across sleep disorders, including clusters in the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex, the amygdala/hippocampus. While this study characterized the regional convergence of abnormalities associated with sleep disorders, the convergence of abnormalities at the level of brain networks is unclear. Additionally, convergent evidence suggests a strong association between variation in macroscale (RSFC) measures and microscale features (neurotransmitter and cellular systems) in sleep disorders. Here, by applying the Convergent Connectivity Mapping (CCM) meta-analytic approach [3], we aimed to characterize convergent macroscale neural correlates of sleep disorders at the brain network level using meta-analytic data [2]. Moreover, we aimed to contextualize the observed network-level abnormalities at the microscales by performing neurotransmitter and transcriptomic profiling of the resulting CCMs.

Methods

We extracted the peak brain coordinates data from a previous meta-analysis [2], including 80 studies on four common sleep disorders. We performed CCM to identify a convergent network-level pattern for disorders with more than 10 reported experiments, including insomnia disorder (26 experiments), OSA (25 experiments), narcolepsy (13 experiments), and RBD (16 experiments).  To perform CCM, we first created a normative group-averaged dense Resting-State Functional Connectivity (RSFC) based on data of 100 unrelated subjects in the HCP dataset. The dense RSFC was used to calculate RSFC maps of foci from 80 unique sleep disorder experiments [2]. These were first averaged across foci within each experiment and then pooled across all experiments (weighted by sample size) to yield an observed (true) RSFC map. This map was tested against a null distribution generated from 1,000 permutations of randomly sampled foci, using the same two-level averaging process. Z-scores, as we referred to them as CCM values, were calculated by subtracting the mean of the null RSFCs from the observed RSFC and dividing by the null distribution's standard deviation. 

Subsequently, we performed neurotransmitter profiling analysis, assessing the spatial association of PET maps for 11 Neurotransmitter Receptor/Transporter (NRT) systems [4] with the CCM values, using variogram-based permutation testing (1,000 permutations) to control for spatial autocorrelation, with all resulting p-values corrected using FDR adjustment (PFDR < 0.05). To explore cellular transcriptomic profiles associated with abnormalities, we used gene expression data from the AHBA dataset. The original expression data for more than 20,000 genes across 3,702 spatially distinct brain tissue samples were processed using the abagen toolbox across 432 brain parcels. Partial Least Square (PLS) regression using the SIMPLS method was applied to study the association of gene expressions and each convergent connectivity map. Using the bootstrapping method with 10,000 iterations, we extracted gene ranks for each regression analysis. Finally, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on ranked genes to identify cell types associated with significantly correlated genes. GSEA was performed using cell-specific gene markers derived from single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) across different regions of the human cerebral cortex [5].

Results

Convergent connectivity maps of transdiagnostic sleep disorders data revealed increased CCM values across the default mode, limbic, salience, and somatomotor networks, as well as subcortical regions, but decreased CCM values within other cortical areas. Among canonical resting-state networks, mean connectivity with the rest of the brain significantly decreased for the dorsal attention (zmean = -0.674, Pspin = 0.014) and frontoparietal (zmean = -0.239, Pspin = 0.019) networks, whereas the default mode network  (zmean = 1.103, Pspin = 0.019) showed significantly increased connectivity relative to null distributions derived from random foci. Insomnia disorder abnormalities showed increased CCM values in somatomotor areas (zmean = 1.489, Pspin = 0.005) and the default mode network (zmean = 0.308, Pspin = 0.005), but decreased CCM values in the frontoparietal network (zmean = -0.171, Pspin = 0.005). OSA demonstrated increased CCM values in default mode areas (zmean = 1.472, Pspin = 0.007), but decreased CCM values in dorsal attention (zmean = -0.973, Pspin = 0.007) areas. Narcolepsy abnormalities showed a similar pattern, with an additional reduction in connectivity to the salience network (zmean = -1.171, Pspin = 0.009). RBD demonstrated significantly decreased CCM values  within the dorsal attention (zmean = -1.517, Pspin = 0.014) network. The transdiagnostic CCM values colocalized significantly with serotonin, cholinergic, dopamine, histamine, and glutamate neurotransmitter systems. The insomnia disorder CCM showed significant associations with serotonin, norepinephrine, and glutamate, while the narcolepsy CCM values were correlated explicitly with the norepinephrine transporter. Additionally, RBD CCM values were significantly correlated with serotonin, cholinergic, dopamine, histamine, and GABA systems. Conversely, the OSA CCM values showed no significant correlation with any of the measured neurotransmitter systems. Finally, gene enrichment analyses indicated that excitatory and non-neuronal cell types were significantly associated with transdiagnostic and disorder-specific patterns, whereas inhibitory cell types appeared particularly relevant in OSA and narcolepsy. Notably, excitatory cells were down-regulated in association with CCM patterns of the transdiagnostic data and of each of the four sleep disorders. Furthermore, non-neuronal cell types were significantly up-regulated. 

Conclusion

Our findings highlight a convergent network-level signature across major sleep disorders, marked by higher convergent connectivity within default mode, limbic, salience, and somatomotor networks, alongside reduced convergent connectivity within attention and cognitive control systems. Disorder-specific patterns further reveal unique connectivity profiles for insomnia, OSA, narcolepsy, and RBD, underscoring both shared and distinct neural mechanisms. Notably, the colocalization of CCM patterns with multiple neurotransmitter systems and relevant cellular profiles provides preliminary evidence that explains symptom expression across disorders. These results advance our understanding of the macroscale neural correlates of sleep disorders and offer promising targets for more precise, biologically informed diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.
