Introduction

Understanding brain's dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) and its pathological alterations could shed light on how neurological pathologies affect brain function and interactions.

However, existing dFC analysis methods only give a coarse view of the dFC, focusing on whole brain dFC and at the group level. In this study, we present an application of a new analysis method that allows a fine-grained extraction of the dFC, specially designed for subject-level analysis. The method is used to study Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB).

The proposed method has two major parts: The first one uses biological priors to define targeted networks and subnetworks then estimates their intra- and inter-network connectivity and their temporal fluctuation. The second one post processes the estimated fluctuations to evaluate differences between subjects and groups in order to identify the affected networks.

Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is the second most common form of neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and is primarily characterized by pronounced fluctuations in cognition and alertness.1  Furthermore, there is considerable clinical variability among patients with DLB. In this context, our approach seems particularly well-suited for investigating the dFC patterns in DLB patients at the subject level. 

Methods

The proposed method2 analyzes the timecourses of predefined regions of interest (ROIs) from a single subject’s resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI). The dynamic functional connectivity matrices are computed as the Pearson correlation between the pairs of timecourses on a sliding window with a sliding step of one sample.

The main step of the method consists in decomposing the list of dFC matrices (denoted C) into predefined (sub)network structures (the biological prior) and estimating their temporal activation. The networks structure is defined as unweighted graphs stored in each column of matrix D. The sparsity and smoothness of the activation is enforced based on the supposition that only few networks are active simultaneously and that their activity evolves smoothly in time (due to the BOLD response time). The output of the model is the temporal evolution of the activation of each network (matrix A) and the connectivity strength between the ROIs within the network (the weights of the edges of the graphs stored in matrix D). These steps are schematized in Figure 1a.

The output of the model is further processed to study how the FC dynamics change between two groups by computing subject level statistics on the estimated activations. In this study, the activations of the networks (rows of matrix A) are thresholded to compute the active and inactive dwell times of each network for each subject, i.e. the average duration of an activation/deactivation of each network. The number of activations of the network is also returned.

The method is applied in a study including 66 DLB patients and 18 healthy control subjects (HCS) from the AlphaLewyMA cohort (NCT01876459). This dataset was previously used for structural and stationary FC analysis.3  Briefly, the images were obtained using a 3 T MR scanner (Verio 32-channel Tim  Siemens scanner) with the following parameters for the 6 minutes rs-fMRI sequence (T2*-weighted gradient echo): 120 volumes; TR=3s; res.=38×64×28 voxels; 4mm isotropic voxels. The images were preprocessed using the SPM12 toolbox and in-house scripts with the steps:  filtering the ASL frequencies (lowpass filter at 0.112 Hz); slice‐timing correction; motion and B0 field inhomogeneity correction; co‐registration of the fMRI images to T1‐weighted anatomical images; spatial normalization to MNI space using the DARTEL approach, including smoothing (8 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel).

The average time series from 30 ROIs are extracted based on voxel-level timecourses, using the 7-functional-networks (30-ROIs) atlas; the ROIs are defined from CONN’s ICA analyses of HCP dataset, from CONN toolbox. To further reduce non-neural noise in the ROI time series, we applied standard preprocessing steps: regression of head motion parameters, cerebrospinal fluid, and white matter signals, followed by a bandpass filter at [0.01, 0.1] Hz and regression of the global gray matter signal. The dFC matrices are computed on a sliding window of length 25 TR (75 s).

We set the structure of the networks on which the dFC matrices are decomposed as the 7 canonical networks, with some of them divided into relevant subnetworks. We also include all the pairwise links between the 7 networks (see Figure 1b) for a total of 45 (sub)networks based on a first dictionary with only the 7 canonical networks (see list in Figure 2b).

Each estimated activation is thresholded to keep a given percentage of the highest values, given the lack of any prior to set the threshold, we used different values in the range 50% to 5%. For each threshold value, the active and inactive dwell times are computed for each network and subject.

These quantities are tested for difference between the control and DLB groups using a two-sided t test. The tests are corrected for family wise error rate (FWER), where each family is a network and the experiments are the different thresholds on the activations. The probability of incorrectly deciding that a network is affected by the pathology is set at 5%.

Results

The results are presented in Figure 2c. An illustration of outputs of the method, for one subject, is shown in Figure 2b. Among the 45 networks and subnetworks studied, 6 were found to have significant fluctuation differences between the DLB and control groups. The general trend is that the DLB group has a larger number of network activations with shorter activation times (dwell times). This aligns with the hypothesis that DLB patients frequently switch between different connectivity states, and is consistent with the findings from a study showing that DLB patients spent less time in a strongly connected state, despite employing a different methodological approach to investigate dFC.4
 
In more details:
The short dwell time observed in the ACC-RPFC part of the SN is consistent with the reduced activity of the SN in DLB, which is a characteristic feature of the disease.
Similarly, the connection between the ACC-SMG part of the SN and the FPN had a reduced dwell time, suggesting that the SN may struggle to effectively engage the FPN and enable external task-related processes.
The increased number of activations observed in the link between the DMN and VIS, coupled with a shorter dwell time, could reflect an impaired ability to integrate visual information in DLB patients, or may be associated with the occurrence of visual hallucinations.

In contrast to the previous findings, the connection between the DMN and the ACC- RPFC part of the SN had a higher dwell time in DLB patients. This is consistent with the reduced functional interaction between the FPN and the ACC-SMG part of the SN within the triple network framework. This may indicate that DLB patients spend less time in a connectivity state between the SN and the FPN, which is crucial for focusing on external tasks, and are instead more engaged in internal processes through the SN-DMN connection.
 
The interpretation of the two other networks remains more ambiguous. The increased dwell time of the DAN in DLB patients does not align with existing interpretations, while the difference in the mean dwell time between VIS and SMN networks, which approaches the selection threshold (p-value just below), may be driven by outliers in the control group who exhibited very high dwell times. These results will require further investigation.

Conclusion

This study presented an application of a new dFC analysis method to study the effect of DLB on brain connectivity. In addition to being usable and sufficiently robust for single-subject analysis, this new approach allows for the analysis of functional connectivity dynamics at different spatial and temporal scales, whereas state-of-the-art methods are constrained to a global temporal dynamics defined at the whole-brain scale.

While we previously demonstrated disruption of the SN in a prior study using stationary functional imaging,3 this novel approach has provided a more nuanced and refined understanding of dFC in DLB. We were able to detect significant variations in the number of activations and mean dwell time at the subject level within key networks known to be involved in DLB. Notably, the frequent transitions between connectivity states observed here may reflect the cognitive fluctuations characteristic of the disease. More broadly, we believe that this approach holds promise for enhancing the understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving the symptoms of neurocognitive disorders.
