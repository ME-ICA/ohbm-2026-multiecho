Introduction

Working memory (WM) refers to the capacity to maintain information in an active state (maintenance) while concurrently performing online operations on that information (manipulation). Neuroscientific research shows that WM relies on widely distributed cortical substrates, with consistent involvement of frontoparietal regions. In contrast, considerably less attention has been directed toward thalamic contributions to WM. Anatomically, the thalamus is densely interconnected with WM-critical cortical circuits, positioning several nuclei as key modulators of maintenance, manipulation, and attentional control. 

The mediodorsal (MD) nucleus, with strong reciprocal projections to the prefrontal cortex (PFC), supports sustained activity required during WM delays, with research in humans and rodents highlighting its relevance1. The pulvinar, with broad reciprocal connections to parietal, occipital and frontal cortices, appears to coordinate synchrony among thalamocortical regions and attentional selection. In parallel, lesion and electrophysiological evidence indicates that the anterior thalamic nuclei (ATN) are also critical for spatial working memory, exhibiting heightened activity during the delay period and with damage in this region producing severe impairments in performance linked to disrupted hippocampal–retrosplenial interactions2. 

In the present fMRI study, participants performed a WM task with maintenance and manipulation components. We expected regions to be more strongly engaged in manipulation than in maintenance conditions. Specifically, we hypothesized that thalamic regions would be mainly engaged during the delay period, when participants were required to hold information in mind. Frontoparietal cortical regions are expected to be involved during the delay phase as well as during the retrieval phase.

Methods

A total of 72 participants (mean age and SD = 25.6 ± 4.6; 45 females) performed a variation of a standard WM task3 with maintenance and manipulation components (see Fig. 1). During the encoding phase, participants were shown three memory items. Each item appeared for 1 s, and a fixation cross was presented for 300 ms between items. Altogether, this phase lasted 3.9 s. Next, a 1.3 s ‘‘instruction cue’’ (‘‘forward’’ or ‘‘backward’’) appeared on the screen indicating how the three presented items should be kept in WM, followed by the delay period (6.5 s) during which participants either maintained (forward) or manipulated (backward) the order of the three objects. Finally, the retrieval phase presented one of the target images and the number 1, 2, or 3 below on the screen for 2.6 s. At this time, participants pressed one of three buttons with their left hand, to indicate whether the probe corresponded to the first, second, or third item in the remembered order.

Whole-brain MRI data was acquired with a 3-T Siemens TRIO whole-body MRI scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions) using a 64-channel whole-head coil. Standard high-resolution MPRAGE T1-weighted structural images were collected (i.e., TR = 2530 ms; TE  = 2.36 ms; FA  = 7°; FoV = 256 mm; voxel size = 1 mm isotropic; 176 slices). Functional MRI data were acquired in two runs using a gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR = 1300 ms; TE = 35 ms; 68 axial slices; 2.4-mm isotropic voxels; 0% gap; FA = 56°; FOV = 210 mm). Each run included 538 volumes; six initial volumes were discarded for T1 equilibration. During scanning, 24 forward and 24 backward trials were presented in pseudorandom order. 

Preprocessing and analyses were conducted in SPM12. Thermal noise was reduced using the NORDIC PCA denoising algorithm prior to further preprocessing4. Images were slice-timing corrected and realigned using rigid-body motion correction, then smoothed with a 3-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Motion parameters informed volume repair with ArtRepair, which identified problematic volumes based on scan-to-scan motion (>0.1 mm) and global intensity fluctuations (>1.3%); bad volumes were interpolated. Participants requiring >15% repaired volumes were excluded. After repair, functional images were coregistered to structural T1 scans. All analyses were conducted in individual-subject space. 

Statistical analyses used a general linear model (GLM). fMRI time series were modeled as impulses convolved with the canonical HRF. Six motion parameters (x, y, z translations; yaw, pitch, roll) were included as covariates of no interest. The design conformed to a slow event-related design with each correct trial being modeled in three contiguous epochs: encoding (3.9 s), delay (7.8 s), and retrieval (2.6 s). These regressors, along with cosine functions for high-pass filtering, formed the GLM. Parameter estimates reflected the amplitude of the best-fitting HRF for each condition.

To investigate the involvement of thalamic nuclei in WM abilities, we performed a time course analysis of thalamic and cortical ROIs. Thalamic ROIs included the MD, ATN, and pulvinar subregions (anterior, lateral and medial pulvinar, PuA, PuL and PuM respectively). They were segmented in each individual based on the probabilistic atlas of the human thalamus5. As cortical regions, we also included areas known to support WM, including 8C, 46 and SPC. For analysis, each trial was segmented into 11 time points: 3 time points capturing the encoding, 6 time points representing the delay interval, and 2 time points corresponding to the retrieval stage. BOLD raw signal intensity time series from each ROI were extracted from each functional run and participant. Mean time courses for each trial were then constructed by averaging together appropriate trial time courses. To assess condition differences over time, paired t-tests were conducted separately at each of the 11 time points.

Results

As expected, the time-course analyses showed that most thalamic ROIs (MD, PuA, PuM and PuL) exhibited greater activation in the Backward than in the Forward condition during the delay phase, consistent with their involvement in WM maintenance and manipulation. In contrast, the ATN and the cortical regions (i.e., areas 8C and 46 from the dlPFC and the SPC) showed similar differences between Backward and Forward conditions, not only during the delay period but also during the retrieval phase (see Fig. 2). 

Conclusion

Taken together, our results underscore the contribution of thalamic nuclei to human working memory, particularly during the delay period when information must be maintained in memory. The selective Backward–Forward differences observed in thalamic ROIs during the delay phase indicate that these nuclei support the manipulation of information specifically while it is being held in memory. Cortical ROIs showed manipulation-related differences during both the delay and retrieval phases, suggesting a broader role in transforming and accessing stored information throughout the task.

Overall, these findings highlight that thalamic regions, and their interactions with cortical networks, should be incorporated into models of human working memory. Future studies should investigate the structural and functional connectivity within thalamocortical circuits to further elucidate these mechanisms.
