Introduction

Mood disorders are characterized not merely by disruptions in specific emotional states, but by dysfunctional dynamics of emotional regulation. In Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), this is often expressed as repetitive negative thinking (RNT), not as a fixed state but as a recurring pattern in which negative thought content is repeatedly experienced. 1 However, the neural mechanisms governing such dynamic transitions remain poorly understood. This study adopts a dynamic systems perspective to investigate characteristic brain dynamics in individuals with MDD compared with healthy controls (HC). Specifically, we examined brain state transitions and their associated activation costs, quantified as transition energy estimated via Network Control Theory, during emotional reflection, along with their relationship to changes in brain-wide synchronization that reflect moment-to-moment coordination across regions. Here, transition energy refers to the minimum control input required to drive the brain from one state to another within a Network Control Theory framework, where the control input is modeled as internally generated brain activation rather than an external perturbation. We hypothesized that transition energy during emotional reflection would differ across emotional valences and would scale with changes in brain-wide synchronization, reflecting differences in network coordination.

Methods

Thirty individuals with MDD (mean age = 40 ± 12 years, 21 females) and 21 HC (mean age = 45 ± 15 years, 16 females) completed a block-design task involving reflection on personal emotional events. The task included three conditions: past negative (Rumination), future negative (Worry), and positive (Positive). Functional MRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep, 2 and a general linear model (GLM) analysis was performed using AFNI, with regressors modeling task-related responses as well as motion and physiological noise components. To examine spontaneous fluctuations in brain states independently of task-evoked activity, we analyzed the residual signal from the GLM.

Preprocessed signals were parcellated into cortical regions using the HCP-MMP atlas, 3 subcortical regions using the Tian atlas, 4 and cerebellar regions using the Nettekoven atlas. 5 Structural connectivity between these regions was derived from diffusion-weighted imaging and processed with MRtrix3. 6 The resulting connectomes were used to compute transition energy based on Network Control Theory. We calculated the minimum energy required to transition between consecutive brain states at discrete time points, defined as the minimum control input needed to traverse brain state space over time, constrained by each individual’s structural connectivity. 7

Instantaneous brain-wide synchronization was evaluated by applying the Hilbert transform to regional time series to extract instantaneous phase. Synchronization was quantified using the Kuramoto order parameter, which measures the coherence of phase angles across all brain regions at each time point. Changes in synchronization between consecutive time points were used to assess how moment-to-moment variations in network coordination relate to the activation cost of brain state transitions.

Statistical analysis was conducted using a linear mixed-effects (LME) model, with transition energy between consecutive time points as the dependent variable. Prior to modeling, the Euclidean norm of head motion (enorm) at each time point was regressed out from the transition energy. Time points with enorm exceeding 0.3 were censored, and transitions that included any censored time points were excluded from the analysis. Fixed effects included group (MDD vs. HC), emotional state condition (Rumination, Worry, Positive, and Rest), synchronization change between time points, their interactions, age, and sex. A random intercept was included for each subject to account for repeated measures.

Results

The LME analysis revealed significant main effects of emotional condition (p < 0.001) and synchronization change (p < 0.001), as well as significant group-by-condition (p < 0.001) and group-by-synchronization change interactions (p = 0.019). Figure 1 illustrates transition energy across emotional conditions for the MDD and HC groups. Post hoc comparisons within the MDD group showed significantly lower transition energy during the Positive condition compared with Rumination (Cohen’s d = 0.11, p < 0.001), Worry (d = 0.13, p < 0.001), and Rest (d = 0.15, p < 0.001). These differences in the Positive condition were not significant in the HC group. Changes in synchronization were positively associated with transition energy in both MDD (p < 0.001) and HC (p = 0.009), with a significantly stronger association observed in MDD (d = 0.48, p = 0.02).

Conclusion

These results demonstrate that individuals with MDD exhibit altered brain dynamics during emotional reflection, as evidenced by reduced transition energy during positive thinking and increased transition energy during negative states such as rumination and worry. Within a Network Control Theory framework, transition energy represents the minimum internally generated control input required to drive the brain from one state to another, rather than metabolic energy expenditure. Higher transition energy therefore indicates that observed state changes require greater control effort given the underlying structural connectivity, rather than occurring spontaneously or passively.

The significantly lower transition energy during positive thinking in MDD suggests reduced flexibility and diminished coordination of brain networks, potentially reflecting difficulty in actively engaging or sustaining positive emotional states. In contrast, the elevated transition energy observed during negative thinking conditions indicates that the brain is not statically “stuck” in a negative state, but instead undergoes repeated, energetically demanding state transitions during negative thinking. This pattern aligns with the behavioral profile of repetitive negative thinking (RNT) and suggests that such mental states are maintained through ongoing network-level reconfiguration rather than stable occupancy of a single state.

Changes in brain-wide synchronization were positively associated with transition energy, indicating that larger shifts in phase coordination across regions require greater control effort to drive brain state transitions. This coupling was significantly stronger in MDD, suggesting that moment-to-moment fluctuations in global network coordination play a more prominent role in shaping brain state dynamics during emotional reflection in this group. This interpretation is further supported by our previous findings showing that, during rumination, individuals with MDD exhibit increased inter-network connectivity 8 and greater fluctuation in DMN synchronization, 9 reinforcing the view that repetitive negative thinking is an active and effortful process rather than a passive or static one.

Together, these findings provide novel insight into the energetic and synchronization-based properties of brain state dynamics during emotional processing in MDD. By characterizing how control effort and network coordination shape emotional brain dynamics, Network Control Theory offers a principled framework for modeling and predicting how brain states may evolve in response to specific control inputs. Such model-based simulations may help identify network targets and control strategies capable of disrupting maladaptive negative state transitions and facilitating access to positive emotional states. Future work should investigate whether interventions that modulate large-scale brain network coordination, such as neuromodulation or other brain-based approaches, can leverage these predictions to alter the energetic landscape of brain state transitions in MDD.
