Introduction

Subjective cognitive impairment (SCI) or subjective memory complaints (SMC) are self-reported preclinical indicators of future functional decline and possible onset of Alzheimer’s or other dementia. These at-risk individuals with SCI are considered at the early stages of the neurodegenerative diseases, often hypothesized to present subtle brain changes forecasting clinical progression 1. Several neuroimaging studies have identified such progression biomarkers reporting cortical thinning in the medial temporal lobe 2, atrophy in hippocampal subfields 3,4, and structural network-related patterns of white and gray matter degeneration 5,6. Importantly, the differences in global population due to demographic and genetic factors, as well as the dynamics of the aging process confounded by cognitive reserve 1,7 induce variability in the spatiotemporal presentation of these biomarkers in terms of anatomical regions and disease stages. In this study, we evaluate MR imaging biomarkers of 57 Iranian SCI and healthy elderly individuals with clinical follow-up over multiple years. We compare the results with an SCI cohort from ADNI to assess similarity of cortical and subcortical patterns presentations in these two populations. 

Methods

The TUMS cohort includes SCI data collected from individuals reporting memory and cognitive issues. Detailed neuropsychological evaluations (MMSE/MoCA equivalent) and medical history was obtained from all individuals by expert neurologists during multiple visits spanned over 4 years. Only individuals scoring within a healthy range at baseline evaluations were included in this study. Individuals with cognitive decline were identified based on an expert in-person assessment and a loss of 2 points or more on neuropsychological tests. The imaging data collection included T1w and FLAIR 3T MRI scans acquired on Siemens Prisma with MPRAGE sequence. 

The Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) 8 is a longitudinal, multi-center, observational study comprising predominantly North American populations. The ADNI sub-cohort in this study includes age-matched SCI individuals with at least 3 longitudinal visits. The individuals showing loss of 2 or more MMSE points within 2-4 years were identified as cognitive decliners. Individuals with unexplained improvements or fluctuating score patterns were considered outliers and excluded from analysis. 

Both TUMS and ADNI cohorts were processed identically using FreeSurfer 7.3.2 within the Nipoppy framework 9. The FreeSurfer outputs were further segmented using Sequence Adaptive Multimodal SEGmentation (SAMSEG) algorithm to calculate subregional volumes of the hippocampus. The cortical thickness measures were aggregated using Desikan-Killiany atlas 10. 

The structural differences between cognitively stable and decliner groups were identified using w-scores. The statistical modeling involves first estimation of linear regressions between regional brain estimates and age, sex, and education in the reference cohort (i.e. stable group), followed by calculation of regional w-scores using the following formula:

𝑤(𝑖) = 𝑟𝑎𝑤_decliner(𝑖) − 𝑒𝑥𝑝𝑒𝑐𝑡𝑒𝑑_stable (𝑖) / 𝑆⁢𝐷(𝑟𝑒𝑠) 

Scores were calculated independently for TUMS and ADNI cohorts and values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. 

Results

No significant differences in regional cortical thickness and subcortical volumetric measures were found across the two population cohorts (Figure 1a,c). The TUMS cohort showed significant differences in cortical thickness measures at bilateral inferior-temporal, left supramarginal, and right superior-temporal regions. There were no significant differences in cortical thickness values in the ADNI cohort however the w-score maps show varying atrophy patterns with some overlap with the TUMS w-score maps at bilateral superior and inferior temporal regions. The volumetric comparisons of hippocampal subfields yielded significant differences in the CA3-head region in the ADNI cohort. No significant volumetric differences were found in the TUMS cohort, indicating that one could conjointly use these cohorts in future studies. 

Conclusion

Neuroimaging studies involving global SCI populations with longitudinal clinical assessments are of importance for biomarker development and early detection of Alzheimer’s disease. The preliminary results suggest similar neuroanatomical markers in the temporal cortical regions in the TUMS cohort as reported in the literature. The absence of significant results in the hippocampal volumetric analysis is possibly due to small sample size. We note the ongoing nature of this study and aim to update these results with additional subjects in the near future.
