Introduction

The extent to which conventionally “motor” and “premotor” brain areas are involved in non-motor cognitive functions has many implications for neuroscience, including understanding the organization, development, and evolution of the frontal lobe. An area of particular interest for addressing this question is near the junction of precentral gyrus and middle frontal gyrus (MFG), approximately where the recently identified and structurally distinct “area 55b” is located. Area 55b is immediately adjacent to motor cortex but seems to be engaged in more abstract processing1. Further, recent studies have shown that a mid-precentral area (i) shows BOLD activation when participants listen to stories compared to baseline1, (ii) is activated by both an expressive and a receptive verbal working memory (WM) fMRI task2, (iii) may support phonological transformations for speech production3, (iv) is hypothesized to support both vocal pitch production and perception4, and (v) is engaged during working memory tasks in auditory, visual, and tactile modalities5. However, a core challenge in making inferences about shared vs. distinct cognitive and motor functions of a particular region based on evidence collected in different studies is the uncertainty in spatial alignment, not only across studies but also across the individual brains in them. Here, we performed a data-driven characterization of the functional profile of precentral regions in individual brains using fMRI data from six tasks that differentially engage speech production, receptive language, verbal working memory, and nonverbal working memory systems. We employed data-driven functional parcellations of individuals’ precentral cortical surfaces to directly address competing viewpoints on the non-motoric functional role of the precentral gyrus and adjacent MFG.

Methods

Task-fMRI, anatomical MRI, and diffusion MRI were collected from 24 young adults with typical speech, language, and hearing. Tasks (and contrasts of interest) included a receptive language localizer (listening to intact > acoustically degraded speech), nonword repetition (repeating 4-syllable > 1-syllable nonwords), real word repetition (4-syllable > 1-syllable), nonword discrimination (indicating if second nonword matched first nonword; 4-syllable > 1-syllable nonwords), a visuospatial sequence working memory task (6-item > 3-item), and an auditorily presented digit span task (6-digit > 3-digit). 

Functional volumes were processed with Lyman v1.0.0 workflows, and contrast parameter estimate volumes were projected to each individual’s cortical surface. Then, for each individual and for each task, the task-fMRI response values within a precentral mask were z-scored and combined in a matrix (surface vertices x scaled task responses). The mask included posterior MFG, middle premotor cortex (midPMC), middle motor cortex (midMC), and ventral motor cortex (vMC) as specified by the SpeechLabeling atlas. Then, k-means clustering was performed on the scaled task-fMRI responses across all subjects. This process was repeated separately for the left and right hemisphere precentral parcels with optimal k-values determined for each hemisphere based on the average sum of squared errors (SSE) and the cluster-specific silhouette scores. In each hemisphere, there was a cluster specifically responsive to receptive language (“language cluster”) and another to overt speech (“speech cluster”). Further, there was another cluster that was engaged by all 6 tasks (“supramodal WM cluster”). Because these three clusters had consistent functional profiles and locations across many k-values, and were of interest due to their functional specializations, the following analyses used only these three clusters.

To investigate the structural distinctiveness of these three clusters, we trained a classifier with nested leave-one-subject-out cross validation (LOOCV) to classify cortical surface vertices as belonging to the language, speech, or supramodal WM cluster. The structural features, represented vertex-wise, included intracortical myelin content (T1w/T2w), curvature, cortical thickness, fractional anisotropy, axial diffusivity, and connectivity with cortical and subcortical (including thalamic nuclei) regions. The connectivity measure was the number of streamlines seeded from a seed vertex that reached a given target region (SpeechLabeling atlas) in probabilistic tractography (FSL’s probtrackx). The classifier was implemented with glmnet’s multinomial regression with an elastic net penalty, allowing for automatic feature selection. Model fit was assessed by the accuracy of left-out-subject predictions. An additional model was fitted on all 24 subjects, and these standardized coefficients were visualized and interpreted as important predictors to the locations of each cluster.

Model accuracy was tested for statistical significance by comparing one-vs-rest AUC-ROC values to chance performance (AUC=0.5) with a one-sided Mann Whitney U-Test. There was one p-value per individual per cluster due to the LOOCV structure (i.e. individual-specific models). We corrected for multiple comparisons with the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and we report the percent of individuals with significant results (corrected p<0.05).

Lastly, because the fMRI-localized language network is typically localized only with the one task, we compared the locations of the supramodal WM cluster and the univariate language fROI. Individual subjects’ language-selective fROIs were defined using only the univariate language localizer contrast. We computed the degree of overlap (intersection / union) between the two on individuals’ surfaces to measure how much the language-selective fROI overlaps with the non-language-specific yet language-selective (i.e., engaged in other domains as well) cluster.

Results

Using a data-driven clustering approach to functionally parcellate the target precentral area, we found a consistent pattern across hemispheres and across subjects of a more anterior language-specific cluster (“language cluster”), a cluster that is speech-specific (“speech cluster”) mostly in ventral motor cortex, and a cluster that is responsive to all 6 tasks (“supramodal WM cluster”) at the junction of these language and speech clusters (see LH results in Fig. 2). The optimal number of clusters (k) was determined to be 7 in the left hemisphere and 6 in the right hemisphere, but these 3 clusters, as well as a default-mode cluster (all negative task responses) and a multiple-demand cluster (responsive to only digit span and Corsi blocks), were present and in consistent locations across multiple k-values.

The structural classifier’s predicted cluster labels for the left-out-subjects’ data were, on average, 56.8% (sd = 8.3%) correct for the LH, and 42.9% (sd = 6.9%) for the RH (correct labels / total parcel vertices). For cluster-specific performance, the average jaccard indices (intersection / union) of the predicted and k-means labels were 0.37 (LH) and 0.32 (RH), 0.24 (LH) and 0.21 (RH) for supramodal WM, and 0.50 (LH) and 0.28 (RH) for speech. The average one-vs-rest AUC-ROC values were 0.74 (LH) and 0.66 (RH) for language, 0.75 (LH) and 0.64 (RH) for supramodal WM, and 0.75 (LH) and 0.60 (RH) for speech. These AUC-ROC values were significantly greater than chance (AUC=0.5) for 96% of individuals in each of the three LH clusters and for 83% (language), 79% (supramodal WM), and 88% (speech) of individuals in the RH clusters.

The two most positive fitted coefficients for the language cluster in each hemisphere were connectivity with pars opercularis (LH and RH) and angular gyrus (LH) or posterior dorsal PMC (RH). For the supramodal WM cluster, these were cortical thickness and connectivity with supplementary motor area (SMA) in both hemispheres. For the speech cluster, these were curvature (LH and RH; i.e. vertices located more sulcally) and intracortical myelin (LH) or connectivity with superior frontal gyrus (SFG; RH). Across the hemispheres, the language cluster was consistently associated with increased connectivity with pars opercularis, posterior inferior frontal sulcus (pIFS), and parietal areas, as well as reduced intracortical myelin. The speech cluster bilaterally was associated with a more sulcal location and increased somatosensory and SFG connectivity. The supramodal WM area in both hemispheres was associated with a more gyral location, increased cortical thickness, and increased connectivity with SMA and precuneus. 

The final feature sets also included many subcortical connectivity predictors, including, notably, connectivity with thalamic nuclei, with distinct hemispheric differences. The most positive thalamic predictors in the left hemisphere were ventromedial (language) and medioventral (speech). In the right hemisphere, these were MGN (language) and LGN (supramodal WM).

When comparing language-selectivity based on the univariate task fROI to language-specificity based on the data-driven clustering analysis, we found that the language-selective fROIs tended to spatially overlap the supramodal WM cluster, with average Jaccard indices across subjects of 0.12 (sd = 0.09) in the left hemisphere and 0.13 (sd = 0.10) in the right hemisphere. On average, 44% (sd = 25%) of the supramodal WM cluster in the LH and 22% (sd = 13%) in the RH was found within the nominally language-selective fROI.

Conclusion

Here, we present evidence that supports and clarifies recent claims that there is (i) a mid-precentral area structurally distinct from motor cortex1, (ii) a mid-precentral area involved in supramodal WM5, and (iii) a mid-precentral area specifically involved in higher-level language processing1,3. Our study bridges these disjoint investigations into the working memory precentral area and the language precentral area, finding that there is a language-specific area directly adjacent to an area engaged in both language and supramodal WM processing. We also novelly report the consistent motif of a pMFG–midPMC language area, a ventral MC speech motor area, and a primarily midMC-located supramodal WM area at the junction of the speech and language areas.

    Our work has implications for region-of-interest-based analyses broadly, emphasizing the importance of functional localization as opposed to averaging values within entire, functionally heterogeneous anatomical areas. These regions may overlap across individuals when normalized to a standard coordinate space, but yet be functionally dissociable in each individual subject. We found that the typical fMRI-localized language network includes a functionally heterogeneous precentral node with a more broadly tuned sub-region, arguing for more fine-grained analyses of functionally localized brain networks. The function of this supramodal WM region remains unclear, and additional work is needed to understand if and how the supramodal WM area and the language area uniquely contribute to language processing.

    This work also relates to the discussions over the criteria for considering a given brain region to be a “cortical area.” The combination of the unique functional profiles demonstrated by fMRI clustering results and the unique structural profiles characterized with the regression model support the notion that these language, supramodal WM, and speech areas may be considered natural kinds in the human brain’s functional organization.
