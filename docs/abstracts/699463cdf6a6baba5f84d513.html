Introduction

Multilingualism is a defining characteristic of Pakistan’s linguistic landscape, where individuals routinely navigate multiple languages shaped by social, educational, and economic factors. Among these, Punjabi, despite being the most widely spoken native language, occupies a complex position due to shifting language attitudes, urbanization, and socioeconomic stratification. While previous research has extensively examined bilingual and multilingual language processing, the role of socioeconomic status (SES) in shaping the neural mechanisms of multilingual language use remains underexplored, particularly in South Asian contexts.

Most existing studies on language and SES rely on self-reported measures, questionnaires, or behavioral tests, offering limited insight into the underlying neurocognitive processes. This methodological gap restricts our understanding of how linguistic exposure, dominance, and proficiency interact with brain-based language control systems. Neuroimaging approaches provide a valuable means to address this limitation by directly observing cortical activation during language processing tasks.

The present study employs functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) to investigate how high- and low-SES Punjabi multilingual speakers process language during auditory comprehension and language-switching tasks involving Punjabi, Urdu, and English. By focusing on the prefrontal cortex, a region crucial for executive control, working memory, and language switching, this research aims to uncover SES-driven differences in neural activation patterns and behavioral accuracy.This study contributes novel neuro-linguistic evidence on heritage language processing, offering insights into how socioeconomic environments shape multilingual language control.

Methods

The study examined the neural processing of Punjabi, Urdu, and English in multilingual Punjabi speakers from high and low socioeconomic backgrounds using functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS). Data were collected with the NIRSport2 and acquired through Aurora, while nirsLAB and Satori were used for preprocessing and channel-activation verification.

 Optodes were positioned over the prefrontal cortex, following the 10–20 system, to capture activity related to cognitive control and language switching. Two experimental paradigms were used. (1) Auditory Task: Participants listened to 3-second Punjabi audio clips (extracted from high-engagement PTV dramas), mentally processed them for 2 seconds, and verbally translated them into Urdu. Each session lasted 4.5 minutes and followed a structured rest task cycle. (2) Language-Switching Task: Using standardized Snodgrass & Vanderwart images, participants produced sentences in Punjabi, Urdu, and English according to a given prompt, switching languages across trials. Fifteen trials were completed following five practice items. All sessions were audio-recorded. Accuracy was scored through a rubric combining Brown’s (2004) assessment criteria, Corder’s (1974) error analysis, and CEFR (2001) descriptors. 40 neurotypical Punjabi speakers (20 high SES, 20 low SES), aged 18–25, were selected using LEAP-Q and Kuppuswamy’s SES scale. Ethical protocols (Declaration of Helsinki) were followed, and participants were screened for health conditions affecting neural signals. fNIRS preprocessing included artifact rejection, a 0.01–0.3 Hz band-pass filter, and conversion to HbO/HbR using the modified Beer Lambert law. Channel activation was identified using the t-value method, applying a two-gamma HRF model and a critical threshold for significant hemodynamic response

Results

Accuracy Outcomes: Low SES participants consistently outperformed high SES participants in Punjabi. – Auditory Task (Punjabi): Low SES = 81.75%; -High SES = 69.75%

 – Language Switching (Punjabi): Low SES = 83.25%; High SES = 66.50%

 – Urdu (Switching): Both groups = 86.50% 

– English (Switching): Low SES = 73.50%; High SES = 68.00% 

A t-test for auditory Punjabi translation yielded a significant p-value (0.0012), confirming stronger auditory–verbal processing in low SES speakers. 

A one-way ANOVA showed: – Punjabi: Significant difference (p = 0.0008), favoring low SES speakers

 – Urdu: No significant difference (p = 1.0) 

– English: No significant difference (p = 0.9875)

Prefrontal Channel Activation Patterns

- The dorsolateral and medial prefrontal regions were seen highly activated in both High and Low SES groups.

- During the auditory task, high-SES participants showed stronger activation in left prefrontal channels (BA 8, BA 9, BA 10, BA 46).

- Low-SES participants exhibited broader bilateral and right-hemispheric activation, especially in BA 9, BA 10, and BA 46.

- In the language switching task, the low SES participants showed dominant activation in right DLPC region, while the high SES participants displayed left and bilateral activation. 


Conclusion

This study demonstrates that socioeconomic status significantly influences both behavioral performance and neural activation during multilingual language processing. Low-SES Punjabi speakers showed higher accuracy and stronger prefrontal activation for Punjabi tasks, reflecting greater language dominance through everyday use. In contrast, high-SES participants exhibited increased cognitive effort and compensatory neural engagement during Punjabi processing. Language-switching tasks further revealed greater right-hemispheric involvement in low-SES speakers, indicating higher executive demands when managing less dominant languages. Overall, the findings highlight the interaction between socioeconomic exposure, language proficiency, and neural resource allocation, emphasizing the importance of considering SES in neurolinguistic research.
