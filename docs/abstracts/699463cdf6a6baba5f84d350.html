Introduction

Research in entrepreneurship has long sought to identify what distinguishes entrepreneurs from others. Early work adopted a trait-based perspective, assuming that entrepreneurs possess unique personality characteristics driving venture creation 1. However, inconsistent findings and heterogeneous measurement approaches have led many authors to conclude that personality traits alone cannot reliably differentiate entrepreneurs from managers or the general population. This shift has prompted increasing interest in the cognitive foundations of entrepreneurial behaviour, giving rise to the field of entrepreneurial cognition.

Within this line of research, several studies have examined cognitive flexibility (CF), the ability to adapt to novel, uncertain, or stressful situations —conditions that are intrinsic to entrepreneurial environments. Empirical work shows that entrepreneurs tend to exhibit higher CF than managers 2,3, suggesting that CF may either predispose individuals to entrepreneurship or develop throughout the entrepreneurial journey.

At the neural level, recent findings further support this view. Ooms et al. 3 reported greater grey matter volume in the left insula in experienced entrepreneurs compared to managers, and the volume of this region was associated with CF. Complementing this, Ooms et al., 4 demonstrated increased resting-state functional connectivity between the right insula and the right frontal pole in entrepreneurs compared to managers. Both regions play a central role in CF and decision-making under uncertainty.

These converging results suggest the existence of neural markers of entrepreneurship linked to CF. Yet, it remains unknown whether these functional differences are mirrored in the structural organization of white-matter tracts. The present work aims to address this gap by investigating structural connectivity differences between experienced entrepreneurs and managers.

Methods

Participants: Twenty-three habitual entrepreneurs (≥3 venture creations; 2 females; mean age = 49.48 ± 8.17) and seventeen managers without entrepreneurial experience (3 females; mean age = 42.35 ± 7.97) participated. This sample is the same as the one reported in Ooms et al., 4 . Groups differed significantly in age (p = 0.009); this variable was therefore included as a covariate in group comparisons.

MRI Acquisition and Preprocessing: Given our aim to assess structural connectivity markers of entrepreneurship, we acquired diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) data and conducted several complementary analyses. Processing was performed in MRtrix3 and FSL. Preprocessing included MP-PCA denoising, susceptibility distortion correction using SynB0-DISCO and TOPUP, brain-mask creation, eddy current and motion correction, and B1 field inhomogeneity correction.

Whole-Brain Diffusion Tensor Analysis: To explore global differences in white-matter microstructure, diffusion tensor imaging metrics—fractional anisotropy (FA), axial diffusivity (AD), radial diffusivity (RD), and mean diffusivity (MD)—were computed for each participant. Whole-brain voxelwise comparisons were conducted using the standard TBSS pipeline. Statistical testing used TFCE with permutation-based inference, including age as a covariate. Multiple comparisons were controlled and maps were thresholded at p < 0.05.

Tractography Analyses: With a multi-shell approach, tractography was used to assess structural connectivity at the tract level. A first hypothesis-driven analysis was conducted. Based on prior resting-state findings, we tested whether entrepreneurs show stronger structural connectivity between the right insula and right frontal pole. ROIs were taken from the Harvard–Oxford atlas, matching the regions used in Ooms et al. (2024). A second exploratory connectome-wide tractography was conducted, to identify broader structural connectivity differences. In this analysis, a whole-brain connectome was generated using ROIs from the Desikan–Killiany atlas. For both analyses, ROIs from the relevant atlases were transformed into each participant’s diffusion space. The following pipeline was applied: estimation of response functions, computation of fibre orientation distributions (FODs), intensity normalization, grey–white matter interface segmentation, and generation of streamlines using anatomically constrained tractography. From the reconstructed tracts linking each pair of ROIs, we extracted streamline density, FA, AD, RD, and MD for group comparison. This produced one pair comparison for the hypothesis-driven analysis, and connectome matrices representing the metric values for each pair of ROIs for the exploratory analysis. Group comparisons were conducted using t-tests. In the hypothesis-driven analysis, a regression analysis including group and age as predictor was implemented. The potential links of the connectivity between insula and frontal pole with CF was also tested with correlation analyses. In the exploratory analysis, FDR corrections were applied across all ROI-to-ROI connections.

This multimodal DWI approach enabled us to test targeted hypotheses on insula–frontal pole connectivity while also exploring whole-brain structural organization differences between habitual entrepreneurs and managers.

Results

Diffusion Tensor Imaging (TBSS): Whole-brain voxelwise analyses of diffusion tensor metrics (FA, MD, AD, RD) revealed no significant differences between entrepreneurs and managers. TFCE-corrected statistical maps did not show any cluster surviving multiple-comparison correction when controlling for age. These results suggest that global white-matter microstructural properties do not differ between groups at the tensor level.

Hypothesis-Driven Tractography (Right Insula–Right Frontal Pole): We tested the hypothesis, derived from resting-state findings, that entrepreneurs would show enhanced structural connectivity between the right insula and right frontal pole. Tractography-based meconfirmed these results: nor grouasures (streamline density, FA, MD, AD, RD) extracted from the reconstructed streamlines showed no significant group differences. Regression analyses including group, age, and their interactionp neither age significantly predicted any structural connectivity measure. Group × Age interactions were not significant. Additionally, no significant correlations were observed between cognitive flexibility scores and any of the structural measures of insula–frontal pole connectivity. Thus, the functional connectivity differences previously observed between these regions do not appear to be mirrored by structural differences in this sample.

Exploratory Connectome-Wide Tractography: The exploratory whole-brain analysis using Desikan–Killiany ROI-to-ROI tractography similarly revealed no significant group differences after false discovery rate correction across all connections and metrics (streamline density, FA, AD, RD, MD). Before correction, some isolated edges showed nominal trends, but none survived FDR adjustment.

Conclusion

Results showed that in a same sample of individuals, the structural connectivity does not reflect functional markers of entrepreneurship. Importantly, the absence of structure–function correspondence is not surprising. Numerous studies have shown that functional connectivity does not necessarily correlate with white-matter anatomy, particularly within higher-order networks such as the salience and frontopolar systems 5. The functional differences previously reported in entrepreneurs—especially within the insula–frontal pole circuit—may reflect dynamic network adaptation that do not depend on long-range structural changes. These mechanisms could support enhanced cognitive flexibility without requiring detectable macrostructural white-matter differences.

Consistent with this interpretation, we also observed no association between cognitive flexibility scores and tract-based diffusion metrics. This further suggests that cognitive-flexibility mechanisms in entrepreneurs may rely predominantly on functional network dynamics, such as salience detection or network switching efficiency, rather than on the microstructural properties of large-scale white-matter pathways.

Limitations: This study represents the first diffusion MRI investigation in a population of habitual entrepreneurs, and the sample size—while typical for advanced neuroimaging—remains limited. White-matter differences between healthy adult groups are likely subtle and detecting them may require larger samples or more sensitive modelling approaches.

Perspectives and Future Directions: Future analyses will aim to refine connectome-level inference using methods tailored to structural networks, such as Network-Based Statistic (NBS), which may improve sensitivity to distributed connectivity differences. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether neural differences observed in entrepreneurs reflect pre-existing predispositions or experience-dependent plasticity. To address this, a new project led by our team is investigating four groups with increasing levels of entrepreneurial experience, allowing us to map trajectories of cognitive flexibility and brain markers across the entrepreneurial journey.

Another promising direction is to integrate structural, functional, and behavioural data into unified network-level models to better characterize the neurocognitive architecture of entrepreneurship. Overall, these findings underscore the importance of multimodal neuroimaging in advancing the understanding of entrepreneurial cognition and its neural foundations.
