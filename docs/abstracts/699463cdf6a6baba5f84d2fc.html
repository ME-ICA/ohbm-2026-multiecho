Introduction

Our understanding of the mechanisms that give rise to the brain’s large-scale functional organization, which is thought to support cognition and behaviour, remains limited. Neural field theory (NFT), an established biophysical mean-field model of large-scale brain activity, offers a physically principled framework for uncovering these mechanisms. By treating the cortex as a continuous medium, NFT describes neocortical activity as emerging from waves of excitation propagating across the cortical sheet1. These waves decompose into resonant standing-wave patterns given by the eigenmodes of cortical geometry, an equivalence given by the Helmholtz equation used in diverse areas of physics and engineering1,2.

Recent work has demonstrated that NFT can account for diverse neurophysiological properties, including macroscale cortical and subcortical dynamics2, regional organization3, and structural connectivity patterns4. Despite this progress, a key prediction of NFT has been difficult to verify; namely, that the eigenmodes of cortical geometry (termed geometric eigenmodes) should correspond to the brain’s functional modes (often termed functional gradients). For instance, the most dominant non-uniform geometric eigenmode spatially varies along the rostrocaudal axis2, whereas the dominant covariance mode of functional connectivity measures varies along the sensorimotor-association (SA) axis5. How can these two spatial organizations be reconciled?

In this work, we use the technique of perturbation theory, borrowed from quantum mechanics, to investigate how spatial variations in cortical properties, such as myelination and thickness, can distort the geometric eigenmodes. Specifically, we show that these distortions change the spatial structure of the geometric eigenmodes that closely follows the empirical functional gradients. These results provide a principled framework for reconciling NFT predictions with empirical data and bridging cortical geometry and function.

Methods

Our approach begins with the geometric eigenmodes of the cortex, obtained by performing an eigendecomposition of the Laplace–Beltrami operator (LBO) of the cortical surface. These modes form an ordered basis of spatial patterns, from long- to short-wavelengths. A key assumption of the standard LBO is that only the intrinsic geometric properties (i.e., spatial relationships between vertices) influence the cortical eigenmodes. However, we know that the cortex is enriched by non-geometric features (e.g., myelination) that influence neural dynamics. Here, we hypothesize that diverse non-geometric cortical maps (e.g., myelination, thickness) (Fig. 1) perturb the LBO, leading to new sets of eigenmodes with spatial patterns that deviate from the original geometric eigenmodes. 

We estimate the perturbation effects of each cortical map using first-order perturbation theory derived from classical formulations in quantum mechanics. Under this perturbation theory, each perturbed mode becomes a weighted mixture of the original geometric modes. The degree of mixing between any two modes depends on: (i) how strongly their spatial patterns overlap with the perturbing cortical maps; and (ii) the proximity of their eigenvalues, which governs how efficiently perturbations couple them. Modes that are both spatially aligned with the perturbing map and spectrally close contribute most strongly. Figure 1 shows a schematic of the proposed framework. 

We will then assess via Spearman correlation whether these perturbations lead to perturbed modes with spatial organization that matches the functional gradients. Note that we will specifically focus on how well the first dominant perturbed mode aligns with the principal functional gradients (Fig. 1).

Results

Figure 2A displays the empirical functional gradient used as the target. Applying perturbation theory with only the myelination map yielded a perturbed eigenmode with a Spearman correlation of 0.65 relative to the functional gradient (Fig. 2B). When combining seven cortical maps selected via LASSO regression as the optimal set of perturbations, the correspondence improved to 0.70 (Fig. 2C). Finally, as a proof of principle, we inverted the problem to identify the single spatial map that maximizes the alignment between the perturbed mode and the functional gradient, achieving a correlation of 0.90 (Fig. 2D).

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that perturbation theory offers a promising framework for linking cortical geometry to functional organization. Thus far, we have focused on recovering the principal functional gradient, but ongoing work will extend this analysis to the second and third gradients.
