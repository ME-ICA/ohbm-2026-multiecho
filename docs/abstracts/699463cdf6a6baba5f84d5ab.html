Introduction

Time-reversibility, reflecting the directional of information flow in a time-series, is critical for understanding brain dynamics. If a time-series can not be distinguished from its reversed counterpart, then it is called time-reversible. Since complete time-reversibility in the brain is rare, we measure the degree of time-reversibility.  

Deco et al. (2022) introduced the TENET framework to directly measure time-reversibility by training a support vector machine to classify forward vs. reversed time-series, with higher accuracy being equivalent to more non-reversiblity.^1  Their results show that the non-reversibility of the Yeo-7 networks depends on the task, such as working memory tasks show highest non-reversibility in the Visual network and lowest in the Limbic network. 

While TENET measures time-reversibility, it leaves two gaps: it neither shows whether causal interactions reverse with time-series reversal, nor directly link non-reversibility to information flow. Although non-reversibility is theoretically a proxy for information flow, no direct measure currently confirms this. 

We address these gaps in our study. For the first one, we define time-reversibility as the degree of causal reversibility/conservability in reversed time-series i.e., whether causal interactions between regions reverse when the time-series are reversed.^2 We test this in Yeo-7 networks during movie watching and resting state, and compare results to TENET to evaluate if time-reversibility reflects true causal interactions.

Secondly, we generate brain surface maps of the degree of causal reversibility between the semantic input of the movie watching task and each of brain regions of the Glasser 360 atlas, and then compare the results with the Transfer Entropy maps from Kumar et al 2025.^3 This directly compares causal reversibility to Transfer Entropy, bridging the gap between time-reversibility and information flow.

Methods

Downloading the HCP dataset: We analyzed resting-state and movie-watching 7 Tesla fMRI data from 182 healthy young adults (age 22–35) downloaded from the Human Connectome Project (HCP, db.humanconnectome.org). Preprocessed and ICA-FIX denoised voxel-wise BOLD signals were used to extract subject-wise time series by averaging signals within the Glasser 360 cortical regions. Movie-watching runs included short rest periods interleaved between clips, and further preprocessing included linear detrending and exclusion of the first and last rest periods of the movie runs.

Mapping the Glasser 360 regions to Yeo 7 networks: We downloaded the masks for the Yeo-7 networks from https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/fswiki/CorticalParcellation_Yeo2011 and each of the Glasser 360 regions were mapped to one of the yeo 7 networks based on the maximum number of overlapped voxels. 

Extracting the semantic time-series: We extracted the auditory time-series from the 7T HCP movie stimulus and transcribed speech using the Google Cloud Speech-to-Text API. Transcriptions were manually checked for accuracy. Two natural language processing approaches were then applied to derive semantic time-series: (1) Sentence-BERT to compute sentence embeddings and their similarity, and (2) WordNet to quantify word concreteness as a measure of semantic depth.^3

Measuring causal reversibility and conservability: To measure interactions within each of the 7 Yeo networks, we fit a multivariate VAR(1) process for each network :

where A is the forward coefficient matrix, and B is the reverse coefficient matrix.

Forward: Xt = A Xt-1 + n

Reverse: Xt = B Xt+1 + e

A complete reversal of causality occurs when

[A]ij = [B]ji OR AT = B OR BT = A

This condition holds only if A is a normal matrix:^2

AT A = A AT

A special case is when AT = A OR A = B, in which case complete conservability occurs.

We derive four metrics of time-reversibility following:^2

  • Deviation from reversibility: dc_norm = ||A - BT||F / ||A + BT||F

  • Deviation from normality: d_norm = ||A AT - AT A||F / ||A AT + AT A||F

  • Deviation from conservability: dc_sym = ||A - B||F / ||A + B||F

  • Deviation from symmetry: d_sym = ||A - AT||F / ||A + AT||F

where ||⋅||F denotes the Frobenius norm.

We compute these four metrics for each of the 7 Yeo networks for individual subjects as well as for subject-averaged data, and compare metrics across networks and with the TENET framework.

To derive the metrics for semantics, we fit 360 bivariate VAR(1) processes, where one time-series is the semantic time-series (either Sentence Similarity or Word Depth) and the other is the BOLD time-series from a given region. Note that the semantic time-series remains the same across all regions, while the BOLD time-series varies. We then calculate the four metrics for all 360 regions and generate brain surface plots to visualize regions that are most non-reversible with respect to the semantics. Finally, we compare these brain-surface plots with Transfer Entropy maps.^3

Results

We divide out results into three sections

Relationship between dc_norm and d_norm, and dc_sym and d_sym: For the subject averaged time-series, dc_norm and d_norm are positively correlated with all the 7 yeo networks roughly fitting a straight line, with d_norm (deviation from normality) predicting the dc_norm (deviation from reversibility). The same holds true for dc_sym and d_sym, also for metrics derived for individual subjects. 

Statistics for subject averaged time-series: Deviation from reversibility (dc_norm) and deviation from normality (d_norm) Movie Watching - R2 = 0.94, p < 0.001, Rest - R2 = 0.94, p < 0.001; Deviation from conservability (dc_sym) and deviation from symmetry (d_sym) Movie Watching - R2 = 0.91, p < 0.01, Rest - R2 = 0.99, p < 0.001.

Statistics over all subjects:  Deviation from reversibility (dc_norm) and deviation from normality (d_norm) Movie Watching - R2 = 0.94, p < 0.001, Rest - R2 = 0.93, p < 0.001; Deviation from conservability (dc_sym) and deviation from symmetry (d_sym) Movie Watching - R2 = 0.98, p < 0.001, Rest - R2 = 0.97, p < 0.001.

Comparison with TENET: Our four non-reversibility metrics calculated from forward and reversed are higher in movie watching than in resting state, which is predicted by the TENET model.^1 For the subject-averaged metrics of the Yeo-7 networks, our results are also in agreement with TENET , starting from the lowest non-reversibility of Limbic, then Control, Visual Attention, Dorsal Attention, DMN, Somatomotor and Visual networks. This order is the same across all four metrics. For individual subjects, the order changes slightly, with Limbic having lowest non-reversibility, then Visual Attention, Dorsal Attention, Control, Somatomotor, Visual and DMN. 

Comparison with Transfer Entropy results: Our results measuring time-reversibility with respect to the input are similar to the Transfer Entropy findings^3 for both Sentence Similarity and Word Depth. Specifically, during movie viewing, Transfer Entropy showed robust positive correlations with the four metrics across the 360 Glasser regions, with consistently stronger effects for Word Depth than for Sentence Similarity (see figure). In contrast, during rest, these associations were weak and non-significant, indicating that absence of information flow also leads to loss of arrow of time. 

Statistics for movie Watching: For Sentence Similarity, TE correlated with dcnorm (Spearman ρ = 0.45, FDR p < 0.05), dnorm (ρ = 0.44, FDR p < 0.05), dsym (ρ = 0.43, FDR p < 0.05), and more weakly with dcsym (ρ = 0.17, FDR p < 0.05). For Word Depth, TE correlated with dcnorm (ρ = 0.58, FDR p < 0.05), dnorm (ρ = 0.54, FDR p < 0.05), dcsym (ρ = 0.53, FDR p < 0.05), and dsym (ρ = 0.53, FDRp < 0.05). 

Statistics for rest: During rest, correlations between Transfer Entropy and all causal-reversibility metrics were weak and not significant after FDR correction for both Sentence Similarity (|ρ| ≤ 0.10, all FDR p ≥ 0.12) and Word Depth (|ρ| ≤ 0.10, all FDR p ≥ 0.14).

Conclusion

In this study, we applied the causal-reversibility metrics introduced previously to brain networks and found them to be meaningful and consistent.^2 This agreement is not theoretically guaranteed but arises because most causal interactions in these data are well captured by a first-order VAR process. This validates the use of VAR(1)-based metrics for quantifying causal reversibility and conservability in fMRI-derived networks.

With confidence in our metrics, we addressed two key questions: (1) do causal interactions reverse when the time-series are reversed, i.e., does causal reversibility reflect true time-reversibility, and (2) can non-reversibility serve as a proxy for information flow between time-series?

The first question is answered by comparing the Yeo-7 networks: the ordering of networks based on our causal reversibility/conservability metrics matches the ordering obtained using TENET time-reversibility metrics.^1 Movie-watching shows overall lower causal reversibility than resting state, consistent with TENET, demonstrating that causal reversibility is equivalent to time-reversibility at the network level. Secondly, we provide experimental evidence that non-reversibility serves as a proxy for information flow. Specifically, during movie watching, all four causal-reversibility metrics show significant positive correlations with Transfer Entropy across the 360 Glasser regions, with stronger effects for Word Depth than for Sentence Similarity. In contrast, during rest, these associations are weak and not significant, indicating that non-reversibility reliably reflects the presence of meaningful information flow from the input to the brain.

One limitation of this study is the use of a VAR(1) model with only the first lag, chosen for computational simplicity. While the metrics perform well for measuring time-reversibility within brain networks, they fail when interactions span multiple lags. This is evident in the Sentence Similarity measures, which not only have weaker correlations with TE, but the metrics themselves fail to replicate the semantic network uncovered by the Transfer Entropy maps due to the longer time window (60 seconds) used to extract the SBERT embeddings (see Results figure). In contrast, Word Depth, calculated at the single-word level, does not include interactions across multiple timelags. This limitation could be addressed by incorporating higher-order lags in the VAR process.

Future directions also include using other aspects of naturalistic input, such as auditory or visual content, to investigate information flow in the corresponding networks. Despite these limitations, our findings bridge the conceptual gap between time-reversibility, causality, and information flow, demonstrating that time-reversibility metrics can meaningfully capture causal information propagation in the brain.
