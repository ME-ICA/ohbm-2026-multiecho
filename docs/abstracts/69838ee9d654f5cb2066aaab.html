Introduction

General intelligence (g) is one of the most robust constructs in cognitive science1, yet its neural implementation in large-scale brain networks remains debated. Functional connectivity studies show that distributed brain networks can predict cognitive performance, but most approaches treat individual edges as independent predictors2. This high-dimensional feature view makes it difficult to identify which collective patterns of connectivity are mechanistically indispensable for g and which reflect behaviorally peripheral variability.

Sloppiness theory3, originally developed in systems biology, offers a complementary perspective: in complex models, a small number of “stiff” parameter combinations tightly constrain system behavior, whereas many “sloppy” combinations can vary with little effect. Here we import this information-geometric framework into human cognitive neuroscience and ask three questions. First, can we identify stiff network modes whose individual variation robustly explains latent g derived from a bifactor psychometric model of multiple domain specific tasks? Second, can the optimal g mode be reconstructed from task-specific stiff modes, providing a mechanistic account of how multiple cognitive demands combine to yield trait-like intelligence differences? Third, do these stiff eigennetworks and the optimal g-related cognitive network reflect underlying molecular architecture and generalize across independent cohorts? To address these questions, we combine Stiff–Sloppy Analysis Method (SAM) on fMRI-based connectivity, structural equation modeling of g, transcriptomic mapping, and external behavioral validation in several public large-scale datasets.

Methods

We analyzed data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) young-adult sample, including structural and resting state functional MRI and a standard cognitive battery. A bifactor structural equation model was fitted to nine cognitive measures (matrix reasoning, visuospatial processing, vocabulary, reading, picture sequence memory, word list memory, set-shifting, flanker/attention, and processing speed). The model specified a general factor (g) plus three domain factors (crystallized ability, memory, processing speed), with shared loadings for paired indicators. Latent scores for g and domain factors were estimated for each participant.

For each subject, we fit a pairwise maximum-entropy model (PMEM) to group-averaged fMRI data to capture the low-order statistics of regional activity patterns. From this model we computed the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) in parameter space, which quantifies how sensitively the model’s predicted activity statistics respond to changes in each connection parameter. Eigendecomposition of the FIM yielded an ordered set of “eigennetworks”: directions in connectivity space ranging from stiff (large eigenvalues, high sensitivity) to sloppy (small eigenvalues, low sensitivity).

To link these eigennetworks to behavior, we used our Stiff–Sloppy Analysis Method (SAM). For each participant, we fitted PMEM to the resting fMRI of each subject and computed the task-related Alignment of Individual Deviation (AID) from the group model, defined as the signed projection of that individual’s deviation of the coupling parameters in PMEM  from the group average onto a candidate cognitive network formed by the linear combinations of FIM eigennetworks. We then searched over the linear combinations to identify an “optimal g cognitive network” whose AID best predicted latent g, using cross-validated linear models and regularization to avoid overfitting. In parallel, we used the same method to derive optimal cognitive network separately for each cognitive task contributing to g, which can best predict the specific task performance. We then formed a simple composite network by summing these task-specific optimal cognitive networks and compared it to the directly optimized g network in both spatial pattern and predictive performance, see Figure 1 for the description of the procedure and the illustration of the task-specific and g cognitive networks. 

To investigate molecular underpinnings, we mapped regional loadings of the g cognitive network onto cortical gene-expression profiles from the Allen Human Brain Atlas, processed with the abagen pipeline. Partial least squares (PLS) regression identified weighted combinations of genes whose spatial expression patterns best matched the network pattern, and gene-set enrichment analyses were performed to identify overrepresented biological pathways, with particular interest in neuromodulatory and synaptic signaling systems.

Finally, we tested the generalizability of the analysis in an independent cohort from the Chinese Color Nest Project4 (CHCP), which includes an fMRI working-memory task and behavioral accuracy measures. We projected each CHCP participant’s connectivity deviation onto the HCP-derived g cognitive network to obtain AID scores without re-fitting the FIM or eigennetworks, and examined whether these scores predicted individual differences in working-memory performance. Results

The FIM spectrum was highly anisotropic, with a steep drop in eigenvalues from the first few stiff modes to the long tail of sloppy modes, with a power-law rank distribution. This indicates that, although connectivity is high dimensional, the model’s behavior is constrained by a small subset of network directions, confirming that large-scale brain network is stiff-sloppy. 

The optimized g cognitive network emerged as a combination of FIM stiff eigenvectors and showed a robust relationship with latent intelligence. Across HCP participants, AID along this g cognitive network correlated strongly with the latent g scores (r ≈ 0.45, p<1E-53), indicating that individual differences in g can be captured by subtle deviations along a single combined sensitive network mode. This predictive relationship remained stable under cross-validation.

Task-specific SAM analyses yielded optimal cognitive networks for each cognitive measure contributing to g. These networks showed partially distinct but overlapping patterns of reweighting among frontoparietal, default-mode, dorsal attention, and sensory systems (Figure 1). A simple composite network obtained by summing the task-specific optimal cognitive networks closely matched the directly optimized g cognitive network in both spatial configuration and functional interpretation. Critically, AID along this composite network preserved most of the predictive power for g (r ≈ 0.43, p<1E-48), demonstrating that g can be mechanistically understood as a low-dimensional combination of task-evoked stiff modes rather than an independent, unrelated pattern of brain network connectivity.

PLS analysis of the g cognitive network’s regional loadings revealed a significant latent component linking the network pattern to cortical gene-expression gradients. Genes with high absolute weights on this component were enriched for G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR)-mediated signaling5 and related neuromodulatory pathways (p<1E-5), including dopaminergic and other monoaminergic receptor families. This suggests that neuromodulatory systems may tune the stiff network dimensions that support general cognitive efficiency.

In the independent CHCP cohort, projecting subject-specific connectivity deviations onto the HCP-derived g cognitive network produced AID scores that significantly predicted working-memory accuracy (200 subjects; memory factor, r=0.1496, p=0.0263; crystallized ability, r=0.1930, p=0.0040). The strength and direction of this relationship were consistent with those observed for g in HCP, despite differences in population, scanner environment, and task implementation. This cross-dataset generalization indicates that the identified g cognitive network captures a trait-like network mode relevant for higher cognition, rather than a dataset-specific artifact.

Conclusion

By importing and developing sloppiness theory into whole-brain modeling, this work identifies a compact set of stiff cognitive networks that mechanistically implement individual differences in general intelligence. Rather than viewing intelligence as emerging from arbitrary high-dimensional patterns of connectivity, our results show that g is primarily expressed along a small number of sensitive directions in network space. Individual deviations along a single optimized cognitive network explain substantial variance in latent g, and this g cognitive network itself can be reconstructed as a simple combination of stiff modes optimized for constituent cognitive tasks. This provides a concrete network-level mechanism through which diverse task demands combine to yield stable, trait-like differences in cognitive ability.

The link between the g cognitive network and cortical gene-expression gradients enriched for GPCR-mediated signaling further suggests that neuromodulatory systems play a central role in tuning these stiff dimensions. In this view, neuromodulatory receptors and their downstream pathways adjust the balance of integration and segregation across large-scale networks, thereby shaping the sensitivity of brain dynamics to parameter changes and, ultimately, supporting efficient information processing.

Externally, the ability of the HCP-derived g cognitive network to predict working-memory and crystalized ability performance in an independent cohort demonstrates that the identified mode is robust across samples and tasks. Together, these findings bridge three traditionally separate levels of analysis: psychometric modeling of g, systems-level network dynamics, and molecular architecture. More broadly, the Stiff–Sloppy Analysis Method (SAM) offers a general framework for compressing high-dimensional connectomes into a small number of behaviorally critical modes, which could be applied to other cognitive domains and clinical populations to disentangle stiff, mechanistically important network dimensions from flexible but behaviorally peripheral variability.
