Introduction

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, AI-based conversational agents (AICAs) have been increasingly deployed to provide emotional support due to their accessibility, low cost, and scalability. Accordingly, substantial efforts have been devoted to developing more human-like AICAs for companionship, counseling, and even psychological intervention. Although prior research suggests that some AICAs can effectively alleviate negative emotions compared to no-intervention conditions 1, direct comparisons with human psychotherapists remain scarce. Moreover, emotion regulation in human–human psychotherapy engages complex social-cognitive and emotion regulation processes and neural circuits. Whether AICA-assisted emotion regulation engages the same neural pathways remains unknown 2-3. To this end we conducted a behavioral and a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) experiment to systematically compare the behavioral efficacy and neural mechanisms underlying emotion regulation guided by AICA psychotherapists versus human psychotherapists.

Methods

In the behavioral experiment, we employed a 2 (Advice Source: AICA psychotherapist vs. human psychotherapist; between-subjects) × 2 (Time: pre-intervention vs. post-intervention; within-subjects) mixed design with healthy university students (N = 49). Participants were randomly assigned to view either an AICA or a human psychotherapist self-introduction video, followed by a brief online interview to enhance perceived credibility. Participants then watched five validated 1-min negative emotional video clips. After each clip, they immediately rated their emotional valence (“how negative do you feel now? 1 = not negative at all; 9 = very negative”). Then they turned to the online platform to share their feelings with the assigned psychotherapist and received regulation advice from them. To control for subtle differences in the advice per se, advice in both conditions was generated using few-shot prompt-tuned ChatGPT-4o based on cognitive reappraisal models.

In the fMRI study, a within-subjects design was adopted with three conditions (AICA psychotherapist, human psychotherapist, no-advice control) in healthy university students (N = 34). The paradigm largely mirrored the behavioral study with several modifications: (a) participants completed an online interview with the AICA therapist and an in-person interview with a human therapist to enhance ecological validity; (b) the experiment comprised four runs (two AICA runs, two human runs), each containing eight trials, including five AICA or human trails and three no-advice control trials; (c) participants read regulation advice before viewing each negative clip and were instructed to apply the advice during emotion regulation; and (d) participants rated their negative emotions after each clip and reported regulation success at the end of each run. All advice was generated by ChatGPT-4o using a social reappraisal strategy.

Results

In the behavioral study, a mixed 2-way ANOVA was conducted to explore the emotion regulation efficiency between AICA therapist and human therapist. The results found that after controlling covariates, the interaction effect is significant (F(1, 42) = 5.94, p = 0.019, ηp2 = 0.124). Simple effect analysis indicated that both AICA and human therapists lowered negative valence after regulation, but the human therapist exhibited a stronger effect to help participants regulation negative emotions (p < 0.001). 

In the fMRI study, we first used one-way ANOVA to test the difference of participants’ negative feelings between the 3 conditions (F(1, 33) = 13.09, p = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.278). The Bonferroni correction showed that compared to control condition, both the AICA therapist and the human therapist could effectively reduce participants’ negative emotions (ps < 0.01), however, the difference between the AICA and human therapists is not significant (p = 1.00) (Fig. 2a). 

For the MRI data analysis, we used SPM25 and preprocessing was conducted using the standard pipeline. At the first level, a general linear model (GLM) was specified for the advice-reading stage and clip-watching stage separately. Task conditions (AICA condition, or human condition, or no advice condition) were modeled as separate regressors and convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function. Twenty-four Friston motion parameters were included as nuisance regressors. Individual contrast images comparing conditions of interest (e.g., AICA > Human) were generated. At group level, one-sample t-tests were conducted on the individual contrast images. Statistical significance was evaluated at a voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.001 (uncorrected) with cluster-level FWE correction at p < 0.05. The results found that in reading advice stage, participants showed higher activation in cuneus when advice came from AICA therapist rather than advice from human therapist (Fig. 2b). In watching clip stage, participants showed higher activation in cerebellum in AICA condition comparing than human condition (Fig. 2c). 

To further explain the cognitive implications of these activation-significant brain regions, we performed a functional meta-analysis using Neurosynth database 4 in advice-reading stage and clip-watching stage separately. The identified cuneus region during reading advice stage showed motor-relevant terms, including stimulated, somatosensory, touch, primary somatosensory. Another interesting result appeared when decoding the activated cerebellum regions in clip-watching stage, the relevant terms were related to mentalization and mind, including mental states, theory mind, mind tom, mental state and mentalizing (Fig. 2d). 

Additional analysis was conducted to explore multivariate pattern when participants regulate emotions from AICA therapist and human therapist using 279 ToPS region-by-region parcellation 5. We used support vector machine as classifier and leave-one-subject out as cross-validation method to avoid overfitting. After FDR correction, no parcel survived; however, four regions showed high classification accuracy (> 0.70) with uncorrected significance (p < 0.001), including the right orbital gyrus, left inferior temporal gyrus, right parahippocampal gyrus, and right superior parietal lobule. (Fig. 2e).

Conclusion

    Emotion regulation guided by AICA psychotherapists recruits largely overlapping neural circuits with regulation guided by human psychotherapists. Notably, differential activation in the cerebellum suggests potential differences in mentalization-related processes during human–AI interactions, highlighting the promise and potential application of AICAs in emotional support contexts.
