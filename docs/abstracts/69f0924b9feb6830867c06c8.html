Introduction

High-density scalp EEG (hdEEG) is widely used to noninvasively study brain dynamics, but its ability to accurately reconstruct source time series has not been fully validated. Electrical source imaging (ESI) methods, such as eLORETA, can estimate cortical and sometimes subcortical sources from hdEEG, yet their accuracy is limited by electrode coverage, source depth, head modeling, and the smoothing inherent to the inverse solution. Most prior studies have focused on validating hdEEG-ESI for localizing time-locked events, leaving it unclear how well ongoing spontaneous activity is reconstructed. While ESI can partially address this question, it cannot disentangle errors arising from forward modeling versus the inverse solution. Here, we ask to what extent hdEEG contains latent information beyond standard ESI reconstructions. To address this, we investigate hdEEG simultaneously recorded with intracranial EEG (iEEG) using two approaches: standard source-based projection (eLORETA) and a data-driven least-squares operator (LSop) that maps hdEEG directly onto iEEG. In this setting, iEEG acts as the ground truth of neuronal activity. We examine how reconstruction performance varies with source depth, frequency band, electrode montage, and interictal spike rate, aiming to quantify the proportion of iEEG variance recoverable from hdEEG and to identify factors limiting accurate reconstruction of neuronal time series.

Methods

We analyzed spontaneous simultaneous hdEEG and iEEG recordings from 33 patients enrolled across two clinical centers (Geneva University Hospitals, HUG; and Niguarda Hospital, Milan).1,2 Recordings were acquired as part of a research protocol integrated into the presurgical evaluation of individuals with pharmacoresistant focal epilepsy. For each patient, a pre-implantation structural T1-weighted MRI (3T) and a post-implantation CT scan were acquired as part of the routine clinical workup (Figure 1 A). HdEEG was recorded at 1000Hz using a 257-channel geodesic elastic net (Electrical Geodesics Inc., Eugene, OR) with conductive paste. iEEG were recorded using ADTech or Dixi electrodes at 2048Hz or 1000Hz. All hdEEG and iEEG signals were synchronized using hardware triggers, then offline downsampled to 200 Hz. Signals underwent zero-phase bandpass filtering (1–45 Hz) and a 50-Hz notch filter was applied. HdEEG preprocessing included independent component analysis for identification and removal of ocular, muscular artifacts, and bad channel reconstruction. To characterize epileptiform activity in the iEEG, we applied an automated interictal epileptic discharge (IED) detector (Delphos)3 and extracted spike occurrence rates for each implanted contact. Patient-specific head models were generated from individual T1-weighted MRI volumes using a boundary element method. As a baseline electrical source imaging method, we reconstructed cortical and subcortical gray-matter activity with eLORETA and projected these source estimates to iEEG electrode coordinates to obtain “virtual” iEEG signals.4 This second step is essential to ensure a one-to-one correspondence between the reconstructed virtual iEEG signals and the recorded iEEG contacts. In parallel, we derived a data-driven optimal linear operator (LSop) that maps hdEEG directly onto recorded iEEG by minimizing least-squares reconstruction error. This operator allowed us to bypass potential biases introduced by the headmodel and ESI, allowing a direct assessment of the shared information content between hdEEG and iEEG (Figure 1 B.1). Reconstruction accuracy was measured using correlations between virtual iEEG and real iEEG and peak localization error (PLE).5 These measures were modelled with generalized linear mixed-effects analyses to compare eLORETA and LSop, and test the influence of electrode depth, iEEG montage, frequency band, hdEEG electrode count, and spike rate on the correlation (Figure 1 B.2). 

Results

Across all frequency bands, LSop significantly outperformed eLORETA (p<0.001). In the broadband condition, the estimated fixed effect for the correlation was 0.07 for eLORETA compared with 0.40 for LSop, illustrating a substantially higher reconstruction accuracy. LSop also showed no measurable localization error (0mm), and its PLE values were significantly lower than those of eLORETA (30mm p<0.001, all frequency bands, Figure 2A). Reconstruction performance of LSop declined with increasing iEEG electrode depth in every frequency band (p<0.001), yet it remained unaffected by spike rate (Figure 2B). In contrast, eLORETA performance also decreased with electrode depth in all frequency bands (p=0.03 in delta; p<0.001 in all others) and was additionally influenced by the rate of interictal spikes in broadband, delta, and theta bands. LSop reconstructed low-frequency activity with higher fidelity: delta and theta band correlations exceeded broadband (p<0.001 and p<0.01, respectively), and all bands outperformed beta band (p<0.001) (Figure 2C). We showed that the performance of LSop was sensitive to the number of hdEEG electrodes, with accuracy decreasing as electrode count decreased (Figure 2C). Notably, the 258-channel configuration provided a statistically significant improvement even when compared to the montage with 128 channels. Finally, reconstruction accuracy was consistently lowest when using a bipolar iEEG montage compared with referential and common-average montages, between which no significant differences were observed.

Conclusion

HdEEG can recover approximately 40% of the variance observed in iEEG signals. Electrode depth remains an intrinsic limiting factor, and narrowband low-frequency activity is reconstructed more accurately than broadband activity. This latter finding warrants caution when interpreting broadband-derived metrics (e.g., power spectral or aperiodic components) from hdEEG-based reconstructions. The absence of a relationship between LSop performance and spike rate (contrasted with the significant association observed for eLORETA) suggests that features of ongoing background activity reach scalp EEG but are attenuated or smoothed by the inverse kernel of standard ESI methods. In contrast, eLORETA reconstruction appears to be more sensitive to large, synchronous events such as interictal spikes, yet fails to adequately capture the ongoing background activity that constitutes the majority of the iEEG signal. Importantly, similar findings were found in another study of the group, with other ESI methods (sLORETA, wMNE, LCMV). Importantly, the least-squares procedure used to obtain LSop does not enforce zero PLE, yet LSop nonetheless yielded negligible localization error. This indicates that, despite the partial dependencies among channels within each modality, the individual sensors still carry sufficiently distinct information to enable accurate localization. Moreover, bipolar iEEG montage was the least accurately reconstructed, likely because the subtraction of adjacent contacts restricts the spatial extent of the recorded field, reducing its correspondence with scalp-level measurements. Taken together, these results reveal latent structure in hdEEG that is not fully exploited by conventional ESI. They underscore the need for next-generation source-imaging approaches that not only localize generators but also more faithfully reconstruct neuronal time series. Ongoing work includes extending this framework to MEG-iEEG validation.
