Introduction

Resting-state functional MRI reveals organized low-frequency brain dynamics that are disrupted in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), particularly within large-scale networks such as the default mode network.^1 While many studies have focused on static functional connectivity, growing evidence suggests that dynamic spatiotemporal patterns capture additional aspects of large-scale brain organization. Quasi-periodic patterns (QPPs) represent recurring spatiotemporal motifs involving coordinated activation and deactivation of distributed networks and reflect fundamental properties of resting brain activity.^2,3 Although QPPs have been characterized in both rodents and humans, whether QPPs learned in animal models generalize to human neurodegenerative disease remains unclear.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were analyzed from transgenic Alzheimer's disease (AD) model rats and wild-type (WT) controls as well as from cognitively normal (CN) and AD participants from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). The rodent dataset comprised 105 scans from 62 rats, balanced across condition (30 AD, 32 WT), sex (33 female, 29 male), and age (6 and 15 months).^5 Rodent data were acquired at a temporal resolution of 1.25s and parcellated using the SIGMA-Wistar functional atlas.^5 QPP pattern lengths for rodents were set to approximately 10 timepoints (approximately 12.5 seconds), consistent with previously reported rodent QPP durations.^2


The human dataset consisted of 20 ADNI participants (10 AD, 10 CN), with equal sex distribution within each group (5 male, 5 female). All human scans were acquired at 3.0T with TR ranging from 0.6-3.0 seconds (typically 3.0s). Human data were preprocessed using the Configurable Pipeline for the Analysis of Connectomes (C-PAC), including motion correction, spatial normalization to MNI space, nuisance regression with global signal regression, and band-pass filtering (0.009-0.08Hz), consistent with prior QPP studies.^3 Cortical time series were extracted using the Schaefer 2018 functional atlas (200 parcels, 7 networks).^4 Human QPP pattern lengths were computed as subject-specific durations targeting approximately 18 seconds, consistent with previously reported human QPP periodicities.^2


Seed-based QPP templates were identified from homologous default mode and task-positive network regions in rats and humans. Each template was phase-normalized by mapping onto a normalized phase axis and resampling to a common length of 10 samples to enable cross-species comparison. These phase-normalized templates were used to train machine learning classifiers on rodent data and evaluated on held-out human ADNI participants (Figure 1).

Results

Human QPPs exhibited reproducible spatiotemporal structure involving default mode and task-positive networks, consistent with prior descriptions of large-scale QPP organization.^2,3 Cross-species training of phase-normalized QPP templates enabled above-chance classification of AD versus CN participants across multiple machine learning models (Multi-Layer Perceptron, Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, Logistic Regression, XGBoost, and Ensemble). All six models demonstrated above-chance cross-species generalization, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) ranging from 0.61 to 0.74 and balanced accuracy from 0.55 to 0.65. The ensemble classifier, combining XGBoost, Decision Tree, and Support Vector Machine models, achieved the highest performance with an AUC of approximately 0.74 and balanced accuracy of approximately 0.65, demonstrating cross-species transfer of QPP-based biomarkers.

Compared to CN participants, AD participants exhibited quantitative differences in QPP amplitude and temporal organization, particularly in interactions between default mode and task-positive networks. While these differences were not necessarily apparent through visual inspection of QPP templates alone, they were captured by machine learning models trained on the phase-normalized QPP representations. These disease-related alterations were detectable despite inter-species differences in temporal scale (1.25s TR for rats, 0.6-3.0s TR for humans) and acquisition parameters, indicating that phase-normalized QPP templates encode network dynamics that are shared across species and relevant to Alzheimer’s disease (Figure 2).

Conclusion

These results show that quasi-periodic large-scale network dynamics are conserved across species and remain detectable in human Alzheimer’s disease. Cross-species transfer of QPPs provides a translational framework linking rodent models to human functional neuroimaging and captures disease-related alterations not reflected by static connectivity alone. QPPs represent a biologically grounded and dynamic marker of large-scale network dysfunction in Alzheimer’s disease.
