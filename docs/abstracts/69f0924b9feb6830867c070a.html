Introduction

Human brains are the most diverse of all primates, showing a large variability in their shapes and sizes, and their degree and pattern of folding. This intricate geometry has traditionally been thought to be genetically encoded and refined through interactions with the environment. In this case, genetically closer individuals should have a more similar brain architecture and behaviour. However, recent findings in non-human primates challenge this long-standing view, revealing that neuroanatomical similarity can emerge independently of genetic similarity. Furthermore, behavioural phenotypes appear to correlate more strongly with neuroanatomical than genetic similarity.1

We hypothesise that important aspects of neocortical anatomy emerge from mechanical morphogenetic processes: changes in shape resulting from the non-linear dynamics of brain growth. These processes should be a major determinant of cortical folding, and also regulate the development of cortico-cortical connectivity, particularly U-fibres. If this is the case, the same phenomena observed across primates should be present among individuals of the same species.

Here we test this hypothesis in a large population of humans. We aim to establish if similarities in human behaviour correlate more strongly with neuroanatomical or genetic similarity. In other words, we test if behavioural similarities result from the emerging brain architecture – shaped by mechanical morphogenetic processes during growth – beyond the influence of genetics.

Leveraging neuroanatomical, genetic and behavioural data from over 40000 individuals, we computed correlations between genetic, neuroanatomical and behavioural similarities. Our findings support our hypothesis: neuroanatomical similarity between humans captures cognitive and behavioural similarity beyond genetics. This shows that mechanical morphogenesis plays a causal role in shaping cognition and behaviour, capturing a part of phenotypic diversity unavailable through genetics

Methods

1. Data

Genetic, neuroimaging and behavioural data was obtained from the UK Biobank project for ~41.000 individuals (https://imaging.ukbiobank.ac.uk). We included whole-genome genotyping data, reconstructed brain surface meshes from T1-weighted MRIs using FreeSurfer 6.0, computed functional connectomes from fMRI time series data using different atlases, and included behavioural data from the categories Cognitive function and Mental Health.

2. Genetic relationship

Genetic relatedness was estimated from autosomal Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms after stringent quality control. Close relatives (until third degree cousins) were excluded. The resulting genotype data were used in Genomic Complex Trait Analysis to build a genomic relationship matrix (GRM).

3. Neuroanatomical similarity

Using FreeSurfer 6.0, we extracted 1275 neuroanatomical phenotypes from the brain surface meshes, including total brain volume, intracranial volume, grey matter, white matter and CSF volumes, and bilateral regional volumes, total and regional surface areas, regional cortical thickness values, grey-level intensities, and grey-white contrast values. Additionally, we combined left and right hemispheres and computed a series of folding measurements including gyrification index, folding number and length, fold width and depth, using our open-source tool “meshgeometry”.

We normalised the neuroanatomical measurements and computed Pearson correlations between all measurements.

4. Neuro-Functional similarity

fMRI time series data was preprocessed (slice-time interpolation, motion correction, coregistration, normalisation). We computed functional connectomes based on 39 to 264 regions, using the different atlases provided with the UK Biobank data. Functional connectivity similarity was computed by forming a subject-by-subject similarity matrix from standardized connectome maps, using the normalised dot product between individual profiles.

5. Behavioural similarity

We obtained behavioural data from the categories Cognitive Function and Mental Health. We controlled for completeness (>0.6) and variable reliability (intraclass correlation > 0.25), imputed missing values and normalised all variables. We finally regressed out confounding factors such as age, sex, imaging centre and genetic principal components. Our final sample comprised 41 phenotypes across 33k individuals from different sub-categories, including Cognitive function and Mental health. We computed the correlation between all phenotypes across all individuals.

6. Morphometricity and heritability

We estimated morphometricity and SNP-heritability of the behavioural phenotypes using Haseman‑Elston regression, quantifying the proportion of phenotypic variance explained by brain‑morphology measures and by common genetic variation, respectively.

7. Comparison of behavioural similarity with neuroanatomical, neuro-functional, and genetic similarity

We computed Pearson correlation between behavioural and genetic similarity matrices, behavioural and neuroanatomical similarity matrices, behavioural and neuro-functional similarity matrices, and neuroanatomical/functional and genetic similarity matrices for ~33k individuals (neuroanatomical features) and ~22k individuals (neuro-functional phenotype). We performed Mantel tests to assess the significance of the correlations.

To test for an effect of neuroanatomy on behaviour independent of genetics, and for genetics independent of neuroanatomy, respectively, we computed partial correlations.

We performed hierarchical clustering based on behaviour using the Ward variance minimisation algorithm and computed the significance of the difference in brain volume between the two main behavioural clusters (Cohen’s d). To assess the importance of different behavioural phenotypes for the clustering, we computed silhouette scores for all variables independently and obtained standard errors using bootstrap.

Results

Humans can have very similar brains irrespective of genetic similarity.

There is a great natural diversity in human brain shape, with a more than two-fold variation in brain size within the normal population. Among 40 thousand human brains measured within the UK Biobank project, the smallest adult brain has a volume of 760 cm3 and the largest brain measures a volume of 1,870 cm3. Interestingly, people with very different genomes can have brains equally large or small, and vice versa, people with very similar genomes do not always have a similar brain (Figure 1). Brain volume has a very strong correlation with brain folding. The larger the brain, the more it will be folded. Here, we show that this neuroanatomical similarity appears independent of genetic relationship, and has consequences on brain function and behaviour.

Humans with similar brain volumes have similar functional connectomes, beyond genetic similarity.

Different neocortical areas show simultaneous activation and form functional networks. The organisation of functional connectivity reflects the underlying anatomical fibre pathways.2 Interestingly, a prominent motif of short associative connections, known as U-fibres connecting neighbouring gyri, follows the folded structure of the cortical surface.3 If patterned gene expression encodes the pattern of neocortical folds and cortico-cortical connectivity, genetically closer individuals should share more similar connectomes than genetically distant people. However, our analyses of whole-brain connectomes from resting-state functional MRI data showed that the connectome of people with similar brain anatomy is more similar (r=0.092, p<0.01), independent of genetic relationship (r=0.003, p<0.01).

Humans with similar brain volumes have similar behaviour, beyond genetic similarity.

We used 41 cognitive behavioural phenotypes from the UK Biobank. Our results showed a higher correlation between neuroanatomical similarity and behavioural similarity (r=0.023, p<0.01), whereas the correlation between genetic similarity and behavioural similarity was significantly lower (r=0.001, p<0.01). We used partial correlations to further test for an effect of neuroanatomy independent of genetics, and found a significant correlation of neuroanatomical and behavioural similarity when controlling for genetic similarity (r=0.0234, p<0.01, Figure 1). By contrast, the correlation between genetic and behavioural similarity when covarying neuroanatomical similarity was much weaker (r=0.0011, p<0.01). Additionally, the correlation of functional connectome similarity with behavioural similarity (r=0.005, p<0.01) is higher than with genetic similarity (r=0.003, p<0.01). 

Morphometricity and heritability 

The heritability of our behavioural phenotypes was 0.07 while their neuroanatomically-based morphometricity was 0.03. Finally, the morphometricity based on the functional connectomes was much higher (m2=0.17).

Clustering based on behaviour

Splitting individuals into 2 groups based on behaviour mixed people with varying genetic similarity and reflected well their neuroanatomical similarity showing a significant difference in brain volume between the two behavioural clusters (Cohen's d: 0.132, p≪1). The importance of different behavioural phenotypes for the clustering was assessed using silhouette scores. The behaviours most strongly different between the 2 clusters were reaction time and fluid intelligence.

Conclusion

If you had to predict the behaviour of a human, would you rather choose a human with a similar genome for reference, or one with a similar brain size? Although brain volume is highly heritable4,5, it is easy to find genetically similar subjects with very different brain volumes, or genetically different subjects with similar brain volumes. Our results indicate that these subjects will present a degree of cognitive and behavioural similarity that is not captured by their genetic similarity. 

This highlights a limitation of the traditional view that neuroanatomical patterns are principally produced by genetically encoded programs. Instead, our results are compatible with the idea that significant aspects of brain architecture emerge from mechanical morphogenetic processes, which play a causal role in shaping our brain function and behaviour. While the effects that we demonstrate are modest compared to cross-species variation, they are highly significant.

Mechanical morphogenesis appears as a fundamental force, producing stable neuroanatomical patterns with a significant effect on brain function and even behaviour, not only across species, but also among individuals of the same species, opening an exciting new perspective for studying both brain development and evolution.
