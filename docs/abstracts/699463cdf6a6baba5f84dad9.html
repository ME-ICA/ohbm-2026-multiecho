Introduction

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) affects approximately 7.2% of youth globally and frequently co-occurs with externalizing (Ext) and internalizing (Int) disorders.¹ These comorbid conditions complicate treatment planning and exacerbate long-term impairments in academic and social domains. To elucidate this interplay, longitudinal studies suggest that these associations vary across developmental stages. For instance, childhood Ext problems may predict adolescent ADHD, while ADHD in adolescence can predict Ext problems in adulthood. Similarly, bidirectional links exist between ADHD and Int symptoms at specific ages.

However, most previous research has relied on latent variable models. While useful, these models conceptualize disorders as global variables, obscuring how specific symptoms directly interact or reinforce one another. To address this, the cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) framework was introduced.² CLPN decomposes psychopathology into distinct nodes (symptoms) to estimate direct associations (edges), allowing for the identification of clinically critical nodes that are highly predictive or susceptible to influence.

Recent studies applying CLPN have demonstrated symptom-level reciprocal associations between ADHD, Ext, and Int domains.³ Yet, existing research often focuses on limited time points or restricts networks to only two domains, failing to capture the broader developmental dynamics from childhood through late adolescence. The current research addresses these gaps by analyzing data from the UK Millennium Cohort Study (MCS), a large nationally representative sample assessed repeatedly from age 5 to 17.⁴ We aimed to examine the developmental consistencies of longitudinal relationships between symptoms and identify how crucial nodes evolve. This approach provides granular evidence to inform specific interventions for individuals with psychopathology at different developmental stages.

Methods

2 Methods

2.1 Participants Data were drawn from the UK Millennium Cohort Study (MCS), a large-scale birth cohort tracking individuals born in the UK between 2000 and 2002 to investigate the impact of social and environmental factors on life courses.⁴ The cohort initially included 19,483 individuals. This study utilized parent-reported data on ADHD, internalizing, and externalizing symptoms collected at ages 5, 7, 11, 14, and 17. Participants with more than two missing data points on core study variables were excluded, resulting in a final analytical sample of 9,604 individuals (50.0% male). The sample represented a diverse socioeconomic range, distributed across income quintiles from lowest (n=1,598) to highest (n=2,050).

2.2 Measures Mental health symptoms were assessed using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ).⁵ ADHD symptoms were evaluated using the Hyperactivity-Inattention subscale (5 items, e.g., "Easily distracted"), capturing inattention and hyperactivity-impulsivity. Externalizing problems were assessed via the Conduct Problems subscale (5 items, e.g., "Often fights"), reflecting aggression and rule defiance. Internalizing problems were measured using the Emotional Symptoms subscale (5 items, e.g., "Often complains of headaches"), screening for anxiety and depression. Items were rated on a 3-point Likert scale (0="Not True" to 2="Certainly True"), with higher scores indicating greater severity. The SDQ subscales demonstrated satisfactory reliability across measurement points, with ordinal alpha values ranging from 0.78-0.86 for ADHD, 0.77-0.85 for externalizing, and 0.76-0.82 for internalizing symptoms.

2.3 Data Analysis Descriptive analyses were conducted using SPSS 29.0. We estimated cross-lagged panel networks (CLPN) using the glmnet package in R, visualized with qgraph. The CLPN models directed edges representing autoregressive paths and cross-lagged effects between nodes. Following Funkhouser et al.,³ we employed the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) penalty with 10-fold cross-validation for parameter selection. This method shrinks small regression coefficients to zero to control for false positives. To ensure robustness, an edge-weight threshold was set at >0.05. All models controlled for age, sex, and socioeconomic status (SES).

Network centrality was estimated using Out-Expected Influence (Out-EI) and In-Expected Influence (In-EI) indices to describe directionality. Out-EI sums the weights of outgoing edges (predictive strength), while In-EI sums incoming edges (susceptibility). Network stability and accuracy were assessed using the bootnet package. We conducted non-parametric bootstrapping (1,000 iterations) to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for edge weights, assessing accuracy. Stability of centrality indices was examined via subsetting bootstrap, calculating the correlation stability (CS) coefficient. A CS-coefficient >0.25 was considered acceptable, and >0.5 indicated good stability.

Results

3 Results 

3.1 Network Estimation of Adolescent ADHD and Comorbid Symptoms

Autoregressive Effects

Figure 1(a) presents the autoregressive effects within the CLPN. Autoregressive effects (mean β = 0.20 – 0.31) were consistently stronger than cross–lagged effects (mean β = 0.09). In the ADHD network, Restlessness (A1, β = 0.27 – 0.37) and Distractibility (A3, β = 0.24 – 0.35) showed the strongest stability. For internalizing symptoms, Somatization (I1, β = 0.23 – 0.37) and Social Anxiety (I4, β = 0.30 – 0.39) were dominant. In the externalizing subnetwork, Irritability (E1, β = 0.27 – 0.42) exhibited the strongest autoregressive effect.

Cross–Lagged Effects: Externalizing to ADHD

As shown in Figure 1(b), longitudinal links primarily flowed from externalizing (Ext) problems to ADHD symptoms. In early childhood (ages 5–7), Aggression (E3) predicted Inhibition (A4, β=0.08) and Distractibility (A3, β=0.06), while Disobedience (E2) predicted Restlessness (A1, β=0.06). In late childhood (ages 7–11), Disobedience (E2), Irritability (E1), and Dishonesty (E4) predicted Inhibition (A4) (β \in 0.05 – 0.10); concurrently, Aggression (E3) predicted Fidgety (A2) and Restlessness (A1). By early adolescence (ages 11–14), effects broadened: Dishonesty (E4) and Irritability (E1) continued to predict Inhibition (A4), while Theft (E5) predicted Distractibility (A3), Fidgety (A2), and Restlessness (A1). In late adolescence (ages 14–17), Theft (E5) predicted Inconstancy (A5, β=0.09), Dishonesty (E4) predicted Distractibility (A3) and Inhibition (A4), and Irritability (E1) predicted Restlessness (A1).

Reciprocal and Indirect Pathways

Reciprocal relationships appeared at specific stages. Bidirectional links between Restlessness (A1) and Irritability (E1) persisted from early to late childhood. Early childhood also showed reciprocity between Inconstancy (A5)–Disobedience (E2) and Inhibition (A4)–Disobedience (E2). In late adolescence, mutual prediction re–emerged between Inhibition (A4) and Disobedience (E2).

The relationship between ADHD and internalizing symptoms evolved from indirect mediation to direct linkage. From ages 5–11, Restlessness (A1) indirectly influenced Depression (I3), Social Anxiety (I4), and Somatization (I1) via Irritability (E1). In adolescence, this mediation expanded through Aggression (E3), Dishonesty (E4), and Theft (E5) to influence Depression (I3). A direct effect emerged only in late adolescence, where Inhibition (A4) prospectively predicted Social Anxiety (I4, β=0.05). No significant pathways from internalizing to ADHD symptoms were observed.

3.2 Centrality Estimation

Figure 1(c) illustrates developmental shifts in node influence. Out–Expected Influence (OutEI) drivers changed distinctly: Aggression (E3, OutEI = 1.41) dominated early childhood (ages 5–7), shifting to Irritability (E1, OutEI = 1.53) in late childhood (ages 7–11). By adolescence (ages 11–17), influence transitioned to conduct problems, with Theft (E5) becoming the primary driver (OutEI: 2.35 at early adolescence; 1.90 at late adolescence). Conversely, In–Expected Influence (InEI) remained stable: Distractibility (A3) was consistently the most influenced node across all stages, though the magnitude decreased over time (InEI = 1.23 – 1.99).

3.3 Network Accuracy and Stability

Bootstrap analyses confirmed high stability for the five cross–lagged panel networks. All CS–coefficients exceeded the 0.25 threshold. Specifically, the CS–coefficient for edge weights and In–Expected Influence (InEI) was 0.75 (95% CI [0.67, 1.00]), and stability for Out–Expected Influence (OutEI) was 0.67 (95% CI [0.60, 1.00]).

Conclusion

Conclusion

This study utilizes a nationally representative cohort to map the dynamic network structure of ADHD, externalizing (Ext), and internalizing (Int) symptoms from childhood to late adolescence. Moving beyond disorder-level frameworks, our symptom-level analysis reveals that the architecture of comorbidity is not static but driven by evolving, stage-specific pathways.

Interplay of ADHD and Externalizing Problems Associations between ADHD and Ext were predominantly unidirectional, with Ext symptoms predicting ADHD. This influence peaked in early adolescence, likely reflecting heightened susceptibility during rapid biobehavioral and social transitions. However, specific reciprocal associations persisted, serving as "bridges" between disorders. Notably, a strong, stable link between Restlessness and Irritability was observed from early to late childhood, suggesting a shared deficit in self-regulation where behavioral dyscontrol and affective impairment reinforce one another. Similarly, the reciprocal pathway between Inhibition and Disobedience highlights a cycle where deficits in inhibitory control undermine rule-following, which in turn further erodes regulatory capacity.

ADHD as a Precursor to Internalizing Symptoms In contrast, the relationship between ADHD and Int problems was characterized by a unidirectional pathway where ADHD precursors drove internalizing symptoms; Int symptoms did not significantly predict ADHD at any stage. In childhood, this pathway was indirect: Restlessness predicted outcomes like Depression and Anxiety largely through the mediation of Irritability. This suggests that early behavioral dysregulation triggers negative social feedback and irritability, which subsequently cascades into emotional maladjustment. By late adolescence, a direct pathway emerged, with Inhibition predicting Social Anxiety. This shift coincides with increased developmental demands for social competence, where inhibitory failures in peer contexts directly precipitate anxiety.

Developmental Shifts in Node Centrality Node-level analyses identified Distractibility as the most susceptible (passive) symptom across development. However, its drivers shifted: in early childhood, it was heavily shaped by externalizing behaviors (e.g., Aggression), reflecting sensitivity to external regulatory failure; in school-aged years, it became driven by internal ADHD components; by adolescence, externalizing inputs re-emerged. Conversely, the most predictive (active) nodes were consistently located within the externalizing domain, yet their identity evolved. Aggression was the dominant predictor in early childhood, serving as a proxy for global regulatory failure. In late childhood, influence shifted to Irritability, reflecting the rise of affective dysregulation. By adolescence, Theft emerged as the central predictor, marking a transition toward covert, crystallized antisocial behaviors that drive broader network dysfunction.

Limitations and Conclusion These findings must be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, reliance on parent-reported data necessitates future multi-informant research. Second, findings from this community-based sample require replication in clinical populations with higher symptom severity. Third, the multi-year intervals between assessments may miss fine-grained symptom reorganization, suggesting a need for intensive longitudinal designs. Finally, while CLPN estimates directed associations, they represent partial correlations; experimental designs are needed to establish causality.

In summary, the comorbidity of youth psychopathology reorganizes developmentally. It is driven by distinct mechanisms: externalizing behaviors exacerbating ADHD, and ADHD dysregulation precipitating internalizing symptoms via specific mediators. By identifying evolving central nodes—from early Aggression to adolescent Theft—this study provides a granular evidence base to inform targeted, stage-appropriate interventions.
