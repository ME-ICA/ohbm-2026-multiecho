Introduction

The locus coeruleus (LC), the brain’s primary source of noradrenaline (NA), is a key component of the ascending arousal system and plays a central role in regulating attention, arousal, stress responses, and adaptive behavior by modulating large-scale brain dynamics1-3. Through phasic and tonic firing modes, the LC-NA adjusts neural gain and supports flexible transitions between focused and exploratory states4. Although theoretical and animal work suggest that the LC-NA organizes macroscale cortical topology5-7, how LC-NA influences the functional organization of the human cortex and how this organization changes with aging—remains poorly understood. Aging is associated with cortical dedifferentiation8-9 and reduced neuromodulatory precision, which may weaken gain control and lead to diffuse cortical organization10-12, with consequences for cognition and emotional well-being8,9,13. However, whether age-related dedifferentiation reflects alterations in LC–cortical organization, and whether such changes are globally distributed or network specific, is unknown.

The objectives of this study were threefold. First, to map the principal manifold of LC–cortical connectivity to characterize how LC influence is spatially embedded within large-scale cortical organization, providing a systems-level mapping of the cortical landscape shaped by LC coupling. Second, to assess age-related differences in LC–cortical organization by quantifying global cortical dispersion, an index of functional dissimilarity, along multidimensional gradient axes, and identifying dedifferentiation within functional networks. Finally, to test whether variation in LC-cortical dispersion links with self-reported emotional well-being, thereby allowing us to assess whether LC–cortical topology had meaningful consequences for affective functioning in later life.

Methods

To characterize the organizational structure of LC–cortical coupling, we analyzed data from 140 participants (72 younger and 68 older adults). Participants underwent ultra–high-field 7 T fMRI while viewing two naturalistic movie clips differing in emotional valence (neutral and negative), along with a LC optimized magnetization transfer–weighted turbo flash (MT-TFL) sequence for LC delineation. Self-reported valence and arousal ratings collected before and after each clip confirmed robust emotional modulation, with negative viewing eliciting lower valence and higher arousal relative to baseline and neutral viewing, with comparable effects across age groups. Following scanning, participants completed an extensive battery of cognitive tasks and emotional well-being questionnaires. Principal component analysis of these standardized measures yielded two composite indices: an Emotional Resilience Index and a Cognitive Functioning Index.

LC–cortical functional organization was quantified using a gradient-mapping approach. For each participant and condition, LC time series were extracted from subject-specific LC masks derived from MT-TFL14 sequence and correlated with time series from 1,000 cortical parcels (Schaefer atlas)15. Resulting LC–cortical connectivity profiles were then used to construct an affinity matrix using normalized angular cosine similarity. Diffusion map embedding was then applied to this matrix to derive low-dimensional gradients capturing the dominant axes of variation in LC–cortical coupling, ordered by explained variance. To interpret these gradients within established cortical hierarchies, LC–cortical gradients were compared with canonical cortical gradients16. In addition, publicly available 13 PET-derived neurotransmitter receptor and transporter density maps17 were incorporated, and dominance analysis18 was used to quantify the relative contribution of each molecular system to LC–cortical gradient organization. 

To quantify age-related differences in LC–cortical organization, we computed dispersion in the two-dimensional gradient space following a previous study8 as a metric of dedifferentiation. Global dispersion was defined as the mean Euclidean distance of all parcels to the cortical centroid, reflecting whole-cortex spread. Within-network dispersion was calculated as the sum of squared Euclidean distances between parcels within a network and that network’s centroid. Between-network dispersion was computed as the Euclidean distance between pairs of network centroids. Because cortical atrophy may affect functional embedding, mean cortical thickness was included as a covariate along with sex and head motion (Figure 1).

Results

Low-dimensional embedding of LC–cortical connectivity revealed two dominant gradients during both emotional movie viewing. Principal Gradient 1 (G1) explained the largest proportion of variance across both emotional contexts and showed a highly consistent spatial topology, differentiating transmodal and motor regions from sensory, limbic, and attentional cortices (Figure 2). In contrast, Gradient 2 (G2) accounted for less variance and exhibited clear state dependence, separating visual–somatosensory regions during neutral viewing and shifting toward a sensory–association hierarchy during negative viewing. 

G1 showed consistent alignment with cortical chemoarchitecture across emotional contexts. Multiple regression models incorporating 13 PET-derived receptor–transporter maps explained a large proportion of G1 variance during both negative (Right LC R² adj =0.678; Left LC R² adj =0.675) and neutral movie viewing (Right LC R²adj=0.667; Left LC R² adj =0.642). Dominance analysis identified dopaminergic (DAT, D2) and noradrenergic (NAT) receptor-transporters as the strongest contributors, jointly accounting for ~46% of explained variance. G1 scores correlated positively with NAT and negatively with DAT, indicating a catecholaminergic molecular axis. In contrast, the G2 showed substantially weaker correspondence with receptor–transporter distributions. Instead, G2 exhibited clear state dependence: during negative movie viewing, G2 aligned with the canonical sensory–association (S–A) axis, whereas during neutral viewing it aligned with a visual–somatomotor (V–S) axis, indicating context-dependent reconfiguration of LC–cortical coupling along established cortical hierarchies.

Age-related differences in LC–cortical organization were then quantified as dispersion within a two-dimensional gradient space defined jointly by the first two LC–cortical gradients (G1 and G2). Using this multidimensional embedding, aging selectively altered LC–cortical topology during negative movie viewing. Older adults showed significantly greater global and between-network dispersion within the right LC–cortical manifold, indicating a broader and less coherent distribution of cortical regions across gradient space. Network-level analyses further revealed increased within-network dispersion across multiple systems, with the strongest and most robust effect observed in the frontoparietal control network (FPN), which remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons. In contrast, no age-related differences were detected during neutral movie viewing or within the left LC–cortical gradient manifold.

Importantly, age-related increases in dispersion within this multidimensional LC–cortical space were behaviorally meaningful. In older adults, greater global dispersion and greater LC–FPN dispersion were associated with lower Emotional Resilience Index scores, whereas no such associations were observed in younger adults. Together, these findings demonstrate that LC–cortical organization becomes less coherent with age under conditions of heightened emotional arousal, and that LC-linked global dispersion, and particularly within the frontoparietal control network, is associated with poorer emotional well-being in later life.

Conclusion

Our findings revealed two gradients of LC–cortical connectivity within the brain’s macroscale architecture. These gradients show that LC influence is embedded along axes jointly shaped by cortical chemoarchitecture and canonical functional hierarchies. Importantly, this embedding was sensitive to aging, during negative emotional movie viewing, older adults exhibited greater dispersion of LC–cortical coupling, reflecting dedifferentiation. This effect was most pronounced within the FPN and was behaviorally relevant, as greater FPN dispersion associated with poorer emotional well-being in late life.
