Introduction

Social dance is a multi-component activity integrating aerobic movement, rhythm and coordination, learning of motor sequences, and social interaction. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that dance-based interventions may benefit cognitive performance in older adults. However, few studies have examined effects on quality of life, and reported effect sizes and reproducibility vary across studies1. In addition, prior research has primarily focused on morphological changes in gray matter, which may obscure the subtle alterations in white matter (WM) microstructure occurring at earlier stages of intervention. Current evidence on WM structure largely relies on diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics, which offer limited fibre-population specificity and can be biased in regions with complex crossing-fiber architecture1. Fixel-based analysis (FBA) addresses these limitations by modelling individual WM fiber populations within a single voxel2. In this study, we evaluated whether a 12-week social dance intervention, compared with usual-life controls, is associated with positive changes across multi-domain behavioral domains and longitudinal white-matter alterations assessed using FBA.

Methods

A total of 92 healthy older adults were recruited, 35 subjects with missing or unqualified DWI data were screened out, and 57 were included in the study. The dance intervention group (n=26; mean age= 64.89±6.39 years; 0% male) received 12 weeks of partner-based swing dance training (90 min/session, once weekly). The control group (n=31; mean age= 68.74±3.59 years; 29% male) maintained usual lifestyle. Assessments were collected at baseline (S1) and post-intervention (S2), the study design is shown in Figure 1. The questionnaire evaluated four primary domains: cognitive, psychological, physical, and social. Independent-samples t tests on change scores (Δ=S2−S1) was conducted to describe within-group trajectories. Outcomes were further analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMM) with fixed effects of time, group, their interaction, and covariates as given baseline imbalances, with participant-level random intercepts. 

Multi-shell DWIs was collected on a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prism with a 20-channel head coil at Children’s Brain Imaging Center, East China Normal University, Shanghai China. Diffusion MRI was preprocessed using the Integrated diffusion image operator (iDIO) pipeline3. FBA was implemented following the MRtrix3 recommended pipeline. Longitudinal templates was created using a two-stage strategy following Genc et al3 to investigate the longitudinal changes in fibre density (FD), fibre cross-section (FC), and fibre density and cross-section (FDC)2.

Results

The independent-samples t test only showed the between-group difference in Mental Component Summary (MCS) change was statistically significant (t (55) = - 2.12, p = 0.039). But the further LMM analysis indicated a non-significant time by group interaction effect on the MCS (β=2.985, SE=1.650, 95% CI [−0.323, 6.293], p=0.076). No other outcomes showed significant effects.

We used paired t tests to assess within-group changes, and the dance group exhibited widespread increases in FD and FDC across white matter, involving the superior longitudinal fasciculus, right anterior corona radiata, right superior corona radiata, and the genu of the corpus callosum (uncorrected p value). However, no result was detected in the usual-life control group at the same significance level in FD/FC/FDC (Figure 2). The general linear model analyses indicated no significant time by group interaction after FWE correction for FD/FC/FDC ( p_FWE>0.05). 

Conclusion

Following the 12-week social dance intervention, the dance group demonstrated significantly greater mental health improvement compared with usual-life control group, suggesting that psychological outcomes may be detectable even over a short intervention period in older adults5. Relative to controls, the intervention group showed more widespread pre–post white-matter changes, providing preliminary support for an effect of social dance on white-matter microstructure. The lack of significant group-by-time interactions across domains may reflect limited power due to the current sample size and the insufficient intervention period1. Ongoing recruitment will allow further evaluation of these effects and facilitate more robust examination of the associations between behavioral measures and neuroimaging markers.
