Introduction

Chronic pain is a debilitating condition whose incidence has steadily increased in the past decade1. Despite its prevalence, there is limited understanding of the neurological basis of pain and how it may be altered by individual experience and psychology2. Interestingly, fMRI studies have shown that chronic pain, regardless of condition, alters connectivity between brain regions and that greater cognitive impairment may be positively associated with pain symptom severity3. This suggests that different chronic pain disorders may alter pain processing similarly but likely differ in severity and mechanism.

This study investigates the altered connectivity present in chronic pain conditions by comparing models of Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) to healthy controls (HCs) and to prior findings in Fibromyalgia (FM). RA was chosen as a model alongside FM because they are both believed to involve a neuro-inflammatory pathology, however, the incidence and severity of cognitive effects—including depression, fatigue, and anxiety—is lower in RA comparative to FM. However, RA-associated pain is generally specific to the affected joints, but FM groups have demonstrated generalized hypersensitivity2,4.

The primary objective of this project is to explore the alterations in pain responses and neural signalling in RA. The secondary objective is to compare and contrast these findings with FM groups to further develop our understanding of the impact of cognitive impairment and chronic pain on pain signalling and perception. We hypothesize that RA, like FM, has altered neural signalling in response to noxious stimuli, However, we further hypothesize that these effects will be distinct in RA from those observed in FM groups. In exploring how RA affects pain pathways in the central nervous system, this study advances our knowledge on chronic pain’s impact on the nervous system, leading to better insights into treatment and diagnosis in the future.

Methods

The present study includes two groups recruited locally; Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) and healthy controls (HCs). Participants attended two fMRI sessions; one to image the brainstem and spinal cord and another the brain and upper brainstem. Participants were aged 25- 65 years old and age-matched between groups ~3 years. Prospective participants were screened using online questionnaires to provide info about their medical history—height, sex, chronic pain history, and prescriptions—providing insights into possible confounders and ensuring participants had no concurrent chronic pain conditions. 

Chosen participants were additionally characterized using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), the State/Trait Anxiety Questionnaire (STAI), the Pain Catastrophising Scale (PCS), the McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ short form), the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score-31 questionnaire (COMPASS-31) which is a 31-item questionnaire for the subjective assessment of autonomic symptoms, and the Rheumatoid Arthritis Disease Activity Index (RADAI-5).

We employed fMRI to study neural processes in the brainstem and spinal cord, both of which are suspected of being important regions relating to pain dysfunction in chronic pain2. Images were acquired using Queen’s University’s 3 tesla MRI system (Siemens Prisma), using T2-weighted single-shot fast spin-echo imaging. In addition to verbal pain ratings, we used eye-tracking technology to provide wider array of physiological responses (EyeLink 1000 Plus system; SR Research, Ontario, Canada). The addition of eye-tracking allows for pupillometric insights into autonomic processing and attention. 

To deliver noxious stimulus in our study, participants received repeated brief heat contacts (in intervals of 2.5s and 5s, fast and slow respectively). Each imaging session was preempted with training and familiarization with study procedures, during which the stimulus was calibrated to individual pain tolerance (using ‘fast’ contact mode).

Structural and Physiological Modelling (SAPM)—a method developed by our lab—was used for functional connectivity analyses which include direction of interactions and distinguishments between inhibitory and excitatory interactions5. The collected fMRI data demonstrates blood oxygenation-level dependent (BOLD) responses, representative of variations in metabolic activity in relation to an altered balance blood supply and oxygenation metabolism. SAPM uses established anatomical and physiological knowledge to construct a network model which defines regions and plausible connections in the collected fMRI data. The method models input and output signalling between every region of an interconnected network. The relative amount of output signalling to different regions is indicated with the scaling factor “D”, and these values are always positive. The contribution of an input signal to a region on the output signalling from that same region is indicated with the scaling factor “B”. Positive B values indicate excitatory effects whereas negative values indicate inhibitory effects. The net effect of the output of one region on the output of another region is thus the product of these two scaling factors and is termed the “DB” value.  This method allows for insights into connectivity patterns of pain perception and regulation pathways in the brain, spinal cord, and brain stem5. The results therefore demonstrate connectivity values between regions for each participant.

Data analysis was done by comparing group average connectivity values by means of two-tailed Student’s T-tests to identify consistent values. Analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) were then used to identify connectivity values that vary across groups and/or in relation to participant characteristics from questionnaires, verbal pain ratings, and pupil sizes. This allowed us to observe how regional connectivity and BOLD responses varied due to these individual factors.

Results

This study expands upon previous SAPM analyses done in the lab, allowing for cross-comparison of both RA and FM with HC, as separate models of chronic pain disorders. The model is comprised of the right dorsal horn of the sixth cervical spinal segment (C6RD), dorsal reticular nucleus (DRt), hypothalamus, LC, nucleus gigantocellularis (NGc), NRM, nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), PAG, medial and lateral parabrachial nuclei and the medial thalamus. At present, the RA group, with the fast-contact condition (RAfast), the strongest connection was between the PAG→NGC, with a DB value of -0.347 ± 0.058. Interestingly, this was also identified as the strongest connection in the HCfast group, with a DB value of -0.177 ± 0.032, but was markedly lower in the FMfast group, with a DB value -0.031 ± 0.057. 

The connectivity between the NGC→C6RD in the RAfast group had a DB value of -0.286 ± 0.086. This connection was of similar strength in the HCfast group with a DB value of -0.343 ± 0.095 but is contrasted by the FMfast group whose DB value was -0.078 ± 0.090. 

Inversely, the C6RD→NGC connectivity had a DB value of 0.064 ± 0.173 in the RAfast group and -0.008 ± 0.044 in the HCfast group, and was more strongly represented in the FMfast group, valued at 0.086 ± 0.052.

In the RAfast group the connection between the LC→thalamus was valued at 0.214 ± 0.362, listed as comparatively less significant. The LC→thalamus regional connectivity in HCfast groups was 0.335 ± 0.120 and 0.368 ± 0.124 in the FMfast group. 

Pain intensity and unpleasantness ratings were also taken into consideration along with average temperatures, which were calibrated and rated as ‘moderately painful’ for individual participants. On average, the RAfast group were calibrated to 50.62 ºC ± 0.91, rated pain intensity as 4.30 ± 0.713 and unpleasantness as 2.64 ± 1.53. The HCfast group was calibrated to an average 49.5 ºC ± 2.3 and rated intensity and unpleasantness to an average 3.9 ± 1.3 and 2.7 ± 1.6. The FMfast group, on average, was calibrated to 48.1 ºC ± 3.8 and rated pain intensity as 5.4 ± 1.5 and unpleasantness as 4.1 ± 1.9. 

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into how chronic pain can alter neural signalling by identifying patterns of connectivity in RA and where they may overlap with those seen in HC and FM groups. Comparisons between Rheumatoid Arthritis and Fibromyalgia to this degree of detail are novel in literature. We observed that the RAfast group showed altered activity within key pain-processing pathways—particularly in interactions involving the PAG, NGC, and C6RD—yet these alterations were generally less pronounced than those previously identified in FM. This suggests distinct alterations in connectivity in RA groups when compared to HC groups. Furthermore, the strength of several inhibitory connections in the RAfast group, compared to their attenuation in the FMfast group, supports the idea that RA may involve altered pain signalling, but possibly in a distinct manner or magnitude from FM.

A key finding is that the RA group showed several connectivity patterns that more closely resembled those of healthy controls than those of FM. Across multiple regional connections, RA and HC groups demonstrated similar DB values, particularly within the descending pain-modulatory pathway. For example, both HC and RA showed relatively strong inhibitory connectivity in pathways such as PAG→NGC and NGC→C6RD, whereas these connections were markedly weakened in FM.

The average calibrated temperature of the RA group, despite being approximately 1.1 ºC higher than the HC group’s, seemed to have yielded closely correlated self-reported pain intensity and unpleasantness ratings. This would suggest that while the RA group may have an altered tolerance for noxious stimuli, their perception is more closely related to HC groups than that observed in the FM group, whose average temperature was calibrated 2.52 ºC lower than RA, but intensity/unpleasantness ratings were 1.1 and 1.46 higher respectively. 

Our results therefore suggest that RA participants may maintain more functional descending pain control compared to FM and that the altered pain processing previously observed in FM may be distinct to the condition and from other chronic pain disorders. Expanding on this, it may suggest that the cognitive effects and associated hypersensitivity of FM is in part responsible for the observed alterations in pain signalling.
