Introduction

Approximately 30% of people with epilepsy fail to achieve sustained seizure control despite multiple regimes of antiseizure medication, ultimately meeting criteria for drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE).1 However, identifying which individuals will develop drug resistance at diagnosis is non-trivial, and people with epilepsy often undergo years of ineffective medication trials, experiencing ongoing seizures, cumulative adverse medication effects, and substantial psychosocial burden. Early identification of individuals at risk for poor seizure control could enable timely consideration of alternative therapeutic strategies, including surgical evaluation, neuromodulation, or precision medication approaches, therefore reducing unnecessary exposure to ineffective therapies. Converging evidence conceptualizes epilepsy as a disorder of large-scale brain network dysfunction rather than isolated focal pathology, with structural and functional connectivity abnormalities detectable near diagnosis.2 However, it remains unclear whether whole-brain functional connectivity alterations at diagnosis reflect early vulnerability to subsequent seizure non-control in adults with a new diagnosis of focal epilepsy. This prospective study aimed to ascertain whether resting-state functional connectivity measured at diagnosis predicts 24-month seizure control outcomes in adults with newly diagnosed focal epilepsy.

Methods

In this prospective cohort study, 104 adults (16–65 years; mean age 35.8±13.5 years; 45% female) with newly diagnosed focal epilepsy underwent resting-state functional MRI within three months of diagnosis.3 Imaging was performed on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner using a multiband echo-planar imaging sequence (TR=0.7s; 2.4 mm isotropic voxels; 800 volumes; ~9 minutes). Preprocessing employed fMRIPrep with motion correction, threshold-free/synthetic susceptibility distortion correction, CompCor denoising, framewise displacement scrubbing (FD>0.5mm), and band-pass filtering (0.008–0.09Hz). We constructed a hybrid atlas integrating 100 cortical parcels (Schaefer 7-network), 32 subcortical regions (Tian atlas), and 7 cerebellar parcels (Buckner atlas), yielding 112 network-level functional connectivity features: within-network connectivity (7 features), inter-network connectivity (21 features), and subcortical-to-cortical network connectivity (84 features). Clinical follow-up occurred at 6-month intervals up to 24 months. Participants were classified as controlled (seizure-free during final 6-month observation period; n=58) or uncontrolled (≥1 seizure during final 6-month period; n=46). Importantly, this classification reflects early seizure control status rather than definitive drug-resistant epilepsy per ILAE criteria, as many participants had not completed adequate medication trials by 24 months. Two complementary analytical approaches were employed: (1) hypothesis-free threshold-free cluster enhancement with permutation testing to identify whole-brain connectivity differences at the parcel level, and (2) nested 10-fold cross-validated predictive modelling using network-level features with Firth-penalized logistic regression to evaluate individual outcome discrimination.

Results

Parcelwise connectivity analysis identified a single significant cluster of reduced functional connectivity in the uncontrolled group (cluster-level pFWE = .034). This cluster comprised 11 parcel-pair connections linking the cingulo-opercular control network—predominantly anterior cingulate and anterior insular regions—with bilateral sensorimotor cortex. The strongest effect was observed between the left sensorimotor cortex and left anterior cingulate region (Cohen’s d = −0.87, t₁₀₂ = −4.44, p < .001). While no individual edge survived false discovery rate correction, the cluster-level finding indicates a spatially distributed network abnormality rather than focal connectivity disruption. Independently, predictive modelling using ten-fold cross-validated Firth-penalized logistic regression identified three functional connectivity features that were consistently selected across folds: Salience/Ventral Attention–Control network connectivity (selected in 10/10 folds; β = −12.25; p < .001), Nucleus Accumbens–Dorsal Attention network connectivity (10/10 folds; β = −7.72; p = .021), and Putamen–Default Mode network connectivity (9/10 folds; β = −4.44; p = .099). All coefficients were negative (i.e. reduced in the uncontrolled group). The parsimonious three-feature model achieved moderate cross-validated discrimination, with a mean AUC of 0.762 (SD = 0.048) and mean classification accuracy of 0.69 across folds. Apparent performance when fit to the full dataset (AUC = 0.79) was consistent with cross-validated estimates.

Conclusion

Adults with newly diagnosed focal epilepsy who failed to achieve early seizure control demonstrated reduced functional integration between critical large-scale brain networks at diagnosis. Convergent findings from the parcelwise connectivity analysis and data-driven ten-fold cross-validated modelling support the interpretation that distributed network dysfunction represents a neurobiological vulnerability marker rather than a definitive prognostic tool. Across modelling approaches, Salience/Ventral Attention–Control network connectivity emerged as the most robust and stable feature, underscoring a central role for impaired salience detection and executive control integration,4 in vulnerability to poor seizure control. The additional identification of Nucleus Accumbens–Dorsal Attention and Putamen–Default Mode connectivity extends this framework beyond cortical control systems to include subcortical–cortical interactions underlying motivational, attentional, and motor–habit circuits, further supporting a network-based model of seizure regulation failure.5 Importantly, while overfitting was limited, and group-level effects and feature stability were robust (as indicated by apparent model performance in the full dataset), individual-level predictive performance remained moderate (cross-validated AUC ≈ 0.76), highlighting the distinction between identifying biologically meaningful markers of risk and achieving clinically actionable prediction.
