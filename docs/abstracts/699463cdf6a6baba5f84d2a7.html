Introduction

Social threat is a central driver of human affect, motivation, and interpersonal behavior, yet experimental paradigms model threat in markedly different ways. Tasks such as social exclusion, rejection, or negative evaluation evoke indirect social threat, where harm is conveyed through loss of belonging or social devaluation.^1 In contrast, provocation, interpersonal conflict, and retaliation paradigms capture direct social threat, involving explicit antagonism or the possibility to harm or be harmed by another agent. Whether these forms of threat rely on a unified “social alarm system” or reflect distinct neural computations related to appraisal, mentalizing, action preparation, and aggressive motivation remains unresolved.

Human neuroimaging highlights roles for anterior insula, cingulate cortex, amygdala, and prefrontal circuits across different threat modalities. Importantly, human provocation research points to partially distinct neural systems supporting reactive aggression and defensive responding, suggesting that indirect and direct threat may map onto functionally separable pathways.^2 Yet, no quantitative synthesis has directly compared these paradigms in humans, leaving their mechanistic distinction and potential cross-species homologies unclear.

To address this gap, we conducted a preregistered coordinate-based Activation Likelihood Estimation (ALE) meta-analysis of fMRI studies examining indirect (exclusion, rejection, negative evaluation) versus direct (provocation, retaliation) social threat. Our aim was to identify (i) neural regions consistently engaged across threat types, reflecting general social threat detection, and (ii) dissociable activation patterns separating socially mediated from interpersonally antagonistic threat. This comparison provides a mechanistic framework for conceptualizing social threat along functional dimensions with relevance for social neuroscience and translational models of psychopathology.

Methods

We performed a preregistered coordinate-based Activation Likelihood Estimation (ALE) meta-analysis following current neuroimaging meta-analytic standards.^3 A systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus (last search: March 2025) using task-, threat-, and paradigm-specific terms. Study selection followed PRISMA guidelines, and all screening decisions were carried out by two independent reviewers.

Studies were included if they: (i) employed task-based fMRI, (ii) examined a manipulation involving social threat, (iii) reported whole-brain activation coordinates in MNI or Talairach space, and (iv) provided a direct threat > control contrast. Experiments were classified as indirect social threat when threat was conveyed via exclusion, social rejection, negative evaluation, or ambiguous interpersonal devaluation (e.g., Cyberball, social feedback, evaluative judgment). Direct social threat included paradigms involving explicit antagonism, interpersonal provocation, or retaliatory choices allowing participants to inflict harm (e.g., Taylor Aggression Paradigm, Point Subtraction Aggression Paradigm, interpersonal conflict tasks). Studies with pharmacological manipulations, ROI-only reporting, resting-state designs, or missing coordinates were excluded.

The final dataset included 29 independent contrasts for Indirect Social Threat (IST), comprising N = 1103 participants, and 28 contrasts for Direct Social Threat (DST), comprising N = 855 participants. Each contrast reflected a threat > control comparison as defined by the original study. All contrasts were treated as independent experiments in the ALE framework.

Coordinates in Talairach space were converted to MNI using the Lancaster transform. For each experiment, activation foci were extracted or reconstructed from tables or digitized from figures when necessary. ALE analyses were performed using GingerALE (version 3.0.2), implementing a random-effects model that estimates convergence across experiments while accounting for sample-size dependent spatial uncertainty. Separate ALE maps were computed for indirect and direct datasets. To identify reliable convergence, we applied cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < 0.05 with a voxel-level cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.001 (5,000 permutations).

To assess shared neural processes, a minimum-statistic conjunction analysis (IST ∩ DST) was performed, identifying regions consistently recruited across both forms of threat. To identify dissociable mechanisms, we conducted two contrast analyses (IST > DST and DST > IST). These direct comparisons used permutation-based subtraction procedures implemented in GingerALE to quantify significant differences in likelihood of activation between conditions.

We further evaluated robustness using cluster-level sensitivity procedures inspired by Fail-Safe N logic adapted for neuroimaging. For each significant cluster, noise experiments were iteratively added to the dataset until convergence was no longer maintained, providing an estimate of the stability of findings against potential unpublished or null studies.

Results

Across all experiments, indirect and direct social threat showed both overlapping and dissociable neural patterns. The contrast analysis revealed stronger convergence for direct threat in five clusters, including the left claustrum, the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex, the right anterior insula, the right mid-cingulate cortex, and a second right anterior insula cluster extending into the inferior frontal gyrus. These results indicate that direct interpersonal threat preferentially recruits regions involved in conflict monitoring, autonomic arousal, and rapid action preparation, consistent with engagement of salience and control-related networks supporting rapid behavioral responses.^4 In contrast, the reverse comparison (indirect > direct) yielded no significant clusters, suggesting that indirect threat did not elicit greater convergence than direct threat in any brain region at the corrected statistical threshold.

The meta-analysis of indirect social threat revealed three significant clusters. The largest was located in the left anterior insula extending into the inferior frontal gyrus, consistent with increased salience detection and appraisal of socially ambiguous or evaluative threat.^4 A second cluster appeared in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, peaking in the left middle frontal gyrus, reflecting engagement of interpretive and mentalizing-related processes during exclusion, rejection, or negative evaluation. A third cluster emerged in the right anterior insula, indicating bilateral insular involvement in processing relational but non-confrontational forms of threat.

The meta-analysis of direct social threat identified three significant clusters. The largest was situated in the left anterior insula and inferior frontal gyrus, extending into the claustrum, while a second cluster in the right anterior insula also extended into the inferior frontal gyrus and claustrum. A third cluster involved the dorsal portion of the anterior cingulate cortex extending into the mid-cingulate cortex. Together, these regions form a network associated with heightened arousal, defensive readiness, and the integration of salience signals with executive control during explicit interpersonal threat.^5

The conjunction analysis revealed a single overlapping cluster in the left anterior insula extending into the inferior frontal gyrus. This shared activation suggests the presence of a common neural mechanism for detecting relational danger across both ambiguous and explicit forms of social threat, centered on core salience-processing circuitry.^4

Robustness analyses using Fail-Safe N procedures showed that the bilateral insula–frontal clusters were the most stable findings across analyses. These clusters remained significant even when large numbers of simulated null experiments were added, whereas clusters in cingulate and temporo-insular regions were more vulnerable. Within indirect threat, only the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex cluster demonstrated robustness. Within direct threat, both insula–claustrum–frontal clusters showed strong robustness, while the dorsal cingulate cluster did not exceed the lower robustness threshold.

Conclusion

The present meta-analysis demonstrates that indirect and direct social threat rely on a partially shared yet clearly differentiated neural architecture. Both threat types consistently recruited the left anterior insula extending into the inferior frontal gyrus, indicating a common system for detecting violations of interpersonal safety and rapidly evaluating the emotional significance of relational danger. This shared mechanism suggests that the human brain treats social threat as intrinsically salient, irrespective of whether the threat is subtle or overt. 

Beyond this core response, pronounced differences emerged. Indirect threat engaged the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and middle frontal gyrus, supporting processes related to interpreting others’ intentions, integrating self-relevant social meaning, and resolving the ambiguity that characterizes exclusion or negative evaluation. In contrast, direct threat elicited stronger convergence in the anterior cingulate cortex, mid-cingulate cortex, bilateral anterior insula, and insula–claustrum–frontal regions. These areas support conflict monitoring, autonomic arousal, and preparation for defensive or retaliatory behavior, capturing the heightened mobilization demands of explicit confrontation. 

Robustness analyses further emphasized this dissociation. Insula–frontal clusters were consistently stable across conditions, particularly for direct threat, whereas cingulate and temporo-insular regions varied more across analytic thresholds. For indirect threat, only the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex cluster demonstrated robustness, underscoring its central role in representing the evaluative and interpretive aspects of socially mediated adversity. 

Together, these findings challenge the assumption that all forms of social threat rely on a single neural pathway. Instead, they support a multidimensional organization in which indirect threat engages meaning-making and perspective-taking systems, and direct threat activates confrontation-oriented and action-preparatory circuits. This refined mechanistic account provides a foundation for improving the classification of social-threat paradigms and clarifies which neural components are expected to generalize across tasks and which are specific to particular forms of interpersonal danger. The framework also carries translational relevance: different clinical populations may exhibit selective alterations in one or both of these systems, shaping vulnerability to rejection sensitivity, unstable social relationships, or reactive aggression. More broadly, the meta-analysis offers a coherent neural model for understanding how humans detect, interpret, and respond to the diverse forms of threat embedded in social life.
