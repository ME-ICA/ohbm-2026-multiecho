Introduction

Functional connectivity (FC) measures the temporal dependency of neuronal activity between spatially distinct brain regions1. FC in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is derived from correlations of blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) signal fluctuations, providing insights into network communication during rest or task performance. Recent advances in neuromodulation techniques have spurred interest in their potential to modulate these networks particularly through photobiomodulation (PBM), a non-invasive approach that uses red or near-infrared light to influence cellular function and cerebral hemodynamics. Transcranial PBM (tPBM) delivers light through the scalp and skull, to cortical structures, while intranasal PBM (iPBM) targets deeper regions via the vascularized nasal cavity. Both methods have the potential to modulate large-scale brain networks2. Previous studies have shown that tPBM can modulate FC, an 808 nm NIR laser applied to the right frontal pole increased FC with multiple cortical areas by up to ~15% during stimulation3. Similarly, 8 minutes of tPBM has been shown to enhance fronto-parietal network FC and improve network efficiency measured with functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS)4. In this study, we investigate how tPBM and iPBM can modulate whole brain FC, quantified using global functional connectivity density (gFCD), as it is seed-independent and has previously been shown to correlate strongly with brain metabolism. 

Methods

15 healthy young adults (age 20-27, 6 M/9 F), underwent four simultaneous BOLD-fMRI-tPBM sessions and four BOLD-fMRI-iPBM sessions, totalling n=60 datasets per stimulation technique. The MRI compatible lasers were provided by Vielight Inc., and the light was delivered through a 10-meter, 400μm optic cable to the MRI. fMRI data was acquired using a Siemens Prisma 3 Tesla System (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with a 20-channel radio-frequency coil. Preprocessing was performed using the CONN toolbox, with the default_MNI preprocessed pipeline (bandpass filter: 0.01-0.1 Hz; regressed confounds: white matter, CSF and estimated participant-motion parameters). gFCD was calculated in MATLAB, by z-scoring each voxel’s time series, computing Pearson correlations with all other grey matter voxels, and applying a threshold of r=0.6 to define functional connections. The first five volumes of each session were rejected to allow the brain to enter steady state and all datasets were registered to MNI space for group analysis. gFCD maps were computed and the log of each voxel was taken for each participant and each condition (pre-stimulus, stimulus and post-stimulus). The stimulus maps and the post-stimulus maps were subtracted separately from the pre-stimulus maps at an individual-session level. 

Group-level statistical testing was performed using the fMRI software library (FSL). To characterize the pattern of gFCD within each condition, group-level t-statistic maps were generated by performing a one-sample t-test across subjects on the mean gFCD within each condition.  For each contrast (Stimulus-Pre and Post-Pre), a separate one-sample t-test was performed across subjects. Statistical significance was assessed using threshold-free-cluster-enchancement (TFCE), employing 5000 permutations. TFCE-corrected p-value maps were thresholded at p < 0.05, to identify regions showing significant increases or decreases in gFCD relative to the Pre condition.

Results

Figures 1a and 2a display the average gFCD maps across all participants, with voxel-wise gFCD ranging from approximately 100 to 5200 connections for tPBM and 100 to 4100 connections for iPBM. Regions with the highest gFCD (yellow) were predominantly located within the default-mode network (DMN), and this pattern was consistent across all three conditions examined. Figures 1b and 2b present the t-statistic maps generated using FSLs randomise, which took the full 4D stack of individual-session gFCD maps as input (n=60). The spatial pattern of these t-values closely resembles the raw gFCD distribution, again highlighting the DMN regions as the strongest contributors, with peak t-scores around ~30. Figures 1c and 2c show the unthresholded t-maps for the two subtraction conditions (1) PBM stimulus minus Pre-Stimulus (PBM-Pre), and (2) Post-stimulus minus Pre-Stimulus (Post-Pre). A clear difference in polarity emerged between these contrasts. The PBM-Pre map is dominated by negative t-values, for both iPBM and tPBM whereas the Post-Pre map contained more extensive positive t-values, reflecting an overall increase in gFCD after the stimulation period. These patterns are further emphasized in Figures 1d and 2d, which show the thresholded t-maps masked by the p < 0.05 TFCE significance clusters. After thresholding the PBM-pre comparison retained only significant negative t-scores, whereas the Post-Pre contrast showed primarily significant positive t-scores. Collectively, these results indicate that gFCD decreases during active PBM, followed by a rebound or overshoot increase post stimulation, consistent with a delayed or accumulating network-level response.  

Conclusion

This is the first study to explore the effects of iPBM on FC. Across 60 tPBM-fMRI sessions and 60 iPBM-fMRI sessions, we note that iPBM can elicit changes in FC comparable to that of tPBM. Spatially, iPBM generated larger PBM-Pre gFCD changes, where tPBM elicited a larger spatial response in the Post-Pre comparison. Both techniques induced a clear connectivity pattern, with a significant reduction in gFCD during stimulation, followed by a significant post-stimulation increase that exceeded pre-stimulus levels. For tPBM, this is the opposite of what has been reported in previous seed-based FC studies. In this work, we focus on gFCD rather than comparing the site of illumination to various brain regions. Our previous work demonstrated that the site of stimulation elicited the weakest tPBM %BOLD response compared to more distal regions5. With this in mind, rather than utilizing a seed-to-ROI approach that has been traditionally done, we examined global functional connectivity density (gFCD). This rebound-like response suggests that the effects of tPBM on large-scale network organizations may unfold over time, potentially reflecting delayed hemodynamic or metabolic adjustments rather than purely immediate neural modulation. These findings reinforce that tPBM can alter whole-brain connectivity across networks, and are the first to show that iPBM can influence FC. Future work should investigate the physiological basis of this pattern.
