Introduction

Cortical territories differ widely in their dynamic functional properties, which are shaped not only by local circuitry but also by subcortical inputs, particularly from the thalamus. This densely connected structure modulates cortical excitability, network coordination, and computational roles, yet is often neglected in human studies of cortical dynamics, especially in whole-brain network analyses. Animal and comparative studies suggest that cortical areas dynamically recruit distinct thalamic subpopulations,1 and, in humans and non-human primates, association cortices project more diffusely within the thalamus than sensorimotor regions2. Together, these findings suggest that variation in corticothalamic anatomical connectivity shapes the ability of cortical areas to engage distinct thalamic populations, contributing to spatial variability in cortical dynamics. To test this idea in humans, we examined how differences in the extent of anatomical connections within the thalamus relate to interregional variation in cortical functional properties. We hypothesized that cortical areas with more diffuse thalamic connections would exhibit more variable functional dynamics than those with more focal connections.

Methods

We used minimally-preprocessed diffusion and resting-state fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project (n=820). In our prior work,2 we introduced a method to quantify the spatial organization of anatomical projections within a target region. Briefly, we thresholded probabilistic tractography data to identify thalamic voxels with high streamline counts to each cortical parcel. We then calculated spatial measures that capture the pattern of these voxels, including Euclidean distance (ED), which quantifies its spatial extent (Fig. 1A). We used this method to produce a single ED value for each of 400 cortical parcels, defined by the Schaefer atlas.3 Lower ED values reflect more focal thalamic projections and higher ED values reflect more diffuse projections. 

To extend this framework, we compared cortical ED values to their resting-state functional dynamics. Specifically, we calculated multiple time series-derived measures of BOLD variability and entropy for each cortical parcel and thalamic voxel (Fig. 1B), and assessed their correspondence with ED across the cortex as well as the functional properties of thalamic regions targeted by each cortical area. Finally, using average controllability (AC) from Network Control Theory,4 we compared connectome models with and without thalamic connections to assess how thalamic projection patterns influence a cortical area’s capacity to control whole-brain dynamics (Fig. 1C). Thalamic connections were then weighted by each parcel’s ED value and cortical controllability was re-calculated, allowing us to quantify how ED-weighted thalamic inputs alter cortical controllability. For comparison, we repeated these analyses using mean structural connectivity (mean streamline count (SC); a commonly used but spatially naïve measure) and compared ED and mean SC–weighted controllability across seven resting-state networks defined by Yeo et al.5

Results

First, cortical areas with more diffuse thalamic connections exhibited greater variability and complexity in their own resting-state dynamics (Fig. 2A). Second, these diffusely projecting cortical areas preferentially targeted thalamic voxels with more variable, but not more complex, resting-state dynamics compared to voxels targeted by focal-projecting areas (Fig. 2B). Third, incorporating thalamic connections altered the topography of cortical controllability (Fig. 2C). Specifically, weighting thalamic connections by ED selectively increased controllability in association cortices, whereas weighting by mean thalamic connectivity (mean streamline count) produced little variation across networks (Fig. 2D). These results demonstrate the spatial pattenring of cortico-thalamic projections are critical for the control of whole-brain dynamics.

Conclusion

These findings show that diffuse anatomical connection patterns within the thalamus are associated with greater functional variability and enhanced cortical controllability relative to more focal thalamic connections. By integrating Network Control Theory, we move beyond purely correlational structure–function relationships to assess how thalamic architecture shapes cortical dynamics and control. Together, this work highlights how the spatial patterning of corticothalamic anatomical connections constrains the dynamic functional properties of the human brain.
