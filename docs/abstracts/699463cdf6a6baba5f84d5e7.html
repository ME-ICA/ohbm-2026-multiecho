Introduction

Dementia is one of the main burdens of disease globally, and its impact is predicted to worsen due to an ageing population1. It is now well recognised that the pathologies that contribute to dementia development and its known risk factors progress over decades and probably across the whole lifespan. Therefore, it is critical to identify the precise underlying biological mechanisms contributing to the disease and particularly those likely to have additive effects throughout life. Indeed, we know from classical twin studies that 60-70% of dementia risk is genetically determined, yet only a modest proportion (~30%) of specific variants have been identified, a phenomenon term “missing heritability”. Similarly, only a fraction of the environmental risk factors for dementia have been identified. The brain is particularly vulnerable to DNA damage since neurons mostly do not regenerate2. It is also very vulnerable to cardiometabolic risk factors which are known to impair cerebral blood flow, promote white matter hyperintensities (WMH) and cerebrovascular disease, through pro-inflammatory processes and oxidative stress3,4. Yet, we have a poor understanding of how variability in DNA repair mechanisms interacts with cardiometabolic risk, and whether such interactions may partly explain the “missing heritability” of complex disease such as dementia. This study aims to address these questions by testing associations between genetic variants in DNA repair genes and cognitive and brain outcomes, accounting for interactions with cardiometabolic risk.

Methods

Participants (n=376,533; 56.9 years (8.0); 54.2% female) of white-British ancestry from the UK biobank, for whom cognitive test results and whole-exome sequencing measures are available, were included. Of these, 37,247 participants have neuroimaging measures available and contributed to the genetic-brain analyses. A total of 3,487 genetic variants in 39 DNA repair genes of interest were tested, including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and indels. Cognitive outcome measures included fluid intelligence (FIQ), symbol-digit matching task (SDMT), visual matching (MATCH), trail making (TRAIL1 and TRAIL2), and prospective memory (PMEM). MRI scans were acquired on 3T Siemens Skyra using a 32-channel head coil with a T1-weighted 3D magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo sequence (sagittal orientation; 1×1×1 mm; FOV=208×256×256) and segmented with Freesurfer (v7.3.2). Brain regions of interest included total brain (TBV), grey matter (GMV), left and right white matter (LWM/RWM), left and right hippocampi (LHC/RHC), as well as WMH volumes. A multidimensional cardiometabolic risk index (CMRI), which encompassed established risk factors such as blood pressure, heart diseases, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, adiposity, smoking and alcohol consumption, and exercise frequency was computed (range 0-17) and examined for its associations with cognitive function and brain outcomes. Genetic associations were tested in linear regression analyses using PLINK2 (v2.0.0-a.7LM), after controlling for covariates (age, sex, APOE ε4 carriership, top 10 genetic principal components to capture ancestry, and outcome-specific covariates). Besides the null model, genetic interactions with CMRI were assessed by further adjusting for CMRI and adding an SNP*CMRI interaction term (interaction model). α was set at < 0.05, Bonferroni corrected.

Results

Every one point increase in the CMRI was associated with poorer cognitive performance (MATCH: % effect = +0.12, p=2.2x10-19; TRAIL2: % effect = +0.18, p=1.4x10-4; PMEM: % effect = −0.09, p=8.2x10-5; FIQ: % effect = −0.34, p=8.4x10-38), reduced brain volumes (TBV: % effect = −0.16, p=6.0x10-83; GM: % effect = −0.17, p=5.8x10-57; LWM: % effect = −0.22, p=1.2x10-54; RWM: % effect = −0.21, p=1.3x10-49), and increased white-matter lesion burden (WMH: % effect = +4.68, p=1.5x10-63). A total of 107 genetic variants across 36 DNA repair genes were associated at Bonferroni significance (p≤1.4x10-5) with neurocognitive and brain outcomes (except RHC) (see Figure 1). Most associations were observed for WMH (43 variants across 27 genes) and SDMT (26 variants across 17 genes). Of the associated variants, 60.8% were detected only when analysing interactions with CMRI, but not in the null model. Significant associations with specific neurocognitive outcomes (including FIQ, PMEM, MATCH) were only detected when genetic interactions with CMRI were considered. Most variants (106 out of 107) were extremely rare, with an averaged minor allele frequency of 1.3x10-4 (or 1.3:10,000 presence ratio). Four associated variants were predicted to have a highly deleterious functional impact, including ‘stop gained’, ‘frameshift’ and ‘missense’ variants, located across the genes PARP2, ERCC3, GTF2H2, and OGG1. We also examined whether these associations were present for dementia as a binary outcome (cases=6,065, controls=370,468). Variants showed nominal significance (p<0.05) across 35 of the 36 genes previously associated with cognitive and brain outcomes.

Conclusion

Variability in DNA repair genes and cardio-metabolic risk were found to be independently as well as synergistically associated with brain structure, WMH, and cognitive function. Associations with dementia status in post-hoc analyses involved predominantly the same genes. Arguably the most important finding is that some significant contributions of genetic variants in DNA repair genes were only identified through their interactions with cardiometabolic risk. Overall, these findings support the hypothesis that interactions between rare genetic variants in biological processes that exert their influence throughout the lifespan, such as DNA repair, and cardiometabolic risk may explain some of the missing heritability and unidentified environmental influence. It is also particularly notable that WMH, known to be promoted by cardiometabolic risk factors, was the brain volume most strongly predicted by DNA repair genetic variability and by its interaction with the CMRI.
