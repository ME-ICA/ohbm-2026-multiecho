Introduction

    While excessive short video consumption and Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) both manifest as addiction-like behaviors, they likely stem from fundamentally distinct cognitive and neural mechanisms. The rapid, self-paced nature of short-form video content promotes a form of consumption typically characterized by a passive, low-cognitive-load state primarily driven by immediate gratification and salience capture. This continuous stream of novel, highly curated content might leverage neural systems designed for rapid attention switching and emotion processing or reward prediction error, potentially bypassing areas associated with deliberate control.1

    In contrast, online gaming, particularly complex real-time strategy games like MOBA (Multiplayer Online Battle Arena) games, represents a goal-directed activity that demands the mobilization of significant cognitive resources. Successful performance in gaming requires the execution of complex, multi-step operations—such as tactical planning, resource management, and precise motor control to defeat opponents—necessitating continuous engagement of executive function.

    While internet gaming was likely driven by traditional dopaminergic pathways,2 short-video consumption may rely on a separate circuitry tuned to immediate affective feedback and stimulus novelty. To investigate these potential differences, we conducted two ecologically valid fMRI experiments using 3T scanners, directly comparing the neural signatures associated with autonomous short-video viewing and active, real-time competitive gaming.

Methods

Participants and fMRI tasks

    Sixty-seven participants were initially recruited for the Short-form Video Watching Task. Five were excluded due to excessive motion (maximum displacement > 3mm or 3º, or the number of time points with Framewise Displacement > 0.5 mm exceeded 15% of the total), task incompletion, or poor image quality, resulting in a final sample of 62 participants (age: 23.10 ± 2.46). For the Discontinuous League of Legends Task, fifty-four participants were recruited, with twenty-two excluded due to excessive motion, leaving 32 participants in the final analysis (age: 22.84 ± 2.03, 2 females). All procedures were approved by the institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Short-form video watching task 

    This task employed a block design consisting of two 6-minute video-watching blocks interspersed with three 30-second rest blocks. A database of 160 short videos (recorded from the Tiktok platform, length: 7s to 104s, Mean: 23.7s) was used. To simulate real-life consumption, participants were instructed to relax and were given full autonomy to switch to the next video clip at any time by pressing a button. 

Discontinuous League of Legends Task

    Participants performed a discontinuous real-time competitive gaming task adapted from League of Legends over a total duration of approximately 20 minutes. The task comprised five 3-minute competitive game blocks where participants engaged in free-form confrontation using their most skilled champion. Each game block was followed by a 30-second rest period during which the screen displayed a uniform gray field with a central fixation cross (“+”). The task was controlled using MR-compatible equipment, including a mouse and keyboard, within the 3T scanner.

fMRI acquisition and GLM Analysis

    Brain imaging data were collected in a Siemens 3.0-T scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma) with a 20-channel coil. Structural images were acquired with a T1-weighted magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo sequence. Functional images were collected using a gradient echo planar imaging sequence with multi-band acceleration (factor = 4, TR = 1000 ms, TE = 34 ms, voxel size = 2.50 × 2.50 × 2.50 mm3, voxel matrix = 92 × 92, flip angle = 50 °, field of view = 230 mm, slices number = 52). Data underwent slice timing and motion correction, followed by normalization to MNI space.

    Both the Discontinuous League of Legends Task and the Short-form Video Watching Task utilized a dual-modeling approach, incorporating both block-wise and event-related General Linear Models (GLMs) to analyze the functional imaging data.

    For the Short-form Video Watching Task, one GLM implemented a block-wise model to assess the overall neural response during video blocks relative to rest. A separate event-related GLM modeled the distinct video types: “Like” type (videos watched to completion) and “Dislike” type (videos skipped before the halfway point) were treated as separate regressors to investigate the neural correlates of preference.

    For the Discontinuous League of Legends Task, one GLM served as a block-wise model, considering the 3-minute gaming periods as the primary regressor. A more detailed event-related GLM was generated by encoding game actions into distinct event-based regressors. Our primary comparison of interest here contrasted the neural substrates of the “Fight” condition (active engagement with enemies) against the “Move” condition (simple locomotion within the game).

Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA)

    To further investigate whether the activation of the amygdala during video consuming reflected reward-related processing, we performed an RSA. The activation pattern of the amygdala under the “Like” condition was compared to a predefined reward pattern (generated from a NeuroSynth search for the term “reward”) and contrasted against the similarity between the “Dislike” condition and the reward pattern. Significance was determined via a 5000-permutation test.

Results

    We conducted a region-of-interest (ROI) analysis, extracting beta values from traditional reward processing regions such as Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA), Nucleus Accumbens (NAcc), Substantia Nigra (SN), and the amygdala, based on its relevance in our prior work on short-video viewing (Figure 1A). 

    Our block-level neural response analysis revealed a striking dissociation between the two tasks (Figure 1B, C). The short-video task significantly activated the amygdala (t(61) = 10.03, p < 0.001) and the SN (t(61) = 5.70, p < 0.001), yet failed to elicit significant activation in traditional dopaminergic reward regions, such as the VTA (t(61) = 0.43, p = 0.671) and NAcc  (t(61) = -1.00, p = 0.323). Conversely, the gaming blocks primarily elicited activation in the SN (t(31) = 4.97, p < 0.001), with no other regions displaying significant activation (all p > 0.05).

    Further event-related analyses provided granular insights. In the short-video task, both “Like” and “Dislike” conditions elicited significant activation in amygdala (like: t(61) = 7.04, p < 0.001, dislike: t(61) = 6.36, p < 0.001) and SN (like: t(61) = 3.74, p < 0.001, dislike: t(61) = 4.65, p < 0.001). In addition, the “Like” condition elicited significantly higher activation in the amygdala compared to the “Dislike” condition (t(61) = 3.85, p < 0.001, Figure 1B), but failed to elicit significant activation in traditional dopaminergic reward regions, such as the VTA (all p value > 0.05) or NAcc. Notably, the NAcc even displayed deactivation. In the gaming task, contrasting “Fight” events (attacking or responding to attacks) with “Move” events (simple locomotion) showed that the VTA (t(31) = 2.30, p = 0.028, Figure 1C) and SN (t(31) = 3.19, p = 0.003) were significantly more active during “Fight” events.

    The RSA results demonstrated that the activation pattern of the amygdala under the “Like” condition was significantly more similar to the predefined reward pattern than random levels ( p < 0.001, Figure 1D). Furthermore, the similarity between the “Like” condition and the reward pattern was significantly greater than the similarity between the “Dislike” condition and the reward pattern ( p < 0.001 ), while the “Dislike” similarity did not exceed random levels ( p = 0.065).

    The cognitive control regions were defined according to the work by Flugge et al.,3 which segmented prefrontal cortex into several sub-regions based on resting-state functional

magnetic resonance imaging data. The results revealed divergent activation patterns: short video viewing appeared to be associated with more inhibition in these regions, whereas gaming tended to induce greater activation (Figure 1E).

Conclusion

    Our findings demonstrate a striking dissociation in the underlying neural mechanisms of short-form video consumption and competitive internet gaming. Internet gaming relies heavily on the traditional dopaminergic reward pathways, evidenced by increased VTA and SN activation during active “Fight” events. In contrast, short-form video consumption appears to be driven by an emotion- and salience-related neural circuitry centered on the amygdala. The amygdala showed heightened activation for “Like” videos, and its activation pattern significantly correlated with an established reward signature in the RSA. This result  suggests that the reinforcing nature of short-video viewing relies on an amygdala-centered pathway, potentially the amygdala-SN projection. This is consistent with the notion that the amygdala-SN pathway is responsible for eliciting reinforcement when linked to voluntary actions but fails to support Pavlovian associations,4 suggesting that the voluntary choice to switch (or not switch) videos leverages this specific reinforcement loop for immediate, affective rewards. The absence of significant NAcc or VTA activation during the video block further supports the hypothesis that this behavior is distinct from the typical goal-oriented reward processing seen in drug addiction or gaming.

    This neural dissociation is further supported by the divergent activation of cognitive control regions (PFC). The observed inhibition of these areas during short-video viewing suggests a state of reduced cognitive effort and passive immersion—a neurophysiological signature of low-demand content consumption. Conversely, the induced activation in these same regions during gaming confirms the high cognitive load and executive resource mobilization required for successful competitive play.

    Collectively, these results strongly suggest that while both behaviors are highly engaging and potentially addictive, they exploit distinct neural mechanisms: short-video consumption is driven by an affective/salience-driven pathway that minimizes cognitive control, whereas competitive gaming is driven by a goal-directed, dopaminergic pathway that necessitates high cognitive control.
