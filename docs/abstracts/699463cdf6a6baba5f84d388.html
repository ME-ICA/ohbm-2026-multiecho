Introduction

Aging is associated with altered sensorimotor function and reorganization of neural networks. In a previous study [1], it was demonstrated that 𝐺𝐴𝐵𝐴𝐴 receptor availability was higher in older adults compared to young adults in the primary sensorimotor cortex. Here, we analyze previously acquired but unanalyzed simultaneous [¹¹C] flumazenil PET–rsfMRI data to investigate whether functional connectivity reorganization spatially corresponds to these GABA-receptor availability changes in the whole brain. Simultaneous PET–fMRI enables linking features of the neurotransmitter system and connectivity, yet lacks standardized pipelines for analysis [2].  Here we propose a framework using simultaneous PET–rsfMRI and motor task scores to localize age effects and regress behavioral performance. 

Methods

Simultaneous PET–rsfMRI data and motor response scores were acquired in young (N=15, 8 females, age 23.40+-2.12) and 15 older adults (N=15, 7 females, age 70.73+-3.97), participants are the same as in [1]. Whole-brain voxelwise comparisons of PET signal were made between older and younger adults using GLMs. The resulting difference masks were then parcellated using the Jülich Histological (cytoarchitectonic) Atlas, which is well suited for this analysis because our PET signal reflects receptor-level biochemistry that closely follows cortical cytoarchitecture. This parcellation allowed us to derive anatomically and biochemically meaningful ROI seeds for subsequent analyses. For each ROI, the mean fMRI BOLD signal was compared between young and older adults using a GLM, and statistical significance was determined using voxelwise FDR correction at p < 0.05. Motor task performance scores were included as behavioral outcomes in PET-only, rsfMRI-only, and combined PET+rsfMRI models to assess age-related imaging differences predict individual motor outcomes.

Results

Whole-brain PET GLM revealed widespread regions in which older adults showed greater GABAergic activity than younger adults, alongside a smaller cingulate cluster where younger adults showed higher activity (voxelwise cluster-corrected p < 0.05). ROI-wise analyses within the PET regions that show higher signal in older than younger participant  demonstrated that younger adults displayed significantly higher mean fMRI BOLD signal (GM Hippocampus (cornu ammonis R, entorhinal cortex L, hippocampal-amygdaloid transition area L), GM Inferior parietal lobule PF L, Secondary somatosensory cortex (Parietal operculum OP3 R), WM Inferior occipito-frontal fascicle L, WM Uncinate fascicle L, GM Insula Id1 L and Thalamus), indicating an age-related inverse relationship between GABAergic activity and fMRI signal intensity (FDR p < 0.05). In contrast, within the PET regions that show higher signal in younger than older participant, younger adults consistently showed greater fMRI connectivity across ROIs, confirming that regions with reduced PET GABA measures in older adults also exhibited reduced functional signal (FDR p < 0.05) (WM Inferior occipito-frontal fascicle R). Behavior–imaging regression analyses are currently ongoing, and therefore motor performance results are not yet available at this stage.

Conclusion

These findings integrate previously unanalyzed rsfMRI data with simultaneously acquired receptor-level PET measures, providing new insights into how aging affects the neurochemical–functional architecture supporting motor learning. Across regions showing age-related increases or decreases in 𝐺𝐴𝐵𝐴𝐴 receptor availability, older adults consistently exhibited reduced BOLD signal, suggesting that multiple physiological mechanisms may link inhibitory receptor changes to functional activity. Potential explanations include age-related dedifferentiation leading to less selective and more widespread neural activation, shifts in the excitatory–inhibitory balance that increase metabolic demand when inhibitory control is reduced, compensatory recruitment of larger neural populations to achieve similar behavioral goals, or age-related alterations in receptor composition that shift inhibition from phasic to more tonic forms, thereby dampening BOLD responses.
