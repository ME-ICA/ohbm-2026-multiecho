Introduction

Aging is accompanied by progressive declines in physical function that increase risk for falls, healthcare burden, and loss of independence. Mobility, an aspect of physical function, is particularly vulnerable to age-related decline and encompasses interrelated but distinct components such as gait, gait variability, and balance. Age-related changes in neuromuscular and sensory systems may disrupt these components, underscoring the importance of examining mobility as a multidimensional construct rather than a single global measure.

Previous research suggests that poor mobility may be associated with reduced integrity of white matter tracts as measured by fractional anisotropy (FA) derived from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). White matter integrity declines gradually across adulthood, with pronounced vulnerability in frontal and fronto-subcortical pathways involved in motor control. Prior studies have linked poorer gait or balance to lower FA in regions including the forceps and cerebellar peduncles.1,2 Most studies to date have examined single mobility measures independently, such as gait speed or impaired balance. Additionally, extant work focuses on older individuals3 or clinical populations4 (e.g., Parkinson’s disease5), limiting understanding of how multiple mobility components jointly relate to white matter integrity across adulthood. Examining individuals across the adult lifespan allows for observations regarding variability and trajectories in mobility and white matter before the onset of overt disease or functional impairment. A lifespan approach may also help identify early markers of neural decline and domain-specific mobility-white matter associations. These insights will inform early risk stratification and mobility-focused interventions aimed at preserving neural integrity and functional independence in aging.

Methods

Data from 203 adults (mean age: 51.7 years, age range: 18-85, 124 females) who participated in the Fitness, Aging, Stress, and TBI Exposure Repository with complete mobility testing and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were analyzed.

Mobility measures were collected using spatiotemporal gait and static balance tasks. Gait was assessed using LEGSysTM accelerometer sensors worn on both shanks while participants walked 40 feet at their usual pace. Data collection began when the midline of the foot crossed the start line and ended when it crossed the finish line, with analyses restricted to the middle 50% of strides to eliminate steps associated with ramping up or down of the gait cycle. Extracted gait metrics included stride velocity (meters/second), cadence (steps/minute), double-support time (%, time spent on both feet), and their coefficients of variability. Balance was evaluated using BalanSensTM gyroscope sensors on the right shank and lumbar spine to measure sway index, or the deviation from center of mass, during four 30-second standing balance conditions of increasing difficulty (eyes open/closed on firm/foam surfaces).

Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to dimensionally reduce mobility variables. Sampling adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Components were retained based on eigenvalues and visual inspection of a scree plot. Varimax rotation was applied, and component loadings exceeding 0.50 were used to define each mobility domain.

MRI data were acquired with a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner with a 32-channel head coil. DTI data were acquired across four runs: two runs with 98 diffusion directions and two runs with 99 diffusion directions, with each pair collected in opposite phase encoding directions (anterior-to-posterior [AP] and posterior-to-anterior [PA]). Acquisition parameters included: field of view (FOV) = 210 mm, repetition time (TR) = 3230 milliseconds, echo time (TE) = 89.2 ms, voxel resolution = 1.5 mm3, b-values = 0 and 3000 s/mm2, and a flip angle = 78 degrees.

Preprocessing and analyses were performed using the Diffusion Toolbox in the Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Brain Software Library (FMRIB, FSL; version 6.0.7.13). Motion, eddy current, and susceptibility-induced distortions were corrected using FSL’s eddy and topup tools. Eddy corrected for head motion and distortion due to eddy currents, whereas topup corrected susceptibility-induced distortions using opposite-phase encoding acquisitions. FSL’s Brain Extraction Tool removed non-brain tissue from non-diffusion-weighted (b = 0) images to create a brain mask. dtifit estimated diffusion tensors to generate FA values for each voxel.

FA maps were processed using Tract-Based Spatial Statistics (TBSS) to conduct voxelwise comparisons of white matter microstructure. All individual FA maps were registered to the FMRIB58 template in MNI152 standard space, and a mean FA skeleton was generated to represent the central white matter structures common to all participants (threshold = 0.20). To examine the relationship between mobility domains and FA, voxelwise analyses were conducted using the FSL’s randomise function. General linear models included covariates (i.e., sex, age), mobility domains resulting from PCA, and an Age x Mobility domain interaction term. Separate models were run for each mobility domain to maximize statistical power and minimize multicollinearity. Threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) was applied to the statistical maps (5,000 permutations). Significant clusters > 50 voxels were identified using the cluster command and the location of clusters were identified using the Johns Hopkins University (JHU) DTI-based white matter atlas and the JHU white matter tractography atlas. Binarized masks for clusters were generated using fslmaths, and FA values within identified clusters were extracted using fslmeants.

Results

PCA revealed four distinct domains of mobility explaining 68% of the variation in the data. Components were labeled as Static Balance (sway: eyes open firm surface, eyes closed firm surface, eyes open foam surface, eyes closed foam surface; 21% variance), Gait (cadence, stride velocity, double support; 19% variance), Gait Variability (cadence variability, stride velocity variability; 18% variance), and Double Support Variability (double support variability; 11% variance). No cross-loadings or multicollinearity were observed between components.

We observed a positive association between Gait and FA in multiple tracts (Figure 1), including the left and right inferior longitudinal fasciculus, right inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, right superior longitudinal fasciculus, and the right superior corona radiata. No associations were observed between other mobility domains and FA, and no Age x Mobility interactions were observed.

Conclusion

In the present study, mobility was shown to comprise separable domains reflecting aspects of static balance, gait, gait variability, and double support variability. This multidimensional structure provided a potential framework for understanding how specific features of mobility may differentially relate to white matter integrity.

Better gait performance, characterized by faster stride velocity, higher cadence, and reduced double-support time, was associated with higher FA across multiple white matter tracts including the inferior longitudinal fasciculus, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, superior longitudinal fasciculus, and superior corona radiata. These tracts support integration of sensory, visual, and motor information necessary for efficient locomotion, suggesting that gait performance may relate to the integrity of distributed white matter tracts. The spatial extent of these findings underscores gait as a potentially robust indicator of white matter health across adulthood.

In contrast, no significant associations were observed between other mobility domains (i.e., static balance, gait variability, or double support variability) and FA. Although prior work has linked balance or gait variability to white matter integrity, such studies examined these measures in isolation or within older-adult or clinical populations. By modeling multiple mobility domains, the present findings suggest that specific features of gait performance may capture unique variance in white matter integrity that is not observed with features of balance or gait variability in healthy, community-dwelling adults.

Notably, the absence of Age x Mobility Domain interactions suggests that the relationship between gait and white matter integrity was consistent across adulthood, rather than specific to an age group. This finding supports the value of an adult lifespan approach, suggesting that gait-related white matter differences are evident prior to overt mobility impairment or advanced age.

Together, these results highlight the importance of examining mobility as a multidimensional construct and recognize that specific domains may be differentially associated with white matter microstructure. Additionally, gait may serve as an objective marker of brain aging. This work extends prior research that has largely relied on a single mobility measure or focused on older adult or clinical populations. Future research is needed to determine whether interventions targeting gait may support preservation of white matter integrity and functional independence later in life.
