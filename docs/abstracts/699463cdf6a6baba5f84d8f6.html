Introduction

Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (RBD) is a parasomnia characterized by REM sleep without atonia (RWA). and dream enactment behavior. RBD is a risk factor for synucleinopathies, including Parkinson’s Disease (PD) and dementia with Lewy Bodies,1 and is therefore a desirable model for studying these diseases in early stages. Changes in white matter microstructural integrity have been described in PD and have been correlated with disease progression using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI),2 although it is difficult to characterize these changes due to the non-specific nature of DTI. Neurite orientation and dispersion imaging (NODDI)3 overcomes some limitations of DTI by using compartmental modeling to derive neurite density index (NDI), orientation dispersion index (ODI), and volume of isotropic free water diffusion corresponding to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). In this study, we use NODDI to investigate white matter structural differences in RBD using these metrics.

Methods

Multi-shell diffusion weighted imaging (ms-DWI) data were acquired on a wide-bore GE Signa Premier 3T MR scanner from 26 controls (84.6% male, mean age = 63.8 years) without RWA on polysomnography, and 43 participants with a sleep study finding RWA (86.0% male, mean age = 64.7 years), most of whom also described dream enactment behavior. Diffusion weighted images were acquired at b=300 (n=9), 850 (n=18), and 2000 (n=36) s/mm2, in addition to ten b=0 images, in opposite phase encoding (AP/PA) directions with TE/TR = 72.7/4000 ms, at 2 mm3 isotropic spatial resolution. Data were acquired in a total of 126 unique diffusion directions between the two phase encoding directions. Data were preprocessed using DESIGNER guidelines4 via MRtrix3 and NODDI model was estimated using Dmipy using the multi-tissue response function to derive NDI, ODI, and CSF. Tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) framework was used to project the NODDI measures onto the deep white matter skeleton. Voxel-wise group differences within the white matter skeleton were evaluated with one-tailed tests, adjusting for age, sex, and years of education. Analyses were conducted using the FSL-PALM tool, with 500 permutations and tail acceleration. To observe combined evidence across ODI, NDI, and CSF, we used non-parametric combination with Fisher’s method with threshold free cluster enhancement (TFCE). Effects were considered significant at p<0.05unc, considering only clusters larger than 500 mm3. These clusters were classified using the Harvard-Oxford subcortical atlas.

Results

NPC Fisher analysis revealed a cluster of significant one-tailed effects. This cluster had a volume of 544 mm3 and lay largely in the brainstem (proportion = 98.4%) (Fig. 1A). Within this cluster, ODI and CSF were lower in RBD subjects than controls (Fig. 1B).

Conclusion

The goal of this work was to identify differences in white matter between polysomnographically-proven RBD patients and controls by examining the specific metrics described by NODDI. Our findings indicate that these differences are most prominent in the brainstem, which aligns with the prevailing understanding that neurodegeneration in PD begins in the brainstem and surrounding structures. Within the brainstem, we specifically observed lower ODI among the disease group. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated lower ODI in the brainstem among patients with isolated RBD,5 which may be interpreted to mean reduced neurite complexity, possibly due to dendritic loss. Interestingly, we also observed lower CSF in the brainstem among RBD patients, which would suggest less inflammation or tissue damage. In our further analyses, we plan to incorporate additional healthy control data to eliminate group size as a potential source of bias, as well as consider additional subgroup analyses (for example, idiopathic versus provoked RBD).
