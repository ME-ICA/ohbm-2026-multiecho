Introduction

Face recognition is a complex and highly developed skill for humans, supported by several face-selective regions predominantly located in the ventral occipitotemporal cortex (VOTC). Face-selective clusters have been consistently detected in the lateral section of the posterior/middle fusiform gyrus (LatFG, “fusiform face area” or FFA1) and the lateral part of the inferior occipital lobe (IOG, “occipital face area” or OFA2). For years now, these areas have been considered as the core of the human cortical network for face processing, although strong face-selective regions have also been identified in the anterior temporal lobe (e.g., anterior fusiform gyrus, antFG3). How these face-selective regions are interconnected and functionally interact within this complex network is still poorly understood. Most studies on the structural and functional connectivity (e.g., through diffusion tensor imaging and resting-state/functional MRI) subtending this network provide incomplete information due to specific limitations (e.g., low temporal resolution and signal artefacts). Thanks to an original effective connectivity approach based on the application of direct electrical stimulation (DES) to a specific region of interest, with the concurrent recording of functional responses in other brain areas through frequency-tagging4, we aim to provide further evidence on the subject. 

Methods

This approach relies on the intracerebral electrodes implanted in patients affected by drug-resistant epilepsy (stereo-electroencephalography, SEEG), which allow us to modulate the activity of a spatially restricted brain area through electrical stimulation while simultaneously observing how this affects the functional responses recorded in other brain areas through frequency-tagged visual presentation (or Fast Periodic Visual Stimulation, FPVS). For each of the 21 patients included in this study (15 females; average age 37.6±9 years; 19 right-handed), intracerebral electrical stimulations were applied between two adjacent pairs of contacts, while they viewed sequences of a frequency-tagged paradigm (familiar face discrimination with familiar natural images; Figure 1). The intensity of the stimulation differed across patients, as it was decided by the neurologist for each patient (average 1.02±0.12mA). For each patient, we selected several stimulation sites by choosing contacts that displayed strong face-selective responses and that were also located in key brain regions, known for their involvement in face recognition: IOG, LatMidFG (including the LatFG, middle FG, and the adjacent occipitotemporal sulcus), antFG (including the antFG and the 2 adjacent sulci, anterior occipitotemporal sulcus and anterior collateral sulcus) and the TP (referring to regions more anterior than the antFG, towards the temporal pole). For each patient, we selected one stimulated site per region (average number per patient: 2.19±0.9), for a total of 46 stimulated sites included in the study (right hemisphere: 3 in the TP, 12 in the antFG, 9 in the LatMidFG, 5 in the IOG; left hemisphere: 5 in the antFG, 10 in the LatMidFG, 2 in the IOG). The patients were not aware of the stimulation onset and termination. The procedure was as follows: a familiar face discrimination with natural images continuous sequence was launched, running for 70s. A bipolar stimulation was manually triggered approximately 20s after the onset of the sequence and lasted for 10s. Then, the sequence continued to run for approximately 40 seconds, without stimulation (Figure 1). 
The SEEG recordings corresponding to the 70s sequences were then divided into periods of 10s: 2 periods before stimulation (Pre1 and Pre2), 1 stimulation period, and 3 periods after stimulation (Post1, Post2, and Post3). Following the quantification of the face response in each of these six time windows, we focused on the amplitude decrease observed during the stimulation, i.e., the face response recorded during stimulation was subtracted from the one recorded in Pre2 and transformed into a Z-score (by computing the difference between the amplitude at the target frequency bin and the mean amplitude of the corresponding 18 surrounding bins, divided by the standard deviation of amplitudes of these 18 bins). A contact was considered as showing a significant amplitude reduction of the face response during stimulation if its z-score was above 2.32 (p < 0.01). Based on the anatomical location of these affected contacts, we inferred the functional connectivity between the stimulated brain regions and the affected regions. To make sure we had good coverage of our regions of interest, we focused our analyses only on areas covered by at least 10 recording contacts. In addition, one region was considered as affected only if we observed at least two affected contacts located in that area.

Results

By observing how the modulation of specific face-selective areas affects the rest of the functional network, we inferred strong bidirectional and unidirectional connections across the core face-selective regions in the bilateral VOTC (Figure 2; in red and blue, respectively). We found evidence of bidirectional effective connectivity between pairs of face-selective regions in the same hemisphere: in the right hemisphere, between the TP and the antFG, between the antFG and the LatMidFG, and between the LatMidFG and the IOG; while in the left hemisphere, between the antFG and the LatMidFG. We also observed strong cross-hemispheric connections, mostly involving the bilateral antFG. Notably, the stimulations of the right LatMidFG and antFG affected almost the entire cortical face network in both hemispheres (5/5 regions and 6/6, respectively). 

Conclusion

By modulating the activity of several face-selective regions in the bilateral VOTC, we were able to get a glimpse of the complex connectivity subtending the cortical face network. The key role of the right LatMidFG/FFA was once again confirmed by the strong connectivity that links this region with most of the face-selective clusters in the bilateral VOTC. The left LatMidFG also displayed strong connectivity with the rest of the network, although it was mostly restricted to the left hemisphere. The pattern of connectivity of the right and left antFG reached most of the key face-selective areas, although it was mostly restricted to intrahemispheric connections for the left antFG. This further strengthens the link between this structure and face recognition in the human brain. These results are not enough to delineate a new model to describe the structure behind this complex functional network. Nevertheless, they point towards a strongly dynamic structure, which includes continuous information flows between the core areas in the right hemisphere and a strong input from the left hemisphere.
