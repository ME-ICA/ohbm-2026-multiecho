Introduction

Methylphenidate (MPH) is a widely prescribed psychostimulant that enhances dopaminergic and noradrenergic transmission by blocking dopamine and norepinephrine transporters, thereby strengthening perception–action coupling and executive control processes1. Although robust behavioral evidence supports MPH’s efficacy in improving attention and cognitive flexibility, its precise influence on large-scale cortical network dynamics remains unclear. Previous neuroimaging work has primarily examined regional activation or directed connectivity2, offering limited insight into how strongly cortical regions share and coordinate task-related neural activity.

Frequency-tagged stimulation paradigms offer a powerful approach to quantify neural entrainment, allowing precise measurement of how cortical regions respond to rhythmic input and propagate stimulus-related activity3. This makes them well-suited for studying how pharmacological agents modulate critical neurotransmitter systems (i.e., catecholamines), such as MPH, alter large-scale communication patterns. However, most existing analytical approaches focus on single connectivity metrics, such as coherence, that capture only isolated aspects of neural coupling and fail to represent the multidimensional, context-dependent organization of cortical networks. To overcome this limitation, we employed a fuzzy connectivity framework, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of MPH-induced changes in cortical network dynamics.

We examined directional connectivity among four task-relevant cortical regions: the anterior lateral temporal (ALT), posterior temporal lateral (PTL), insular cortex (IC), and inferior frontal cortex (IFC), across three cognitive phases: planning, standby, and perception. This approach enables identification of how MPH dynamically reshapes inter-regional communication during task execution, providing mechanistic insight into its therapeutic action and informing optimization of stimulant treatment strategies.

Methods

We analyzed electroencephalogram (EEG) signals from 54 healthy adults (25 females, 28 males; mean age 24.7 ± 2.5 years) who completed a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study. One participant was excluded from connectivity analyses due to excessive artifacts (final n = 53). Each participant completed two experimental sessions, receiving oral methylphenidate (MPH; 0.25 mg/kg, mean dose 18.7 mg) in one session and a placebo in the other, with session order pseudorandomized and balanced for biological sex and task condition. All participants performed a forced-choice action-effect binding task with frequency-tagged visual stimulation4. The task required participants to press a key (left or right CTRL) based on a colored cue (red or blue circle) following a 2000 ms fixation period. Correct responses within a 1000 ms response window triggered immediate presentation of a visual action effect (flickering cartoon images of a sheep or cat at 4.5) and a corresponding auditory effect (sheep or cat call at 55 dB) for 2000 ms. The task comprised three distinct phases: planning (the pre-stimulus period during cue presentation and action preparation), standby (the waiting period between the keypress and the onset of the action effect), and perception (the post-stimulus period during action effect processing). Each session consisted of 30 practice trials followed by 150 experimental trials. EEG data were recorded using a high-density electrode array and subsequently source-localized using linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamforming to extract time series from four regions of interest: anterior lateral temporal (ALT), posterior temporal lateral (PTL), insular cortex (IC), and inferior frontal cortex (IFC). These regions were selected based on their established roles in action-effect binding, attention, executive function, and sensory processing, which are known to be modulated by MPH.

Fuzzy connectivity analysis was implemented by integrating three complementary membership functions that quantify different aspects of neural entrainment: (1) canonical correlation between observed signals and expected 4.5 Hz stimulation patterns (including 1st harmonic), which captures how well regional activity matches the entrainment signal; (2) time-frequency power at the stimulation frequency, which measures the strength of entrainment; and (3) phase stability across trials, which quantifies the consistency of phase relationships. Fuzzy subsethood (FSH) connectivity5 was then derived to capture directional connectivity between regions, providing information about how strongly one region's entrained activity is contained within another region's activity. Connectivity matrices were computed trial-wise for each subject, pharmacological condition (placebo vs. verum), and task phase.

Statistical analyses focused on MPH effects (verum - placebo) across task phases. MPH effect matrices were computed for each phase by subtracting the group-mean connectivity in the placebo group from that in the verum group. Repeated-measures ANOVA was employed to assess phase-dependent effects within each group, with task phase (planning, standby, perception) as the within-subjects factor. MPH/placebo × phase interactions were examined to identify phase-specific MPH effects. Pairwise comparisons between phases were conducted using paired t-tests with Bayesian factor analysis (BF10) to quantify evidence strength, providing both frequentist and Bayesian perspectives on the data. Effect sizes were computed using Cohen's d for paired comparisons and partial eta-squared ( ) for ANOVA models. All analyses were performed on FSH connectivity measures across all ROI pairs.

Results

MPH effect analyses (verum - placebo) revealed phase-dependent modulations of FSH connectivity that varied substantially across task phases (Figure 1, A, B, and C). MPH effect matrices for planning, standby, and perception phases demonstrated distinct patterns of MPH modulation across all ROI pairs. The most pronounced MPH effects occurred during the standby phase, with IFC→IC connectivity showing the largest reduction under MPH (Cohen's d = -0.58, p = 0.003, BF10 = 0.84). IFC→ALT connectivity also showed significant reductions during standby (d = -0.49, p = 0.014, BF10 = 0.94) and perception (d = -0.46, p = 0.02, BF10 = 0.97), indicating consistent MPH-induced decreases in frontal-to-temporal pathways. IC→IFC connectivity showed reductions during both standby (d = -0.45, p = 0.023, BF10 = 0.98) and perception (d = -0.4, p = 0.042, BF10 = 1.04) phases. During the perception phase, ALT→IFC and IC→IFC connections showed moderate reductions (d = -0.39, p = 0.045, BF10 = 1.04; d = -0.40, p = 0.042, BF10 = 1.04), while most other connections showed non-significant MPH effects (Table 1).

The phase-dependent nature of MPH effects was particularly evident in bidirectional connectivity trajectories (Figure 1, E, F, G, H, I, and J). Within-group analyses revealed robust phase-dependent effects in the verum group: PTL→IFC connectivity showed the largest phase-dependent increase from planning to perception (d = 1.01, p < 0.001, BF10 = 3.01), while ALT→PTL showed strong phase-dependent reductions (d = -0.99, p < 0.001, BF10 = 2.90). However, direct comparisons (verum vs placebo) revealed that MPH primarily modulated connectivity during standby and perception phases, with the strongest effects observed in frontal-insular and frontal-temporal pathways. MPH effects during the planning phase were generally smaller and non-significant, suggesting that MPH's modulatory influence is most pronounced during action-effect processing rather than action preparation. The comprehensive view across all six ROI pairs (ALT-PTL, ALT-IC, ALT-IFC, PTL-IC, PTL-IFC, IC-IFC) revealed that bidirectional connectivity patterns were differentially modulated by MPH, with some connections showing stronger MPH effects in one direction than the other (Table 1).

Repeated-measures ANOVA within the verum group confirmed robust phase-dependent effects across all ROI pairs (all p < 0.001,   > 0.93), with strong Bayesian evidence (BF10 median pairwise > 1.5) for phase differences in most connections. The mean MPH effect across all connections (Figure 1, D) showed consistent MPH modulation across all three phases, with standby and perception phases showing the largest overall MPH effect magnitudes. These findings demonstrate that MPH's effects on cortical network connectivity are not uniform but rather phase-specific, with distinct modulatory patterns during planning, standby, and perception phases.

Conclusion

Our fuzzy connectivity framework successfully captured phase-specific effects of MPH on cortical networks during frequency-tagged stimulation, as comprehensively visualized in Figure 1. The effects (verum - placebo) varied substantially across task phases, demonstrating that MPH's modulatory influence is context-dependent rather than uniform. The findings reveal that MPH primarily reduced connectivity in frontal-insular and frontal-temporal pathways during standby and perception phases, with the strongest effects observed in IFC→IC and IFC→ALT connections during standby. During perception, similar reductions were observed in IFC→ALT, IC→IFC, and ALT→IFC connections (Table 1). The bidirectional connectivity trajectories shown in Figure 1 illustrate that MPH’s effects are not symmetric: because FSH directionality reflects how much the task-related activity of one ROI is contained within another, some connections show stronger MPH-induced changes in one containment direction than the reverse. These phase-specific MPH effects indicate that MPH selectively reorganizes how task-related information in one region is embedded within others, leading to distinct patterns of network communication across planning, standby, and perception phases.

The integration of canonical correlation, time-frequency power, and phase stability through fuzzy logic provided a more comprehensive characterization of neural entrainment than any single measure alone, capturing multi-dimensional aspects of how cortical regions respond to and propagate stimulation signals. The robust phase-dependent effects within groups (  > 0.93) and MPH effects between groups (Cohen's d up to -0.58) demonstrate the sensitivity of this approach to detect both phase-dependent and pharmacological-related changes in network organization. As illustrated in Figure 1, K, the comprehensive mapping of MPH effects across all ROI pairs and phases reveals the full complexity of MPH's modulatory influence on cortical networks. These findings advance our understanding of how MPH modulates cortical network dynamics and suggest that the drug's therapeutic effects may be mediated, at least in part, by enhanced coordination of frontal-temporal circuits during perception and by altered temporal-posterior communication during stimulus processing. The fuzzy connectivity approach may serve as a valuable tool for characterizing pharmacological mechanisms and potentially predicting individual pharmacology responses. Future work should investigate how these connectivity changes relate to behavioral performance measures and clinical outcomes, and explore whether connectivity patterns can serve as biomarkers for treatment optimization.
