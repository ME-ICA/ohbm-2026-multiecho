Introduction

Schizophrenia (SCZ) is increasingly conceptualized as a disorder of disrupted neural dynamics driven by breakdowns in excitation–inhibition (E/I) balance.1 While neuroimaging reliably shows structural abnormalities and large-scale dysconnectivity, these findings largely describe spatial architecture rather than temporal organization. Because cognition depends on intrinsically generated activity that is both temporally persistent and flexible, weakened inhibitory control is expected to erode the normal persistence and complexity of spontaneous activity, impairing information maintenance and integrative processing.

Detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) applied to resting state fMRI (rsfMRI) provides a principled measure of this temporal domain. The DFA-derived Hurst exponent (DFA-Hurst) quantifies scale-free long-range temporal correlations, indexing how strongly regional signals depart from uncorrelated noise toward persistent “long-memory” dynamics.2 Since temporal persistence reflects recurrent excitation under inhibitory regulation, DFA-Hurst provides a noninvasive proxy of local circuit integrity and E/I balance. Although localized reductions have been reported in SCZ, it remains unclear whether temporal abnormalities are dispersed or organized by a broader architectural principle.

This study tests the hypothesis that SCZ perturbs the unimodal-to-transmodal cortical hierarchy, resulting in a systematic compression that disproportionately affects transmodal hubs. We further define epicenters as regions showing pronounced local DFA-Hurst deficits coupled with concordant impairment across their functional connectivity–weighted neighborhoods.3 By leveraging a large, harmonized multisite cohort comprising discovery and replication samples, the research maps alterations in the DFA-Hurst, identifies such epicenters under stringent null models, and relates the associated temporal signatures to clinical symptom dimensions through multivariate brain-behavior analysis.

Methods

Multisite studies benefit from a unified protocol to minimize variability from site-specific scanning parameters. Here, we assembled a large dataset of T1-weighted and rsfMRI data from 7 sites using two scanner brands, and split it into a discovery cohort (SCZ-D) and a replication cohort (SCZ-R1). SCZ-D included 637 participants (311 HCS, 326 SCZ) scanned at 4 hospitals on Siemens Trio Tim 3T systems, while SCZ-R1 included 430 participants (221 HCS, 209 SCZ) from 3 hospitals using GE Signa 3T scanners.4 All rsfMRI data were preprocessed with a uniform BRANT (v3.35) pipeline, including removal of the first 10 volumes, slice-timing correction, co-registration/normalization, nuisance regression, band-pass filtering, and global signal regression to mitigate motion and physiological noise. BOLD signals were averaged within 246 Brainnetome ROIs to form a 246×230 time-series matrix, and static functional connectivity (FC) was computed using Pearson correlations between ROI pairs. T1-weighted images were processed with CAT12 (SPM), including denoising, intensity normalization, and linear/nonlinear registration to a standard template.

To characterize long-range temporal correlations, we treated each ROI time series as a fractionally integrated process and applied DFA. In practice, this involve first integrating the raw signal, segmenting it into overlapping windows of varying lengths, and removing linear trends within each segment. The fluctuation magnitude was then computed across all window size, and a log-log regression of fluctuation amplitude versus window length yielded DFA-Hurst. By iterating this procedure over all regions, we obtained DFA-Hurst per ROI, reflecting how strongly each region’s spontaneous activity departs from uncorrelated (white-noise) behavior toward persistent (long-memory) dynamics. We then estimated the collective neighbors’ deficits of i-th brain region (Di=Σdj×Connectionij)as the mean weighted deficits of all the brain regions that are connected to node i by network connection, where dj was the deficit of the j-th neighbor of node i,  Connectionij was the FC strength between node i and j, and Ni was the total number of neighbors that were connected to node i (i.e., node degree). 5

Spearman correlations quantified the association between node-level deficits and weighted neighbor deficits, with significance evaluated using two null models: (1) a spatial permutation (spin) test preserving spatial autocorrelation via 10,000 spherical rotations, and (2) a degree- and edge-length preserving rewired network null generated by swapping edges within Euclidean distance bins (10,000 iterations), with p-values defined as the proportion of null correlations exceeding the empirical value.

Finally, we used partial least squares correlation (PLSC) to link imaging and clinical measures. Here, the imaging matrix X comprised regional DFA-Hurst from brain areas showing significant case-control differences, and the clinical matrix Y consisted of 30 item-level PANSS scores. Both matrices were z-scored across participants prior to analysis to ensure comparability. We computed the cross-covariance matrix R = XTY and applied SVD (R = USVT), where U and V represent imaging and clinical salience vectors and S is a diagonal matrix containing singular values. Each latent component produced subject-specific imaging and clinical scores (latent-space projections), whose correlation indexed imaging-clinical coupling. Component significance was tested with 10,000 permutations by shuffling rows of Y, and feature stability was assessed with 10,000 bootstrap resamples; bootstrap ratios were used to identify reliable brain and PANSS contributors at the 95% confidence threshold.

Results

Across healthy participants, DFA-Hurst map showed a systematic spatial hierarchy across cortex and subcortex. Cortical regions generally exhibited higher DFA-Hurst than subcortical structures. At the cortical network level, mean DFA-Hurst varied systematically across canonical functional networks. Primary sensory systems showed relatively lower DFA-Hurst values, consistent with fast, stimulus-driven processing, whereas transmodal association networks such as the frontoparietal control and default mode network showed higher DFA-Hurst values, consistent with slow, integrative processing and persistent activity. This pattern indicates that DFA-Hurst follow a unimodal-to-transmodal hierarchy.

Case-control comparisons revealed widespread alterations of the DFA-Hurst in SCZ, affecting both cortical and subcortical regions. The majority of alterations were characterized by significant reductions, predominantly located in the default mode and frontoparietal networks, whereas elevations were sparse and mainly confined to subcortical regions, particularly the basal ganglia. Although DFA-Hurst deficits are spatially widespread, not all regions contribute equally to large-scale dysfunction. We therefore tested whether a subset of regions act as epicenters. The epicenter in this framework is a region that not only shows local abnormalities but is also embedded in a correspondent pattern with the abnormalities of their neighborhoods, suggesting a capacity to drive network-level deformation. Accordingly, for each region, epicenter likelihood was defined as a combination of its own alteration and the mean alteration of its functionally connected neighbors, with higher values indicating stronger epicenter potential.

Node deficits increased monotonically with FC-defined neighborhood deficits (Spearman’s r = 0.54, ppermutation < 0.001). These correlations were significantly stronger than expected under spatially constrained null models (brainsmash) and under degree -preserving rewired network nulls. High likelihood epicenters clustered in posterior cingulate, and parietal association cortices. Permutation testing further identified these regions as significant epicenters (ppermutation < 0.05). These regions are transmodal association hubs that play central roles in self-referential processing and internal mentation suggesting that instability of their intrinsic timescales may propagate through brain networks.

We assessed clinical relevance at cross-sectional symptom expression level. PLSC analysis was performed to identify multivariate associations between DFA-Hurst from epicenters and clinical symptomatology scores (PANSS). The principal latent component (LC-1) captured shared variance between neural and clinical measurements (Pearson’s r = 0.28, p < 0.001), linking DFA-Hurst disruption to symptom severity at the individual level. The PANSS loading profile of LC-1 indicates most negative symptoms and some positive symptoms such as conceptual disorganization contribute to this association. 

To assess the generalizability of primary findings, we performed replication analyses in SCZ-R1. Group-level comparisons revealed widespread reductions in the DFA-Hurst, with a spatial distribution highly consistent with that observed in SCZ-D (Pearson r = 0.50, ppermutation < 0.001). Epicenter mapping identified a set of influential nodes largely overlapping with those found in SCZ-D, prominently featuring cingulate regions (Pearson r = 0.76, ppermutation < 0.001). Furthermore, the clinical relevance of these epicenters was confirmed, as their spatial pattern was significantly associated with negative symptom severity. Collectively, the consistent replication of DFA-Hurst reductions across two independent samples, encompassing diverse scanners, sites, and acquisition parameters, supports the conclusion that DFA-Hurst is a robust and generalizable feature of SCZ.

Conclusion

Using a large harmonized multisite dataset, this study provides convergent evidence that SCZ involves not only altered spatial connectivity but also a systematic disruption of intrinsic temporal organization. RsfMRI was analyzed with detrended fluctuation analysis to quantify long range temporal correlations via the DFA-Hurst. In healthy individuals, DFA-Hurst values exhibit a robust unimodal to transmodal hierarchy. Primary sensory systems show lower temporal persistence, whereas transmodal association networks, including default mode and frontoparietal control systems, show higher values. Elevated DFA-Hurst reflects slower, more integrative, and more persistent endogenous dynamics, supporting intrinsic timescales as a fundamental organizing principle of brain function.

In SCZ, deviations in DFA-Hurst are widespread and topographically structured rather than random. The predominant alteration is reduced temporal persistence, most pronounced in transmodal association cortex. Increases in temporal persistence are sparse and primarily confined to subcortical regions such as the basal ganglia. These patterns are consistent with accounts of excitation inhibition imbalance in which diminished inhibitory regulation destabilizes recurrent dynamics. Such destabilization may compromise sustained information maintenance and integrative processing that are typically supported by transmodal systems.

Beyond mapping regional abnormalities, the analysis identifies areas with disproportionate network level influence. By integrating local DFA-Hurst deficits with functional connectivity weighted neighborhood impairment, the study delineates epicenters that show marked temporal persistence reductions alongside concordant abnormalities in their connected neighborhoods. The strongest epicenter candidates converge in posterior cingulate and parietal association cortices. As key hubs for internally directed cognition and self-referential processing, disruptions in intrinsic timescales within these regions may propagate through large scale networks and contribute to broader system level dysregulation.

Clinically, epicenter related temporal signatures are meaningfully associated with symptom expression. Multivariate brain behavior modeling links DFA-Hurst of epicenters to overall symptom severity, with prominent contributions from negative symptoms and selected positive or general symptoms. This suggests that impaired temporal persistence in transmodal hubs is not merely an imaging correlate but relates to phenotypic heterogeneity in SCZ. Crucially, principal findings including hierarchical compression, epicenter topology, and symptom associations replicate in an independent cohort scanned across different platforms and sites, supporting the robustness and generalizability of DFA-Hurst as a biomarker of SCZ.

In conclusion, our study provides a network-based model of SCZ pathophysiology. We demonstrate that SCZ is characterized by a systematic compression of intrinsic neural timescales, which is (1) rooted in local microcircuit instability of E/I balance, (2) spatially organized by network architecture around pivotal epicenters, and (3) clinically meaningful for explaining negative symptoms. This framework synthesizes spatial, temporal, and network perspectives on SCZ, offering a path toward biomarkers grounded in system neuroscience and novel therapeutic targets aimed at stabilizing aberrant neural dynamics.
