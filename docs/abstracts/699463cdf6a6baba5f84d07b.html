Introduction

Pregnancy induces profound endocrine, immune, metabolic, and neural adaptations that support fetal development but may also increase vulnerability to mental health difficulties [1]. Emerging neuroimaging studies have revealed pregnancy-related reductions in gray matter volume (GMV), especially in regions supporting social cognition and higher-order executive control [3]. Although these changes are often interpreted as adaptive neuroplastic remodeling that may facilitate caregiving behaviors, evidence also suggests that GMV reductions in specific regions may confer emotional or cognitive vulnerability [3]. 

Women enter pregnancy with diverse environmental exposures, including diet, sleep, substance use, physical activity, social support, and socioeconomic status, that are known to influence brain structure, neuroplasticity, and stress regulation. However, no study has systematically examined whether these modifiable preconception factors shape neural resilience in brain regions that later undergo pregnancy-related structural change.

Core maternal brain circuits involved in emotion regulation, reward processing, and caregiving overlap with pathways implicated in psychiatric risk [4]. Maternal psychological functioning, in turn, powerfully shapes children’s early socio-emotional development [5]. Yet it remains unknown whether individual differences in preconception GMV within pregnancy-vulnerable regions predict maternal mental health across the perinatal period and/or carry intergenerational significance.

Here, leveraging longitudinal neuroimaging and phenotypic data from the Singapore PREconception Study of long-Term maternal and child Outcomes (S-PRESTO) cohort [6], we aimed to identify pregnancy-vulnerable brain regions, determine how modifiable preconception environments shape this brain structural variability, and test whether these neural signatures forecast maternal cognitive and emotional health as well as children’s subsequent socio-emotional development.

Methods

Participants were drawn from the S-PRESTO cohort, a prospective preconception cohort [6]. Between 2015 and 2017, 1,032 women aged 18–45 years planning pregnancy within 12 months were recruited and followed through conception and early childhood. Women with pre-existing diabetes, current pregnancy or breastfeeding, recent use of fertility or hormonal medications, or major medical exclusions were not enrolled. 

For the present analysis, we included 194 women with preconception (PCV) MRI scans (age 19.49–43.58 years; 31.09 ± 3.66) and 61 women with MRI at 3 months postpartum (PNV) (age 19.81–42.21 years; 31.82 ± 3.92), including 35 women scanned at both timepoints (age 21.37–38.64 years; 30.33 ± 3.50). Child socio-emotional outcomes at 4 years of age were available for children born to 40 PCV mothers (4.05 ± 0.10 years; range 3.89–4.40) and 52 PNV mothers (4.05 ± 0.08 years; range 3.89–4.31). All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the SingHealth Institutional Review Board.

At PCV, women completed a comprehensive assessment capturing 52 lifestyle and environmental factors, including sociodemographic status, diet quality, physical activity, smoking and passive smoke exposure, alcohol use, sleep quality, recent life events, personality traits, and social and family support. Cognitive performance (executive function, working memory), mental health (depression, anxiety, perceived stress, general distress, self-efficacy, need to belong), and metabolic indicators (BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, glucose regulation, insulin resistance) were also assessed at PCV, with depression and anxiety reassessed at 3 and 6 months (M03, M06) postpartum. At 4 years of age, children’s socio-emotional and behavioral problems were evaluated using the CBCL 1.5–5, yielding syndrome, DSM-oriented, and internalizing/externalizing scores.

Structural T1-weighted MRI was acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma using a 1 mm isotropic MPRAGE sequence. Images underwent visual quality control and were processed with FreeSurfer 6.0 (standard and longitudinal pipelines). Cortical GMV was extracted from the Schaefer 400-parcel atlas and subcortical GMV from 14 Desikan–Killiany regions (total 414 ROIs) [7, 8].

To isolate pregnancy-related effects from aging, we first modeled age–GMV relationships at PCV (n=194) using linear regression for each ROI. For the 35 longitudinal participants, age-expected GMV at each visit was predicted from these models, and GMV deviation scores were computed as observed minus expected GMV [9, 10]. Paired t-tests on GMV deviation (PCV vs PNV) identified “pregnancy-vulnerable” ROIs after Holm–Bonferroni correction (p<0.05).

To determine which pregnancy-vulnerable regions were shaped by environmental  influences, Partial Least Squares (PLS) analysis was used to relate preconception GMV in these regions to the 52 lifestyle and environmental variables (n = 122 with complete data), identifying environmental-factor–linked pregnancy-vulnerable GMV regions.

To evaluate functional significance, we (i) conducted a PLS relating GMV in environmental-factor–linked ROIs to 34 cognitive, mental health, and metabolic measures at PCV; and (ii) calculated subject-specific brain scores from the GMV–environmental-factor pattern to predict postpartum depression and anxiety (PCV brain scores →PNV M03/M06 symptoms; PNV M03 brain scores → PNV M03/M06). Finally, Pearson correlations tested associations between maternal brain scores (PCV, PNV) and children’s socio-emotional outcomes at age 4.

Results

Across 414 ROIs, 93 regions showed significant pregnancy-related GMV deviation reductions from preconception to 3 months postpartum (Holm–Bonferroni corrected p<0.05; Fig. 1A). These pregnancy-vulnerable regions were widely distributed yet functionally specific, involving default mode (medial prefrontal, posterior cingulate/precuneus, inferior parietal, lateral temporal), frontoparietal control (lateral prefrontal, intraparietal, temporal control), salience/ventral attention (fronto-insular, orbitofrontal, opercular), dorsal attention (parieto- and temporo-occipital, superior parietal), and visual extrastriate networks, as well as bilateral hippocampus and right caudate. All showed GMV loss beyond age-expected levels, consistent with coordinated cortico-limbic–striatal remodeling across pregnancy.

A PLS relating preconception GMV in these 93 regions to 52 lifestyle and environmental factors revealed one single significant latent variable (LV1; permutation p<0.001) explaining 38.7% of shared variance (Fig. 1B). Thirty-seven ROIs, predominantly in default mode, frontoparietal, salience, limbic, and hippocampal/striatal areas, loaded reliably on LV1. Higher GMV in this pattern was associated with more advantaged socioeconomic status (education, income, perceived social standing), healthier diet and dietary supplement use, better sleep quality, nonsmoking and lower passive smoke exposure, higher conscientiousness, lower neuroticism, and stronger social and family support. In contrast, smoking, poor sleep, and lower support were linked to reduced GMV, indicating diminished structural resilience.

This environmental-factor-linked pregnancy-vulnerable GMV pattern showed strong functional relevance. A PLS linking GMV in these 37 regions to 34 PCV outcomes identified a robust brain–behavior relationship (LV1; p<0.001; 46.9% covariance; Fig. 1C). Specifically, higher GMV in medial/dorsomedial prefrontal, temporal, inferior parietal, ventrolateral prefrontal, anterior insular, and orbitofrontal/temporoparietal regions was associated with better executive function (higher mixed-switch accuracy, faster working-memory performance), lower depression, anxiety, and perceived stress, and more favorable metabolic health, particularly lower waist-to-hip ratio at preconception.

Brain scores derived from the environmental-factor-linked pattern showed prospective associations with postpartum emotional outcomes (Fig. 1D). Higher preconception brain scores predicted lower anxiety at 3 months (reduced total and state anxiety, higher positive affect/anxiety-reduction scores) and continued to predict lower anxiety at 6 months. Brain scores computed at the postpartum MRI session similarly correlated with lower concurrent anxiety and predicted more favorable anxiety profiles three months later, indicating enduring emotional resilience.

Finally, maternal brain scores were prospectively associated with children’s socio-emotional outcomes at age 4 (Fig. 1 E-F). Higher PCV brain scores predicted fewer withdrawn behaviors and depressive problems, whereas higher PNV brain scores were associated with lower withdrawn and internalizing symptoms. Thus, greater maternal structural robustness in pregnancy-vulnerable, environmental-factor-linked regions was associated with reduced socio-emotional difficulties in offspring.

Conclusion

This longitudinal preconception–postpartum study shows that pregnancy is accompanied by coordinated GMV loss across distributed cortico-limbic–striatal networks, beyond age-expected variation. Crucially, the degree of preconception vulnerability or robustness in these pregnancy-vulnerable regions is not random: it is systematically shaped by modifiable lifestyle, socioeconomic, psychological, and social-support factors.

Women with more advantaged environments, healthier behaviors, and stronger social support exhibited greater preconception GMV in pregnancy-vulnerable regions, along with better executive functioning, more favorable metabolic profiles, and fewer depressive and anxiety symptoms. The same environmental-factor-linked GMV pattern prospectively predicted lower postpartum anxiety and fewer socio-emotional difficulties in children at age 4, indicating that maternal neural resilience which was linked to modifiable factors before conception carries implications across the perinatal period and into the next generation.

These findings highlight preconception brain structure as a promising target for early identification of risk and for interventions aimed at improving sleep, diet quality, smoking cessation, stress management, and social support. Supporting women before they conceive may strengthen structural resilience in key maternal brain networks and promote healthier outcomes for both mothers and their children.
