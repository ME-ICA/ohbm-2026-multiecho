Introduction

Infant brain MRI segmentation remains difficult in early infancy, especially around 1–4 months, when myelination rapidly alters T1 contrast and gray/white matter can become nearly isointense. In this window, widely used tools (iBEAT, Infant FreeSurfer, Adult FreeSurfer, SynthSeg) show complementary strengths but also predictable failure modes, so relying on a single method often leads to substantial manual correction and limits scalability for larger cohorts. Here we present Medley, a physics-aware multi-tool curation workflow that treats existing segmenters as complementary specialists rather than competitors. Medley stabilizes tissue contrast using tissue-guided T1 intensity enhancement, corrects size/scale mismatches with controlled affine-based structural enlargement, and consolidates outputs into a single training-ready label map using anatomy-informed rules and targeted repairs. We evaluate Medley on T1-weighted infant scans from the Marcus Autism Center and the Baby Open Brains dataset, focusing on the 1–4 month isointense period.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing
T1-weighted MRI were acquired from naturally sleeping infants at the Marcus Autism Center using a 3T Siemens scanner (~0.8–1.0 mm isotropic). Additional validation used the Baby Open Brains dataset, which provides manual reference segmentations. Analyses were restricted to the 1–4 month age range (Baby Open Brains subset, N = 24), corresponding to the most challenging isointense period when myelination-driven T1 changes reduce GM/WM contrast. Standard preprocessing applied iBEAT for three-tissue maps (CSF/GM/WM) and SynthStrip for skull stripping/brain masking. Each scan was then processed independently by multiple segmentation tools—Adult FreeSurfer (v7.3.2), Infant FreeSurfer (v0.2), and SynthSeg (Robust variant, v2.0)—to generate anatomical labels used as inputs to Medley. No tool received manual initialization or subject-specific parameter tuning.

Quality control protocol
Visual QC used a binary salvageability criterion rather than graded scores. An experienced rater (>500 infant scans) classified each automated segmentation as either (i) requiring only localized touch-ups (small boundary fixes, removal of isolated mislabeled islands, correction of focal leakage) feasible within ~10 minutes, or (ii) exhibiting major structural failures requiring extensive redrawing of large regions. Category (i) scans passed QC and category (ii) scans failed. This definition reflects practical scalability constraints for large cohorts.

Medley pipeline
Medley produces a single training-ready label map from multiple imperfect segmentations in three stages.

  1. Physics-aware intensity enhancement: T1 intensities are normalized within the brain mask and adjusted using tissue-guided targets derived from the iBEAT tissue map to increase WM/GM/CSF separability. Target values are spatially smoothed near tissue boundaries to avoid artificial edges. This step modifies contrast but does not deform anatomy.

  2. Scale-aware structural enlargement: To reduce systematic under-segmentation associated with adult priors, Medley applies a controlled isotropic enlargement implemented through affine-based resampling (typical z≈1.25; explored 1.16–1.33). Intensities are resampled with trilinear interpolation and labels with nearest-neighbor interpolation, while keeping the volume center fixed in world space.

  3. Anatomy-aware label consolidation: Outputs are consolidated using deterministic, anatomy-informed rules rather than probabilistic fusion. Inputs include FreeSurfer aseg, SynthSeg labels, the SynthStrip brain mask, iBEAT tissue maps, and CHARM-derived head/brain segmentation. Deep gray nuclei and other high-confidence subcortical structures are preferentially taken from FreeSurfer when consistent with tissue class. Cortical GM is built by combining a high-confidence intersection across tools with a broader candidate union, followed by constrained growth, trimming to the brain mask, and limited gap filling guided by tissue class and local neighborhood consistency. Extracranial labels (e.g., skull/scalp) are imported from CHARM where appropriate. Small isolated islands and common artifacts are removed or reassigned using connected components and tissue/neighborhood agreement.

Results

Overall performance

We evaluated Medley on the Baby Open Brains 1–4 month subset derived from Marcus Autism Center T1-weighted scans (N = 24). Manual segmentations were used as reference on a subset of scans, and visual QC ratings were performed across the full 1–4 month cohort. QC pass rate was defined as the proportion of scans that did not require major manual intervention. Medley more than doubled QC pass rate relative to the best single tool (90% for Medley vs. 44% for SynthSeg; p < 0.001, McNemar’s test). For scans that still required edits, average manual touch-up time decreased from hours to minutes, supporting more scalable processing in the 1–4 month age range.

Comparison with baseline methods

Table 1 compares Medley against Robust SynthSeg on the same 24-subject cohort. Robust SynthSeg was used as the primary baseline because it is a state-of-the-art, contrast-agnostic method designed for challenging imaging conditions. Robust SynthSeg occasionally achieved higher Dice on specific structures (e.g., cerebellar cortex: 0.88 vs. 0.74; putamen: 0.81 vs. 0.77). However, Medley showed stronger whole-scan consistency, with higher micro Dice (0.84 vs. 0.75) and improved boundary accuracy (HD95: 2.3 mm vs. 3.5 mm). Importantly, Medley achieved a 90% QC pass rate compared with 44% for Robust SynthSeg, indicating more reliable performance across structures within individual scans rather than isolated improvements.

On the manually labeled 1–4 month subset (17 labels), Medley achieved macro Dice 0.74 and micro Dice 0.84. Performance was strongest for larger structures (e.g., cerebral white matter, cerebral cortex, thalamus, caudate, brainstem) and weaker for smaller or more variable regions (e.g., cerebellar white matter, accumbens area, ventral DC).

Component contributions

Ablation analysis showed that all three stages contribute to final performance. Removing the deterministic anatomy-aware consolidation step reduced QC pass rate from 90% to 52%. Removing per-tissue enhancement or scale-aware enlargement yielded intermediate QC pass rates (71% and 76%, respectively) but increased the amount of manual touch-up required; under a strict touch-up budget, effective QC dropped to 44%, comparable to the best single-tool baseline. Overall, the results suggest that contrast stabilization, simple geometric correction, and anatomy-aware consolidation work together to achieve high QC pass rates during the isointense window.

Conclusion

Accurate infant brain MRI segmentation remains difficult during the early isointense window, where standard tools show complementary strengths but also consistent failure modes. We introduce Medley, a physics-aware multi-tool curation workflow that integrates existing segmenters through (i) tissue-guided T1 intensity enhancement, (ii) controlled scale correction, and (iii) deterministic, anatomy-informed label consolidation. On the Baby Open Brains 1–4 month cohort (N = 24), Medley substantially improved practical usability: QC pass rate increased to 90% compared with 44% for the best single-tool baseline, boundary accuracy improved (HD95 2.3 mm vs 3.5 mm), and required manual touch-up time decreased from hours to minutes. Ablation results indicate that all stages contribute, with anatomy-aware consolidation providing the largest single gain in QC pass rate.

These findings support the idea that, for early infancy, reliability is better achieved by orchestrating imperfect tools than by relying on a single method. Medley produces training-ready labels that can reduce the manual burden of large developmental studies and enable more scalable model training and downstream analyses during the isointense period. Future work will expand evaluation to larger, more heterogeneous cohorts and will quantify downstream benefits for infant segmentation models trained on Medley-curated labels, with particular attention to remaining challenges in smaller structures (e.g., cerebellar white matter and ventral regions).
