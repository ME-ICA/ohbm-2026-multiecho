Introduction

Accurate early identification of dementia disorders is essential for effective intervention and drug development. However, the subtle and overlapping clinical symptoms between different types of dementia often hinders timely and accurate diagnosis, especially in the early phase.1 While neuroimaging is increasingly used to support diagnosis, no biomarker to date reliably captures disease-related brain changes across all stages and subtypes. Machine learning (ML) offers the ability to identify complex patterns of neurodegeneration in medical images, creating unprecedented opportunities for improved diagnosis and prognosis of dementia.2 One emerging ML-derived image-based biomarker is the brain age gap (BAG), defined as the difference between an individual’s predicted biological brain age and their true chronological age. Elevated BAG values have been linked to neurodegeneration in specific dementia subtypes, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD).3 However, traditional whole-brain BAG estimates provide only a single scalar value that cannot capture spatially heterogeneous patterns of neurodegeneration. Recently proposed methods extend whole-brain BAG estimation to the voxel level, producing spatially resolved BAG maps that may offer more anatomically specific insights into disease-related brain changes. However, voxel-wise BAG signatures have not been systematically evaluated across the full dementia spectrum yet, and their potential to differentiate dementia-specific morphological fingerprints remains unexplored. Thus, using structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data from the Canadian Consortium on Neurodegeneration in Aging (CCNA),4 we aimed to examine whether voxel-wise BAG predictions can reveal distinct neurodegenerative patterns across nine diagnostic groups in the dementia spectrum. By evaluating both spatial patterns and distributional properties, this work aims to establish voxel-wise BAG as a promising biomarker for dementia subtype differentiation. 

Methods

Structural T1-weighted MRI scans from CCNA participants across nine diagnostic dementia subtypes were processed using a standardized neuroimaging pipeline consisting of skull stripping (HD-BET), N4 bias-field correction, and affine spatial normalization. Voxel-wise biological brain-age predictions were computed employing a 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) trained on MRI scans from more than 5,000 cognitively healthy UK Biobank (UKBB) participants, enabling the model to learn normative patterns of age-related structural brain changes. For each CCNA subject, voxel-wise BAG maps were computed as the voxel-wise difference between predicted and chronological age. 

To remove age-related bias and ensure comparability of BAG values across cohorts, all BAG maps were corrected using an age-dependent linear regression model derived exclusively from the normal cognition (NC) CCNA group. This regression model was then applied to participants in all other diagnostic dementia subtype groups to obtain bias-corrected BAG values. 

The final dataset included NC (n=120), subjective cognitive impairment (SCI; n=140), mild cognitive impairment (MCI; n=150), AD (n=102), frontotemporal dementia (FTD; n=31), Lewy body dementia (LBD; n=25), vascular MCI (vMCI; n=23), AD with vascular pathology (AD-VaD; n=26), Parkinson’s disease MCI (PD-MCI; n=34), and PD dementia (PDD; n=18), with patient ages ranging from 45–80 years. For each participant, all voxel-wise values were extracted from the corrected 3D BAG map to form an empirical distribution, characterizing the overall magnitude and variability of predicted brain-age deviations across the brain. These individual distributions were then pooled within diagnostic groups for statistical comparison. Pairwise two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) tests were performed to assess whether voxel-wise BAG distributions differed significantly between the dementia groups investigated.  

As a secondary analysis, voxel-wise BAG maps were averaged within each diagnostic group to generate group-level, spatially-resolved BAG signatures (Figure 1). To quantify these patterns anatomically, each subtype-averaged BAG map was further analyzed within regions of the CerebrA parcellation atlas,5 yielding regional mean BAG values. These region-level features were then compared across groups to assess inter-subtype differences and to evaluate their correspondence with known neurodegenerative patterns. 

Results

Voxel-wise BAG distributions demonstrated clear dementia-specific differences (see Figure 2). Pairwise KS testing showed statistically significant distributional differences across nearly all diagnostic dementia groups (p<0.05), with the exception of PD dementia, whose distribution was more similar to those of LBD and AD-VaD. FTD exhibited the most broadened and right-shifted BAG distribution, reflecting pronounced structural degeneration, whereas SCI and NC displayed narrow, near-zero distributions. Overall, most dementia subtypes exhibited wider variance than prodromal conditions such as SCI and MCI. 

Group-averaged BAG maps revealed distinct, biologically coherent neurodegeneration profiles. More precisely, AD and FTD exhibited widespread BAG elevations consistent with advanced cortical and subcortical degeneration, whereas earlier-stage conditions, such as SCI and MCI, showed only mild regional BAG increases relative to normal controls. To further characterize the spatial distribution of high-BAG signals, regional analyses were performed using the top 25% of BAG values within each dementia group. In more advanced dementia groups, high-BAG voxels were concentrated in hallmark brain regions affected by neurodegeneration, including the basal ganglia, ventricular boundaries, and medial temporal structures. In addition, prominent high-BAG involvement in the cerebellar cortex and amygdala was observed across all dementia groups as well as vMCI and PD-MCI, whereas these regions exhibited minimal BAG values in NC, SCI, and typical MCI. 

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that voxel-wise brain-age-gap (BAG) maps capture distinct neurodegenerative fingerprints across a broad spectrum of dementia groups and hold promise as a biomarker for dementia subtype differentiation. BAG distributions differed significantly between most diagnostic groups, and group-averaged BAG maps revealed coherent spatial patterns that aligned with known disease-specific regions of vulnerability. Regional analyses further highlighted key neuroanatomical involvement, including the basal ganglia, medial temporal structures, and cerebellum, indicating that voxel-level BAG maps may provide sensitive and anatomically informative markers of neurodegeneration. 

Alongside subtype differentiation, these results show that voxel-wise BAG mapping may support enhanced clinical assessment and patient progress monitoring, offering a complementary tool for identifying atypical aging patterns and tracking disease progression. 

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Some diagnostic groups investigated were represented by relatively small sample sizes, which may reduce statistical power and the reliability of spatial patterns presented for those groups. Additionally, differences in age distributions across patient groups could introduce residual confounding, even after age-bias correction. Future work should validate these findings in larger and more balanced cohorts, and evaluate the clinical utility of BAG maps longitudinally.
