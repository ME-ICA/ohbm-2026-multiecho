Introduction

Human brain function exhibits a striking combination of stability and variability. Resting-state fMRI reveals that large-scale functional connectivity (FC) is highly organized and reproducible across individuals, reflecting conserved principles of cortical organization and information integration. At the same time, individual differences in FC reliably relate to cognition and behavior, raising the question: how does behavioral variability arise within a largely shared functional architecture?

Most existing approaches characterize individual differences either at the level of specific connections or through low-dimensional embeddings learned directly from individual FC matrices. 

However, these approaches often conflate two distinct sources of variation: differences in the global wiring principles that organize functional networks, and differences in how individuals deviate from these shared principles. Disentangling these components requires a principled reference space in which global organization is defined independently of individual variability.

Here, we propose a network-based framework that explicitly separates global wiring rules from structured deviations in functional brain networks. Global wiring principles are learned from the group-averaged FC and interpreted as dominant propagation patterns reflecting network topology. Individual variability is then characterized as residual structure relative to this shared scaffold. We show that these deviations are low-dimensional, spatially structured, and strongly associated with both functional individuality and behavior, providing a neurobiologically grounded account of how individual differences are organized within a common functional architecture.

Methods

Dataset
Resting-state fMRI data were obtained from the Human Connectome Project (HCP). We analyzed data from N = 700 healthy adults, each of whom completed a single 15-minute resting-state scan. Preprocessing followed the HCP minimal preprocessing pipeline and included gradient distortion correction, motion correction, spatial normalization to MNI space, ICA+FIX denoising, and registration to the CIFTI grayordinate space. The cerebral cortex was parcellated into 1,000 regions using the Schaefer atlas.

Residual functional connectivity
We define a population-level reference architecture using the group-averaged functional connectivity. Global wiring rules are estimated at the group level by capturing dominant indirect propagation patterns supported by the network topology. Residual connectivity is defined as the component of FC not explained by these shared wiring rules, isolating long-path and higher-order interactions beyond low-order network organization.

Individual-level deviations
For each individual, residual FC is computed by removing the group-level wiring component from the individual FC matrix. This yields subject-specific deviations quantified relative to a common population reference, ensuring that individual variability reflects departures from shared functional organization rather than differences in global structure estimation.

Deviation modes and projection
Spectral analysis of the group-level residual reveals a low-dimensional structure dominated by long-range communicability modes. The leading eigenvectors define a deviation space onto which individual residual FCs are projected to obtain compact coordinates summarizing individual differences along dominant deviation axes.

Results

Regional variability in the predictability of functional connectivity
Using higher-order network interactions, we fitted the group-averaged functional connectivity to identify shared wiring rules of large-scale brain organization. A substantial portion of functional connectivity was well reconstructed by a common wiring rule, but residual connectivity varied systematically across cortical regions. Primary sensorimotor systems showed low residuals, indicating high predictability, whereas association regions, particularly frontoparietal and higher-order integrative networks, exhibited larger residuals, reflecting pronounced regional differences in conformity to the shared wiring rule.

Residual connectivity exhibits structured, low-dimensional organization
Residual functional connectivity showed a clear and systematic organization across the cortex. Analysis of the group-level residual revealed that variability was dominated by a small number of coherent long-range integration patterns, consistent with Katz-type multi-step propagation in functional brain networks. These dominant patterns spanned sensorimotor and association systems, highlighting structured deviations that preferentially involve distributed, cross-network interactions rather than localized or unstructured connectivity differences.

Residual-based representations enhance individual identification
At the individual level, removing the group-level wiring component substantially improved individual identification compared to raw functional connectivity. Residual functional connectivity captured stable, subject-specific patterns that were reproducible within individuals across sessions. Individual differences were concentrated along a small number of dominant deviation modes, suggesting that functional individuality is organized within a constrained deviation space defined relative to shared wiring rules.

Behavioral performance is associated with deviations from shared wiring rules
Individual differences in behavioral performance were significantly associated with deviations in rule-based functional connectivity. Projection of individual residual networks onto dominant deviation modes outperformed models based on raw functional connectivity. These results indicate that behaviorally relevant variability is specifically linked to structured deviations from shared wiring rules rather than to overall connectivity patterns.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduce a framework that dissociates shared wiring principles from structured individual variability in functional brain networks. By defining global organization at the group level and quantifying deviations relative to this reference, we provide a principled account of how large-scale functional architecture simultaneously supports stability and individual diversity.

Our findings demonstrate that deviations from shared wiring rules are low-dimensional, anatomically structured, and behaviorally meaningful. Functional individuality and behavioral differences do not arise from arbitrary network variability, but from constrained displacements along dominant long-range propagation modes within a common functional scaffold. Global wiring rules define a shared scaffold, whereas structured deviations capture regionally specific and individual-specific variation relevant to behavior. This perspective reframes individual differences in functional connectivity as structured departures from shared organizational principles, offering a unified link between population-level architecture, individual network identity, and behavior.
