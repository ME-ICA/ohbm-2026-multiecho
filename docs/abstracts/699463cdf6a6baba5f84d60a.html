Introduction

The superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) is the major association fiber tract system connecting the ipsilateral prefrontal and posterior parietal areas (reviewed in¹). Although it is relatively well studied in non-human primates, the organization of human SLF cortical branches is still not fully clear. In the human and non-human primate literature, there is a general agreement that this fiber system is composed of three separable tracts, which are globally organized in a dorsomedial to ventrolateral fashion. A few studies have used anatomically-derived parcellations such as the Glasser atlas or functional-derived regions to investigate frontoparietal structural connectivity on the parcel-to-parcel level.²,³ However, there has been no systematic investigation into the spatial organization of the white matter within the three SLF tracts, with most structural network approaches on the whole-brain level that lack the specificity of a targeted approach and the few region-specific frontoparietal investigations were interested in only a limited number of areas that do not cover the entirety of the SLF tracts. We hypothesize that the internal organization of each SLF tract will mirror the dorsomedial to ventrolateral and anterior/posterior (i.e. more posterior frontal areas connected to more anterior parietal areas) organization, similar to that of the entire SLF.

Methods

High-resolution diffusion images from 50 healthy subjects (24F/26M, Age Range=22-35) of the Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA) cohort⁴ were used for this pilot study. Diffusion MRI protocol: multiband of 3, Spin-echo, TR=5520ms, T=89.5ms, 78deg flip angle, FOV=210x180, 1.25 isotropic voxels, b values=1000, 2000, and 3000 s/mm2, 90 L/R, R/L phase encoding directions per shell. Standard HCP-YA preprocessing was applied to the diffusion images, including susceptibility and eddy current distortion correction, brain extraction, structural and diffusion image co-registration, and freesurfer registration. Targeted probabilistic tractography was run for all 3 tracts for both hemispheres using a constrained spherical deconvolution algorithm (dhollander) in MRTrix3 (for software references, see https://mrtrix.readthedocs.io/en/latest/reference/references.html). Frontal and parietal inclusion regions of interest for each of the three tracts were individualized Glasser parcels selected from previous work identifying SLF tract terminations.⁵ An additional inclusion mask consisted of a midcortical plane. We also used an exclusion mask consisting of a midsagittal and temporal plane as well as the cingulum (from the HCP atlas), corpus callosum, and internal and external capsules (all from the JHU atlas) to prevent modeling of nearby tracts. Streamlines were seeded from the gray matter-white matter interface for anatomically constrained tractography. Standard parameters for streamline generation (MRTrix3) with the iFOD2 algorithm were used, excepting number of streamlines generated (5000) and maximum streamline length (500mm). Tractography was first seeded from the frontal to the parietal regions of interest and then in reverse, and the two outputs were added together. Resultant tracks were then cleaned using a scilpy script (scil_bundle_reject_outliers). The tck2connectome command was used on each tract to identify its parcel-level connectivity using both average fractional anisotropy and streamline proportion (number of streamlines between two parcels / total number of streamlines in track). Connectomes were then averaged across the 50 participants.

Results

We were able to replicate the canonical three part SLF system in both hemispheres for all participants (average FA: SLF-I: 0.55 +/- 0.02 (both hemispheres), SLF-II: 0.51(Left)/0.53(Right) +/- 0.03, SLF-III: 0.56(Left)/0.55(Right) +/- 0.02). For each tract, group-averaged connectomes revealed some consistent patterns of parcel-specific connectivity based on streamline proportion. For the SLF-I, connectivity seems to be driven by the frontal areas 6ma and SCEF and parietal areas 7am/7al. 6ma seems to have a stronger connectivity with 7al than 7am, and SCEF shows the reverse pattern, suggesting a somewhat dorsal/ventral as well as an anterior/posterior organization. For SLF-II,  connectivity is mostly driven by the parietal PFm (more connected to 55b than 8av) and PGs (more connected to 8av than 55b), again suggesting an anterior/posterior dissociation. For SLF-III, connectivity was highest between frontal 6r and parietal PF (the most dorsal regions), although frontal areas FOP1 and BA44 and parietal areas PFcm, RI, and PSL also displayed connectivity (areas FOP3, BA45, and FOP5 did not show appreciable connectivity). To note, there was also high standard deviation for streamline proportion across subjects, even for many edges with high proportions. When considering fractional anisotropy, edges with relatively high streamline proportions did show higher FA, but it was not a linear relationship.

Conclusion

Our observed streamline parcel-to-parcel connectivity is qualitatively similar to previously reported frontoparietal connectomes², although there were a number of network edges where we did not find significant connectivity and the previous authors did, and vice versa. Preliminary analysis revealed some preferential frontoparietal node connectivity that suggests a dorsal/ventral or anterior/posterior preference for streamline connectivity in SLF-I and II, supporting our hypothesis. However, further statistical analysis is necessary to determine whether the edge-level differences reflect topographical differences of connectivity. Furthermore, given the prevalence of SLF streamlines to originate or terminal in a limited number of Glasser parcels, analysis should be repeated with a finer-grain parcellation to better evaluate the organizational properties.
