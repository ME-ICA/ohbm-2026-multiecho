Introduction

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is a severe facial pain disorder traditionally attributed to aberrant trigeminal nerve activity, yet increasing evidence suggests that TN also involves broader network-level dysfunction.1 The claustrum, a thin, bilateral subcortical nucleus with dense and widespread cortical connectivity, has been attributed diverse functions and has recently emerged as a key node in pain processing and perceptual modulation in both animal models and human studies.2 Despite the potential role of the claustrum in pain, no study has examined whether claustral morphology is altered in TN. Here, we leveraged a large, public dataset to evaluate potential claustral structural changes in TN.

Methods

Dataset and claustrum segmentation. We analysed data from the Primary Trigeminal Neuralgia dataset (https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds005713/), comprising 119 TN patients (mean age 59.1 years, 70 female) and 53 healthy controls (HCs; mean age 55.2 years, 31 female). Analyses reported here focus on whole-brain T1-weighted MRI available at 3-Tesla with 0.5mm isotropic resolution, from which claustrum segmentations were derived via non-linear warping of a validated cross-modal probabilistic claustrum atlas also at 0.5mm resolution (Fig. 1A), which has demonstrated high anatomical fidelity compared to histology.3 Diffusion and functional MRI data are available in the same dataset, and analyses of claustral connectivity and function are currently underway.

Statistical analyses. Present analyses focus on claustrum volume derived from T1-weighted MRI segmentations. Group differences between TN and HC were tested using linear models adjusting for intracranial volume, age, and sex. Within the TN cohort, associations between claustrum volume and clinical characteristics (including pain severity, Sindou grade, and pain laterality) were examined using analogous covariate-adjusted linear models, with multiple comparisons controlled within families of related tests. Additionally, we examined the spatial distribution of structural differences within the claustrum using voxel-wise comparisons between TN patients and HCs, as well as within the TN cohort comparing high pain severity (≥5 on a 1 to 10 scale; n=37) versus low pain severity (<5; n=35) subgroups. Between-group differences were visualized through sagittal projections, highlighting claustral subregions that are either more frequently localized or more dense in one group relative to the other. This exploratory spatial analysis aimed to identify specific claustral subregions preferentially affected in TN or associated with pain severity, thereby informing targeted hypotheses for subsequent connectivity and functional analyses.

Results

Claustrum localization and validity. Claustrum volumes in both TN (989±107mm3) and HC (1024±125mm3) fell within the expected range for 0.5mm isotropic voxels in MRI, as established by comparison with resolution-matched reference values from a histological atlas in healthy adults,3 and with segmentations produced by a deep-learning approach,4 indicating that TN diagnosis does not preclude reliable atlas-based parcellation. In both TN and HC, the dorsal claustrum (defined as superior to the rhinal sulcus) was robustly identified in all participants with near-complete core overlap, encompassing the region most densely interconnected with prefrontal and cingulate cortices implicated in pain processing.5

Statistical results. No significant differences in claustrum volume were observed between HC and TN groups (Fig. 1B). In contrast, within the TN cohort, pain severity showed a positive association with claustrum volume, evident for total bilateral volume (p=0.016) as well as for left (p=0.019) and right (p=0.027) claustra examined separately (Fig. 1C). Spatial analysis revealed that structural differences were regionally specific rather than uniform. In the TN vs HC comparison, the ventral claustrum showed reduced spatial localization, while the claustral core remained consistently preserved in TN (Fig. 1B). Within the TN cohort, the ventral claustrum showed reduced localization in high pain severity cases compared to low pain severity cases, especially in the right hemisphere, whereas the core region showed relatively preserved or increased density in high pain cases (Fig. 1C).

Conclusion

No difference in overall claustrum volume was observed between TN and HC, which may reflect clinical heterogeneity and the limited sensitivity of volume as a morphological measurement. The positive association between pain severity and claustrum volume within TN is consistent with evidence that the claustrum participates in pain modulation and salience processing in humans.2 Spatially localized differences suggest that TN preferentially affects specific claustral subregions rather than the nucleus uniformly. The preserved core region may reflect compensatory or pathological engagement of claustrocingulate circuits previously shown to modulate nociceptive thresholds and pain-related behaviour in preclinical models.5 Conversely, the reduced ventral claustrum localization in high pain cases may indicate loss of inhibitory pain modulation, consistent with recent evidence that claustrocingulate pathway dysfunction contributes to pain hypersensitivity.5 Taken together, these findings suggest that the claustrum may encode clinically meaningful aspects of pain experience in TN through distinct subregional circuits and motivate future analyses of claustral connectivity and function, currently underway in the same dataset.
