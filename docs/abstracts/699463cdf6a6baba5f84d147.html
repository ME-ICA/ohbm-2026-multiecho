Introduction

Trigeminal neuralgia is characterized by severe, typically one-sided, stabbing facial pain attacks in the distribution of the trigeminal nerve (CNV). Classical TN (cTN) accounts for roughly three-quarters of all trigeminal neuralgia cases and is associated with neurovascular compression (NVC) of the CNV by an adjacent blood vessel. This NVC results in morphological changes at the root entry zone (REZ), a transitional region of the CNV as it shifts from peripheral to central myelination.1 

Although NVC is readily visualized on high-resolution T2-weighted MRI, structural imaging alone does not fully explain symptom severity, treatment variability, or the presence of bilateral CNV abnormalities reported in some studies (even though pain is experienced on one side of the face). Advanced techniques such as diffusion MRI (dMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) may provide deeper insights into CNV microstructure in cTN.2 

The purpose of this study was to investigate microstructural alterations in CNV at the REZ and at various locations along its cisternal segment in cTN patients and healthy controls (HCs) using a novel, high-resolution, CNV-specific DTI protocol less susceptible to partial volume effects.3 Our central hypothesis was that microstructural metrics, such as fractional anisotropy (FA) would be reduced, along with increased mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD) in the ipsilateral CNV in comparison to the contralateral CNV in cTN patients and HCs. These characteristics would be consistent with diminished axonal integrity and demyelination. Furthermore, we predicted that microstructural alterations in CNV would be most marked at the site of the REZ, corresponding to the spatial location of NVC.

Methods

We conducted a single-centre, prospective cohort study including 26 patients with classical trigeminal neuralgia (cTN) with medically refractory unilateral pain awaiting first-time microvascular decompression (MVD), and 14 healthy controls (HCs). Age and sex were compared using t-tests and Fisher’s exact tests. cTN participants were scanned within 30 days prior to MVD; HCs underwent a single scan. 

All participants received a previously validated, high-resolution, cranial-nerve–specific axial-oblique slab FLAIR-DTI sequence (NS-FL) optimized for visualization of CNV3 along with a T1-weighted anatomical scan. Diffusion images underwent correction for eddy currents, motion, signal drift, and Gibbs ringing using ExploreDTI.4 Corrections included affine registration of all diffusion-weighted images and b-vectors to the b0 reference volume. 

Four ROIs were placed on the FA colour map in the coronal plane in various locations along each CNV (Figure 1): 

  1. REZ ROI, at the point of trigeminal nerve emergence from the pons; 
  2. ROI3, 1.8 mm distal (3 axial slices) to the REZ; 
  3. ROI6, 1.8 mm distal to ROI3; 
  4. ROI9, 1.8 mm distal to ROI6. 

Microstructural metrics such as FA, MD, AD, RD, and eigenvalues (λ1, λ2, λ3) were extracted from each ROI from the ipsilateral and contralateral CNV in cTN patients and in left/right CNVs in HCs. Partial-volume signal in any given ROI was included. 

Microstructural metrics were compared across ROI locations within each CNV using non-parametric Friedman tests with post-hoc comparisons. To evaluate corresponding ROI differences in ipsilateral vs contralateral CNVs (along with left vs right CNVs in HCs), Wilcoxon signed-rank comparisons were performed. Left and right ROI metrics in HCs were also averaged to generate a single HC value per ROI. Percent-change measures (REZ ROI to ROI9) were added to quantify proximal-to-distal microstructural changes. Lastly, linear regressions were modelled to estimate FA slopes from proximal to distal along ipsilateral and contralateral CNVs in cTN, and along left and right CNVs in HCs.

CNV volumes were segmented from T1-weighted scans for each participant. Volume comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs test in ipsilateral vs. contralateral CNVs in cTN patients and left vs. right CNVs in HCs. Similar to microstructural metrics, we averaged left and right HC CNV volumes and compared them to cTN ipsilateral and contralateral CNVs using a Mann-Whitney U-test. 

Neurovascular compression (NVC) severity was graded in cTN patients on the ipsilateral and contralateral CNV using a validated four-point scale (0=no contact; 1=contact without deformity; 2=compression; 3=clear distortion) by a blinded expert neurosurgeon. 

A two-way ANOVA assessed interactions between NVC severity and FA change across ROI levels.

Results

cTN and HC groups were well matched in age (TN: 57 ± 11 years, range 27–74; HC: 55 ± 9 years, range 39–72; p = 0.4361), and there was no difference between groups in sex distribution (12F/14M and 9F/5M, p = 0.33). The number of cTN patients was 0/9/10/7 and 15/8/3/0 for ipsilateral and contralateral NVC scores of 0/1/2/3, respectively. 

cTN patients showed a striking bilateral proximal-to-distal microstructural gradient along the CNV. Significant differences were observed bilaterally in FA (p < 0.0001), MD (p < 0.0001), RD (p < 0.0001), and contralaterally in AD (p < 0.001). Post-hoc comparisons revealed several changes between ROI locations (Figure 2). 

Conversely, HCs did not show the same degree of microstructural change along ROI locations in the CNV. There was significant change in FA in the left CNV (p < 0.01). When averaging corresponding ROIs in the left and right CNV in HCs, FA changes became more prominent (p < 0.001). AD was another exception, showing significant change in the left CNV of HCs (p < 0.05). There were no differences observed in MD and RD in L, R CNVs, or when averaged. Similarly, no differences were observed across ROI locations in FA or AD in the right CNV of HCs. 

In comparing ipsilateral and contralateral ROIs in cTN patients, FA was significantly lower in ipsilateral ROI3 (p < 0.01) and ROI6 (p < 0.05). No differences were observed in left and right ROIs in HC CNVs. 

Percent change revealed a FA decline 27.2% (ipsilaterally) and 31.2% (contralaterally) in cTN vs ~16% in HCs. RD increased 24.8% (ipsilaterally) and 35.2% (contralaterally) in cTN vs near-zero change in HCs. MD increased 13–18% bilaterally in cTN vs minimal change in HCs.

Regression models revealed that FA in the contralateral CNV of cTN patients showed the steepest decline compared to left and right FA slopes of HC CNV (p<0.001). Additionally, ipsilateral FA in cTN patients was significantly steeper compared to the left FA slope in HC CNVs (p < 0.05). No differences were observed when comparing FA slopes in ipsilateral and right CNVs of cTN patients and HCs, respectively. 

No differences in CNV volume were found between cTN patients and HCs (between-group) or within cTN and HCs (within-group comparisons). 

Lastly, the degree of NVC did not correlate to the observed microstructural gradients in FA along the CNV. 

Conclusion

Using a high-resolution, nerve-specific FLAIR-DTI protocol, we demonstrated bilateral, spatially organized microstructural abnormalities along the CNV in cTN. Rather than being confined to the side of pain or to the specific site of vascular compression, diffusion metrics revealed a progressive proximal-to-distal decline in FA accompanied by bilateral increases in RD and MD, a pattern most consistent with impaired myelination and neuroinflammatory changes. 

Importantly, microstructural gradients in cTN were not explained by CNV volume, symptom laterality, or NVC severity, suggesting that focal neurovascular conflict alone does not account for the breadth of nerve pathology. Instead, our findings support a model in which cTN involves broader bilateral vulnerability of trigeminal nerve microstructure, potentially mediated by systemic or localized inflammatory processes affecting myelin integrity. The dissociation between NVC grade and diffusion abnormalities further challenges the traditional view that cTN results purely from NVC at the REZ.
