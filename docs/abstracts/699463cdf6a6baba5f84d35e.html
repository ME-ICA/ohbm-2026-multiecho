Introduction

The Geschwind-Galaburda hypothesis posits that high androgen levels in utero, as occur in male more than female fetuses, inhibit left hemispheric growth and thus result in a rightward shift of hemispheric asymmetry in men compared to women. While prenatal androgen exposure is generally higher in males than females, congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) is a rare condition that results in elevated prenatal androgen exposure in affected girls, but not boys. Thus, the Geschwind-Galaburda hypothesis would predict a rightward shift in asymmetry in girls and women with CAH compared to unaffected controls, similar to that observed in boys and men (albeit not necessarily as pronounced). Here we set out to test the hypothesis by examining global hemispheric asymmetry in the largest imaging cohort of people with CAH to date. In line with the hypothesis, we expected a significant diagnosis-by-sex interaction due to a rightward shift in asymmetry in both men with CAH and control men as well as in women with CAH (but not in control women).

Methods

T1-weighted MRI scans of 53 participants with CAH (33 women, 20 men) and 53 controls matched for sex, age, level of education, and verbal intelligence were processed via the CAT12 toolbox in SPM12. The T1-weighted images were corrected for magnetic field inhomogeneities, classified as gray matter (GM), white matter (WM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and spatially normalized to the MNI152NLin2009cAsym template as provided with the CAT12 toolbox. The normalized tissue segments were then modulated by the Jacobian determinant derived from the normalization matrix to preserve the original voxel-wise tissue volume. Subsequently, the infratentorial structures were removed and the cerebral parenchymal tissue volume (GM + WM) was calculated for each hemisphere. Using the resulting hemispheric volumes, a global asymmetry index was then calculated as AI = [right-left]/[0.5*(right+left)]. In addition, the total intracranial volume (TIV = GM + WM + CSF) was calculated to be later included as a covariate in the statistical model. The statistical analysis was performed using a general linear model to assess the effects of diagnosis (control vs. CAH) and sex (female vs. male), as well as the diagnosis-by-sex interaction (i.e., the potential effect of prenatal androgens, see Introduction). The Asymmetry Index constituted the dependent variable; diagnosis, sex, and the diagnosis-by-sex interaction the independent variables; and age and TIV the variables of no interest. Significance was confirmed at p≤0.05 using Monte-Carlo simulations with 10,000 permutations.

Results

There was no significant diagnosis-by-sex interaction. However, there was a significant main effect of sex, with a rightward shift in hemispheric asymmetry in males compared to females (Cohen’s d = 0.42, p=0.037). In addition, there was a significant main effect of diagnosis, with a rightward shift in hemispheric asymmetry in participants with CAH compared to controls (Cohen’s d=0.57, p=0.007).

Conclusion

The lack of a significant diagnosis-by-sex interaction suggests that any effects of prenatal androgens on brain asymmetry may be minimal, at least on a global scale with respect to hemispheric asymmetry. At first glance, this appears to contrast with the Geschwind-Galaburda hypothesis, which posits that prenatal androgens inhibit left-hemispheric growth and therefore produce a rightward shift in asymmetry. Notwithstanding, we observed a significant main effect of sex, with men (CAH and controls combined) showing a rightward shift in rightward asymmetry compared to women (CAH and controls combined). Thus, one might argue that this is broadly in line with the prediction of the Geschwind–Galaburda hypothesis, albeit not necessarily due to the proposed mechanism. In other words, given the aforementioned lack of a significant interaction, the observed shift in asymmetry in men may not be solely attributable to androgen effects, but could also reflect genetic, epigenetic, or environmental influences, perhaps in addition to any hormonal effects.

Interestingly, we also observed a significant main effect of diagnosis, with CAH participants (men and women combined) showing a rightward shift in asymmetry relative to controls (men and women combined). This observed shift in asymmetry might be attributable to genetic effects, as CAH is caused by mutation in the gene CYP21A2, and/or to treatment effects since CAH requires a lifelong substitution of glucocorticoids and sometimes mineralocorticoids.
