Introduction

Sleep is composed of Rapid-Eye-Movement (REM) and Non-REM (NREM) sleep stages, the latter further classified in N1, N2, N3 stages from light to deep sleep. Sleep stages are defined by specific electroencephalographic (EEG) signatures, and EEG rhythms are closely linked to brain hemodynamic activity, measurable with functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) or functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS). Recent EEG/fMRI studies revealed distinct oscillations in regional blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal during N2 and N3 sleep, correlating with the occurrence of  sleep spindles and slow waves 1. An enhancement in vasomotor (<0.1Hz) and respiratory (0.16-0.33Hz) oscillations has also been reported during NREM sleep which was linked to increased EEG slow oscillations 2. However, most of these findings were obtained from relatively short EEG/fMRI sleep recordings, and REM findings remain absent, highlighting the necessity of whole-night investigations. EEG/fMRI sleep studies are limited in duration due to immobility constraints, and the acoustic noise impedes reliable investigation of REM phasic/tonic microstructure. Alternatively, fNIRS emerged as a promising approach for prolonged sleep monitoring, allowing measurement of hemodynamic fluctuations by estimating cortical oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) concentration changes. Few EEG/fNIRS sleep studies reported sleep-stage specific HbO/HbR oscillations in healthy brain and were usually restricted to ~2-4 forehead channels3. We propose whole-night polysomnography (PSG)-EEG-fNIRS, using an optimized locally dense montage to maximize fNIRS sensitivity to targeted bilateral frontoparietal regions, allowing to investigate sleep-state-specific and region-specific patterns of HbO/HbR oscillations in healthy subjects.

Methods

Whole-night simultaneous PSG-EEG-fNIRS were recorded from 15 healthy young adults together with auxiliary signals (respiratory belt, pulse oximetry and mean arterial pressure (MAP)), monitoring systemic physiology. Sleep staging was completed by an expert neurophysiologist following the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommendations. Our personalized fNIRS optimal montage, targeting bilateral frontoparietal regions (2 light-sources and 4 detectors per region) were computed for each participant using individual anatomical information to optimize fNIRS optodes placement 4,5. Once optimal position of fNIRS montage were estimated for each participant, a 3D neuronavigation device was used to identify those positions on the participant’s head, and low profile optic fibers were finally glued to the head of the participant using a clinical adhesive, therefore allowing locally dense investigation of the 4 regions, while limiting the impact of motion during sleep, following our personalized fNIRS workflow5 (Figure 1). Standard fNIRS pre-processing included: filtering (0.002-0.5Hz), short distance channel regression to reduce signal influence from non-brain regions, oxy/deoxyhemoglobin estimation using modified Beer-Lambert law. We then applied time-frequency analysis using Morse analytical wavelet, using the Taeger-Kaiser normalization to disentangle oscillatory from aperiodic (1/f) components of HbO and HbR signals. HbO and HbR time-frequency results were considered to estimate HbO and HbR power spectra for each stable sleep state (~8min of arousal-free). Considering wakefulness fNIRS oscillations as our reference, we assessed whether relative changes of hemodynamic power during each sleep state were significantly different from the awake state, using general linear mixed effect model. To assess systemic physiological contributions to the fNIRS signals, similar time-frequency analysis was also applied to auxiliary signals (respiration, pulse oximetry, and MAP).

Results

Our results, based on 15 subjects (7 females, 18-35 years, mean sleep efficiency:90.3%), show sleep-state-specific signatures of hemodynamic oscillations in frontal and parietal regions. In frontal region, N1 was associated with a significant increase in vasomotor-band power (<0.1Hz) relative to wakefulness in HbO, (non-significant in HbR). On the other hand, N2 and N3 sleep showed progressive reductions within vasomotor power in both HbO and HbR. REM sleep was characterized by a re-emergence of low-frequency power, particularly in a very-low-frequency (< 0.01 Hz) range. In contrast, hemodynamic oscillations within the respiratory-band power (0.16-0.33Hz) were selectively enhanced during NREM sleep—with the largest increases observed in N3—and returned to wake-like levels in REM for both HbO and HbR. These patterns were similar but of weaker amplitude in parietal regions. Complementary analysis of auxiliary signals revealed a dominant oscillatory component in pulse oximetry at ~0.2Hz and cardiac band (~1Hz), MAP at vasomotor ~0.1Hz and respiration belt at ~0.2Hz, suggesting that sleep-stage-specific hemodynamic oscillations were not solely driven by systemic physiology. 

Conclusion

Whole-night personalized EEG/fNIRS appears as a new promising modality to study sleep physiology, suggesting that sleep stage-specific hemodynamic signatures could be used to further characterize NREM and REM sleep in healthy and pathological conditions. Our results identify distinct patterns of vigilance states in fNIRS-measured hemodynamics and extend previous EEG-fMRI/ EEG-fNIRS findings by investigating all sleep stages including N3 and REM sleep. Finally, our personalized fNIRS optimal montage targeting bilateral frontoparietal regions with a dense montage, improves spatial specificity and supports accurate reconstructions of the hemodynamic signal on the cortical surface that will be considered in subsequent analyses. Whole night EEG/fNIRS offers a unique possibility to investigate hemodynamic fluctuations and cortical metabolism (oxygenation) across different vigilance states.
