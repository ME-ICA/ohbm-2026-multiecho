Introduction

Accurate segmentation of acute ischemic stroke lesions on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) is critical for timely diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and treatment planning. However, manual delineation remains labor-intensive and is subject to substantial inter-observer variability, motivating the development of automated segmentation approaches. Recently, deep learning–based methods have demonstrated substantial improvements in stroke lesion segmentation. 

The recent studies have sought to further enhance segmentation performance by incorporating multi-modal diffusion inputs, most commonly combining DWI with apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps1. Inter-scanner and inter-site variability in ADC has been reported under nominally similar protocols, and formal standardization efforts emphasize that protocol harmonization may reduce expected bias2. In acute stroke cohorts, diffusion protocol heterogeneity has been shown to bias ADC distributions, alter ischemic core estimates, and distort associations with clinical measures, raising concerns regarding the robustness of ADC-dependent models in multi-center settings3. 

In contrast, high-b-value DWI is universally acquired as part of standard acute stroke MRI protocols and exhibits greater consistency across clinical workflows4. From a deployment perspective, segmentation models that rely solely on DWI may therefore offer improved robustness to inter-site heterogeneity and reduced vulnerability to missing or corrupted auxiliary diffusion maps. In this study, we propose a DWI-only infarct segmentation framework that leverages a majority-voting ensemble of three complementary UNet based architectures, DC-UNet, LambdaUNet, and nnUNet. By aggregating their predictions, we aim to improve segmentation robustness and stability across lesion sizes while minimizing dependence on potentially unstable quantitative diffusion metrics such as ADC.

Methods

Data Acquisition

The study utilized clinical DWI acquired from 380 acute ischemic stroke patients, who consented and were enrolled under an IRB-approved protocol (IRB No: B-2404-893-103) using a Philips 3T MRI scanner (TR = 3000 ms and TE = 70-73 ms, b-value = 1000 s/mm²). The ground truth (GT) masks for the stroke lesions were manually delineated by clinical experts and served as the reference GT.

Data Preprocessing

Data preprocessing was performed prior to developing deep learning models. First, the original DWI volumes were resized to achieve consistent matrix sizes across the entire dataset. To improve model robustness and reduce overfitting, data augmentation was applied by performing random horizontal and vertical flipping on the training samples.

Model Training

DC-UNet is an enhanced U-Net variant that incorporates two key modifications: a Dual-channel block (DC-block) in the encoder and a Residual path in the skip connections. The DC-block extracts feature through two parallel convolutional streams and then output of streams are concatenated and fused. The Residual path, composed of repeated convolutional unit, refines encoder features before they are merged with the decoder. For training, a slice-based input strategy was employed, and the model was optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1×10⁻⁴ and a combined Focal and Dice loss as the loss function. The batch size was set to 4 for all experiments. To ensure robust performance and generalization, 5-Fold Cross Validation was executed, result in five distinct models. The final prediction was derived through a soft voting ensemble of those five models. 

The LambdaUNet model extends the UNet architecture by integrating lambda layers into encoder to capture global, local, and inter-slice contexts, which are particularly beneficial for 2.5D medical image segmentation. Following the original design, a 2.5D training strategy was adopted, in which a fixed number of consecutive slices were grouped and processed using a slice-wise 2D network. This approach allows the model to retain inter-slice contextual information through the lambda layers while maintaining computational efficiency. The model was trained with a batch size of 4 using Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss and RMSprop with a learning rate of 1×10⁻⁴. A 5-fold cross validation was also executed, and the best model was selected on validation dice score. Final predictions were obtained via soft voting across five folds.

The nnU-Net framework was employed as a strong baseline due to its self-configuring nature, which automatically adapts architecture, preprocessing, and training configurations5. In this study, the 3D full-resolution configuration automatically determined by the nnUNet framework. Training utilized a combined Dice and cross-entropy loss with SGD optimizer. Five models were generated through 5-fold cross-validation, and the best model from each fold was selected based on validation dice score. Model Evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using the Dice score and the lesion-wise F1 score. The Dice score quantified the volumetric overlap between the predicted lesion mask and the GT. Furthermore, we measured the absolute volume difference and the absolute lesion count difference between the predicted lesion mask and the GT. In addition, performance across different lesion volume sizes was evaluated.

Results

Among the individual models, DC-UNet achieved the highest Dice score (0.779 ± 0.173), whereas nnUNet showed the lowest absolute lesion count difference (2.145 ± 4.624). The LambdaUNet model recorded the lowest dice score (0.765 ± 0.163) and lesion-wise F1 score (0.757 ± 0.256) among the individual architectures. The majority voting ensemble outperformed all individual models in terms of dice score (0.786 ± 0.169) and lesion-wise F1 score (0.786 ± 0.249), demonstrating the benefit of aggregating predictions. Representative qualitative examples of the segmentation results obtained using the majority voting ensemble are shown in Figure 2, illustrating its ability to capture ischemic stroke lesions across varying sizes. 

For small lesions (volume < 1 ml), the majority voting achieved a dice score of 0.756 ± 0.199 and the lesion-wise F1 score of 0.870 ± 0.200, indicating high sensitivity to very small lesions. For lesions of intermediate size (1-15 ml) showed the highest dice score (0.802 ± 0.156). In contrast large lesions (> 15 ml) showed slightly lower dice score (0.790 ± 0.113) and the lowest lesion-wise F1 score (0.617 ± 0.243).

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated that a majority-voting ensemble of three heterogeneous deep learning architectures achieves robust and competitive infarct segmentation performance using DWI alone. The ensemble consistently outperformed individual models in both Dice score and lesion-wise F1 score, indicating that aggregating complementary decision boundaries improves not only volumetric overlap but also lesion detection accuracy. These findings are consistent with prior work showing that ensemble strategies can improve robustness and generalization in medical image segmentation by mitigating model-specific biases. Notably, the proposed DWI-only ensemble achieved strong performance across a broad range of lesion volumes. In particular, the high lesion-wise F1 score observed for very small infarcts (<1 ml) suggests sensitivity to subtle diffusion abnormalities that are often clinically relevant yet difficult to detect. Intermediate-sized lesions exhibited the highest Dice scores, while performance for large lesions remained stable, albeit with reduced lesion-wise F1 scores, likely reflecting lesion merging effects that influence lesion-count–based metrics. These findings underscore the importance of complementing global overlap metrics with lesion-wise evaluations in stroke segmentation studies.

Compared with recent approaches that rely on multi-modal diffusion inputs, our results demonstrate that competitive segmentation performance can be achieved using DWI alone. This observation is particularly relevant given extensive evidence that ADC maps are susceptible to scanner-, protocol-, and vendor-dependent variability, as well as to artifacts such as motion and T2-related effects. By avoiding explicit dependence on ADC, the proposed framework may offer improved robustness and practical deployability in heterogeneous clinical environments. Several limitations should be acknowledged. All data were acquired on a single-vendor scanner with a consistent protocol, and external validation across multi-vendor or multi-center datasets was not performed. While the DWI-only design is intended to enhance generalizability, empirical validation in heterogeneous datasets remains necessary, particularly given known inter-site variability in diffusion imaging. Future work will focus on external validation, efficiency optimization, and uncertainty-aware ensemble modeling to further enhance clinical applicability.
