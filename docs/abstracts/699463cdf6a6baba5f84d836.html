Introduction

Effective verbal communication involves not only linguistic information, or what we say, but also paralinguistic information, or how we say it. Prosody, the modulation of paralinguistic speech features (e.g., rhythm, pitch) imbues additional meaning to speech beyond that conveyed by linguistic information. One important function of prosody is to convey emotional information (affective prosody). Impairments in affective prosody (e.g. speech that lacks emotional intonation) can lead to challenges in social interactions and are associated with several psychiatric (e.g., autism) and neurological (e.g., stroke) disorders. Prior work on the neural basis of affective prosody production has highlighted several right-lateralized brain regions including the frontal cortex, basal ganglia (BG), temporal voice areas (TVAs), insula, and parts of the cerebellum. While the precise role of underlying white matter connections between these regions remains unclear, it has been suggested that prosody production deficits may be associated with lesions to more dorsal (e.g., arcuate fasciculus - AF) than ventral stream regions and tracts (e.g., uncinate fasciculus - UF), respectively.1

The precise nature of functional and structural brain network-behavior relationships remains unclear. Individuals who develop impairments in affective prosody, referred to as aprosodia, after focal brain lesions can provide unique insights into the critical underlying neuroanatomy. Previous work applying the lesion method has typically only used lesion location in combination with functional or structural network data and has generally focused on recognition over production processes. Here, we performed exploratory whole-brain analyses to investigate how expressive aprosodia relates to lesion location and affected functional and structural connections in a large sample of participants with focal brain lesions.

Methods

Data were obtained from the Iowa Neurological Patient Registry. Participants were included if they acquired a focal brain lesion after 18 years of age, had research-quality structural neuroimaging available, and had a clinical prosody rating collected during the chronic phase of recovery (>3 months after lesion onset). Participants were excluded if they had pre-existing intellectual disability, neurological or psychiatric disorders. Affective prosody was rated by a trained neuropsychologist during a participant’s neuropsychological assessment on a three-point scale as described in previous work.2 A score of one indicated preserved prosody, a score of two indicated borderline aprosodia (i.e., mild to moderate impairments), and a score of three indicated aprosodia (i.e., severe impairments). Aprosodic participants were characterized as having flat, monotone, or blunted speech that lacks affective prosodic variation. Participant groups (prosodic, n = 576; borderline n = 47; aprosodic n = 50) did not significantly differ for age at lesion onset, age at scan, or education (Kruskal-Wallis H test: all p > .05), sex or lesion laterality (Chi-square x2: all p > .05) but differed in lesion volume (H = 27.62, p < .001) with the aprosodic group exhibiting the largest lesion volume (x̄ = 99,285.6, σ = 107,041.6), followed by the borderline group (x̄ = 56,891.9, σ = 65,000.2), and then prosodic group (x̄ = 41,694.8, σ = 63,321.7).

Each participant’s lesion was manually traced from structural MRI or CT images by trained study personnel blind to the hypotheses of the current study. Anatomical accuracy was reviewed by a neurologist (A.D.B) in both native and standard space (MNI152). Lesions negatively impact transformation into the MNI space, so we used enantiomorphic normalization which extracts the voxel intensities from the nondamaged homolog of the lesion volume and inserts it in place of the manually traced lesion mask. For bilateral lesions, masks were converted to a cost-function mask and used to assist in spatial normalization.

We used functional and structural connectivity data from healthy individuals to estimate the effect of focal brain lesions on distributed networks. Each participant’s three-dimensional lesion mask was used as a seed region to derive a network map reflecting the functional or structural connectivity patterns of lesioned tissue in healthy individuals. For the functional lesion network maps, we used normative resting-state fMRI data from the Genome Superstruct Project (N = 1000).3 For each lesion seed, a t-statistic map was created reflecting the estimated average (across normative subjects) functional connectivity of the lesion to the rest of the brain. We then used spatial correlation to indicate the strength of similarity between the functional lesion network statistical map and a canonical functional map (Yeo 7-network parcellation). For the structural lesion network maps, we used normative diffusion MRI data from the Human Connectome Project’s 32-fold group connectome.4 Deterministic tractography analysis was performed using the Lead-DBS software tool with each participant’s lesion mask as a seed region, resulting in an average (across normative subjects) streamline map reflecting the structural connectivity of the lesion to the rest of the brain. 

For statistical analyses (see Figure 1), we used the Iowa Brain-Behavior Modeling Toolkit to analyze associations between clinical ratings of affective prosody and lesion location (LSM), functional lesion network maps (fLNM), and structural lesion network maps (sLNM).5 We conducted a mass-univariate analysis using partial Spearman rank correlation with lesion volume as a covariate. Statistical significance was assessed using permutation testing (10,000 permutations) with continuous family-wise error rate (cFWER) control (corrected p < .05), applying a voxel count threshold v = 1000.

Results

We first investigated the relationship between lesion location and prosody rating. Lesion-symptom mapping (see Figure 2A) revealed significant associations between lesion location and aprosodia in the right hemisphere with the most prominent associations between the right insular and frontal opercular cortex, encompassing the pars triangularis and pars opercularis of the inferior frontal gyrus, as well as underlying white matter. Associations were also observed in portions of the right frontal cortex, somatosensory and motor cortices, and basal ganglia. Notably, associations were also observed in the left putamen. 

Second, we investigated the relationship between structural lesion network maps and prosody rating. The thresholded structural lesion network maps (see Figure 2B) implicated disconnections of multiple white matter pathways including corticothalamic (CT), frontopontine (FPT), arcuate fasciculus (AF), superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), frontal aslant tract (FAT), and interhemispheric frontal connections through the corpus callosum (CC). Notably, the sLNM also implicated left hemispheric projection pathways, consistent with the observed lesion location results in the left putamen and adjacent white matter. 

Third, we investigated the relationship between functional lesion network maps and prosody rating. Results from the thresholded functional lesion network maps (see Figure 2C) were compared with seven atlas-derived large-scale resting-state networks using spatial correlation. The highest correlation was with the ventral attention/salience network (r = .674), followed by the somatomotor network (r = .400), the dorsal attention network (r = .256), and the frontoparietal network (r = .168). Associations were also observed in regions including bilateral cerebellum, bilateral basal ganglia, bilateral insula, right mid and anterior cingulate cortices, and medial prefrontal cortex.

Conclusion

We used lesion-symptom and lesion network mapping analyses to investigate the neuroanatomical correlates of affective prosody production impairment in participants with focal brain lesions. Aprosodia was most prominently associated with lesions to the right fronto-insular area, underlying white matter pathways, and ventral attention/salience networks.

The results replicated previous work identifying the right frontal cortex, particularly inferior frontal regions, as an important neural substrate for affective prosody production. Additionally, the presence of the insular cortex and parts of the basal ganglia in the lesion symptoms maps was in line with prior findings suggesting that affective prosody production is accompanied by activations in multisensory integration (e.g., insula) and sensorimotor integration (e.g., dorsal striatum) regions. It is interesting that we also observed associations in the left putamen. Similar findings have been previously reported in participants with left unilateral lesions and aprosodia, with minimal or absent aphasic deficits. 

The sLNM results suggested aprosodia was most prominently associated with disconnections to right-lateralized tracts (e.g., CT, FPT) including tracts in the proposed dorsal stream (e.g., AF, SLF, and FAT). We found, however, also associations with the CC and left hemisphere tracts. These additional white matter tracts, previously not associated with affective prosody, may help further characterize the specific role of structural network connectivity in the neuroanatomical basis for affective prosody production.

The fLNM results suggested that aprosodia was most correlated with lesions disrupting connectivity to the ventral attention/salience network bilaterally, including bilateral insula and basal ganglia. We also observed spatial correlation between the fLNM and the ventral attention network which includes regions in the proposed dorsal stream for affective prosody production spanning the temporoparietal junction to anterior insula. Given our findings further implicate other functional networks (dorsal attention, somatomotor, and frontoparietal), lesions to multiple brain networks may be associated with different functional aspects of aprosodia.

Limitations of the study included the use of a subjective rating. Although the study used clinical ratings from a trained neuropsychologist and we believe these ratings are sufficient to capture neuroanatomical correlates that may be relevant in general affective prosody production. Future work could benefit from using objective measures including discrimination, sentence reading, or picture description tasks. Additionally, while the overall sample size was relatively large (> 600), the sample only included 50 participants with severe aprosodia. Nevertheless, the results still robustly identified regions and networks associated with affective prosody production consistent with prior literature

Taken together, the combination of LSM, sLNM, and fLNM to investigate affective prosody production with a lesion-deficit approach provided unique insight into the complex functional and structural associated networks. Additionally, future work investigating both expressive and receptive aprosodia in a large sample of participants would help further characterize the shared and/or distinct regions needed for both functions and the role of left-lateralized subcortical structures and white matter tracts. A better understanding of the neural basis of affective prosody may have the potential to support the development of improved personalized outcome predictions and intervention strategies (e.g., targeted speech therapy).
