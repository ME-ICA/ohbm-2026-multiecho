Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by impairments in social communication and interaction, alongside restricted and repetitive behaviors, interests, or activities that vary along a continuum of severity1,2. Substantial heterogeneity in both etiology and phenotypic expression presents a major challenge for clinical and translational research in ASD. Most efforts to stratify individuals with ASD have relied on behavioral criteria; however, such clinical subtyping does not capture underlying neurobiological mechanisms and offers limited predictive value for developmental outcomes. Moreover, it remains unclear whether, and to what extent, atypical brain functional connectivity accounts for individual differences in ASD-related symptoms and behaviors. The aim of this study was to identify clinically meaningful subgroups of young children with ASD based on distinct patterns of brain functional connectivity, thereby advancing our understanding of the neural substrates underlying ASD heterogeneity.

Methods

We integrated resting-state EEG data from four independent datasets comprising 541 children with ASD aged 2–12 years to estimate and stratify measures of brain functional connectivity. EEG preprocessing and artifact removal were performed using a fully automated, multi-stage pipeline. Functional brain networks were computed across multiple frequency bands (delta: 1–4 Hz; theta: 4–8 Hz; alpha: 8–13 Hz; beta: 13–30 Hz; gamma: 30–45 Hz) using an HD-EEG source connectivity approach as described by Hassan et al.3. We then conducted an unsupervised clustering analysis of cortical network properties using a data-driven similarity network fusion (SNF) approach4. SNF integrates features from multiple data sources into a single fused similarity network, capturing both shared and complementary information and enabling the assessment of inter-individual relationships. In this study, SNF was applied to combine similarity networks derived from functional connectivity features across frequency bands. Finally, we compared the clinical profiles of the resulting clusters to identify symptom-linked dimensions of brain connectivity. 

Results

Clustering of functional connectivity features across multiple frequency bands led to the identification of four distinct ASD subgroups. Cluster stability analysis demonstrated high robustness, with an average robustness coefficient of 81% across 100 iterations. The four groups differed in demographic composition while remaining broadly comparable in age distribution. Group 1 (G1) included 190 participants (82 males; mean age = 9.74 years), Group 2 (G2) comprised 115 participants (55 males; mean age = 8.94 years), Group 3 (G3) included 89 participants (62 males; mean age = 8.81 years), and Group 4 (G4) comprised 147 participants (90 males; mean age = 8.36 years). Across all subgroups, the majority of participants were younger than 10 years. Dataset contributions varied across groups, with G1 and G2 drawing predominantly from the HBN and ABCCT datasets, whereas G3 and G4 were primarily composed of ABCCT participants.

Analysis of brain networks that most strongly differentiated the identified subgroups revealed that the sensorimotor network (SMN) showed the largest effects in both the beta and gamma frequency bands. The default mode network (DMN) also demonstrated strong discriminative power in these bands, followed by the dorsal attention network (DAN) and the visual network (VIS), all of which exhibited significant group differences in beta and gamma frequencies.

This framework enabled the characterization of distinct neurophysiological profiles for each subgroup. Specifically, G1 was marked by reduced SMN power in low frequencies and elevated SMN power in the beta band, whereas G2 showed increased alpha-band power within the DMN. Elevated alpha-band power in the fronto-temporal network (FTN) was common to both G1 and G2. In contrast, G3 was characterized by increased SMN connectivity alongside reduced connectivity within the DMN and FTN in the alpha band, as well as reduced SMN connectivity in the beta band.

To further quantify subgroup differences, we derived subject-level connectivity metrics by integrating network-specific connectivity measures across frequency bands. This analysis revealed significantly higher averaged functional connectivity in G4 compared to G1, G2, and G3 across all networks and frequency bands. Conversely, G1 exhibited significantly lower connectivity values relative to the other subgroups. Differences between G1 and G2 were limited, reaching significance only for SMN beta-band power, with higher values observed in G1.

Conclusion

Our findings shed light on atypical brain network topology conferring risk for specific phenotypic manifestations of ASD, which may implicate unique underlying neurobiological mechanisms. Cross-validation stability hints at a solid stratification model to challenge ASD heterogeneity. Collectively, the stratification of well-defined neural signatures that give rise to the clinical heterogeneity of ASD has the potential to provide more accurate prognosis and help to select the optimal strategy for therapeutic intervention.
