Introduction

Portable low-field MRI scanners are emerging as a transformative technology for global neuroimaging, offering increased accessibility, reduced cost, and bedside imaging capabilities that enable new clinical and research applications [1]. These systems make frequent brain imaging feasible in settings such as intensive care, outpatient neurology, and community-based health environments. However, their lower signal-to-noise ratio, reduced spatial resolution, higher cadence of software updates, and prominent geometric distortions raise fundamental concerns about whether existing image-registration algorithms can deliver reliable voxel-level alignment.

Accurate registration is essential for many quantitative neuroimaging workflows, such as longitudinal tracking of neurodegeneration, template normalization, segmentation propagation, and multimodal fusion. While classical, learning-based, and synthesis-based registration methods achieve near-ceiling performance on high-field MRI, their behavior on portable low-field data remains poorly characterized.

Here, we systematically evaluate three state-of-the-art registration frameworks, represented by three prototypical methods: classical optimization (NiftyReg [2]), learning-based registration (SynthMorph [3]), and synthesis-based registration (SynthSR [4] combined with NiftyReg). These are evaluated with simulated low-resolution images, real low-field cross-sectional scans, and real low-field longitudinal acquisitions. Our experiments disentangle the effects of resolution versus scanner-induced artifacts and identify practical strategies for robust alignment in portable low-field MRI. 

Methods

We designed a three-part experimental framework to evaluate registration performance across simulated, cross-sectional, and longitudinal low-field settings (Figure 1a). Our goal was to disentangle the effects of reduced spatial resolution from those arising from scanner-specific distortions, and to compare three representative state-of-the-art paradigms: classical optimization, learning-based registration, and synthesis-based registration (Figure 1b).

1. Simulated low-resolution experiment (ADNI3)

We began by assessing whether the limited spatial resolution available on portable MRI scanners is itself a fundamental barrier to accurate registration. High-field 1 mm isotropic T1 and FLAIR scans from ADNI3 were artificially downsampled to two resolutions achievable on a commercial 64 mT system (Swoop system from Hyperfine, Inc): 3 mm isotropic, which supports rapid acquisition, and 1.6×1.6×5 mm axial, the default “stock” sequence (Figure 1c, left). For each resolution, we evaluated both T1-T1 (intra-modality) and T1-FLAIR (inter-modality) pairs. Registration was performed using: (i) NiftyReg, representing classical optimization with established similarity metrics (local normalized cross-correlation or LNCC for intra-modality, and mutual information or MI for inter-modality); (ii) SynthMorph, a contrast- and resolution-agnostic learning-based method trained through broad domain randomization; and (iii) SynthSR + NiftyReg, which first synthesizes a 1 mm isotropic T1 image from any MRI contrast or resolution and then registers this harmonized volume using NiftyReg using LNCC. Registrations performed directly on the original high-field scans served as a reference for the upper bound of achievable accuracy in all comparisons. These were registered with NiftyReg and LNCC (directly, in the intra-modality scenario, or after SynthSR to harmonize contrast, in the inter-modality scenario).

2. Real low-field cross-sectional experiment (40 subjects)

To understand how real low-field artifacts alter registration accuracy, we evaluated paired high-field and portable low-field T1/FLAIR scans acquired at 64 mT in an outpatient neurology clinic (Figure 1c, right). A dedicated phantom experiment preceded these analyses and revealed substantial slice-dependent geometric distortions at low field. Using multi-session phantom data acquired across hardware and software versions, we identified a constrained nonlinear B-spline deformation model (with 20 mm control spacing) capable of correcting these distortions without overfitting. This model was then used to propagate high-field segmentations to each subject’s low-field scan, thereby generating consistent subject-specific label maps for quantitative evaluation. We then applied the same three registration methods to low-field T1-T1 and T1-FLAIR alignments at both resolutions. This experiment isolates the impact of real-world noise, intensity inhomogeneity, distortion, and contrast variability (factors not captured by simple downsampling).

3. Real low-field longitudinal experiment (8 subjects)

To characterize longitudinal performance (critical for tracking neurodegeneration), we analyzed subjects who underwent two low-field 64 mT T1 scans separated by months, each paired with high-field clinical imaging. As in the cross-sectional study, high-field labels were propagated to each low-field time point to yield consistent reference segmentations. For each method, registration accuracy was measured via Dice overlap between propagated reference labels and labels warped from the opposite time point. Additionally, we implemented a practical longitudinal pipeline: low-field scans were super-resolved to 1 mm using SynthSR, aligned with NiftyReg, segmented with SynthSeg [5], and fused across time using established longitudinal label-fusion procedures. This allowed us to compute low-field trajectories for hippocampal, ventricular, and whole-brain volumes.

Results

1. Simulated low-resolution data
When low resolution was simulated by downsampling high-field scans, all three registration frameworks maintained high accuracy across both intra- and inter-modality tasks. Median Dice values (Figure 2a) remained above 0.80 in all settings, and accuracy decreased only slightly relative to the high-field reference registrations. At 3 mm isotropic, SynthMorph and SynthSR+NiftyReg achieved Dice scores nearly indistinguishable from the 1 mm baseline; at the stock anisotropic resolution, Dice dropped modestly but remained stable, particularly for the learning- and synthesis-based methods. In inter-modality T1-FLAIR cases, SynthMorph and SynthSR+NiftyReg continued to perform well, whereas classical NiftyReg exhibited the expected degradation across contrasts. These findings indicate that reduced resolution alone does not substantially impair deformable registration, and modern algorithms tolerate the resolution levels achievable with portable scanners. Figure 2a summarizes Dice performance across simulated conditions.

2. Real low-field cross-sectional data
Accuracy decreased distinctly when registering real low-field acquisitions. Across both modalities and resolutions, Dice scores (Figure 2b) were consistently lower than those from simulated low-resolution experiments, demonstrating that the chief challenges of portable imaging arise from scanner-specific artifacts (geometric distortion, noise, contrast inconsistencies) rather than spatial resolution itself. Performance also differed more sharply between resolutions: 3 mm isotropic yielded substantially higher Dice scores than the stock 1.6×1.6×5 mm protocol (consistent with our prior results [1]). Across methods, SynthSR+NiftyReg delivered the most robust and consistent alignment, typically achieving median Dice values near 0.80 and showing less variability across subjects. SynthMorph showed moderate performance but was more sensitive to the heterogeneous artifact structure of real low-field scans. Direct registration of raw low-field images with NiftyReg performed worst in both T1-T1 and T1-FLAIR settings.

The phantom experiment (illustrated in Figure 2c) confirmed the presence of slice-dependent geometric distortions, including measurable differences (~5%) in apparent diameter between high- and low-field images. Applying a constrained nonlinear transformation effectively corrected these distortions, supporting its use for generating reference labels and for improving alignment in low-field applications.

3. Real low-field longitudinal data
Longitudinal accuracy remained high (>0.90 median Dice, Figure 2d) for downsampled high-field images, indicating that temporal alignment is preserved when artifacts are limited to resolution changes. For real low-field longitudinal scans, Dice scores were lower and more variable. At 3 mm isotropic, the SynthSR+NiftyReg pipeline again achieved the best accuracy, with median Dice values of ~0.88-0.89, adequate for many (but not all) subjects. Several cases showed lower Dice, highlighting sensitivity to session-specific distortion differences. At the stock anisotropic resolution, performance dropped further across all methods. 

A practical test of longitudinal volumetry mirrored these results. Using SynthSR, NiftyReg, and SynthSeg, we obtained biologically plausible trajectories (ventricular enlargement and hippocampal/whole-brain decline), primarily at 3 mm isotropic, whereas stock-resolution scans exhibited noisier and occasionally inconsistent trajectories, especially in subjects with shorter inter-scan intervals (Figure 2e).

Conclusion

This work provides the first systematic evaluation of modern registration approaches for portable, low-field brain MRI across simulated, cross-sectional, and longitudinal scenarios. A central finding is that reduced spatial resolution alone is not the primary limitation: when low-field resolution is mimicked by controlled downsampling of high-field scans, all three evaluated methods (classical optimization, learning-based registration, and synthesis-based alignment) achieve Dice accuracies close to high-field baselines. These results indicate that the coarse resolutions typical of portable systems do not inherently compromise deformable registration.

In contrast, real low-field MRI introduces artifact-driven inconsistencies that present a far greater challenge. Across cross-sectional and longitudinal experiments, Dice scores were substantially lower than in the simulated-resolution setting. This performance gap was strongly associated with low-field specific artifacts (especially geometric distortion, intensity inhomogeneity, and noise) that are not captured by simple downsampling. Consequently, scanner-induced distortions, rather than resolution limits, are the dominant obstacle to reliable registration in portable MRI, and their variability across sessions makes longitudinal alignment particularly difficult.

Among the evaluated approaches, the SynthSR+NiftyReg pipeline consistently delivered the highest Dice accuracy on real low-field data by harmonizing images into a 1 mm isotropic T1 representation before registration. Nonetheless, even this method did not fully overcome low-field distortions: for some subjects, especially those acquired at the anisotropic stock resolution, accuracy remained below the level needed for detecting subtle anatomical changes across months. This highlights that current methods, while promising, require further development for robust quantitative longitudinal analyses at low field.

These results point to three priorities for future advancement. First, improved modeling and correction of low-field geometric distortion is essential, building on the phantom experiments that demonstrated systematic, correctable misalignment. Second, registration algorithms should be adapted or trained explicitly for low-field artifact profiles, which differ substantially from the conditions under which existing learning-based models were developed. And third, broader evaluation on bigger and more diverse datasets will help us understand the accuracy and robustness of registrations methods with real-world low-field scans. 

As portable MRI continues to expand access to neuroimaging in clinical and resource-limited settings, accurate registration will be crucial for enabling reliable quantitative measurements, including those relevant to aging, dementia, and treatment monitoring. By defining the current limitations and identifying clear avenues for improvement, this work provides a foundation for developing next-generation registration strategies tailored to the unique constraints of portable low-field MRI.
