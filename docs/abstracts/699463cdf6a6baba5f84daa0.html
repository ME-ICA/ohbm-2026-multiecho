Introduction

The Think Aloud Paradigm (TAP) is a protocol in which participants verbalize their ongoing stream of thoughts.^1 Recent research has used TAP to examine how thoughts arise and unfold in real time without perceptual input or external tasks,^2 a phenomenon called self-generated thoughts. Within the Dynamic Framework of Thought,^3 self-generated thoughts span unconstrained forms (dreaming, mind-wandering, creative thinking), deliberately constrained forms (goal-directed thinking), and automatically constrained forms (rumination and intrusive, obsessive thinking).

Self-generated thoughts are altered in many clinical conditions.^4 In internalizing disorders such as depression and anxiety, thoughts often become excessively and automatically constrained.^4 Individuals experience intrusive, persistent thoughts that are hard to disengage from, making it difficult to return to the external environment or flexible, goal-directed thinking. This ruminative pattern is typically rigid, negative, perseverative, uncontrollable, cognitively draining, and unhelpful for problem-solving.^5

Despite its clinical relevance, the mechanisms underlying dysfunctional self-generated thoughts remain poorly understood. While previous studies have used TAP to investigate spontaneous thought patterns and rumination,^1 no research has examined how different cues influence the dynamics and content of self-generated thoughts in individuals with depression or anxiety.

In this study, we examined how cues affect the dynamics and content of repetitive negative thoughts in participants with depressive and anxiety symptoms. Using a prompted variation of TAP (pTAP), participants were audio-recorded verbalizing thoughts in response to structured prompts and later rated their emotional valence. This study provides a behavioral foundation for a larger program investigating the neural bases of repetitive negative thinking in depression and anxiety and informs future work combining pTAP with neural recordings.

Methods

We recruited 50 volunteers (34 females; mean age=21.93 ± 4.5) for this experiment. Twenty-five participants met diagnostic criteria for Major Depressive Disorder and/or Generalized Anxiety Disorder, and comprised the Depression-Anxiety group (D/A), and twenty-five participants had no history of psychiatric disorders and comprised the Healthy Control group (HC).

Participants completed four 5-minute p-TAP sessions. Each pTAP session began with a prompt, and participants were instructed to verbalize their thoughts aloud in response to it (Figure 1A). They were told that they did not need to remain focused on the prompt throughout the 5-minute recording period; rather, the prompt was simply intended as a starting point for their stream of thought.

Two of the prompts were designed to elicit repetitive negative thoughts (“How happy or sad do you feel?” and “What do people think of your personality?”), comprising the self-reference condition. The remaining two prompts served as control prompts (“What do you think about the change of seasons?” and “What do you consider makes a joke funny?”), comprising the distraction condition. Prompts order was semi-randomized to ensure that prompts belonging to the same condition did not appear consecutively. 

Following each pTAP session, participants listened to each 5-minute recording after it ended, split into 6-second fragments, and were asked to rate each fragment in emotional valence using a sliding scale (range=1-9, 1-very negative, 9-very positive) (Figure 1B).

Audio from the pTAP sessions was transcribed using Whisper AI, manually corrected, and segmented into discrete thought units, each corresponding to a single topic (Figure 1C). For each thought unit, we computed an overall valence score by weighting the participant’s ratings of the 6-second fragments according to their temporal overlap with that unit. Each thought unit was classified as positive or negative according to its valence (positive defined as valence > 5), which was used to explore the dynamics of thought during pTAP.

Results

Lower overall valence during p-TAP sessions was observed in the D/A group compared to HC (Figure 2A). Across conditions, the D/A group reported lower valence than controls during both distraction (diff = 0.80, p = .006) and self-reference (diff = 2.16, p < .001). A mixed-effects model revealed a significant group × instruction interaction (b = −1.36, SE = 0.15, t = −9.13, p < .001). Post-hoc analyses showed that self-reference decreased valence in the D/A group (diff = −0.75, p < .001) but slightly increased valence in controls (diff = +0.61, p < .001). 

To explore how participants transitioned across affective states during the TAP task we fitted a binomial generalized linear model predicting the probability of remaining in a positive state as a function of group, condition (distraction vs. self-reference), and their interaction. For positive-to-positive transitions, the model revealed a significant main effect of group, with the D/A group showing a lower probability of remaining in a positive state compared to controls (b = −1.01, p < .001). There was no main effect of condition (b = 0.12, SE = 0.24, p = .61). However, the group × condition interaction was significant (b = −0.76, SE = 0.30, z = −2.54, p = .011), indicating that self-reference selectively reduced the probability of remaining in a positive state in the D/A group, while having little to no effect in the control group. In contrast, for negative-to-positive transitions, the model showed a significant main effect of group (b = −1.16, SE = 0.34, z = −3.38, p < .001), indicating that the D/A group was less likely than controls to transition from negative to positive states across conditions. Neither the main effect of condition (b = −0.41, SE = 0.42, p = .34) nor the group × condition interaction (b = −0.07, SE = 0.47, p = .88) was significant (Figure 2B).

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that self-referential prompts exacerbate negative thinking in individuals with depression and anxiety. The D/A group exhibited lower overall valence during pTAP sessions compared to healthy controls, particularly under self-reference instructions. Self-referential prompts decreased the likelihood of remaining in a positive state in the D/A group, whereas controls showed little or even slightly increased positive affect. Additionally, individuals in the D/A group were less likely than controls to transition from negative to positive states, regardless of prompt type. These results suggest that self-focused cognitive cues selectively amplify repetitive negative thinking in depression and anxiety, highlighting the importance of considering thought content and dynamics in understanding dysfunctional self-generated thoughts.  This study provides a behavioral foundation for a broader research program investigating the neural bases of ruminative thoughts in depression and anxiety and will inform future studies combining pTAP with neural recordings.
