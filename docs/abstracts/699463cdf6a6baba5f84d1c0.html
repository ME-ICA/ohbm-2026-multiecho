Introduction

Early life adversity refers to exposure to challenging or harmful circumstances before adulthood that require psychological, behavioural, or neurobiological adaptation by an average individual. Adversity is a major risk factor for schizophrenia1 and has been associated with alterations in brain morphology2. Despite considerable evidence for sex differences in schizophrenia risk, course, and treatment response, it remains unclear whether adversity-related brain abnormalities in schizophrenia are specific to particular subcortical regions and differ by sex3. To address these issues, two main challenges must be overcome. The first concerns sample size, which should be large enough to assess higher-order interactions between diagnosis, adversity, and sex on brain morphology. The second lies in the heterogeneity of adversity measures (questionnaires) and definitions of adversity adopted across studies, which poses a major data-harmonisation challenge. Here, within the Enhancing NeuroImaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis (ENIGMA)-Schizophrenia working group, we developed a cumulative adversity measure harmonised across eight international sites, using a simulation-based imputation pipeline. We then tested whether cumulative adversity interacts with diagnostic differences in subcortical volumes, and whether these effects differ by sex.

We made three conjectures: (i) diagnosis × adversity × sex interactions would be associated with variations in brain morphology, revealing sex-specific neuroanatomical correlates of adversity in individuals with a diagnosis of schizophrenia. This three-way interaction would be clarified by: (ii) a positive association between higher adversity and volumes in stress-sensitive regions, independently of diagnosis and sex; and (iii) a diagnosis × sex effect, independently of adversity, in overlapping brain regions with the three-way interaction.

Methods

Neuroimaging and questionnaire data were collected from eight independent sites, resulting in a total cohort of 2525 adults, that included 778 individuals with schizophrenia (SZ), and 1747 (psychiatrically) healthy controls (HC), with a nearly even sex ratio of 51% male. Whole-brain structural 3D T1-weighted MRI data were processed with FreeSurfer to compute subcortical volumes, and quality-controlled using standardised ENIGMA protocols.

Adversity was assessed using multiple instruments: one site used the Childhood Experience of Care and Abuse Questionnaire (CECA-Q), one the Childhood Adversity Questionnaire (CAQ), and six the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ). To harmonise these instruments, we combined a sub-typing and cumulative adversity framework. All questionnaire items were mapped onto six binary adversity dimensions: emotional neglect (EN), emotional abuse (EA), physical neglect (PN), physical abuse (PA), sexual abuse (SA), and household problems (HH; e.g., caregiver mental illness, substance misuse, or domestic violence). For each participant, we summed these dimensions to obtain a cumulative adversity score (range 0–6).

Because no instruments covered all six dimensions (e.g., CAQ does not measure PN, CTQ does not measure HH), raw cumulative scores were biased (underestimated) by missing domains. To address this, we used a probabilistic approach based on cross-instrument calibration to estimate missing values. Using reference sites with complementary coverage, we fitted logistic regression models to estimate the conditional probability of each missing dimension given the observed dimensions, plus sex and age. For instance, using CAQ data, HH was modelled as a function of EA, PA, SA, sex, age, and the resulting regression coefficients were then applied to CTQ sites to estimate the probability P(HH) for each participant. Then, rather than performing a rigid correction by hard thresholding these probabilities (e.g. if P(HH)>0.5 then HH=1 else HH=0 ), we treated them as Bernoulli’s distribution parameters to simulate 20 datasets per site4. For each simulated dataset, missing and observed adversity dimensions were stochastically drawn from the corresponding conditional probabilities (observed dimensions being treated as either 0% or 100% probability), and new adversity cumulative scores were computed.

 Within each site and for each simulated dataset, we ran ROI-wise linear models including the 3-way interaction diagnosis × adversity × sex and all lower-order terms for diagnosis, sex and adversity, plus a correction for age. Subcortical volumes were adjusted for individual differences in intracranial volumes. Using R notation, the model is: ROI_volume ~ Diagnosis * Adversity * Sex + Age. Rubin’s rules were used to pool across simulations site-level β coefficients estimates, along with associated standard errors that reflect imputation uncertainty for each ROI and each term in the model. With this approach, a trade is made between the reduction of the large bias caused by missing adversity dimensions and with an increase in variance (standard error) due to the probabilistic modelling uncertainty.

Summary statistics were collected at each site using R scripts that implemented the adversity harmonisation and regression models, and returned site-level summary statistics and regression estimates for meta-analysis. These site-level pooled estimates and standard error were then entered into random-effects meta-analyses implemented in R package metafor8, yielding inverse-variance–weighted pooled effects for each ROI. Multiple comparisons were controlled using Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate (FDR) correction. We focused on significant Diagnosis × Adversity × Sex interactions and examined lower-order terms to contextualise the patterns. N.B.: the zero-level/reference group of Diagnosis × Adversity × Sex term in the model is Diagnosis=HC × Adversity=0 × Sex=F. Groups distribution are presented in Figure 1.

Results

Meta-analyses revealed significant three-way interactions effect on subcortical volumes, indicating sex-specific neuroanatomical correlates of adversity in schizophrenia. Significant associations between the Diagnosis × Adversity × Sex interaction term and left pallidum (β=+0.16, 95% CI: [0.09, 0.23], p=1.8×10⁻⁵, pFDR=2.8×10⁻⁴) and right pallidum (β=+0.16, 95% CI [0.05, 0.26], p=3.1×10⁻³, pFDR=0.025) were found. Under standard coding with female HCs as the reference group, these positive 3-way interaction terms indicate that each additional unit of cumulative adversity strengthens the difference in pallidal volume between individuals with schizophrenia and controls, and that this is more strongly evident in males than in females. In other words, the adversity–volume slope in the pallidum becomes progressively more positive/less negative in males with schizophrenia than in control females, such that the largest deviations are seen when moving from low-adversity female HCs to high-adversity male SZ.

Lower-order effects help to clarify this pattern. Strong main effects of diagnosis were observed across subcortical regions: A diagnosis of schizophrenia was significantly associated bilaterally with larger ventricles, caudate, and pallidum volumes (all β>0.16, all pFDR<0.018), and smaller hippocampal volumes than HC (β_right=–0.44, β_left=–0.49, both pFDR<0.004). Main effects of sex reflected significantly smaller thalamic and caudate volumes in males (all β<–0.35, all pFDR<0.026). The Diagnosis × Sex interaction term was significantly negatively associated with variation in the right pallidum volume (β=–0.56, pFDR<0.05), reflecting schizophrenia related pallidal enlargement which was attenuated in males at low adversity levels. The significant and positive Diagnosis × Adversity × Sex terms indicated that with increasing adversity, this sex-specific attenuation is reversed: the schizophrenia–control contrast in pallidal volume increases more steeply in males than females for each step on the adversity scale. Consequently, while schizophrenia is associated with larger pallidal volume overall, the combination of being male and having higher cumulative adversity drives the most pronounced pallidal enlargement relative to low-adversity female HCs. Beyond these interactions, a robust main effect of cumulative adversity was significantly associated with variations in the right amygdala (β=+0.10, 95% CI [0.06, 0.14], p=1.3×10⁻⁶, pFDR=2.1×10⁻⁵), indicating larger amygdala volume in individuals exposed to higher adversity across groups. Conclusion

Together, these patterns suggest that male individuals with schizophrenia show an altered relationship between early adversity and pallidal volume, over and above the main effects of diagnosis, adversity, and sex, and that the impact of adversity on subcortical structure is best captured by higher-order interactions between diagnosis, sex, and adversity, rather than simple additive effects. 

Using an original imputation pipeline and harmonisation for adversity across eight international sites, we show that cumulative early adversity is linked to sex-specific alterations in subcortical structure in schizophrenia. Schizophrenia was associated with the expected pattern of larger ventricles and basal ganglia volumes and smaller hippocampal volumes, consistent with prior ENIGMA findings5 and higher adversity was associated with larger right amygdala volume across groups. Critically, cumulative adversity amplified diagnostic differences in pallidal volume specifically in males, such that high-adversity male patients with schizophrenia showed the largest pallidal enlargement relative to low-adversity female controls.

These findings highlight the importance of accounting and modelling adversity, diagnosis, and sex jointly when studying brain structure in schizophrenia. They suggest that early adversity contributes to subcortical alterations in a sex-dependent manner, with the pallidum emerging as a key cortical area where cumulative adversity and schizophrenia interact in males. More broadly, our harmonisation framework illustrates how heterogeneous adversity measures can be integrated in large-scale neuroimaging consortia, even without direct access to the data, to test mechanistic hypotheses in severe mental illness.
