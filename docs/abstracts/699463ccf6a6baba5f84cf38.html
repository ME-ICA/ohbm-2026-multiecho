Introduction

Evidence has been accumulating regarding brain-mind interactions, whereby internal bodily status is affected by emotional phenomena. These interactions may be particularly disturbed in patients with mood disorders, who often exhibit over- or under-estimation of their physical discomfort, in extreme forms manifesting somatic delusional states such as Cotard syndrome. However, novel biomarkers representing these disturbances remain unclear.

Interoception, the sensing and perceiving of internal bodily states, plays a central role in understanding these brain-mind interactions. Cardiac interoception, particularly heartbeat sensitivity, is closely associated with interoceptive awareness and emotional intensity. Previous studies have shown that depression is associated with reduced heart rate variability (Kemp et al., 2010), while anxiety disorders are characterized by heightened interoceptive sensitivity (Paulus & Stein, 2010), suggesting divergent alterations in interoceptive processing across mood disorder subtypes.

Heartbeat evoked magnetic fields (HEFs), consisting of post-heartbeat neural responses emerging around 200-300 ms after individual ECG R-peaks, have been proposed as neural indices of interoceptive processing. These signals arise from fronto-central regions including the insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and somatosensory cortex. Our previous study demonstrated that emotional arousal evoked by disgusting sounds alters HEFs through right insular activity, with enhanced connectivity between the right anterior ventral insular cortex (av-Ins) and left ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC) in the beta frequency range, particularly at 19 Hz and 23 Hz (Kato et al., 2020). These findings established the methodological framework for investigating whether such connectivity patterns are disrupted in patients with mood disorders. The present study extends this work by comparing HEFs between healthy volunteers and patients with mood disorders.

Methods

Thirty healthy volunteers (14 males, 16 females; mean age: 40.2±7.18 years) and twenty-two patients with mood disorders in depressive states (10 males, 12 females; mean age: 45.6±10.21 years) participated after providing written informed consent. Patients were diagnosed according to DSM-5 criteria: 18 with major depressive disorder, 4 with bipolar disorder (depressive episode). All participants were right-handed with normal vision and hearing. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Gunma University Graduate School of Medicine.

Following our previous protocol (Kato et al., 2020), MEG was recorded in a magnetically shielded room using a 306-channel whole-scalp neuromagnetometer (Vectorview, Elekta Neuromag, Helsinki, Finland) at 1,000 Hz sampling rate. Head position was monitored using head-position indicator coils. Individual head shapes were digitized for co-registration with anatomical MRI.

Participants passively listened to disgusting and control sounds from the International Affective Digital Sounds (IADS-2; Bradley & Lang, 2007) through MEG-compatible earphones, with eyes open focused on a central fixation cross. The experiment consisted of 3-minute blocks alternating between control and disgust presentations (12 minutes total). Each block contained 15 types of sound sets (6 seconds each, presented twice consecutively: 12 seconds total). Disgusting stimuli included human vomiting, child abuse with body hitting sounds, human fighting and screaming, coughing, and belching sounds. Control stimuli were emotionally neutral and acoustically matched: baby laughing, children playing, animal sounds, and cheers in baseball stadium.

Data preprocessing included notch filtering at 50 Hz, and bandpass filtering at 0.1-100 Hz. Independent component analysis (ICA) removed eye blinks, eye movements, and cardiac artifacts. ECG R-peaks were detected using automated algorithms with visual verification.

Heartbeat evoked fields (HEFs) were calculated by averaging MEG signals time-locked to ECG R-peaks. Epochs of 500 ms (-100 to +400 ms relative to R-peak) were extracted, with -100 to 0 ms serving as baseline. Each participant contributed 410-570 epochs per condition. Epochs exceeding amplitude thresholds (400 fT for magnetometers, 4,000 fT/cm for gradiometers) or containing artifacts were excluded.

Source currents were estimated using minimum norm estimates (MNE) implemented in MNE-Python with anatomical constraints from individual T1-weighted MRI scans. Cortical surfaces were reconstructed using FreeSurfer with the Desikan-Killiany atlas. A three-layer boundary element model was constructed. Inverse solutions employed depth-weighted minimum norm estimation (depth weighting: 0.8). Based on our previous findings (Kato et al., 2020), bilateral insular cortices were parcellated into six sub-regions: anterior ventral (av-Ins), anterior dorsal (ad-Ins), and posterior (p-Ins) for each hemisphere.

Inter-trial coherences (ITC) were calculated in 8-24 Hz using Morlet wavelets (5 cycles) for -100 to 400 ms to assess phase consistency across trials. Phase lag indexes (PLI) were calculated in 14-24 Hz beta band between 100-400 ms post-R-peak to assess functional connectivity, with right av-Ins as seed region.

Statistical comparisons between conditions used paired t-tests for ITC values and Mann-Whitney U tests for PLI values within each group. Between-group comparisons used independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Source current estimation revealed distinct cortical activity around 250 ms post-R-peak, primarily in right insular cortices and superior temporal sulcus during Disgust condition in both groups. Since the most prominent responses were found in the insular cortices, six insular sub-regions were defined as anterior ventral, anterior dorsal, and posterior regions for each hemisphere for subsequent analyses.

Source-evoked time courses were calculated from these six insular sub-regions in both groups. Time courses peaked around 250 ms post-R-peak in both conditions and groups. The right anterior ventral insular sub-region in both groups showed a trend toward increased activity in Disgust condition, though this did not reach statistical significance. The right anterior ventral region showed mean amplitude increases of 15-20% in healthy volunteers during Disgust compared to Control. In addition, the right posterior insular sub-region was increased in patients during Disgust. Patients showed variable patterns with no consistent modulation across conditions.

Inter-trial coherence (ITC) analysis was performed in the 8-24 Hz frequency range for -100 to 400 ms time window using Morlet wavelets (5 cycles). ITC values were normalized to Z-scores using the mean and standard deviation of the right anterior ventral insular cortex during Control conditions as baseline. Statistical comparisons between Disgust and Control conditions using independent t-tests revealed significant differences in specific time-frequency distributions.

Healthy volunteers elicited increased ITC in the right anterior ventral insular cortices during Disgust at frequencies between 17 and 20 Hz at latencies between 150 and 300 ms, whereas the other five insular sub-regions demonstrated similar activities in both conditions. On the other hand, patients with mood disorders exhibited unstable ITC augmentations in the right anterior ventral insular cortices in both conditions, suggesting baseline dysregulation rather than stimulus-specific modulation. In addition, patients showed enhanced activities in left anterior dorsal, right anterior dorsal, and right posterior insular cortices during Disgust conditions, indicating less focal and potentially compensatory activation patterns.

Phase lag index (PLI) analysis was calculated between right anterior ventral insular cortex and all other cortical regions in the beta frequency band (14-24 Hz) during 100-400 ms post-R-peak window. In healthy volunteers, significant increases of PLI between right anterior ventral insular cortices and left ventromedial prefrontal cortices were found during Disgust, replicating previous results (Kato et al., 2020). Additional connectivity increases occurred between right anterior ventral insula and left pars opercularis, right posterior cingulate cortex, and left superior temporal sulcus.

On the other hand, no significant correlation represented by PLI was found in patients with mood disorders between right anterior ventral cortices and any other cortical regions during Disgust, including VMPFC, pars opercularis, posterior cingulate, and superior temporal sulcus. This absence of connectivity modulation suggests fundamental impairment in interoceptive-emotional integration. 

Conclusion

This study revealed fundamental disturbances in cortical connectivity during interoceptive processing in mood disorders, extending our previous findings (Kato et al., 2020). While disgusting sounds alter heartbeat evoked magnetic fields through right insular activity in both groups, connectivity between right anterior ventral insular cortex and left ventromedial prefrontal cortex observed in healthy volunteers is significantly diminished in patients, representing core dysfunction in neural circuitry underlying emotional awareness and regulation.

The insular cortex serves as the primary cortical target for interoceptive information and generates subjective emotional feelings. According to the Somatic Marker Hypothesis, emotional experiences arise from the brain's perception of physiological changes. The VMPFC triggers somatic patterns and serves as an endpoint where bodily changes are referred back via insular cortices, forming the "as-if-body loop."

Healthy volunteers showed enhanced right av-Ins to left VMPFC connectivity during disgust in the beta frequency range (17-20 Hz). Beta oscillations play important roles in somatic information processing. ITC increases at 17-20 Hz suggest frequency-specific modulation, where higher beta frequencies facilitate interoceptive-emotional integration. This pattern was absent in patients.

The absence of connectivity modulation suggests patients' interoceptive systems fail to engage prefrontal evaluative mechanisms during emotional challenges, manifesting as anhedonia, somatic anxiety, or somatic delusions. These findings suggest disrupted insular-prefrontal connectivity represents a common neurobiological substrate across mood disorders, providing a potential mechanistic explanation for disturbed body-mind interactions in these conditions.

The baseline dysregulation in patients' insular cortices may represent a trait marker for early detection. Widespread insular activation during disgust may reflect compensatory mechanisms, though insufficient for maintaining normal connectivity with prefrontal evaluative systems.

Clinically, HEFs may serve as objective biomarkers for mood disorders, particularly when subjective report is unreliable. Impaired beta-band connectivity could target neuromodulation interventions such as transcranial magnetic stimulation or neurofeedback. Understanding patients' difficulties can inform psychotherapy, particularly interoceptive awareness training that focuses on improving awareness of bodily signals and their emotional significance.

Limitations include focus on core disgust, lack of gastric activity measurement, and absence of systematic interoceptive sensitivity assessment. Despite these, our findings provide compelling evidence for impaired beta-band information propagation from right anterior ventral insula to left VMPFC as a specific mechanism underlying disturbed body-mind interactions in mood disorders.

In conclusion, we identified diminished beta-band connectivity between right anterior ventral insular cortex and left ventromedial prefrontal cortex as a neural signature of impaired interoceptive-emotional integration in mood disorders. This may represent a novel biomarker for assessment and treatment, providing theoretical insights and practical directions for developing objective biomarkers and targeted interventions.
