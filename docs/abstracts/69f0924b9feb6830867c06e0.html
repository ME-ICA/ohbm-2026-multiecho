Introduction

Amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulation emerges heterogeneously across the cortex in the earliest phases of Alzheimer’s disease (AD).1 While traditional staging models assume a single spatiotemporal trajectory, recent evidence demonstrates multiple distinct Aβ accumulation subtypes with distinct epicentres, spatial sequences, and biological risk profiles.2 This heterogeneity is likely to influence prognosis, trial stratification, and early therapeutic targeting, but remains insufficiently characterised in preclinical populations.

Multiple network-based mechanisms have been hypothesised to explain how Aβ propagates across the cortex, including trans-synaptic spread along functional pathways, spatial proximity, hub-mediated vulnerability, and modular segregation.3,4 However, whether different biological subtypes of early Aβ accumulators rely on distinct network propagation mechanisms is unknown.

In this study, we integrate Aβ-PET imaging with functional connectivity and demographic factors to characterise mechanistic heterogeneity in preclinical AD. Our aims were to (i) identify data-driven subtypes of Aβ accumulation in a preclinical population, (ii) characterise subtype-specific epicentres and longitudinal trajectories of Aβ deposition, and (iii) determine whether subtype-specific Aβ progression is governed by differential network mechanisms.

Methods

Cohorts

Data from AMYPAD-PNHS, ADNI, and A4 were included for individuals aged > 50 years with baseline CDR-Global < 1, structural MRI, and Aβ-PET. Aβ positivity was defined using global Centiloids (cut-off = 24.1). Where available, baseline rs-fMRI and longitudinal Aβ-PET were collected.

Neuroimage processing

Structural MRI scans were bias-field corrected, intensity normalised and parcellated using the Schaefer atlas (17 networks, 100 cortical regions). Cortical thickness and total intracranial volume were derived using FreeSurfer (v.7.1.1). rs-fMRI scans were preprocessed using fMRIPrep (v.23.0.1), including motion correction, co-registration, normalisation, and nuisance regression (WM, CSF, motion parameters, and global signal). Individual functional connectomes were computed via pairwise Pearson’s correlations. Standardised Uptake Value Ratio (SUVR) was computed using study-specific reference regions from preprocessed Aβ-PET scans, averaged within cortical regions and z-scored against site-specific and tracer-specific controls, defined as being CDR = 0 and Aβ-negative.

Subtype definition

Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) modelling was applied to baseline regional SUVR values to identify subtypes of Aβ accumulation.5 Briefly, we used the Mixture SuStaIn implementation in the pySuStaIn software package, which is appropriate for modelling  bimodal distributions, such as those observed in regional SUVR. The optimal number of subtypes was selected using out-of-sample metrics under ten-fold cross-validation. For computational reasons we reduced biomarkers’ dimensionality: Schaefer ROIs were assigned to corresponding Desikan-Killiany (DK) ROIs, based on Euclidean distance. SUVR values from Schaefer ROIs assigned to the same DK ROI were averaged, weighted by voxel count. Subsequently, as previously described,2 DK ROIs were assigned to macroregions that were further averaged across hemispheres to obtain 16 bilateral regions of interest: medial and lateral orbitofrontal gyri; lateral parietal lobe; precuneus; anterior, posterior, and isthmus cingulate gyri; supramarginal gyrus; insula; inferior, middle, and superior frontal gyri; lingual gyrus; occipital lobe; lateral temporal lobe; basal temporal lobe. The derived subtypes and stages were mapped back to Schaefer ROIs for further analyses. 

Network properties computation

Functional connectomes were averaged within subtypes to derive group-level graph properties reflecting hypothesised propagation mechanisms, including trans-neuronal degeneration, hubness, segregation, and geometric distance to the disease epicentre. Trans-neuronal vulnerability was quantified as cortico-cortical neighbourhood Aβ increase over time by averaging the Aβ rates of change of each region’s first-degree neighbours, weighted by the strength of their functional connections. Hubness and segregation were quantified by computing betweenness centrality and the inverse of the clustering coefficient, respectively. Finally, geometric distance was calculated as the weighted average Euclidean distance between each node and all others, weighted by the regional rank derived from SuStaIn stages.

Statistical analysis

Differences in demographics were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, Pearson’s chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Linear mixed-effects models estimated subtype-stratified regional rates of SUVR change, with random intercepts and slopes per participant, adjusting for baseline age, sex, education years, total intracranial volume and regional cortical thickness. Spatial correlations were computed between graph measures and regional longitudinal SUVR change within each subtype using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Statistical significance of spatial correlations was assessed using 10,000-rotation spin permutation testing to account for the inherent spatial autocorrelation of brain maps. Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.4.0), with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participants

We included 3537 cognitively unimpaired older adults, mean age 70.2 ± 7.2 years, 1698 (48%) females. Longitudinal Aβ-PET was available for 1458 subjects, with a total number of 2281 follow-up visits (mean follow-up time = 2.3 ± 2.3 years). Baseline resting-state fMRI was available for a subset of 2756 individuals.

SuStaIn identified Aβ accumulation subtypes

Consistent with previous work,2 the model with three subtypes was deemed optimal. We found frontal, parietal, and occipital Aβ accumulation subtypes, differentiated by early abnormalities in the orbitofrontal cortex, medial parietal cortex, or visual cortex, referred to as subtype-specific epicentres hereafter (Figure 1). After excluding all subjects with a low assignment probability (<50%) and/or a stage of 0 (no significant regional amyloid accumulation), 1215 participants were retained (mean age = 71.0 ± 6.4 years, 533 females [43.9%]). Within these, 1019 had baseline fMRI and 674 had longitudinal Aβ-PET (mean follow-up time = 4.3 ± 1.3 years). Subtype-specific cohort characteristics are presented in Table 1. The majority (n = 723 [59.5%]) was assigned to the frontal, followed by the occipital (n = 250 [20.6%]) and parietal subtypes (n = 242 [19.9%]). Age (p = 0.2), sex (p = 0.2) and years of education (p = 0.8) did not differ between subtypes. The distribution of global CDR scores differed modestly across subtypes (p = 0.048), with most participants being cognitively unimpaired (CDR = 0; 89–94% across subtypes) and a small proportion showing very mild impairment (CDR = 0.5; 6–11%). There was a significantly higher percentage of amyloid-positive participants within the frontal subtype (601, 83%) compared to the occipital (157, 63%) and parietal (168, 69%) subtypes (p < 0.001). Follow-up duration and number of visits differed modestly across subtypes, while PET tracer distribution did not (Table 1).

Subtype-specific longitudinal Aβ deposition and network propagation mechanisms 

Regional SUVR change showed distinct longitudinal trajectories across subtypes (Figure 1). In the parietal subtype, Aβ rates of change were positively correlated with cortico-cortical neighbourhood Aβ increase over time (Spearman’s rho = 0.26, pspin = 0.047), regional betweenness centrality (Spearman’s rho = 0.22, pspin = 0.03), and geometric distance from the epicentre (Spearman’s rho = 0.34, pspin = 0.001). In the occipital subtype, Aβ deposition was positively correlated with the inverse of the clustering coefficient (Spearman’s rho = 0.27, pspin = 0.004) and negatively correlated with the geometric distance from the epicentre (Spearman’s rho = -0.28, pspin = 0.007). No significant correlations were found for the frontal subtype.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified subtype-specific network correlates of Aβ progression across three established subtypes of early Aβ accumulation (frontal, parietal, and occipital) in a cognitively preserved population. 

Network-based analyses revealed distinct, subtype-specific putative mechanisms of Aβ progression already present in preclinical and early disease stages. In the parietal subtype, longitudinal Aβ deposition was positively associated with functional neighbourhood amyloid increase and betweenness centrality, indicating preferential accumulation in highly connected integrative hubs. Regions strongly connected to the parietal epicentre (particularly the precuneus within the posterior default-mode network) were the first to become abnormal, with propagation extending ventrally from medial parietal to occipital regions. This spatial trajectory aligns with the known functional hyperconnectivity between default-mode and visual networks in preclinical AD and supports the hypothesis that hyperconnected pathways may facilitate transneuronal propagation. Animal models further corroborate this interpretation, reporting early Aβ-related neuronal hyperactivity and eventual synaptic failure within parietal–visual circuits. Aβ rates of change were also positively correlated with geometric distance from the epicentre, such that regions further from the parietal epicentre exhibited higher annualised increases. This finding likely reflects a staging effect, whereby regions closest to the epicentre reach higher Aβ levels earlier and subsequently show slower rates of increase, while more distant regions are engaged later and exhibit faster accumulation.

In contrast, the occipital subtype showed accelerated Aβ accumulation in weakly segregated, low local clustering regions and in those generally closer to the epicentre, indicating increased susceptibility of less locally interconnected regions. This pattern may reflect regional vulnerability, consistent with mechanisms proposed for spontaneous cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Although rapidly accumulating Aβ, the regions closest to the occipital epicentre were not always the earliest to become abnormal. For example, the lingual gyrus showed rapid accumulation yet remained late in the abnormality sequence, which may reflect its highly segregated unimodal architecture or the clustering of many individuals around this stage, resulting in fast accumulation despite late abnormality. These findings suggest that occipital Aβ accumulation may be driven by intrinsic regional vulnerability, modulated by local network organisation. 

For the frontal subtype, no network properties predicted longitudinal Aβ accumulation, suggesting a more diffuse or globally driven trajectory that is less constrained by functional topology. The higher global amyloid burden observed in this subtype may partly obscure network-specific effects or, alternatively, reflect network mechanisms less dependent on functional pathways.

While functional connectivity captures patterns of neural communication, Aβ pathology also affects white-matter integrity. Future work integrating diffusion MRI-derived structural connectomes is therefore essential for a more complete brain network-based mechanistic characterisation. Additional work is also needed to determine the extent to which these Aβ propagation results truly reflect intrinsic brain network organisation versus other biological factors, such as selective regional vulnerability.

Together, these findings show that Aβ propagation is not driven by a universal network mechanism but instead arises from subtype-specific interactions between functional architecture, geometric organisation, and regional vulnerability. This mechanistic heterogeneity carries important clinical implications, offering a path toward network-informed prognostic models, subtype-targeted therapeutic strategies, and more individualised risk assessment and prevention approaches in preclinical AD.
