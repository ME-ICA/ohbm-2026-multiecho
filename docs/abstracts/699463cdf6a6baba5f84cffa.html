Introduction

The human brain is supported by intertwined biological processes operating across molecular, cellular, and systems levels. No single neuroimaging modality captures this complexity in full, making cross-modal contextualization an essential step in interpreting novel findings. When we identify localized cortical thinning or distributed functional disruptions, it is often helpful to carry out systematic comparisons against a library of multimodal brain maps to clarify what biological processes might characterize these regions. The recent availability of open-access annotated brain maps, most notably from neuromaps, has greatly simplified such workflows. Researchers can now conveniently correlate any cortical pattern against dozens of reference annotations across modalities and biological scales. 

Yet despite widespread use of spatial correlation to compare brain maps, surprisingly little attention has been paid to the intrinsic statistical and spatial properties of these annotations. Brain maps differ not only in what they measure but also in how their values are distributed across the cortex, where extreme values cluster, and how spatial dependencies propagate across the cortical sheet. These properties have direct consequences for statistical inference: spatial autocorrelation inflates apparent degrees of freedom, and multimodal distributions may reflect distinct spatial regimes that a single correlation coefficient cannot capture. Ignoring these properties risks spurious associations, misinterpretation of null models, and inappropriate statistical inference. Here we systematically characterize 73 cortical brain maps across modalities. We examine how these maps organize into distinct clusters, how their statistical distributions differ, and how spatial autocorrelation structures vary across annotation types, providing a foundation for more principled multimodal integration.

Methods

We assembled 73 cortical annotations from the neuromaps repository, encompassing gene expression profiles derived from microarray data, neurotransmitter receptor densities from positron emission tomography, frequency-band power amplitude from magnetoencephalography, functional gradients and inter-subject variability from resting-state functional MRI, cerebral blood flow and glucose metabolism from metabolic imaging, and patterns of cortical expansion across development and evolution. All maps were parcellated to the Schaefer atlas to enable direct comparison.

We characterized each map along three complementary dimensions. First, we computed pairwise similarities across all 73 maps and applied community detection (Leiden algorithm) to identify stable groupings, then visualized these relationships using spectral embedding. Second, we quantified distributional properties including skewness and kurtosis with L-moment estimators for robust estimation; these were summarized using L-moment ratio diagrams to visualize how maps relate to canonical extreme value distributions. Third, we estimated empirical variograms to characterize spatial autocorrelation structure, resolving anisotropy by alignment with the principal axis of geodesic distance across the cortical surface.

Results

Brain maps occupy a structured similarity space rather than forming random collections. Community detection revealed four stable but partially overlapping clusters that cut across imaging modalities, each reflecting distinct biological constraints. The first cluster captured hierarchical organization along the unimodal-to-transmodal axis, grouping intrinsic neural timescales with laminar differentiation markers and developmental expansion patterns. The second cluster emphasized synaptic architecture, bringing together synaptic vesicle markers, dendritic complexity indices, and metabotropic receptor densities concentrated in the association cortex. The third cluster organized around neurotransmitter systems, linking monoamine transporter distributions with limbic structural markers enriched in the insula and cingulate cortex. The fourth cluster isolated metabolic specialization, aligning aerobic glycolysis maps with mitochondrial markers in late-developing territories. Notably, maps within the same modality, such as different serotonin receptor subtypes, were often separated in embedding space, suggesting distinct cortical patterning principles, while maps from disparate modalities sometimes clustered together, revealing shared spatial organization.

Statistical distributions varied systematically and carried mechanistic information. Maps ranged from approximately Gaussian to heavily skewed and multimodal, with distribution shapes predicting spatial organization. The L-moment ratio diagram suggested that brain maps followed extreme value distributions in interpretable ways that should be considered when performing spatial correlations. Variogram analysis uncovered diverse autocorrelation structures whose spatial scales varied by modality. The spatial granularity reflects the intrinsic resolution of the underlying biological substrate and predicts which annotations a given map may correlate with.

Conclusion

Our systematic characterization of 73 cortical brain maps reveals that multimodal annotations differ not merely in biological content but in their fundamental statistical and spatial architecture. The emergent community structure spanning hierarchical organization, synaptic architecture, neurotransmitter systems, and metabolic specialization reflects biological constraints that transcend measurement modality. These clusters represent parsimonious patterns underlying brain organization that persist across measurement techniques and expose intrinsic similarities among multimodal brain maps. Statistical signatures further provide mechanistic information: distributional shape predicts potential generation processes, while spatial autocorrelation scale indexes the granularity of the underlying biological process. Rather than treating spatial correlation as a monolithic operation, our results provide a blueprint for more nuanced exploratory analyses that move beyond gross spatial correlation toward a more granular understanding of what makes brain maps similar, different, and biologically interpretable.
