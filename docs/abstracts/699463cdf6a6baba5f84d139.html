Introduction

When emotional content disrupts the interpretation of word meaning, the increased competition between emotional and semantic information can hinder accurate semantic judgment. In particular, individuals with higher levels of autistic traits are more prone to such emotion-driven disruption of semantic control¹, revealing variability in how emotion affects meaning processing. Because autistic traits vary continuously in the general population², identifying the mechanisms underlying this variability is essential. Such emotion-driven interference increases the effort required to select, retrieve, and integrate semantic information, especially when relationships between emotional concepts are less straightforward. One factor that may further complicate this process is alexithymia, a difficulty in recognizing and describing emotions that often co-occurs with autistic traits³. Processing emotional meaning involves detecting affective salience and exerting top-down semantic regulation: the insula signals socially relevant or threat-related salience⁴, whereas the left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) supports controlled semantic selection⁵. This study tested whether negative emotional content magnifies semantic-control demands across autistic-trait levels, and whether differences in neural responses are dimensionally associated with alexithymia.

Methods

Thirty-two Taiwanese adults (age range: 19–29 years; 18 females) completed the Mandarin versions of the AQ-10 and TAS-20 and were assigned to a High-AQ (AQ-10 ≥ 5) or Low-AQ (AQ-10 < 5) group (n = 16 per group). Groups were matched in age, education, sex, and handedness. During fMRI, participants judged whether two emotional Chinese words were related in meaning. Emotional valence (negative, neutral, positive) and association strength (weak vs strong) were orthogonally manipulated to vary demands on semantic control. We tested whether negative emotion magnified the difference between weak and strong semantic associations, compared with the corresponding difference under neutral emotion, operationalized as the interaction contrast [NEG (weak–strong) – NEU (weak–strong)]. We further examined whether this modulation differed between High-AQ and Low-AQ groups. Mean beta estimates from significant clusters were extracted and correlated with TAS-20 scores within each group.

Results

The High-AQ group showed greater left insula and left IFG activation than the Low-AQ group when negative emotion led to a larger weak–strong difference than neutral emotion. Within the High-AQ group, stronger activation in these regions was positively associated with alexithymia severity (insula: r = .57, p = .022; IFG: r = .54, p = .032). No reliable correlations between mean beta estimates (insula/IFG) and alexithymia were observed in the Low-AQ group, indicating that the coupling between regional neural responses and alexithymia was specific to individuals with higher levels of autistic traits.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that emotion-driven disruption of semantic control represents a trait-linked vulnerability in individuals with higher levels of autistic traits. The selective coupling between alexithymia and neural responses in semantic-control regions indicates that difficulty processing emotional meaning emerges more strongly at the neural level among those with elevated socio-emotional traits. Emotion-induced semantic conflict may thus be a key mechanism contributing to variability in semantic interpretation across the autism-trait continuum.
