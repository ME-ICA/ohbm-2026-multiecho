Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative bone and joint disease affecting hundreds of millions of individuals globally, with the knee being the most commonly affected joint. Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is associated with chronic pain, reduced mobility, and high disability, yet there are no disease-modifying treatments. Total knee arthroplasty (TKA), where the knee joint is replaced with orthopedic hardware, is performed as a final treatment option, following failure of other pain management strategies, but ~20% of patients report ongoing chronic pain following recovery from TKA1. 

There is evidence that chronic pain in KOA causes central sensitization, where central nervous system (CNS) pain signaling is altered to produce heightened or abnormal pain responses2. Central sensitization in KOA may explain why some patients still experience significant residual pain following TKA and likely diminishes the efficacy of typical analgesic and musculoskeletal therapies. Understanding the mechanism of central sensitization in KOA will help guide future work in developing more effective treatments for persistent pain after TKA. Arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI produces cerebral blood flow (CBF) maps, a marker of regional brain activity that can enhance our understanding of CNS-based pain pathophysiology in chronic conditions like KOA3. Previous studies have reported that OA patients show significantly altered CBF in pain-processing regions of the brain when compared to pain-free controls4,5.  

In this study, we compared regional CBF between pain-free controls and patients with end stage KOA both before and 3 months after TKA to assess differences in CBF associated with KOA and determine whether surgical intervention leads to alteration or normalization of CBF towards pain-free levels. This is the first study to assess CBF in KOA patients before and after TKA. 

Methods

Participants were females aged 40-75 years, without chronic pain symptoms other than KOA and without major cardiovascular conditions. Data were collected from KOA participants both before and 3 months after TKA, and at one timepoint for pain-free controls. T1-weighted imaging and pseudo-continuous ASL data were acquired using a 3T GE 750W scanner, and CBF maps were created on the scanner. An example CBF map is shown in Figure 1A. We assessed CBF in 8 pain-related brain regions of interest (ROIs): primary sensorimotor cortex, precuneus, medial dorsal thalamus, putamen, insula, anterior and posterior cingulate cortices, and periaqueductal gray, as well as a control region in the occipital cortex4,5. Binary ROI masks are shown in Figure 1B. Brain regions were defined using Harvard-Oxford and Talairach atlases or literature-based coordinates5. Our pre-processing workflow included FreeSurfer Synthstrip (version 7.3.2) for brain extraction, and various tools from ANTs (version 2.5.0) and FSL (version 6.0.0).  

We performed paired t-tests to assess the differences in CBF across all 9 ROIs in the 10 complete pre-post data pairs. Independent t-tests were used to assess the differences in CBF between the KOA pre-TKA and controls, as well as the KOA post-TKA and controls. Five confirmatory linear mixed-effects models were performed in R-4.3.0 and the lme4 and emmeans packages to confirm differences in CBF between our three groups: KOA pre-TKA, KOA post-TKA, and pain-free controls. Models were specified as: CBF_ROI ~ Group + (1|Subject), to account for random subject effects and to model CBF in each ROI across groups. False discovery rate corrections were applied to estimated marginal means for multiple comparisons.

Results

45 females participated in this study; 23 with KOA and 19 controls. There were 20 participants in the pre-TKA session and 17 returned for the post-TKA visit. Three participants only had post-TKA visits. There was a total of 49 CBF maps across all sessions that were usable following assessments for motion error and artifacts.  

In the putamen, KOA participants showed hypoperfusion relative to controls at both timepoints (pre-TKA: β = −5.12, t42 = −3.44, p = 0.001; post-TKA: β = −4.96, t45 = −3.09, p = 0.003). This same pattern of reduced CBF in KOA participants was also observed in the medial dorsal thalamus, insula, anterior cingulate cortex, and posterior cingulate cortex at both pre- and post-TKA timepoints (all p < 0.05). As an internal control, there was no significant difference in our control ROI in the occipital cortex. CBF values for all significant ROIs are visualized in Figure 2.  

For all significant ROIs, post hoc assessments confirmed that the differences in CBF were driven by differences between KOA pre-TKA and KOA post-TKA outcomes relative to controls, rather than by random effects. Still, there were no significant differences between the pre- and post-TKA KOA groups. False discovery rate corrected p-values for all the pre-control and post-control contrasts were p<0.05, indicating that pre- and post-TKA KOA values had significantly lower CBF in these ROIs than controls.   

Conclusion

In this study, we used ASL to assess CBF in eight brain regions associated with chronic pain in a cohort of individuals with end-stage KOA before and after TKA, as well as in pain-free controls. We found that CBF was significantly lower in the putamen, medial dorsal thalamus, insula, and the anterior and posterior cingulate cortices in people with KOA compared to controls, both before and 3-months post-TKA. The CBF differences in the putamen and medial dorsal thalamus suggest alterations to the brain’s pain integration and modulation pathways in KOA. We also saw hypoperfusion of the insula, and anterior and posterior cingulate cortices in both of our KOA groups, implicating brain regions involved in pain integration and affective states in end-stage KOA.  

We found no significant CBF differences between groups in the sensorimotor cortex, precuneus, or periaqueductal gray, despite previous literature showing altered perfusion in these regions in a KOA population5. This difference may be attributable to the disease-stage or duration, as our study specifically assessed patients with end-stage KOA pursuing TKA.  

We did not detect a significant change in CBF post-TKA compared to pre-TKA. This suggests that the potential period of neural recovery following TKA exceeds the 3-month window of our study. Our further work will extend this follow-up period to 6-months post-TKA to determine whether surgical recovery is associated with normalization of CBF the levels observed in pain-free controls. Longer follow-up times will allow us to investigate whether the CBF alterations observed in KOA are permanent or simply show protracted normalization. In future studies, we will assess the relationship between CBF in these crucial pain processing regions and patient reported pain outcomes from validated questionnaires as well as quantitative sensory testing data.
