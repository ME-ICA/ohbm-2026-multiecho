Introduction

Sustained and divided attention are essential for goal-directed behaviour and flexible adaptation to task demands. The Trail-Making Test1 (TMT) measures these abilities: Part A indexes sustained attention, while Part B assesses divided attention and cognitive flexibility. In adults, these functions depend on well-characterized left frontal networks, with voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping (VLSM) showing that impaired TMT-B performance correlates with lesions to the dorsolateral and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and insula2. However, the neural mechanisms supporting attentional development in childhood remain poorly understood. While functional MRI studies display diffuse frontal activation in children compared to focal adult patterns, these findings are correlational. Here, we test two competing developmental hypotheses using a causal neuropsychological approach: the Early Frontal Core Hypothesis, suggesting undifferentiated frontal regions supporting attention that gradually specialize3; and the Functional Differentiation Hypothesis, proposing early specialization of frontal subregions for distinct attentional components.

Methods

We examined 43 paediatric patients (aged 7-16 years; mean age 12.51 ± 2.38 years) undergoing surgical resection of drug-resistant frontal lobe epilepsy. Inclusion criteria: right-handedness, left-hemisphere language dominance, non-pathological fluid intelligence, and seizure-freedom post-operatively (Engel class I) (only 12% of the sample was prescribed anti-epileptic drugs after the surgery). Pre- and post-surgical neuropsychological assessments (baseline = T0; 6-months follow-up = T1) included Trail-Making Test administration. TMT performance was converted into a progressive index representing change from baseline to post-surgery, accounting for pre-operative epilepsy-related deficits. VLSM analysis was conducted on 41 participants for TMT-A (sustained attention) and 35 for TMT-B (divided attention and cognitive flexibility) using post-surgical MRI lesion maps normalized to MNI152 standard space. Voxel-wise t-tests (α = 0.05, one-tailed) were adjusted for lesion volume and age at surgery. Statistical significance was determined using permutation testing (3000 iterations) with family-wise error correction. Only voxels with less than 10% lesion overlap were considered. 

Results

For TMT-A, 56% of participants (n=23) improved post-surgically (T1); 32% declined. For TMT-B, 57% (n=29) improved; 43% declined. VLSM analysis revealed 725 significant voxels for TMT-A: 416 in the left supplementary motor area (z=-3.30), 198 in the left middle frontal gyrus (z=-3.28), and 111 in the left superior frontal gyrus (z=-3.29). TMT-B analysis identified 75 significant voxels, primarily in the left middle frontal gyrus (69 voxels; z=-3.23). Critically, the left middle frontal gyrus showed overlapping lesion-symptom associations for both sustained and divided attention tasks. All significant clusters were left-lateralized despite balanced hemispheric lesion distribution, consistent with adult TMT studies and the left hemisphere’s role in motor control and alphanumeric processing.  

Conclusion

This causal lesion-mapping study provides novel evidence for the neural architecture of attentional control in childhood. The left supplementary motor area and left middle/superior frontal gyri support both sustained and divided attention in children, mirroring adult-like organization. The overlapping left middle frontal gyrus involvement across both tasks aligns with the Interactive Specialization framework3, suggesting that frontal regions initially support multiple attentional functions within broader, less specialized networks. With developmental refinement, these regions become increasingly efficient and selective3,4,5. Our findings support a developmental trajectory from an early, multipurpose frontal core towards progressively optimized and distributed networks, providing causal rather than merely correlational evidence for frontal maturation. These results advance understanding of cognitive development and offer translational implications for detecting atypical frontal development in neurodevelopmental disorders.
