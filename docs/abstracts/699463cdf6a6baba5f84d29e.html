Introduction

Developmental dyscalculia is a neurodevelopmental disorder, characterized by deficits in number processing and calculations, with a prevalence of up to 6%. Dyscalculia and poor numeracy are associated with negative consequences for school success, professional career and well-being. The difficulties persist into adulthood. In a meta-analysis it has been shown that interventions have positive effects on math performance in children with math difficulties and that it seems to be important tailoring interventions on individuals’ difficulties^1. Moreover, interventions in children with dyscalculia lead to adaptations of neuronal networks (e.g. Kucian et al., 2011^2). However, to date, up to our knowledge, there is no evaluated intervention for adults with dyscalculia available and research on neuronal plasticity of numerical cognition in dyscalculic adults is limited. Therefore, our research project aimed to develop a support program for affected adolescents and adults and to evaluate its effectiveness in terms of behavioural and neuronal changes. The intervention focuses on basic mathematical knowledge, which could not be acquired due to dyscalculia.

Methods

In total, we aim to recruit 60 participants between 14 and 27 years of age. To date, 31 adolescents and young adults (24 female, mean age = 17.3) with dyscalculia and 10 matched controls (8 female, mean age = 19.1) have been recruited. Exclusion criteria include cognitive impairments, neurological disorders, medication with impacts on neuronal activity, and contraindications according to magnetic resonance safety criteria. Furthermore, participants with dyscalculia should not score within the criteria “basic mathematical knowledge given” in the Basis-Math 4-8^3 test. Participants with dyscalculia receive 12 units of an individually tailored educational intervention within 14 to 28 weeks. The intervention focuses on the use of manipulatives (e.g. base ten blocks) to build up mental representations of quantities and numbers. Additionally, participants receive digital exercises for at-home training of numerical cognition. Control participants do not receive any intervention but are assessed twice. To evaluate the intervention’s short- and long-term effects, we measure math performance (with the Basis-Math 4-8 and Sinus ^4 test)  and conduct magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) before and after the intervention, as well as at a follow-up point 14 to 28 weeks after the intervention. To make sure that effects are specific to the intervention, we measure some participants additionally 14 to 28 weeks before the intervention. We use a 3T MRI scanner from GE HealthCare and assess T1-weighted and diffusion tensor imaging to investigate structural changes. Moreover, we assess brain activity (fMRI) during resting state and during a magnitude comparison and a place value task. The place value task was newly developed and aims to capture the understanding of the place value system (Figure 1). In detail, participants must decide whether a particular place value of a number changes after they performed an addition or subtraction calculation. In addition to the assessment of mathematical performance, the behavioural part of the project includes a comprehensive battery of math related aspects, as well as intelligence quotient, working memory, and comorbidities.

Results

In our preliminary behavioural analyses of the ongoing study, we investigated whether (1) group differences between dyscalculics and controls can be observed in the place value task and (2) whether dyscalculics show improvements in math performance on a behavioural level after the intervention. The results of the fMRI place value task showed that dyscalculics' performance is impaired compared to the control group in the addition (Mann-Whitney-U-test, left-sided, z = -3.28, p < 0.001) and the subtraction condition (Mann-Whitney-U-test, left-sided, z = -4.26, p < 0.001). Furthermore, analysis of the intervention effects shows that dyscalculics' math performance increases in the Basis-Math 4-8 (Wilcoxon-test, left-sided, z = -3.49, p < 0.001) and Sinus test (Wilcoxon-test, left-sided, z = -3.18, p < 0.001) after the completion of the intervention. This effect seems to be specific to the intervention and cannot be explained by test-repetition effects, which were measured by the comparison between the performance of 14 to 28 weeks before and the performance shortly before the intervention (Basis-Math 4-8, Wilcoxon-test, left-sided, p = 0.1055) (Figure 2). We are currently analyzing group differences and intervention effects at neuronal levels.

Conclusion

Our preliminary results in our newly developed fMRI task indicate an impaired understanding of the place value system in dyscalculic participants. Allowing for a better characterization of difficulties in individuals with dyscalculia and building the bases of investigating these effects at neuronal levels. Furthermore, our results demonstrate that existing mathematical gaps in adolescents and young adults with dyscalculia can be substantially reduced through targeted, structured interventions. Importantly, this study is the first to provide empirical evidence that such improvements are achievable beyond childhood, as previous research has almost exclusively focused on children with dyscalculia. Our study contributes to a better understanding of neuronal learning processes in dyscalculia and provide concrete support to those affected.
