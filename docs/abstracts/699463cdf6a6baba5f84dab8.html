Introduction

Classical neuroimaging analyses typically involve dividing the brain into discrete areas and studying intra- and inter-regional properties of brain structure and function. This reliance on discrete areas imposes a specific spatial scale on the analyses, determined by the particular discretization used (e.g., a specific brain parcellation), which fails to account for the intrinsic multiscale organization of many anatomical and functional features of the brain. Recently, spectral approaches have been developed, such as frameworks inspired by graph signal processing, connectome harmonics, and neural field theory^1, that offer an intrinsically multiscale characterization of any brain map by viewing such maps as resulting from the preferential excitation of the resonant modes, collectively referred to as eigenmodes or modes, of brain anatomy.

Here, we present a new Python toolbox for enabling rapid and efficient mode-based spectral analyses of brain-wide neuroimaging data. The toolbox is centred on the geometric eigenmodes framework inspired by neural field theory^1 and shown in recent work to provide a compact and parsimonious description of diverse aspects of brain function^2, regional organization^3, connectivity^4, and group differences in anatomy^5, although its core modules on general spectral analyses can easily be extended to accommodate other types of anatomical eigenmodes (e.g., those derived from connectomes). The toolbox offers a new spectral window into the relationship between brain anatomy and function across individuals, development, evolution, and disease.

Methods

Our package is currently structured into four main modules, two modules under development, and one helper modeule (io.py) for loading and manipulating meshes (Figure 1). The core functionality resides in eigen.py, where cortical modes can be computed from a triangular surface mesh (e.g., the fsLR 32k midthickness template). Specifically, we leverage the LaPy toolbox to solve a Laplace-Beltrami eigenvalue problem, discretized via the finite element method. Optionally, a cortical map (e.g., myelination, cortical thickness) may also be provided as a source of heterogeneity, enriching the modes with anatomical detail.

Since the modes form an orthogonal basis set, basis.py can then be used to decompose any cortical map into a linear combination of modes, revealing the spatial frequencies that dominate neural features of interest^2. Conversely, any cortical map can be reconstructed from a subset of modes, with the accuracy of this reconstruction reflecting the degree to which relatively simple geometric features constrain the spatial patterning of the map. In waves.py, cortical activity can be simulated using a two-parameter biophysical model based on neural field theory^2. Here, any external inputs to the cortex can be specified across space and time to produce wave propagation. This link to dynamics occurs because geometric eigenmodes correspond to standing waves of dynamics in systems dominated by propagating waves of excitation. In connectome.py, simulated cortico-cortical structural connectivity can be generated via a two-parameter model derived from neural field theory recently shown to accurately capture diverse aspects of connectome topology and topography across different species^4.

In parcellation.py (under development), parcellations can be generated for any cortical or subcortical mesh, with any number of parcels, by using the first non-constant mode for recursive bipartitioning^3. These geometrically-derived parcellations have been shown to define more homogeneous parcels than nearly all other available atlases^3.  Finally, morphometry.py (under development) uses the modes to quantify multiscale anatomical differences across groups, affording a more reliable, spectral alternative to classical surface-based morphometric analyses^5.

Results

neuromodes is a pip-installable package available at https://github.com/NSBLab/neuromodes. Before undertaking any analysis, users can compute cortical modes via eigen.py. For ease of use, we provide standard group-average midthickness surface meshes for human, macaque, and marmoset cortices, as well as group-average human cortical maps for heterogeneity, all of which can be accessed using io.py.

The remaining modules can then be used to address a range of research questions, for example: a) How do the spatial frequencies of common cortical properties vary across mammalian species? (basis.py); b) Can wave-like propagation explain the cortex’s responses to different stimulation modalities (e.g., transcranial magnetic stimulation, optogenetics)? (waves.py); c) Is structural connectivity more strongly constrained by cortical geometry earlier in neurodevelopment? (connectome.py); d) Does accounting for individual-specific variations in cortical geometry improve parcel homogeneity? (parcellation.py); e) Can the reliability of grey matter atrophy patterns in psychiatric disorders be improved by accounting for multiscale variation? (morphometry.py).

To guide users through this diverse range of functionality, we have a Jupyter notebook tutorial for each of the modules, available alongside an API reference at https://neuromodes.readthedocs.io/en/latest/. The stability and validity of our package are also upheld by our minimal set of dependencies and wide test coverage for almost all functions.

Conclusion

neuromodes serves as an open-source, expanding hub for researchers to conveniently and rigorously incorporate mode-based methods within their own investigations. The toolbox integrates and standardizes a broad range of mode-based approaches, enabling access for the wider computational neuroscience community. It has already been used to replicate significant findings in the field, and has contributed to novel developments for understanding brain structure and function. Moreover, neuromodes remains well-supported and will continue to be under active development, including adding more functionalities and will be registered on the Python Package Index repository before publication. Future work will support the computation of volumetric modes, allowing for investigations of brain structures that cannot be approximated as a surface (e.g., human subcortex or mouse isocortex). We hope that the toolbox encourages the further development of methods that will complement and extend classical neuroimaging analysis.
