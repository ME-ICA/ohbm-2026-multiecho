Introduction

Suicide remains a leading cause of death and continues to challenge clinical prediction frameworks. Despite decades of research, suicide risk assessment largely relies on self-report and clinician judgment, approaches that show limited reliability for identifying who is at imminent risk and when intervention is most needed. This reflects a broader gap in the field: the absence of objective, biologically grounded markers capable of capturing dynamic changes in suicide vulnerability over time.

Neuroimaging offers a promising avenue for addressing this gap. Functional brain alterations linked to mood regulation, impulse control, threat processing, and reward valuation have been implicated in suicidal behavior. However, most neuroimaging studies are cross-sectional, focus on short-term outcomes, or lack sufficient temporal resolution for prospective prediction. As a result, the potential of brain-based biomarkers to support early intervention remains largely unrealized.

Here, we examine whether quantitative single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging combined with machine-learning models can provide individualized, time-resolved predictions of suicide risk across extended follow-up periods. Using longitudinal outcome data, we generate monthly suicide-risk trajectories for up to 15 years following a single brain scan, adjusting for demographic and site-related variability. Beyond identifying individuals at elevated risk, we test whether changes in brain-based risk estimates reliably precede suicide by clinically meaningful intervals.

By integrating functional neuroimaging with temporal prediction modeling, this work aims to shift suicide prevention from retrospective risk characterization toward proactive, biologically informed forecasting. Demonstrating that suicide risk follows detectable neurobiological trajectories would represent a critical step toward scalable, precision-based prevention strategies enabling intervention well before crisis onset.

Methods

Study Cohort and Outcomes

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using quantitative resting-state SPECT scans obtained in routine clinical care. The cohort included 23,769 individuals, comprising 178 patients who died by suicide during follow-up and 23,591 depressed patients who did not die by suicide and were treated as censored observations. Suicide deaths were identified via recorded suicide dates. Censored individuals had no suicide date and were followed until last known contact or death from other causes. Follow-up extended up to 15 years after brain imaging, with time measured in months.

Propensity Scoring and Weighting of Censored Controls

To improve covariate comparability between suicide cases and censored depressed controls, propensity scores were estimated using age, biological sex, imaging site, and clinical diagnosis indicators. Overlap weighting was applied to the censored control group to emphasize individuals with covariate profiles most comparable to suicide cases and to reduce the influence of extreme propensity values. Weights were trimmed at the 99th percentile, and covariate balance was assessed using standardized diagnostics computed before and after weighting. These weights were incorporated into all subsequent survival and prediction analyses.

Image Processing and Feature Generation

SPECT scans underwent quality control and preprocessing, including spatial normalization to standard space and intensity scaling. Voxelwise perfusion values were z-scored, and an eroded MNI152 brain mask was applied. To address multi-site heterogeneity, imaging site was handled through stratification in survival models and inclusion as a covariate in downstream prediction models.

Voxelwise Hazard Modeling and Multiple Comparisons

Voxelwise associations with time to suicide were estimated using penalized Cox proportional hazards models incorporating overlap weights and robust variance estimation. Models adjusted for age and sex and used site-stratified baseline hazards. Voxelwise statistical maps were corrected for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate. Signed significance maps were generated by separating positive and negative hazard coefficients, and FDR-significant peak coordinates were extracted and quality-checked.

ROI-Based Prediction and Evaluation

FDR-significant peaks were summarized into spherical regions of interest (6 mm radius; minimum 12 mm separation) to form imaging predictors. Regularized prediction models incorporating imaging features, age, sex, and site were trained to generate horizon-based and time-resolved suicide risk estimates. Performance was evaluated using threshold sweeps, quota-based high-risk policies (e.g., top 1–10% predicted risk), calibration analyses, and death-aligned assessments of predicted risk trajectories.

Results

Voxelwise Hazard Associations

Voxelwise Cox proportional hazards models identified distributed brain regions in which resting-state perfusion was significantly associated with time to suicide after adjustment for age, sex, and site and incorporation of overlap weights. Both positive and negative hazard coefficients were observed, indicating regions where relatively higher or lower perfusion was associated with increased suicide hazard. After false discovery rate correction, significant clusters were detected across cortical and subcortical regions.

FDR-significant peak coordinates spanned multiple large-scale functional networks, as defined by the MNI v7 atlas, rather than being confined to a single anatomical locus. Surviving regions were distributed across association, limbic, and regulatory systems, consistent with networks supporting affective regulation, cognitive control, and self-referential processing (Figure 1). This distributed pattern indicates that suicide risk is associated with system-level functional alterations rather than isolated focal abnormalities. Peak coordinates from FDR-significant voxels were extracted and retained for downstream predictive modeling.

Identification of High-Risk Individuals

Using features derived from FDR-significant peak-centered regions, regularized prediction models generated individualized, time-resolved suicide risk estimates. Across multiple prediction horizons, the models demonstrated strong sensitivity for identifying individuals who later died by suicide. At balanced decision thresholds, 82% of individuals who died by suicide within 6 months were correctly identified, and 84% were identified within 24 months prior to death.

When analysis was restricted to individuals in the top 5% of predicted risk at any point during follow-up, approximately one-third of all suicide deaths were captured. Suicide incidence within this high-risk group was approximately seven-fold higher than in the full cohort. Depending on the prediction horizon, between 30 and 44 individuals required monitoring to identify one true suicide case.

Temporal Prediction of Suicide Risk

Predicted risk trajectories exhibited structured temporal patterns preceding suicide. Across multiple timing approaches, including rolling-peak and differential-peak methods, the predicted month of suicide was within ±3 months of the observed date in approximately 36–38% of cases. The median absolute error between predicted and observed suicide timing was approximately six months. In most individuals who died by suicide, a sustained increase in predicted risk was detectable 6–12 months prior to death.

Calibration and Policy-Based Evaluation

Risk predictions demonstrated acceptable calibration across evaluated horizons. Policy-based analyses showed that quota-driven strategies, such as flagging the top 1–10% highest-risk individuals, consistently concentrated suicide events into small, clinically actionable subgroups. Death-aligned analyses further confirmed that predicted risk increased as individuals approached the time of suicide, supporting the temporal validity of the modeled hazard trajectories.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that quantitative brain imaging combined with survival-based modeling can identify meaningful neurobiological signatures of suicide risk and its temporal evolution. Using voxelwise Cox proportional hazards models with propensity overlap weighting, we show that suicide risk is associated with distributed patterns of resting-state brain perfusion spanning multiple large-scale functional networks rather than isolated focal abnormalities. These findings support a systems-level view of suicide vulnerability involving circuits related to affective regulation, cognitive control, and self-referential processing.

Beyond identifying risk-associated brain regions, this work shows that imaging-derived features can be translated into individualized, time-resolved risk estimates. Models based on FDR-significant hazard-associated regions demonstrated strong sensitivity for identifying individuals who later died by suicide and concentrated risk into small, clinically actionable subgroups. Importantly, predicted risk trajectories exhibited structured temporal patterns, with sustained increases in risk detectable months to years prior to death in many individuals. This temporal signal suggests that suicide risk is not a random or instantaneous phenomenon but follows detectable neurobiological trajectories over time.

These results highlight the potential value of integrating functional neuroimaging with survival analysis and modern weighting approaches to improve suicide risk stratification. While this study is retrospective and does not establish causality, it provides evidence that objective brain-based markers can complement traditional clinical assessments by identifying both who is at elevated risk and when risk is increasing. Such approaches may help shift suicide prevention from reactive crisis response toward proactive, biologically informed intervention.

Future work will focus on external validation in independent cohorts, integration of additional clinical and behavioral data, and prospective evaluation of how brain-based risk estimates can inform real-world prevention strategies. Together, these findings support continued investigation of neuroimaging-informed hazard modeling as a pathway toward more precise, scalable, and timely suicide prevention.
