Introduction

Brain activity can be recorded at high temporal resolution with electrophysiological methods, including the non-invasive methods magneto- and electroencephalography (M/EEG) and invasive methods like electrocorticography (ECoG). 

The power spectrum in electrophysiological recordings typically is investigated in the range 1 - 100 Hz and exhibits several peaks over a background that decays exponentially with frequency. Peaks in the spectrum mostly represent neuronal oscillations, i.e., collective fluctuations in excitability. 

The dominant peak at rest is usually situated in the alpha band (range 7–14 Hz), and most pronounced when eyes are closed. The exact value of the alpha peak frequency (APF) varies strongly between individuals, and can be seen as a trait-like characteristic1. It is also altered in a number of clinical conditions.

The part of the spectrum not captured in peaks is called the “aperiodic slope”, decaying as a function of frequency in a 1/f𝛽  exponential manner. This component is nowadays widely seen as relevant aperiodic “background activity” instead of mere noise. Like the APF, the exponent 𝛽 also varies individuals and is altered in various disorders2. It has been suggested that 𝛽 is linked to the excitation-inhibition ratio in the brain, although recent research indicates that this relationship is not necessarily straightforward3.

However, not much research has been done on how the alpha peak frequency and the aperiodic exponent are related to each other and to other characteristics of brain activity such as long-range temporal correlations (LRTCs) and oscillation-based functional connectivity (FC).  

Here, we assessed in a data-driven approach the aperiodic slope, the APF, LRTC scaling exponents, and two forms of FC in two large datasets of source-reconstructed resting-state MEG recordings. We report the inter- and intra-individual variability, the effect of closed vs. open eyes, as well as the statistical relationships between these metrics.

Methods

MEG datasets

We here analyze two large MEG datasets. 

The first, the “UH” dataset, consists of 328 recordings of 10-min resting-state (290 eyes-open, 38 eyes-closed) from 92 healthy participants (51M, 41F) over a time span of 9 years, with the number of eyes-open recordings per participant varying from 1 to 16. 

The second, the “LEAP” dataset, consists of 75 healthy participants (45M, 29F) and 263 patients (163F, 87M) with major depressive disorder, from each of whom one 15-minute eyes-open resting state recording was obtained. Severity of depression symptoms and comorbid symptoms such as anxiety, PTSD, substance use, anhedonia, and others, were assessed with 11 questionnaires. 

All participants gave informed consent, and studies were approved by ethical boards and carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Recordings and preprocessing

All MEG recordings were carried out with 306-channel Megin Triux systems at Helsinki University Hospital and Aalto University in Helsinki and Espoo, Finland. Anatomical MRI scans were also recorded for all participants and preprocessed with Freesurfer software. MEG data was preprocessed and source reconstruction with MRI-derived head models was carried out with custom python code based on the MNE-python package. Source time series were then collapsed to 200 cortical parcels of the Schaefer atlas. 

Assessment of oscillations and aperiodic slope

We computed the power spectrum (PSD) at 202 equally-spaced frequencies from 1 to 100 Hz. We used the fooof Toolbox2 to separate the PSD into peaks and the 1/f𝛽 aperiodic slope and obtain the APF and 𝛽 both for whole-brain average PSD and individual parcels. 

Additionally, we also used the recently introduced phase autocorrelation function (pACF) method to assess oscillations at the parcel level4. This method allows the estimation of average oscillation lifetime per parcel and frequency. We used the frequency in the alpha range with the largest lifetime as an alternate estimate of the APF.

Assessment of long-range temporal correlations and oscillation-based functional connectivity

For these analysis, we filtered broadband parcel time series into 41 narrowband time series at 41 log-spaced frequencies. We assessed long-range temporal correlations for each recordings and each parcel with detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA). This yields a scaling exponent which for brain activity lies in the range 0.5 - 1 (indicating positive autocorrelation across time scales).

We also assessed two forms of oscillation-based functional connectivity, phase synchrony (PS), and amplitude correlations (AC) with the imaginary phase-locking value (iPLV) and orthogonalized correlation coefficient (oCC), respectively. These metrics are insensitive to zero-lag spurious interactions arising from linear mixing and volume conduction. PS and AC were computed for each recording, each pair of cortical parcels, and each frequency. Whole-brain averages of FC strength were obtained by taking the mean over the whole 200x200 connectivity matrix, and parcel (nodal) FC strength as the mean over all connections between one parcel and all others, respectively.

Assessment of relationships between metrics

We assessed the relationships between estimates of APF, 𝛽, pACF lifetimes, DFA exponents, and PS and AC with Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Differences between eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions were assessed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and group differences between the patient and control cohorts in the LEAP dataset were assessed with the Mann-Whitney-U test. We further also assessed group differences between 5 biological subtypes of MDD which we had identified in another analysis5 of the LEAP datasets with the Kruskal-Wallis test.

Results

APF and 1/f exponent in healthy participants

For participants with multiple resting-state eyes-open recordings, we observed that estimates for the APF, the aperiodic 1/f exponent 𝛽 , and the alpha-band DFA scaling exponent 𝛼 remained similar between recordings and varied more between than within individuals (Figure 1A-C). Many subjects exhibited considerable regional variation in APF and 𝛽, where APF was often bimodal, whereas 𝛽 was generally continuous. At the cohort level, mean APF peaked in somatomotor and 𝛽 in temporal and posterior medial regions of the cortex. 

Estimates using different parameters for the fooof algorithm generally yielded similar results, and estimates for APF from pACF oscillation lifetimes yielded similar results to fooof algorithm (Spearman’s r=0.68, p < 10-8).

Comparing matched eyes-closed and eyes-open recordings from the same day for 33 individuals, we found APF decreased (by 0.42 Hz on average; p = 0.006 Wilcoxon test); while both 𝛽 and 𝛼 increased (by 0.07 and 0.01, resp, p = 0.0004 and 0.003 Wilcoxon test).

Interestingly, we found that across eyes-open recordings, APF was negatively correlated with both 𝛽 (Spearman’s r = -0.33, p < 10-6) and 𝛼 (r = -0.3, p < 10-5), while 𝛽 and 𝛼 were positively correlated with each other (r = 0.21, p < 10-8). These correlations were also significant across subject means (r values -0.3, -0.36, 0.27, and p values 0.004, 0.0008, 0.008, see correlations of APF and 𝛽 in Figure 1D). Across eyes-open recordings, APF was also negatively correlated with pACF lifetimes, and whole-brain PS and AC strength at the APF, while all these were in turn positively correlated with each other; however, these correlations were not significant at the subject level.

APF and 1/f exponent in clinical data

In the “LEAP” clinical dataset, no significant group differences in APF and 𝛽 were found between a cohort of patients with major depressive disorder and a cohort of healthy controls. However, we found significant differences for both APF (p = 0.03, Kruskal-Wallis test) and 𝛽 (p = 1.3*10-7) between 5 different neurobiological subtypes of MDD that had been identified from MEG-based functional connectivity in another study5.

Conclusion

We here explored in a data-driven approach the inter- and intra-individual variability and inter-relatedness of two important aspects of the electrophysiological spectrum, the alpha peak frequency and the exponent 𝛽 of the aperiodic background slope in two large MEG datasets.

Our results show that both metrics are relatively stable within individuals, affirming their trait-like characteristics1,2. For APF, similar findings were obtained using the fooof method2 and choosing the frequency with the longest oscillation lifetimes estimated with the phase autocorrelation function4 affirming the validity of these estimates.

Importantly, we found that APF and 𝛽  were negatively correlated with each other, i.e. a higher APF value was likely to go with a lower value of 𝛽 and thus a flatter spectral slope. In addition, APF also was correlated negatively with the alpha-band LRTC scaling exponent 𝛼, while 𝛽 and 𝛼 were positively correlated with each other. This indicates that alpha oscillations and the aperiodic component of the electrophysiological spectrum are linked mechanistically and together represent linked aspects of how neuronal activity is organized in the individual  brain. 

Notably, the positive correlation between aperiodic exponent and LRTC exponent does not match with simple assumptions about how these are related to the excitation-inhibition (E/I) balance in the brain. In the framework of critical dynamics, healthy brain activity is assumed to be in the slightly subcritical side of an extended critical-like regime, the Griffiths Phase, wherein LRTCs are assumed to increase as a function of the E/I ratio (and then decrease on the supercritical side). For the aperiodic component, in contrast, a higher value of the 1/f exponent reflects a steeper slope, i.e. less “background” activity in higher frequencies, which has sometimes been seen as indicative of a lower E/I ratio. 

However, previous work has already indicated that this simple assumption about the aperiodic exponent may hold true only under certain conditions3. We plan to conduct further and more thorough analysis to better understand these results, in the hope that our findings may open the door to a more refined understanding of oscillatory dynamics in the brain. Specifically, we are planning to further look at regional variation in APF and 𝛽 and their inter-relatedness and relationships to LRTCs, PS, AC, and pACF at the parcel-level in these datasets. 

Further, our results from the analysis of the MDD dataset confirm that APF and 𝛽 are clinically relevant and show potential as biomarkers and for personalized medicine approaches. Further, they emphasize the validity and importance of our previous approach to finding neurobiological subtypes of MDD5. While the whole MDD cohort did not differ significantly from the control cohort in both metrics, significant group effects were observed when comparing the subtypes which we had identified in another study5, further confirming that depression is a highly varied disorder and identification of neurobiological subtypes is crucial to better understand this disorder. In upcoming analyses, we plan to further evaluate if inter-individual variability in APF and 𝛽 is related to the individual symptoms of depression that were assessed in this dataset.
