Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is increasingly recognized as a disorder involving large-scale network dysconnection, yet the macrostructural architecture of white matter (WM) and its molecular determinants remain poorly understood. Diffusion-based studies have revealed abnormalities across major WM pathways, but these measures capture only limited biological properties and are vulnerable to methodological variability1,2. White matter morphometric similarity networks (WM-MSNs), derived from T1-weighted MRI, provide an individualized and reproducible framework for quantifying macrostructural similarity across WM regions3. However, the network-level alterations of WM-MSN in MDD and their transcriptional underpinnings remain unknown. This study integrates large-scale neuroimaging and transcriptomic profiling to delineate WM-MSN disruptions in MDD and identify associated molecular pathways across genes, cell types, and developmental windows.

Methods

Structural T1-weighted MRI data were obtained from the REST-meta-MDD Consortium4, comprising 1,300 MDD patients and 1,128 healthy controls (HCs) across 25 sites. After rigorous quality control—excluding participants with missing clinical data, poor image quality, age <18 years, or sites with small sample sizes—1,154 MDD patients and 1,026 HCs remained for analysis. Demographic and clinical variables were compared using chi-square tests, t-tests, or Mann–Whitney U tests as appropriate. T1-weighted images were preprocessed using DPARSF, SPM8, and VBM8 (http://dbm.neuro.unijena.de/vbm), followed by DARTEL normalization to MNI space. White matter volume (WMV) maps were parcellated into 48 regions based on the JHU WM atlas. For each region, voxel-wise WMV distributions were transformed into probability density functions, and Jensen–Shannon divergence was used to compute inter-regional similarity, yielding individualized 48×48 WM-MSN matrices. Similarity scores were ComBat-harmonized to control for site effects.

Network topology was quantified using global measures (γ, normalized clustering coefficient; λ, normalized path length; σ, small-worldness; Eg, global efficiency; Eloc, local efficiency) and nodal properties (DC, degree centrality; BC, betweenness centrality; NE, nodal efficiency) across a sparsity range of 0.09–0.30 (interval was 0.02). Area-under-the-curve values were used for statistical analyses. Group differences in edge-level connectivity were assessed with the network-based statistic (NBS; P < 0.001, 10,000 permutations). Global and nodal metrics were compared using two-sample t-tests with age, sex, and years of education as covariates. Statistical significance was defined as FDR-adjusted P < 0.05. To assess clinical relevance, partial correlation analyses were performed between HAMD-17 depression severity scores and (a) the connectivity strength of NBS-identified edges, (b) global small-world attributes showing significant group differences, and (c) nodal metrics of regions exhibiting significant alterations. All correlations controlled for age, sex, and years of education, and significance was defined as FDR-adjusted P < 0.05.

The transcriptomic analysis used 15,633-gene microarray data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA, http://human.brain-map.org) database5. Following standard preprocessing of abagen toolbox (https://github.com/netneurolab/abagen), tissue samples were assigned to 48 WM regions, yielding expression profiles for 40 regions with available data. Partial least squares regression (PLS-R) examined spatial associations between gene expression and regional WM alterations (case–control differences in BC, DC, NE). Spatial autocorrelation was addressed with BrainSMASH null models (1,000 permutations). Significant genes (FDR < 0.05) were identified via bootstrap-derived Z-weights. Functional enrichment was performed using Gene Ontology, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways, cell-type specificity analysis, and spatiotemporal expression analysis. The overall study design and workflow are illustrated in Figure 1.

Results

At the edge level, NBS revealed two significant dysconnected components (both P < 0.001): one comprising 118 hyperconnected edges across 46 WM regions, and another comprising 45 hypoconnected edges across 35 regions. Globally, both groups showed typical small-world architecture, but MDD patients exhibited significant deviations, including higher γ, σ, Eg, and Eloc, and reduced λ (all FDR-corrected P < 0.05). At the nodal level, widespread alterations were observed across major associative and commissural tracts. Differences were detected in 9 regions for BC, 9 for DC, and 16 for NE. Affected regions included the corpus callosum, tapetum, cingulum, and uncinate fasciculus—tracts central to emotional regulation, interhemispheric integration, and fronto-limbic communication (Figure 2). Several nodal metrics correlated with HAMD-17 scores, suggesting links between localized WM disruptions and depressive symptom severity. Specifically, the BC of the right superior cerebellar peduncle (FDR-adjusted P = 0.007, r = 0.084), as well as the NE of the left uncinate fasciculus (FDR-adjusted P = 0.050, r = 0.061) and the right tapetum (FDR-adjusted P = 0.028, r = 0.069), were positively associated with HAMD-17 scores.

PLS-R revealed that PLS2 explained 23.0% of variance and was significantly associated with MDD-related nodal alterations (Pperm = 0.001). A total of 2,393 genes (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05) contributed significantly, forming PLS2+ (672 up-weighted) and PLS2− (1,721 down-weighted) groups. PLS2+ genes showed enrichment for oligodendrocyte-related cell types, lipid and sterol biosynthesis, axonal development, cytoskeletal remodeling, and vascular morphogenesis—implicating disrupted myelination and membrane metabolism. Spatiotemporal analyses indicated broad developmental expression across cortical, limbic, and subcortical regions. Conversely, PLS2− genes were enriched for mitochondrial and Golgi function, transcriptional regulation, ER stress, and oxidative processes, reflecting vulnerabilities in cellular homeostasis and metabolic resilience.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that MDD is characterized by widespread disruptions in WM-MSNs across edges, global topology, and nodal architecture. Integrating neuroimaging with transcriptomics revealed two complementary molecular pathways: myelination- and lipid-related genes contributing to WM reorganization, and stress- and metabolism-related genes compromising network stability. These results bridge macrostructural WM abnormalities with microscale molecular processes, advancing mechanistic understanding of MDD and identifying potential biological targets for intervention.
