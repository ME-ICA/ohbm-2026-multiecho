Introduction

Mastery of language is one of the chief abilities which set human beings apart from other animals. Accordingly, the quest to map language onto the brain has been a long-standing endeavour. Over the past decades, advanced techniques have revealed that previously established models were too simplistic, demonstrating that vast regions of the brain, well beyond the classical ones, are relevant for language, giving way to what might be termed a 'localisational nihilism'1. This raises the question: Is there anything specific in the human brain at all that defines or enables language? 

The present study was also motivated by two unresolved issues. Current accounts treat syntax and semantics as separate domains, without specifying how they interact. Second, although the anterior temporal lobe (ATL) is consistently implicated in semantic processing, it occupies a marginal position in most models. We hypothesized that linguistic abilities depend on the integrity of a structural brain network which includes, on the one hand, hubs necessary for the integration of syntax and semantics, and on the other, connections to the ATL.

Studying the brain's connections may be more effective in advancing our understanding how it supports higher-level functions than examining cortical parcellations. In addition, relating patients’ symptoms to affected tracts may improve understanding the impairments. Most current concepts superimpose lesion masks onto templates of known tracts and calculate their affection. However, canonical tracts cover only a minor part of all fibres. Furthermore, assignment of streamlines to tracts is arbitrary and follows no convention. Lastly, a single tract can be affected more than once in its course. We therefore developed a new technique, streamline based lesion symptom mapping (SLSM), relating aphasia severity in acute stroke to disruption of individual streamlines in a whole-brain analysis.

Methods

From a prospective stroke registry (the Freiburg large-scale project) comprising over 1,000 patients, who were carefully examined within one week of a first-ever stroke caused by a single ischaemic infarct in the middle cerebral artery (MCA) territory on MRI, we selected 208 patients with aphasia following a left-hemispheric stroke (72 women; mean age 63.5 ± 14.5 years; NIHSS score on admission 9.0 ± 6.0). All patients were assessed using the Aachen Aphasia Test (AAT).

We included a diffusion weighted sequence (voxel size = 1.8 × 1.8 × 5 mm³), a FLAIR sequence (voxel size = 0.94 × 0.94 × 5.00 mm³, 23 slices) and an anatomical high-resolution isotropic T1-weighted MPRAGE (voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³) for the normalization procedure. Lesion delineations using a customized region-of-interest toolbox implemented in SPM8 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm8) were further refined manually. Nonlinear normalization into the stereotactic Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space (ICBM152 2009b asymmetric) was obtained using the DARTEL approach. Normative dMRI and rsfMRI data were drawn from the Human Connectome Project (see 2 for further methods). 

Streamline based lesion symptom mapping (SLSM) begins with the construction of a normative connectome using global tracking (GT) on a DWI sequence across healthy HCP participants 3. GT avoids the biases inherent in predefined start- and end-points or binary tract definitions. The normative structural connectome, derived by GT is used to calculate on the streamline level the relative overlap with the lesion mask of each subject in the study, expressed in percent. The approach generates a matrix of streamlines for each patient. Then, statistics are based on the streamline level. Our approach is comparable to previous approaches4 as it indirectly estimates the degree of structural disconnection in patients. However, by using GT, our approach is truly data driven, on the streamline level. The global tracking is performed only once prior to any statistical analyses, and fibre selection takes place in a predefined normative connectome, which is less time-consuming. We used the ‘sparse’ fibre preset setting, resulting in approx. 50,000 streamlines. A whole-connectome, streamline-level regression was then performed, modelling aphasia severity—operationalised as the subtest performance profile level h (a reliability-weighted average of T-norm subtest scores) from the AAT. The resulting disconnectome map identified streamlines whose disconnection was significantly associated with aphasia severity across the cohort.

We aimed to characterise anatomically the streamlines highlighted in the disconnectome map. To this end, we used the normative connectome to delineate established white-matter tracts and correlated these with the streamlines identified in the aphasia-severity disconnectome map. To identify cortical regions critical for language, we searched for shared cortical terminations of the affected tracts. We applied a relatively novel technique, streamline conjunction, to determine the shared cortical endpoints of these tracts, hereafter referred to as “hubs”. GT enables the extension of streamlines to cortical regions along the pathway of the fibres' terminal segments, resulting in cortical termination maps. Cortical conjunction maps may then be generated by computing the voxel-wise product of terminal densities from various streamline bundles at the individual level, subsequently transformed into the MNI group space2. Finally, we used the functional connectivity data from the normative connectome between the anatomical hub regions as input for hierarchical agglomerative cluster analysis of functional connectivity strengths, as well as for a network analysis.

Results

SLSM, using the profile level of the AAT as a global indicator of aphasia severity, resulted in three large bundles of association fibres within the left hemisphere and some transcallosal streamlines (Fig.). Two fibre bundles connect the frontal and posterior lobes, above (“dorsal”) and below (“ventral”) the sylvian fissure, while a dense cluster of streamlines behind the central sulcus interlinks the temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes. Some streamlines traverse interhemispherically via the anterior commissure and corpus callosum, extending towards the contralesional inferior frontal gyrus and temporal lobe. 

Visual inspection of the association fibres indicated that one bundle might belong to the arcuate fasciculus (AF) and superior longitudinal fascicles (SLF), another to a continuum of fibres within the extreme capsule (EmC) associated with the uncinate fasciculus (UF), temporo-frontal extreme capsule fasciculus (TFecF), and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF). Additionally, the streamlines included fibres connecting the parietal and occipital lobes with the temporal lobes reminiscent of the middle longitudinal fasciculus (MdLF)5. To substantiate these observations, we delineated the recognised tracts within the normative connectome. The analysis revealed that the SLSM streamlines associated with aphasia severity are comprehensively represented by three tract systems, explaining 88% of the affected streamlines: i: dorsal pathway ii: ventral pathway and iii:  MdLF. The affected streamlines not explained by the normal connectome map can be divided into two groups. Some follow the course of the MdLF but fall outside its designated mask, The other group includes more vertical fibres linking the inferior parietal cortex (IPC) to the posterior temporal lobe, aligning with MdLF fibres during their course. Remarkably, analysing the association of the individual subtest performance with the streamline damage resulted in a core set of dorsal, ventral and MDLF streamlines which affect the performance in all subtests when damaged.

The conjunction analysis of the affected dorsal and ventral bundles revealed one hub in the frontal lobe and one in the posterior lobes. The frontal hub is centred on the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG, areas 47/45; 44), extending into anterior middle frontal gyrus, anterior prefrontal operculum and anterior insula. The posterior hub is centred on the posterior MTG, extending into inferior and superior temporal gyrus, summarized as posterior temporal lobe (PTL) and into the ventro-anterior part of the inferior parietal lobule (TPJ, SMG, AG, TOTZ). Affected MdLF streamlines were found to converge with the dorsal pathway in IPL (angular gyrus and TOTZ) and a separate small region in the anterior STG (aSTG).  MdLF streamlines converged with the ventral pathway (UF fibres) in the anterior temporal lobe (ATL) (more specifically in the most anterior part of MTG), and in the occipital gyrus (OG). The hubs are defined as the common terminations of the three affected streamline bundles. However, the hubs also have other connections. The posterior regions are all interconnected by fibres aligning with MdLF fibres, pMTG is also connected to ATL via MdLF fibres, ATL is connected to IFG along EmC fibres.

The functional connectivity analyses differentiated two main groups of larger networks. The posterior regions, anatomically all behind the central sulcus form a cluster of strong functional connectivity. And the two dorsal/ ventral hub regions are strongly connected functionally despite their great anatomical distance. The hub in the occipital lobe (OG) as a result from the conjunction of the ventral pathway with the MdLF exhibits a more heterogenous functional connectivity pattern and may be attributed to the fact that the rsfMRI data in the HCP set were recorded with “eyes open”.

Conclusion

SLSM is a novel technique that maps whole-brain connectomes at the level of individual streamlines, without relying on any a priori tract definitions or regions of interest, allowing direct correlation with clinical variables.

We propose a new anatomical model explaining the architecture of the brain’s language network. Three long association tracts, the arcuate fasciculus, the extreme capsule fibres, and the middle longitudinal fasciculus conjugate in four classical language zones of the cerebral cortex: the inferior frontal gyrus, posterior temporal lobe, angular gyrus, and ATL. The structural and functional connectivity data suggest an organisation into two separate groups: 

  • IFG and PTL are hubs in the frontal and the posterior lobes, interconnected via dorsal and ventral pathways, the dual-loop. 
  • The association cortex regions of the posterior lobes are interconnected with one another and linked to the ATL via MdLF fibres. It is proposed to term this latter system the “caudal pathway”.

The “caudal pathway” is the anatomical backbone for semantics. It integrates information flow from modality-specific sources in more posterior areas to an amodal “semantic hub” with the highest abstraction level in the anterior temporal lobe (ATL), the “hub-and-spoke model”. However, comprehension requires inference, and thus the frontal lobe. The caudal pathway is interwoven with the dual-loop through the ventral pathway. The ventral pathway needs to be intact for the subject to form connections between elements based on structure or meaning. The ventral pathway interacts with the caudal pathway to form and retrieve invariant abstract concepts (ATL) through analogy. In essence, comprehension arises through the integration of the ventral and caudal pathway. In the “dual loop system”, the ventral pathway’s hierarchical analysis, independent of the elements’ position is combined with the processing of sensory and motor sequences based on element order in the dorsal pathway for syntax. Syntax and semantics are intrinsically interwoven through the ventral pathway. Language uses this network to combine syntax with semantics.

If we define a network comprising of connections and interconnected brain regions, our model is the only recent anatomical language network, derived from patient data, directly combining streamlines of long association tracts and cortical regions, the hardware explaining the structural relations between the cortical language regions.

All language function is grounded in this network; it is truly essential, as its lesioning leads to aphasia, and all functions assessed in the subtests of the AAT depend upon it. The main differences between the subtests lie in additional task requirements, such as executive functions or visual processing, and the corresponding involvement of brain structures such as the frontal aslant tract or the inferior longitudinal fasciculus. Language is embedded within other functions, including cognition, social interaction, and emotion. The dual-loop hubs connect the language network with the medial structures, to emotion and biographical memory, forming the brain’s meta-loop2. 

Comparison with other cognitive functional networks, such as the multiple-demand network or the default mode network, revealed a high degree of similarity, as reflected by substantial DICE scores2. All of these cognitive networks can be interpreted as hubs of the major long association tracts. Thus, language is not merely embedded within cognition; rather, the language network itself forms an integral component of the broader cognitive networks. It follows that this anatomical framework is not specific to language alone. We contend that it subserves a wide range of cognitive functions. The essential language network is therefore not designed exclusively for language, but can be recruited to produce and understand language.
