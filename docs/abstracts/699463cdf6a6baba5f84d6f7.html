Introduction

Human memory is reconstructive. The phenomenon where an individual's memory of an original event is altered due to exposure to misinformation following the event is known as the misinformation effect ^1. The misinformation experiment comprised three stages. First, participants witnessed an event. Then, they were exposed to misinformation. Finally, they underwent a memory test on what they had seen in the original event. If participants misattribute the post-event misinformation seen in the second stage to having occurred during the original event stage, they will form false memories. According to encoding specificity theory, memory retrieval is most efficient when the retrieved format matches the one that was encoded ^2. Previous behavioral studies have found that post-event information presented in image and text formats produces different effects ^3. Previous functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have examined the effects of misinformation in image or text form on eyewitnesses' memories of the original event ^4 ^5. However, no direct comparison has been made of how these two forms of post-event information trigger the neural reinstatement of events in eyewitnesses. Thus, this study used fMRI to obtain functional brain imaging data from two groups of participants when they viewed original events and post-event information (presented in image or text format). Then, all participants completed text-based recognition tests about the original events outside the scanner. For each group of participants, we examined the similarity in neural activity patterns between the original event stage and the post event information stage, and compared the differences in neural reinstatement between true and false memories. Finally, by comparing the two groups of participants, we investigated the impact of the format of post-event information on the neural reinstatement.

Methods

We recruited two groups of participants: one group was exposed to materials presented in image form, while the other group was exposed to materials presented in text form. Each group consists of 36 participants (20 females and 16 males per group). The participants' mean age was 22 ± 2 years. There were no significant differences in age or gender. All participants were right-handed Chinese college students with normal vision and no history of psychiatric or neurological disorders. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at State Key Laboratory of Cognitive Neuroscience and Learning at Beijing Normal University, China.

The study included three stages: the original event stage, the post-event information stage, and the recognition test stage. The first two stages were conducted inside the fMRI scanner, with a three-hour interval between them; the final stage was scheduled for the following day and performed outside the scanner. Structural and functional images were acquired using a 3.0T Siemens Prisma scanner. Participants in the image group viewed information presented in image form during both the original event stage and the post-event information stage. In contrast, participants in the text group viewed information presented in textual form during both stages. Both groups were ultimately required to complete a text-based recognition test based on the content they had seen during the original event stage.

This study used images and written descriptions of eight events as experimental materials. These materials were developed for our previous research ^5. The presentation procedure for the materials is consistent with our previous research. Each event consisted of 50 color images and 50 corresponding textual sentences. Each item is displayed only once, with each display lasting 3.5 seconds. Of the 50 items in each event, 12 were critical items and 12 were control items that would be tested later. The content of the critical items was inconsistent between the original event stage and the post-event information stage. Each critical item had two versions, and the two versions were balanced among participants. In contrast, the content of the other items remained consistent content across both stages.

The recognition test for each event contains 24 questions. Each question has three possible answers. For critical items, the options are original information (i.e., from the original stage), misinformation (i.e., from the post-event stage), and foil (i.e., neither from the original nor the post-event information stage). Selecting the original information for critical items is considered a true memory, while selecting misinformation is considered a false memory. For control items, the three options are: original information (i.e., from the original and post-event stages) and two foils (i.e., neither from the original nor the post-event stage). Selecting the original information for control items is considered a correct control, while selecting a foil is considered an incorrect control.

Regarding behavioral performance in the recognition test, we first examined the differences in endorsement rates between the two groups on critical items and control items. For the fMRI data, we calculated the degree of similarity between the neural activity patterns of the original event stage and the post-event information stage for each group of participants. Then, we compared the differences between true and false memories and between correct control and incorrect control. Finally, we compared the differences between the two groups of participants. We used the whole-brain searchlight analysis to identify brain regions that showed differences in neural representations (threshold of Z > 2.3).

Results

Based on the behavioral results of the recognition test, both groups exhibited a significant misinformation effect. For participants in the image group who viewed original and post-event information in image format, the recognition rate for misinformation was higher than that for foils on critical items (35% vs. 15%, p < 0.001). For participants in the text group who viewed original and post-event information in text format, the recognition rate for misinformation was higher than that for foils on critical items (35% vs. 12%, p < 0.001). Compared to the image group, participants in the text group demonstrated a higher rate of correct control on control items (64% vs. 55%, p < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of the recognition rate of original information or misinformation.

First, we performed the neural pattern similarity analysis on critical items for which the content was inconsistent between the original event stage and the post-event information stage. In the image group, neural activity patterns between the original event stage and the post-event information stage were more similar for true memory than for false memory, primarily in the bilateral ventrolateral occipital lobe, bilateral superior parietal lobe, and left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex. In the text group, neural activity patterns between the original event stage and the post-event information stage were more similar for true memory than for false memory, primarily in the left inferior parietal lobe, bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and right anterior temporal lobe. Group comparisons revealed greater neural pattern similarity for true memory than for false memory in the bilateral ventrolateral occipital cortex for the image group and in the left inferior parietal lobe for the text group.

Next, we performed the neural pattern similarity analysis on control items for which the content was consistent between the original event stage and the post-event information stage. In the image group, neural activity patterns between the original event stage and the post-event information stage were more similar for correct control than for incorrect control, primarily in the bilateral and medial parietal cortex and bilateral middle frontal gyrus. In the text group, neural activity patterns between the original event stage and the post-event information stage were more similar correct control than for incorrect control, primarily in the left middle temporal gyrus and bilateral dorsolateral occipital cortex. Group comparisons revealed greater neural pattern similarity for correct control than for incorrect control in the right lateral prefrontal cortex for the image group and in the cingulate gyrus for the text group.

Conclusion

The behavioral results of the recognition test indicate that when the textual content of the original event and the post-event information are consistent, people are more likely to remember the textual content than the image content. However, when the textual content of the original event and the post-event information are contradictory, people are more likely to remember information from both stages and confused. Previous fMRI studies showed that, when original information is presented as images and post-event information is presented as text, neural reinstatements of the original event in the ventrolateral occipital cortex when reading post-event information can predict true memory after exposure to inaccurate post-event information, while those in the inferior parietal cortex can predict correct control after exposure to accurate post-event information ^5. The current study showed that, when both original and post-event information is presented as image, neural reinstatements of the original event in the ventrolateral occipital cortex when viewing post-event information can predict true memory after exposure to inaccurate post-event information, while those in the lateral prefrontal cortex can predict correct control after exposure to accurate post-event information. However, when both original and post-event information is presented as text, neural reinstatements of the original event in the inferior parietal cortex when reading post-event information can predict true memory after exposure to inaccurate post-event information, while those in the cingulate gyrus can predict correct control after exposure to accurate post-event information. In sum, when the presentation format of the original event and the post-event information is identical but their content differs, people's reinstatement of the original event images occurs in the visual cortex, while reinstatement of original event texts occurs in the inferior parietal lobe. However, when the presentation format and content of the original event and the post-event information are identical, people's reinstatement of the original event images occurs in the lateral frontal region, while reinstatement of the original event texts occurs in the medial frontal region. These findings supported and expanded upon the encoding specificity and sensory reactivation theories.
