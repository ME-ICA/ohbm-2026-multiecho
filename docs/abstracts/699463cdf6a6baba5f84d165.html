Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a heterogeneous psychiatric disorder. Childhood trauma (CT) represents a major risk factor for MDD and is associated with earlier onset, increased symptom severity, and poorer treatment outcomes1. CT is increasingly recognized as a transdiagnostic risk factor that shapes emotion regulation processes across both clinical and non-clinical populations2. Neuroimaging studies indicate that CT is linked to altered emotion regulation circuitry, particularly involving heightened salience network reactivity and disrupted prefrontal control. However, most prior work has predominantly focused on negative emotion processing and has treated CT as a unitary construct, despite evidence for distinct deprivation- and threat-related subtypes3. Whether CT differentially modulates emotion regulation of negative and positive emotions, and whether distinct CT subtypes reflecting deprivation and threat exert separable neural effects in adults with MDD, remains insufficiently understood. Clarifying these mechanisms may help explain heterogeneity in depression and identify trauma-informed neural markers of emotion regulation dysfunction.

Methods

Data were obtained from the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety (NESDA)4. The sample comprised 89 individuals with a lifetime diagnosis of MDD, including both currently remitted and currently depressed participants; about 87% of them had a comorbid lifetime diagnosis of an anxiety disorder. In addition, 37 healthy controls with no lifetime psychiatric history were included. Childhood trauma was assessed using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ). Based on validated CTQ cut-off criteria, participants were classified into the CT+ group (moderate or greater exposure on at least one CTQ subscale, n = 53) or the CT− group (no trauma exposure across all subscales, n = 33). Trauma subtypes were further defined as a deprivation-only group (moderate or greater emotional or physical neglect; n = 21), a threat-only group (moderate or greater emotional, physical, or sexual abuse; n = 4), and a combined deprivation and threat group (co-occurring neglect and abuse; n = 28). Due to the insufficient sample size, the threat-only group was excluded from subtype analyses. Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (IDS), cognitive reactivity to sad mood using the Leiden Index of Depression Sensitivity (LEIDS), and anxiety symptoms using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI). Behavioral indices of emotion regulation were derived from self-reported mood ratings obtained during an fMRI emotion regulation task. Group differences were first examined using two-group comparisons between CT+ and CT− groups. Subsequently, three-group comparisons were performed to assess subtype-specific effects by directly contrasting the combined deprivation and threat, deprivation-only, and CT− groups. The emotion regulation task involved passive viewing of negative, positive, and neutral images, as well as instructed cognitive reappraisal to downregulate negative emotion and upregulate positive emotion. Whole-brain analyses focused on contrasts of negative attend versus baseline, positive attend versus baseline, downregulation versus negative attend, and upregulation versus positive attend. At the group level, main effects of CT presence and CT subtype were examined while controlling for age and sex. Results were thresholded at voxel-level p<0.001 with cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction at p<0.05. Analyses were first conducted in the full sample to assess transdiagnostic effects of CT presence, and were subsequently repeated in the MDD group to factor out effects related to the (lifetime) presence of depression. In this subsample, MDD participants were classified into a CT+ group (n = 48) or a CT− group (n = 16). Trauma subtypes were further defined as a deprivation-only group (n = 25), a threat-only group (n = 4), and a combined deprivation and threat group (n = 19). Due to the insufficient sample size, the threat-only group was again excluded from subtype analyses.

Results

In the combined sample of participants with a lifetime diagnosis of MDD and healthy controls (Figure 1), the CT+ group reported higher cognitive reactivity (U = 318, p < .001), greater depression severity (U = 398, p < .001) , greater anxiety severity (U = 414, p < .001), and lower positive mood following both positive task conditions (positive attend: U = 1125.5, p = .026; positive regulate: U = 1117, p = .032) compared with the CT− group.

A significant subgroup subgroup effect was also observed for mood ratings following positive emotion regulation (H(2) = 6.20, p = .045). Post hoc pairwise comparisons with adjusted p values indicated that this effect was driven specifically by the deprivation+threat group, which reported significantly lower positive mood compared with the CT− group (p = .041). No significant differences were observed between the CT− and deprivation-only groups (p = .53) or between the two CT exposure subgroups (p = 1.00).

At the neural level, during negative emotion regulation (negative regulation > negative attend), the CT+ group showed significantly lower activation than the CT− group in the left posterior parietal cortex and the precuneus. No significant effects were observed in other task conditions, and no subgroup-related differences in neural activation were detected.

Restricted to individuals with MDD (Figure 2), the CT+ group showed higher cognitive reactivity (U = 238, p = .024) and greater anxiety severity (U = 244.5, p = .031) compared with the CT− group. At the neural level, the CT+ group exhibited lower activation in the bilateral posterior parietal cortex and the precuneus during negative emotion regulation. In contrast, no significant group differences were observed in depression severity, mood ratings following any task conditions, or neural activation during other emotion regulation or attention conditions.

Significant subgroup effects were found for cognitive reactivity (F(2, 57) = 3.30, p = .044), depression severity (F(2, 57) = 4.91, p = .011), and anxiety severity (H(2) = 6.08, p = .048). Higher depression and anxiety severity was restricted to the deprivation-only group, whereas cognitive reactivity was only higher in the deprivation+threat group. No differences across CT exposure subgroups were observed for mood ratings and brain activation during the emotion regulation task.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that CT is associated with altered neural mechanisms of negative emotion regulation, suggesting decreased recruitment of resources to sustain representational and self-referential processes, given that the precuneus is a core hub of the default mode network (DMN), whose dysfunction has been implicated in impaired emotional regulation5. Although no trauma-related neural effects were detected during the regulation of positive emotions, trauma exposure was associated with subjective mood ratings following positive emotion processing. This dissociation between behavioural and neural findings indicates that other processes than upregulation of positive affect may underpin lower positive mood reactivity. Furthermore, our results provide no support for specific mechanistic underpinnings of effortful mood regulation resulting from either deprivation-only or deprivation+threat. This may be due to the overlap between the two subgroups, as both have experienced deprivation, although the deprivation score is higher in the deprivation+threat group than in the deprivation-only group. In addition, the relatively small sample size in the subgroup analysis may have limited the statistical power and thus constrained the results.
