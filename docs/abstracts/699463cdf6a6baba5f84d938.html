Introduction

Emerging evidence suggests that glioblastoma (GBM) co-opts the structural and functional architecture of the brain to sustain its growth and invasion1,2. As such, GBM should not be considered as an isolated histopathological entity but as a network disease that evolves within and perturbs the brain connectome. Hence, GBM evolution cannot be fully understood without considering the underlying topology of brain connectivity. White matter tracts, which form the backbone of structural connectivity, represent preferential pathways for tumor invasion. As GBM cells infiltrate along these tracts, they distort and rewire the host connectome, revealing a hidden layer of disease complexity that links the topology of involved connectivity to clinical heterogeneity in symptoms, treatment response, and survival. In fact, recent work3 has taken an indirect approach using normative models of white matter to avoid tumor-induced distortions in the reconstructed fiber tractograms4 to prove how the structural interactions between GBMs and the white matter shape patient prognosis. However, although successful, that approach assumed an oversimplified numerical complexity of the relationship between the tumors and the white matter. Here, rather than attempting to reduce the information into a single marker, we fully embrace the idea that the brain-glioma circuitry contains sufficient information on its own to inform and distinguish survival trajectories. 

Methods

Imaging and clinical data: A total of 867 patients diagnosed with glioblastoma (GBM) according to the WHO 2021 guidelines were included in this study. Patients underwent multimodal MRI (i.e., T1, T1 contrast-enhanced, T2, and FLAIR) that allowed for accurate tumor segmentation masks. Clinical data consisted of overall survival (OS) measured in days from diagnosis, IDH1 mutation type, MGMT promoter status, extent of resection (EOR), age, and sex. We used the T2 images to normalize all images to a common MNI 1.5 mm isotropic resolution, masking the pathological tissue to avoid warping in abnormal brain tissue areas3. Following these steps, using a normative tractogram of 12M streamlines, we extracted the subset of fiber tracts intersecting the tumor and computed the associated tract density maps (i.e., number of streamlines traversing a given voxel). With this procedure, we obtained a patient-specific set of white matter tracts that were indirectly involved with the GBM, thus providing a circuit-based perspective of GBMs.

Spatial factors of structural neuron-GBM interactions: For all patients, tract density maps of white matter-GBM interactions were flattened and masked for empty voxels outside the brain tissue, z-scored, and used to calculate the first 100 Principal Components (PCs). Subsequently, we backprojected each component to the MNI template to study both common and individual patterns of spatial involvement in our sample. 

Survival analysis: We used Linear and Cox Proportional Hazard models to investigate the relationship between the circuitry of human GBM and patient prognosis. Baseline models included common clinical covariates such as sex, age, MGMT promoter status, and EOR. More specifically, we evaluated the added value of each PC to the statistical accuracy by sequentially adding the components to the set of predictors. We computed the concordance index (C-index) as a measure of the improved model capabilities in ranking survival outcomes. Both linear and Cox models were fit using L1 and L2-regularization schemes to account for correlations between multiple predictors. PCs were ranked based on their individual correlations with OS to study whether patient prognosis was decodable from individual spatial modes or rather the entire white matter circuitry.

Results

More than 50% of the variance in the involvement of white matter-GBM interactions could be explained with 3 PCs, but the 90% threshold was reached with no less than 50 spatial components, highlighting that individual reconstruction required the combination of heterogeneous spatial patterns (Fig. 1a). Tumors tended to involve ipsilateral white matter pathways supported by either anterior or posterior cross-hemispheric connectivity but not both simultaneously (Fig. 1b). When tested individually, PCs showed weak correlations with OS and only marginally improved prognosis estimation. Including multiple PCs simultaneously in Cox models improved predictive performance (C-index=0.7215) with respect to a model with only clinical covariates (C-index=0.669), as reflected by a progressive increase in the C-index with each additional component. Yet, the hazard ratios of most individual PCs remained small and not statistically significant, indicating that no single axis of connectivity involvement was sufficient to explain survival differences on its own (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, a connectivity-based model without common clinical covariates cannot account for a successful ranking of survival effects (C-index~0.65; Fig. 1d). Permutation tests, on the specific order of appearance in the sequential inclusion of PCs to the model, revealed that, although the sequence mattered, the final C-index was increased as more components were added, reaching similarly high scores regardless of the order in which components were included presented (Fig. 1e). 

Conclusion

This apparent paradox, improved global prediction but non-significant individual predictors, leads us to hypothesize that the prognostic signal is distributed across components, with each contributing subtly to a cumulative effect. These findings highlight a key principle: prognostic information in glioblastoma connectivity is distributed and cumulative. It cannot be captured by the involvement of a single tract or the dominance of a single PC but rather emerges from the integrated contribution of multiple connectivity features. Notwithstanding these preliminary findings, future work should address the robustness of this approach with external validation datasets and more sophisticated survival models designed for large numbers of covariates.
