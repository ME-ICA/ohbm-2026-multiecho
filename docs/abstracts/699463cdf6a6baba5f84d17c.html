Introduction

Alcohol use disorder concerns around 3 million people in Germany alone, with detrimental effects to health, mental well-being, and social functioning. With conventional treatment strategies, around two-thirds of patients relapse within six months.1 Individuals with AUD often struggle to suppress urges to drink and experience craving, both leading to an increased risk of relapse. As an add-on treatment, transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is implemented in this study population as it may enhance inhibitory control and reduce craving.2 More specifically, anodal stimulation of the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) could lead to decreased craving and lower rates of relapse compared to sham stimulation, but findings are heterogeneous.2 The DLPFC plays a key role in regulating craving and inhibitory control. Structural abnormalities in the prefrontal cortex of substance dependent individuals were observed, like reduced grey matter volume and density in the prefrontal cortex.3 These abnormalities translate into impairments of inhibitory control and are examined with electroencephalography (EEG) and event-related potentials (ERP) in individuals with AUD. ERPs can be generated during Go/NoGo tasks that require withholding responses. ERPs provide neurophysiological correlates for inhibitory control and may help evaluate the effects of tDCS on brain function.4 Previous studies have reported reduced NoGo N2 and P3, indicating altered response control in individuals with AUD.4 ERP markers have also been linked to craving and alcohol use severity. The hypotheses for the study are: anodal tDCS over the right DLPFC improves inhibitory control, thus increases NoGo P3 and NoGo N2 amplitudes, and reduces craving in comparison to sham stimulation, stimulation of the occipital cortex, inhibition training and sham stimulation.

Methods

Recently abstinent AUD patients, who voluntarily enter a qualified detoxification treatment program, were examined at the Department of Addictive Behaviour and Addiction Medicine at the Central Institute of Mental Health in Mannheim, Germany or the Clinic for Addiction Therapy and Withdrawal at the Psychiatric Center Nordbaden in Wiesloch, Germany. The first N=28 participants of this placebo-controlled single-blind study, in which participants do not receive any information about electrode placement or active vs. sham condition, were analyzed for piloting reasons. Subsequent to the randomized assignment (block randomisation with permuted blocks of size 12) to one of six groups, four groups received 20-minute tDCS sessions using the Sooma Duo™ device (Sooma Oy, Helsinki, Finland) over five consecutive days at an intensity of 2 mA (≈0.08 mA/cm²). The four groups are as follows: (1) anode over the right DLPFC (F4) and cathode over the left DLPFC (F3); (2) anode over the left DLPFC (F3) and cathode over the right DLPFC (F4); (3) anode over the occipital cortex (O1) with the cathode placed at Cz; and (4) a sham-stimulation control group. Group (5) received a computer-based inhibition training, group (6) followed treatment as usual. Eligible participants completed questionnaires and computer-based neuropsychological assessments. Participants received an EEG-recording with 32 channels during one part of the neuropsychological assessment, namely during a modified Go/No-Go paradigm including various working memory loads.5 The time points of assessment were T1, T5 (last day of treatment) and T8 (3 months post treatment). The EEG recordings utilized the BrainVision Recorder software package (32 channels with active electrodes; sampling rate 1000 Hz; Brain Products GmbH, Gilching, Germany). Using BrainVision Analyzer (BrainVision Analyzer, Version 2.2.0, Brain Products GmbH, Gilching, Germany), EEG data was resampled to 250 Hz, filtered, and re-referenced. Following artifact correction using independent component analysis (ICA), the data was segmented into epochs and baseline-corrected. N2 was defined as the negative peak at 150-300 ms post-stimulus, and P3 as the positive peak at 350-600 ms. Two channels were occupied by the tDCS electrodes (for groups 1,2 and 4: F3 and F4; for group 3: Cz and O1).  The primary outcomes of NoGo P3 and NoGo N2 amplitudes were analyzed using a repeated-measure ANOVA and linear regression models.

Results

At baseline, significant NoGo P3 and NoGo N2 differences between Go and NoGo trials confirmed their role as reliable markers of inhibition. Conscious, subjective craving and greater alcohol use severity significantly predicted smaller NoGo P3, whereas automated craving significantly predicted larger NoGo P3. Concerning T5, there were visual electrophysiological distinctions between group 2 and other groups in both ERP components after one week of respective treatment. This suggests gains in inhibitory control through tDCS especially in the second treatment group receiving anodal stimulation over the left DLPFC.

Conclusion

Baseline and T5 analyses showed robust Go/NoGo ERP effects and early tDCS-related gains in inhibitory control, particularly with left-DLPFC stimulation. These results demonstrate the effects of tDCS on EEG markers of inhibitory control and craving. As feasibility was successfully demonstrated, the main study will include N = 162 individuals.
