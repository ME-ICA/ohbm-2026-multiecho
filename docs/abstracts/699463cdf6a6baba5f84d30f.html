Introduction

Rumination is a core cognitive process in major depressive disorder (MDD), yet most research relies on self-report measures that capture trait-level tendencies rather than the actual behavior occurring during psychotherapy. Recent work introduced an in-session rumination observational measure that showed strong reliability and consistent associations with depression severity1, 2. However, the neural correlates of this behaviorally observed rumination remain unknown.

Methods

Using data from the PReDICT (Predictors of Remission in Depression to Individual and Combined Treatments) study3, we included 22 participants with treatment-naïve MDD who completed resting-state fMRI at baseline and at week 12, along with video-recorded CBT sessions rated for in-session rumination. Baseline sessions corresponded to the scan at baseline, and end-of-treatment sessions corresponded to the last available session near the scan at week 12. In-session rumination was quantified using a validated observational coding system. Raters assessed negative, repetitive, and passive discussion of ruminative topics within 10-minute segments, and rumination duration was scored on a five-point scale representing the proportion of session time spent ruminating. Segment scores were averaged to yield a session-level score. Functional connectivity (FC) was examined for the subcallosal cingulate cortex (SCC), hippocampus, dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (DMPFC), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), anterior insula, and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). These regions were selected based on prior evidence linking them to depression-related networks, including the affective, salience, executive control, and default mode networks4. Baseline associations were tested using a generalized linear model (GLM) to predict FC from rumination duration, adjusting for age, sex, and baseline depression severity (HDRS-17). Longitudinal models assessed whether changes in FC were associated with changes in rumination duration while adjusting for age, sex, and changes in depression severity.

Results

At baseline, longer in-session rumination duration was associated with decreased FC in SCC-superior frontal cortex and PCC-DMPFC; inversely, rumination duration was positively associated with FC in DMPFC-anterior insula, hippocampus-S1/IPL, and anterior insula-precuneus. Overall, these patterns reflect decreased within-DMN connectivity, loss of anticorrelation between DMN and salience/executive control networks and increased subcortical–cortical connectivity. Longitudinally, reductions of in-session rumination duration were associated with strengthened connectivity in PCC-angular gyrus, and decreased connectivity in hippocampus-inferior frontal gyrus.

Conclusion

Rumination duration was related to patterns of within- and between-network connectivity involving the default mode, salience, and executive control networks. These results support the utility of in-session observational measures to capture clinically meaningful cognitive processes and indicate that such behavioral measures have identifiable neural correlates. Overall, the study suggests potential targets for intervention and a mechanistic understanding of cognitive processes in MDD.
