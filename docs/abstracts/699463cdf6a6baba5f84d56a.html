Introduction

Humans constantly interpret others’ emotions from subtle, fluctuating social signals. Incorrect inferences, also known as “empathic error,” have social costs ranging from minor misunderstandings, to intense conflict, and social isolation. A better understanding of how the brain forms these inferences can lead to clinical and technical innovations that improve human communication and increase empathy. Empathic inference is a complex process, therefore, here we investigate its neural mechanism by applying tools from complexity science to a pre-existing naturalistic fMRI dataset.

Methods

In this fMRI dataset1 (3T GE; TR = 2 s, 46 slices at a 2.9 mm thickness, voxel size = 2.9mm3), 100 observers (59 Women, 37 Men, 4 No Response, average age = 25.23 STD = 9.96) inferred the emotions of people (targets) describing true life events on video. There were 24 videos (19 unique targets or storytellers), each from the Stanford Emotional Narratives Dataset (SENDv12). Due to counterbalancing of cue-conditions in the original study, approximately one third of observers saw the same exact stimuli. The present preliminary analysis is restricted to a single video stimulus that was viewed identically by 33 participants to allow for a direct comparison of neural responses across observers. Stories were transcribed and LLM (Llama 3.1) was used to derive estimates of narrative “surprise" or linguistic entropy for each token (word; Eq. 1). By using LLMs to quantify word-level surprise in narratives, we can operationalize the moment-to-moment predictability of linguistic input as experienced by listeners, providing a standardized metric for the informational structure of communication.

Observer accuracy, the root-mean-squared error (RMSE) between target self-ratings and observer inference ratings, was measured, and observer fMRI data were spatially aggregated to 274 brain regions from the Brainnetome Atlas. From the timeseries within these regions we extracted two information-theoretic features: (1) spectral noise, quantified as the 1/f exponent of BOLD power spectra (Eq. 2) and (2) Shannon entropy of the patterns of fluctuations in the fMRI signals. Specifically, we compute a variation of Shannon entropy using transition probabilities (Eq. 3). We then compared these metrics to individual differences in empathic accuracy via Pearson’s correlations.

Results

Preliminary results (N = 33) revealed that greater accuracy was linked to higher entropy in the medial temporal gyrus, a small portion of the temporoparietal junction (TPJ) and primary somatosensory cortex (S1), along with a steeper 1/f-noise slope across TPJ, S1, medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), and the visual cortex. In contrast, lower accuracy corresponded to reduced entropy in the insula and primary motor cortex (M1). Sudden peaks in narrative surprise quantified by Llama3.1 coincided with fluctuations in TPJ activity.

Conclusion

We are currently extending these analyses across the full dataset (N = 100) and will integrate narrative-surprise metrics from all 24 videos. Current preliminary fMRI patterns suggest richer and longer information processing within theory-of-mind (ToM) networks known to be involved in social cognition,3 while reduced entropy in M1, may reflect certainty in motor responses during ratings (i.e., button presses). Correlations among TPJ activity and narrative surprise, suggests the TPJ may track and exploit structured variability in dynamic social interactions. Together, these findings refine our understanding of TPJ function, supporting the view that it rapidly integrates multimodal information to update internal models during complex social inference and decision-making.
