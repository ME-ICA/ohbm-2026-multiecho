Introduction

Negative emotion contributes to problem drinking. Previous studies suggest that men and women may differ in the mechanisms of negative emotion processing and the pathophysiology of alcohol misuse. For instance, a substantial clinical literature suggests that women relative to men may be more vulnerable to the effects of negative emotions on drinking behavior and alcohol use severity. However, few have investigated whether or how men and women may engage different behavioral and neural processes during exposure to negative emotions and differ in the impact of alcohol misuse on these processes. It is of critical significance to characterize the behavioral and neural markers of negative emotion processing in relation to the severity of problem alcohol use. These findings may help in identifying drinkers at risk and in developing treatment tailored to sex and individual differences in vulnerability to dependent alcohol use.

Methods

We addressed this issue in a community sample of 148 drinkers (71 women) who were evaluated with Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT) and Spielberg State Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) as well as functional imaging during matching of negative and neutral emotional images in a Hariri task. In the Hariri task, participants matched negative and neutral images of the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) in one neutral, followed by two negative, and another neutral block. Each block started with a fixation period of 2 seconds, followed by 6 stimuli each lasting 6 seconds. The 6 stimuli were presented consecutively without an inter-stimuli gap. The blocks lasted approximately 152 seconds (~2.5 minutes). During imaging, subjects responded by pressing one of two buttons, allowing for the determination of accuracy and RT. Subjects were told that the stimuli would be presented long enough for them to make an accurate match but were not explicitly instructed to respond as fast as possible. This allowed us to assess the natural preferences and/or behavioral tendencies in emotion processing across subjects

Brain imaging data were acquired with 3T scanner and processed with published routines. All imaging findings were evaluated at a corrected threshold.

In the analyses of behavioral and brain imaging data, we followed the same analytic framework to identify sex differences in the impact of problem drinking severity, as indexed by AUDIT score, on 1) behavioral performance, specifically the difference in reaction time (RT) between negative and neutral trials - RT (Neg - Neu); and on 2) regional brain responses to the contrast of "Neg - Neu" using the following regression model, with age and individual anxiety, as indexed by the Spielberger Stat/Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) Trait score, as covariates of no interest :

RT (Neg-Neu)/voxel BOLD signals = AUDIT score + Sex (1= men, 2 = women) + [AUDIT score × Sex] + STAI score + age

For regional brain activities identified from the regression, we tested the hypotheses whether the regional activities reflect sex differences in RT (Neg - Neu) and/or support higher-level emotional processes of relevance to drinking behavior, as evalauted by the 42-item Inventory of Drinking Situations (IDS-42) Negative Emotion score.

Results

Behavioral performance
In the regression model to examine sex as a modulating factor in the impact of alcohol misuse on RT (Neg-Neu), we observed that, with STAI score and age accounted for, AUDIT score was associated with significantly longer RT (Neg-Neu) in women than in men (Sex × AUDIT Score: t = 2.93, p = 0.004). Women had shorter RTs than men at AUDIT = 0 (t = -3.05, p = 0.003). Thus, at low AUDIT scores, women showed shorter RTs than men; however, with AUDIT scores increasing, women’s RTs increased, though only at trend-level significance (t = 1.91, p = 0.058), whereas men’s RTs decreased significantly (t = -2.07, p = 0.041). Imaging data
Evaluated at voxel p< 0.001, uncorrected in an F test, men and women showed significant differences in the correlation with AUDIT scores in two clusters, each in the right middle frontal gyrus (MFG; x = 32, y = 39, z = 30, voxel Z = 4.60, 386 voxels) and left MFG (x = -32, y = 36, z = 30, voxel Z = 4.19, 174 voxels). These clusters are shown in Figure 2A.
    To examine the source of the sex differences, we performed linear regression of the β’s on AUDIT score with the same covariates for men and women separately. Both right and left MFG β showed a significant, positive correlation with AUDIT score in men (Right MFG: t = 3.70, p< 0.001; Left MFG: t = 2.36, p = 0.021) and a significant, negative correlation with AUDIT score in women (Right MFG: t = -2.43, p = 0.018; Left MFG: t = -3.53, p = 0.001). The differences in the regression slopes are visualized in Figure 2B (Right MFG: t = -4.18, p<0.001; Left MFG: t = -4.24, p<0.001). Thus, bilateral MFG responded to matching of negative vs. neutral images in positive correlation with the severity of problem drinking in men but in negative correlation in women.

Brain behavior relationship
We next evaluated whether bilateral MFG activities may support behavioral performance and/or clinical features of the participants. We tested two possibilities: 1) bilateral MFG activities support down-stream motor processes, in which case the β estimates – βLMFG and βRMFG, respectively, would be correlated with RTNeg-Neu; and 2) bilateral MFG activities may reflect higher-level cognitive processes in regulating negative emotions in link with the urge to drink. In this case, we used the IDs-42 negative emotion score as the dependent variable. We tested these two hypotheses separately for the left and right MFG. Since the results were identical for both MFG clusters, we combined the two clusters here for presentation:

1) RT (Neg-Neu = βMFG) + Sex + [βMFG × Sex] + STAI score + age
2) IDS-42 Negative Emotion score = βMFG + Sex + [βMFG × Sex] + STAI score + age

Neither the main effect of βMFG (t = 0.31, p = 0.754) or βMFG × Sex interaction effect (t = -0.85, p = 0.396) was significant, suggesting a role of bilateral MFG responses other than supporting the RT in matching negative vs. neutral images (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the second model showed that, with STAI score and age accounted for, MFG β was associated with significantly lower IDS-42 negative emotions score in women than in men (βMFG × Sex: t = -2.56, p= 0.012). Women and men did not differ in IDS-42 negative emotion score at MFG β = 0 (t = -1.20, p= 0.234). At low MFG activity levels, women had higher IDS-42 negative emotions score than men; however, with increasing MFG activity, women’s IDS-42 negative emotions score decreased (t = -2.31, p= 0.023) but men’s score increased, albeit not significantly (t = 1.21, p= 0.228). The data points in residuals and the regression lines are shown in Fig. 3B. Thus, AUDIT and IDS-42 negative emotion scores were positively correlated and both were negatively correlated with MFG activity in women. <Note that Figure 3 cannot be shown here, per Abstract guidelines>

Conclusion

By combining functional brain imaging and the Hariri task, we investigated sex differences in the behavioral and neural markers of negative emotion processing in relation to the severity of problem alcohol use. We observed that AUDIT scores were significantly and positively correlated with RT (Neg-Neu) in women but negatively, though not significantly, in men, with sex difference in the correlation confirmed by a slope test. We also observed significant sex differences in activities of bilateral middle frontal gyrus (MFG) during matching of negative vs. neutral images in correlation with AUDIT score. These findings confirmed sex differences in the behavioral and neural markers of negative emotion processing in link with the severity of problem drinking. Further, bilateral MFG activities were sex-differently associated with IDS-42 negative emotion score, with women showing higher activities in link with lower negative emotion score and men showing the opposite trend. Together, these findings highlight the importance in characterizing the behavioral and neural markers of negative emotion processing to fully understand its role in potentially sex-specific pathways to problem and dependent drinking.
