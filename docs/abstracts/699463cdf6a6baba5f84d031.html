Introduction

Hierarchically nested natural stimuli like movies can be structured based on the temporal duration of their different levels. Hence, scenes of a movie naturally take more time than the individual movie shots, that are nested within them [1]. Movie shots thereby are defined as a continuous run of frames, uninterrupted by a cut, i.e. abrupt change in visual scenery. Whereas movie scenes are classified by their location and time of day as specified in the screen play. Previous research demonstrated a range of temporal receptive windows (TRW) across the cortex, indicating that more temporally persistent segments of the stimulus, i.e. movie scenes, engage additional, high-level areas, while more transient stimuli only evoke activation in the primary sensory cortices [1]. More recent investigations however have shown that the activity patterns in higher-order cortical areas shift in accordance with the event-structure of a stimulus, suggesting that not only the temporal duration, but also the depicted content affects cortical engagement [2]. As temporal duration and complexity of the stimulus’ content are naturally confounded [1], no study so far attempted to disentangle the separate contributions of Duration and Content Complexity. In this study, we independently manipulated the Duration of a segment and its Content Complexity, by presenting frames extracted from movie Scenes or movie Shots for 4s, 12s or 36s. Using inter-subject correlation (ISC), we investigated the differences in cortical areas engaged by the levels of Duration and Content Complexity. Based on previous research [1,2], we suspected that both factors play distinct roles in shaping the processing of naturalistic stimuli, like movies. Since past results indicated an extension of both TRWs and processing complexity along the uni-to-multimodal gradient, we also investigated the effects of Duration and Content Complexity in regions of interest (ROIs) representing the visual processing hierarchy [2].

Methods

As stimuli, we used material from the movie “Forrest Gump” that was fully annotated as part of the StudyForrest project (https://studyforrest.org/). We selected Scenes and Shots from this movie, balancing the number of characters, emotional arousal of the characters and segment duration. For the frame-wise manipulation, we isochronically extracted different numbers of frames from each pre-selected movie segments. Frames in the experiment were presented at 3 FPS, i.e. we extracted 12, 36, or 108 frames, respectively, to create the three different levels of Duration (4s, 12s, 36s). Amongst these frames, we controlled for differences in low-level visual features. The two levels of Content Complexity (Scenes, Shots) and three Durations created six different conditions that were presented in a counterbalanced, block-wise manner to subjects in an fMRI experiment. To control familiarity with the movie material, all participants watched “Forrest Gump” two days prior to their fMRI session. We collected data of 48 healthy, right-handed participants (40 female, age = 22.52 ± 2.44 years). Functional MRI data was acquired with a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma MR scanner (TR/TE = 1500/30 ms; FA = 71°, FOV = 210 mm, 63 slices, slice thickness = 2.5 mm, acceleration factor = 3) in separate runs for each experimental block, supplemented by the acquisition of phase-inverted scans for fieldmap correction. In preparation of the ISC analysis, data were preprocessed including corrections for slice timing and field map deviation, realignment, normalization to MNI standard space, smoothing using a 6 x 6 x 6 mm³ Gaussian kernel and high-pass filtering at 0.01 Hz. The following nuisance regression removed variance related to movement and the top five components from the white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signal. The residuals from this nuisance regression entered the ISC analysis. We specifically opted for the pair-wise ISC approach that correlates the voxel-wise time course of each subject with every other subject. Applying this approach, we were able to model the ISC values within a linear mixed-effects modelling framework using crossed random effects of the pair of involved subjects to specifically consider the convoluted covariance structure of ISC data [3]. Within this framework we not only calculated the average ISC within each of our six experimental conditions but also compared the ISC between the conditions to estimate the specific effects for the different levels of Durations and Content Complexity. The whole-brain effects were corrected for multiple comparisons through simulated family-wise error rate-corrected cluster thresholds. Beyond the whole-brain results we also investigated the effects of Duration and Content Complexity in a ROI analysis along the visual processing hierarchy. In accordance with [2], the ROIs included the primary visual cortex (V1), an extrastriate visual area (hV4), the angular gyrus (AG), a posterior medial cluster of the default mode network (pmDMN) as well as the primary auditory cortex (A1) as an unrelated control area. Similarly to the whole-brain models, the ROI analysis was conducted using linear mixed-effects models under consideration of the crossed random effect of the subjects involved in each pair.

Results

Overall, the whole-brain analysis resulted in cortical engagement in large parts of the occipital and parietal lobes including the superior parietal lobe (SPL) and intraparietal sulcus (IPS), while extending to the temporal lobe along the superior temporal sulcus (STS) as well as to the frontal lobe specifically the middle frontal gyrus. Contrasting Scenes > Shots revealed areas belonging to the posterior visual scene network [4], including the occipital place area (OPA) in the middle occipital gyrus as well as the posterior parahippocampal place area (pPPA). Beyond the scene network, this comparison also localized engagement in the SPL, right IPS, right middle temporal gyrus (MTG) and left supramarginal gyrus (SMG) specifically for Scene stimuli. In contrast, the effects of longer compared with shorter Duration conditions identified areas belonging to the anterior visual scene network [4] comprised of the retrosplenial cortex (RSC), the anterior parahippocampal place area (aPPA) and the caudal intraparietal lobe (cIPL). Moreover, longer stimuli also additionally engaged the SPL, superior frontal gyrus (SFG) and right inferior frontal junction. Specifically investigating the interaction of higher Content Complexity, i.e. a movie Scenes, and longer Duration, demonstrated engagement within but also outside the visual scene network encompassing a set of high-order areas along the STS culminating in the temporal pole as well as the precuneus. The additional ROI analysis similarly showed an interaction effect of Duration and Content Complexity, as V1 did not distinguish between Scenes and Shots, while higher-order areas differed in their processing of longer Scenes from those of longer Shots.

Conclusion

This study is the first to independently manipulate the Duration and Content Complexity of hierarchically nested, naturalistic stimuli. Despite the frame-wise manipulation we were able to replicate the results of [1], who used continuous movie segments as their stimuli, validating our experimental paradigm. Contrasting the factors of Duration and Content Complexity we localized clusters of cortical engagement contained within the anterior and posterior scene network, which was previously related to the processing of static visual scenery, however more recently subdivided into functionally distinct anterior and posterior halves [4,5]. On the one hand, the posterior portion containing the OPA, pPPA, which we found specifically more engaged for movie Scenes than Shots irrespective of their Duration, was associated more closely with the presented visual input. In accordance with findings related to the individual areas of this subnetwork, the Scene-driven engagement likely suggests additional perceptual processing necessary to integrate across the multiple egocentric perspectives, i.e. camera shots, contained within a Scene. This interpretation is further supported by clusters from outside the visual scene network, as also the SPL, SMG and MTG were previously indicated for the processing of multiple egocentric perspectives [5]. On the other hand, with increasing Durations the movie stimuli engaged areas that belonged to the anterior visual scene network - specifically the aPPA, RSC and cIPL – which were all associated with assisting spatial navigation through the integration of content from memory [4]. Appropriately, all three areas as well as the moreover engaged SPL and SFG were previously linked to less visually focused and more imagery-based processes, suggesting that the integration from memory becomes more pronounced with longer stimulus Durations [5]. Looking at the specific effects of longer Scenes, i.e., stimuli of both high Duration and high Content Complexity, we found engagement in areas like the anterior STS or the precuneus which prior studies using movie stimuli related to the high-level processing of a coherent plot structure [2]. Overall, the whole brain results highlighted that especially longer stimuli containing more complex and appealing content engage higher-level cortical areas. This was further supported by the ROI analysis investigating the cortical engagement along the visual processing hierarchy, which demonstrated an interaction effect between Duration and Content Complexity, exemplified by higher ISC in the AG and pmDMN clusters - which represent the upper end of the uni-to-multimodal functional gradient [2] - for Scenes of 12s and 36s Duration. In summary, our study underlines the distinct but also collaborative roles of both Duration and Content Complexity in the processing and comprehension of hierarchically nested, naturalistic stimuli like movies.
