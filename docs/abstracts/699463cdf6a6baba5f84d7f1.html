Introduction

Recent studies have revealed that significant neurobiological changes take place in adolescence, during which synaptic pruning, myelination and structural reorganization jointly facilitate neural adaptation.^1 Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is an MRI-based technique for reconstructing the pathways of white matter tracts, which characterizes the brain microstructure and is widely used in the research of structural neurodevelopment among adolescents.^2 Longitudinal analyses of neuroimaging DTI data provide dynamic insights into individual developmental trajectories and topological organization of the structural connectome during adolescence.^3 The main challenge in longitudinal connectomics is to disentangle the commonalities of population-level connectome as well as the particularities of individual-level connectome, and to reveal how these commonalities and particularities link to behavioral phenotypes. However, most of the existing research on structural network focused on the network topology measurement, and few studies analyze the graph structure of the network directly.^4 Many previous methods for network modeling were designed for a single network and lacked the ability to handle multiple networks data.^5

Methods

In this study, quality-controlled DTI structural connectivity matrices were derived for 689 adolescents aged 14–23 years from the IMAGEN study, with an average of 2.54 repeated measurements per participant across three assessment visits. We proposed a longitudinal analytical framework for modeling the common structure and individual-time-specific deviations of network-valued data, where the deviation was approximated via a low-rank eigenmatrix representation and a two-stage iterative estimation procedure was implemented. Next, comprehensive simulations were conducted to evaluate the predictive performance using the area under ROC curve (AUC) and the residual sum of squares (RSS), and to quantify structural recovery by correlating key topological measures computed from the estimated versus true networks. Our model was applied to adolescent brain connectomics data from IMAGEN study, and regional weights and individual eigenvalues were extracted to respectively characterize brain region’s contribution to structural connectivity and between-subject difference. Finally, post hoc analyses at each follow-up visit were performed to examine cognitive differences between data-driven subgroups derived from structural connectivity and sub-networks were identified by testing edge-level associations between connectome deviations and cognitive scores.

Results

The proposed approach extended existing common-subspace independent-edge models by explicitly modeling individual-time-specific deviations in repeated-measures multi-network data, enabling the framework to capture both individual heterogeneity and temporal variation. Simulation study results demonstrated that the two-stage iterative algorithm converged rapidly to a stable local mode within relatively few iterations given well-chosen and data-driven initialization. We further evaluated the performance of model inference under two repeated-measurement designs that mirrored the real-data scenarios (Fig. 1a): in both designs, the predicted adjacency matrices for each observation achieved mean AUCs exceeding 0.98, and structural recovery assessed by network density, degree mean, and related topology summaries showed a systematic increase as the number of repeated measurements increased. When applied to brain networks in adolescent, the inferred network topological measures exhibited a non-linear developmental trajectory declining from ages 14–19 and increasing again before age 24, consistent with maturational reorganization of structural connectivity. Brain regions with the largest contributions to connectivity were predominantly located in fronto-parietal areas across visits (Fig. 1b). Importantly, multiple dimensions of model-derived individual connectome features were significantly associated with different cognitive functions (Fig. 1c), and the identified cognition-related subnetworks highlighted coordinated patterns of connections whose strengths tracked cognitive performance over time.

Conclusion

Our study proposed a longitudinal modelling framework for structural brain networks that simultaneously captures common connectivity patterns and individual‑specific development trajectories, revealing the evolution of brain networks over time and their associations with behavioral phenotypes.
