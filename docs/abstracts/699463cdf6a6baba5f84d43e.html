Introduction

Translating fMRI methods into clinical settings has been a challenging direction. This is partly due to substantial methodological heterogeneity in resting state functional MRI (rfMRI) pipelines arising from analytical flexibility. One crucial component of analytic flexibility lies in the midpoint between preprocessing and downstream statistical comparisons. It is computationally expensive to perform statistical comparisons across all voxels, so researchers often derive a low-dimensional summary of preprocessed rfMRI data, i.e., brain representations. Over the decades, these heterogeneous brain representations have catalyzed divergent methodological silos, limiting replicability and clinical translation. 

Many teams have attempted to compare brain representations and their effects on downstream analyses. However, expecting research teams to replicate their results across representation choices is both computationally costly and unrealistic. Instead, the advent of deep learning and artificial intelligence offers a new potential remedy: translation. Rather than re-analyzing data based on other brain representations, it is possible to circumvent this process entirely and translate from one connectome flavor to another, while preserving individual differences.1 Translations in rfMRI literature are currently limited to connectomes, validating the need to expand translation efforts to other brain representations and pave the way towards interpretable clinical biomarkers. To enable integration of results across brain representations, we propose a deep learning model capable of translating ICA spatial maps into connectomes while retaining individual differences. We validate our model with brain map reconstructions, demonstrate robust translation accuracy, and reveal preserved downstream brain-behavior associations.

Methods

Datasets. The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) cohort is a publicly available dataset with a large sample size of typically developing children (N=8,648) from ages 9-11 recruited across 21 different sites across the United States.3 Data were acquired across three types of 3T scanners (Siemens Prisma, General Electric 750, and Philips) running standard echo-planar imaging sequence protocol (TR=800ms, TE=30ms, flip angle=52°, multiband factor=6, 2.4mm isotropic voxel resolution). ABCD data were preprocessed and distributed with the standard fMRIPrep and ABCD-BIDS pipelines. Each subject had four 5-minute resting state scans, which when concatenated resulted in a total of 20 minutes of resting state data. To account for site and family effects, 14 sites were used as the training data, 3 as the validation data, and 3 as the testing data, resulting in a final split of Ntrain = 7104, Nval = 920, and Ntest =624. 

Brain representations: We focused on translations between two kinds of brain representations: 15 ICA spatial maps and full correlation connectomes (Schaefer atlas 100 & 300).2,4 Group ICA was performed on the rfMRI data concatenated across all subjects at a dimensionality of 15, followed by dual regression to estimate subject-specific surface network maps. For correlation matrices, each subject’s data was spatially parcellated into N (N_{low}=100, N_{high}=300) regions based on their respective granularity. Time series from each region were extracted and correlated with every other to construct a pairwise bivariate full correlation matrix.

Transformer Framework. A Surface Vision Transformer (SiT) was used to properly encode ICA spatial maps into a latent space for translations (Fig. 1).5 We validated SiT performance on rfMRI data though ICAi→ICAi reconstruction (separately for i ∈{1..15} maps) and demonstrated robust translations from spatial maps into connectomes at two dimensionalities.4 

For an in-depth description of the SiT, we refer to the original paper.5 We followed original surface data preprocessing steps resulting in a 15x320x153 triangular patching per hemisphere per subject. Functional connectivity is largely symmetrical across hemispheres, so choosing one hemisphere reduced computational costs and redundancy. We chose the left hemisphere over the right hemisphere, because there is slightly more lateralized language and motor activity. Our transformer based framework encoded each subjects’ ICA maps across the left hemisphere onto a shared lower dimensional latent space. Then, latent embeddings were projected through one final linear layer to both reconstruct (ICAi→ICAi) and translate (all ICA→Connectome). For translations, model output was the upper triangle elements for each subject separately. Elements were reshaped and transposed to create full correlation heat map matrices. Model performance was assessed based on demeaned correlations between true and predicted connectomes in the held-out validation dataset.

Downstream Analysis. Translating across brain representations was intended to support cross-pollination of results. To this end, we used the NIH Brain Connectivity Toolbox cognitive summary measures for both crystalized and fluid intelligence on the ABCD cohort. For each connectome, we calculated their edge-wise associations with crystalized and fluid intelligence separately. This resulted in two symmetrical matrices which reflect edge-wise associations with behavior. To determine if downstream brain-behavior associations were preserved in the translated connectomes, we repeated this procedure using translated connectomes and both cognitive measures separately, also resulting in two matrices that reflected brain-behavior correlation values across translated connectomes. Brain-behavior correlations derived from true and predicted connectomes were compared using Pearson’s correlation coefficients between upper triangles.

Results

Reconstruction. Deep learning models in rfMRI are relatively new. As such, no current ICA brain map embedding exists. Notably, ICA brain maps are orders of magnitude bigger than connectomes, rendering linear encoders computationally inefficient and unwieldy. To determine if the SiT could effectively encode information from spatial maps for this translation task, we validated its encoding strength by attempting to reconstruct each ICA brain map separately. Reconstruction efforts were robust to demeaning and to out-of-sample test sets, averaging a reconstruction of  (correlationrecon=0.73(0.007))(Fig. 2A) across all 15 components. This validates the SiT as capable of encoding the high dimensional information in ICA brain maps. 

Translation. To fully assess translation accuracy, we tested translated connectomes with various network metrics. Specifically, we assessed translation accuracy by comparing the demeaned vectorized upper triangle of the true connectome with its respective prediction for all subjects across all connectomes. Prior rfMRI brain representation translations have shown that non-demeaned correlations inflate results due to deep learning models converging to the training average. Demeaning, therefore, more accurately assesses if individual differences were successfully translated. The original translation to Schaefer 100 performed the best on the out-of-sample test set with an average accuracy of correlationschf100 = 0.42(0.08) (Fig. 2A). For the higher dimension parcellation, performance was lower but comparable to other current translation methods (correlationschf300 = 0.32(0.06)).

Brain-behavior Associations. The initial intent to translate across rfMRI brain representations was two fold: To streamline comparisons by bypassing re-analyses efforts and to facilitate cross-pollination across the field. To the latter point, we performed brain-behavior associations to determine if behaviorally-relevant individual differences were retained in the translated connectomes. The Schaeffer 100 parcellation had the highest brain-behavior translation accuracy for the validation data. Edge-wise associations across subjects for crystallized intelligence had a moderate translation accuracy (correlationcryst,schf100 = 0.44)(Fig. 2C). Translated fluid intelligence also maintained a moderate degree of accuracy (correlationfluid,schf100 = 0.42)(Fig. 2D). Brain-behavior associations for higher dimensionality appeared more challenging to preserve. (correlationcryst,schf300 = 0.35; correlationfluid,schf300 = 0.29)

Conclusion

The rfMRI landscape is wide and ever expanding. Brain representations vary across various metrics and new methods with promising results will continue to appear. Translation offers a unique path towards integrating these methods and results. To date, translations of rfMRI images have been limited to connectomes. To integrate the ever expanding landscape of brain representations, it is imperative to create a translator that can encode different rfMRI brain representations and translate them across a shared latent space. Deep learning models excel in this domain. Taking advantage of large rfMRI datasets, we can train different models that can learn a mapping from one rfMRI representation to another.

    In this project, we therefore proposed a SiT to encode ICA brain maps across the ABCD dataset. By properly encoding ICA brain maps, we were able to decode them through a simple linear layer that was able to construct each subject's individual connectomes. Translations were robust across different dimensions, performing on par with current state of the art connectome translator deep learning models. This project confirmed the presence of shared information across seemingly disparate MRI brain representations and establishes that transformations between disparate brain representations can be learned and mapped with deep learning models. In future extensions of this project, we will be focusing on leveraging statistical deep learning models to learn data distributions and reconstruct from that approximated statistical space.
