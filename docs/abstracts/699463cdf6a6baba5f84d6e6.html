Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), the most common form of motor neuron disease, is a progressive, invariably fatal, and clinically heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorder.1 It is classically characterized by the degeneration of both upper and lower motor neurons. Multiple studies have also demonstrated consistent involvement of motor and extra-motor cortical regions, particularly within the frontotemporal dementia-ALS continuum. Clinically, symptoms usually begin in a single body region and then spread to contiguous areas. A similar pattern has been proposed for neurodegeneration patterns in ALS, with early focal and selective brain involvement followed by prion-like “anatomical dissemination”. Whether, and to what extent, specific brain regions show early selective vulnerability to neurodegeneration remains unresolved. Moreover, it is unclear whether such vulnerability is confined to early disease stages, how it relates to different clinical onset phenotypes, and whether motor areas are invariably the first regions affected. Modern machine-learning algorithms applied to large patient cohorts provide an unprecedented opportunity to model grey matter structural alterations to detect anatomical subtypes of ALS with distinct disease trajectories. In this study, we used multimodal MRI and PySuStaIn2,3, a recently developed subtype and stage inference algorithm validated in several neurodegenerative diseases, to derive data-driven neuroanatomical ALS subtypes and disease stages, and to characterise their clinical and functional correlates, thereby disentangling temporal and phenotypic heterogeneity in our cohort. 

Methods

A total of 312 wild-type ALS patients was retrospectively included from a single tertiary ALS center (Regional Expert Center for ALS in Turin - CRESLA Center, A.O.U. Città della Salute e della Scienza, Molinette Hospital). 
All patients were diagnosed with motor neuron disease based on the El Escorial diagnostic criteria and underwent extensive clinical, neuropsychological, and neuroimaging assessment. The present study included only wild-type ASL patients (i.e., patients carrying mutations in SOD1, TARDBP, and FUS genes, or C9orf72 hexanucleotide expansions, as determined by genetic analyses, were excluded). Moreover, patients with severe neurological or psychiatric  comorbidities as well as focal or diffuse brain damage were excluded. 
Neurological assessment was performed at the CRESLA Center, collecting demographic and lifestyle data and detailed disease history (age, site, side and type of onset, age at diagnosis). 
High-resolution 3DT1-weighted images (TR = 11.82 ms, TE = 4.92 ms, voxel size = 0.47 × 0.47 × 1 mm3, 120 axial slices) were acquired for each patient with a GE Signa HDxt EchoSpeed 1.5T-MRI scanner.An eye-closed resting-state fMRI acquisition (TR = 2250 ms, TE = 5 ms, voxel size = 3.28 × 3.28 × 5 mm3, 585 volumes) was obtained for a subset of patients (N=213). 
All images were inspected for quality assessment using MRIQC. For each subject, 3DT1 anatomical images were preprocessed with FreeSurfer; functional images were preprocessed with the CONN toolbox. Volumetric and cortical thickness measures were converted to z-scores-based effect size using a control population, the NOMIS normative database4, including head size, age at MRI acquisition, and sex assigned at birth in the computation. These scores represent the distance of each subject from the normative population.  
Z-scores from  bilateral atrophic regions  (i.e., group average value < 0 , indicating thinner cortical thickness than control reference) were selected as candidate biomarkers to be entered in PySuStaIn disease progression and staging algorithm. For each biomarker, disease progression was modelled as a trajectory across predefined z-score thresholds. The model was run across increasing numbers of subtypes, with multiple random initialisations, and Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling to quantify event-order uncertainty, and 10-fold cross-validation to select the optimal solution. The final output consisted of subtype-specific regional progression sequences and, for each patient, the probabilistic assignments to a subtype and a disease stage. 
Demographic and clinical variables were compared between the pySuStaIn-derived morphological subtypes.  

Additionally, resting-state fMRI functional connectivity analyses were performed to compare the identified subtypes using a seed-based approach. Seeds were defined as bilateral ROIs corresponding to pySuStaIn biomarkers. A general linear model was defined including subtype as the main factor and sex assigned at birth, age, ALSFRS score, and SuStaIn stage as covariates. Cluster-level inference was based on 1,000 permutation tests using cluster mass with a cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.01 and FDR-corrected cluster significance set at p < 0.05. 

Results

Eleven bilatera atrophic regions were indentified as candidate biomarkers. The pySuStaIn model revealed two anatomical subtypes: (subtype 1, N = 180) an occipito-temporal subtype with early involvement of visual and temporal cortices, and (subtype 2, N=56) a rolandic-frontal subtype with predominant motor cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal atrophy (Figure 1). A third group of patients, representing individuals classified at stage 0 of pathology spreading, (Group 0, N = 76) showed minimal abnormalities. Subtype 2 patients were significantly older at disease onset (mean age in years = 68.65 ± 8.3, H = 18.05; p < 0.001) and showed more frequent bulbar onset compared to the other subgroups (χ2 =21.22, p = 0.002). 
Resting-state fMRI analyses revealed decreased functional connectivity in subtype 2 relative to subtype 1, particularly between the caudal middle frontal gyrus and rolandic areas (Figure 2). Subtype 2 compared to Group 0 showed decreased functional connectivity  in central and occipital regions. No significant difference was observed when comparing Subtype 1 and Group 0 patients.

Conclusion

In this study, we applied a data-driven machine-learning subtyping and staging algorithm to a large multimodal neuroimaging dataset from a clinically homogeneous ALS population. Our findings suggest the presence of anatomically and clinically distinct ALS subtypes, each following a specific trajectory of grey-matter atrophy and functional network disruption. Using pySuStaIn, we identified a predominantly rolandic subtype (Subtype 2), characterized by early involvement of rolandic and premotor regions, more frequent bulbar-onset and older-onset patients, and reduced connectivity between dorsolateral prefrontal and primary motor cortices. Notably, bulbar-onset ALS is a severe variant of the disease, as it is characterized by the fastest decline and the shortest survival, and a significantly reduced quality of life. Identifying an independent MRI biomarker capable of distinguishing this subgroup, potentially at an early stage, could be of great clinical significance. 

In contrast, Subtype 1 showed earlier posterior involvement with later motor-cortex engagement, while Group 0 captured individuals with minimal, non-specific structural change. 
These imaging-defined patterns underscore the added value of MRI-based stratification beyond standard rating scales. Together, these results support a structural degeneration/functional disintegration model in which ALS propagates along anatomically contiguous pathways following two partially dissociable trajectories. This combined structural and functional profiling provides a fine-grained, biologically informed stratification of ALS that may prove useful for prognosis, therapeutic targeting (e.g. subtype-specific trial enrichment), and biomarker development, particularly for identifying patients who may follow a more aggressive disease course.
