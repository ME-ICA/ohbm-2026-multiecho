Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease is highly heterogeneous, with marked differences in onset age, rate of progression, comorbidities, and symptoms.1 While this heterogeneity is well-known clinically it is often overlooked in disease modifying drug trials, which typically rely on group-average analyses and strict inclusion criteria. Such trial designs risk obscuring treatment-responsive individuals and may explain repeated trial failures. 

Neuroanatomical normative modelling is an emerging framework that aims to address a limitation of group-average neuroimaging analyses by quantifying individual-level deviations in brain structure. This approach compares regional volumes or thickness values to age- and sex-adjusted normative distributions derived from large reference datasets, generating personalised Z-score brain maps (which have previously revealed substantial neuroanatomical heterogeneity in Alzheimer’s disease).2,3 Here, we apply neuroanatomical normative modelling to a large preclinical Alzheimer’s disease trial cohort that did not demonstrate clinical benefit,4 and investigate whether neuroanatomical heterogeneity (1) is present in either treatment or placebo group assignment, and (2) influenced trial outcomes.

Methods

The A4 Study (NCT02008357) enrolled cognitively unimpaired, amyloid-positive adults aged 65–85 across 67 sites, randomised 1:1 to solanezumab (1600 mg IV every four weeks) or placebo, with the primary endpoint of Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite (PACC) change. All participants underwent serial 3T T1-weighted MRI for up to 240 weeks (mean 4 visits per participant).

T1-weighted scans were processed using FreeSurfer recon-all (A4 data: v7; normative modelling reference data: v6), extracting cortical thickness (148 Destrieux regions) and subcortical volumes (20 aseg regions). Normative models were trained on ~58k healthy controls from 82 sites using warped Bayesian linear regression model to account for non-linear and non-Gaussian effects modelling age, sex, and site.⁵ Individualised deviation profiles were generated for A4 and LEARN participants across 168 regions. To reduce residual site and acquisition effects, the adaptation dataset calibration consisted of LEARN study participants that were not recruited to the A4 trial but scanned at the same sites (591 scans). Our test data consisted of 4,711 A4 scans, from a total of n=1120 unique individuals (placebo=562, solanezumab=558; mean age 72.1 years), which were processed to generate regional Z-scores at each brain region.

Extreme deviations (Z < –1.96) were classified as outliers and summarised into a total outlier count (tOC) per individual, per scan. Baseline tOC differences between trial arms were tested using negative binomial regression (covariates: age, sex). An adjusted negative binomial model tested whether baseline tOC was associated with last-visit PACC, controlling for age and sex. Longitudinal tOC change was analysed using a negative binomial mixed-effects model, using time (weeks), treatment arm, age, sex, and a time × treatment arm interaction, with participant-level random intercepts. Dissimilarity in regional brain outlier patterns were quantified using Hamming distance. Group-level regional outlier frequencies were mapped to brain regions at baseline and the trial end-point at 240 weeks.

Results

Baseline tOC did not differ between solanezumab and placebo groups (β = 0.058, SE = 0.084, p = 0.49). Higher baseline tOC was associated with poorer final cognitive performance (PACC score) (β = –0.062, SE= 0.019, p = 0.001), independent of age and sex. Although treatment arm was unrelated to cognitive outcome (p = 0.79). Hamming distances indicated similar within-group spatial heterogeneity in both individuals receiving treatment and placebo (median: placebo = 4; solanezumab = 3; p > 0.05).

Regional outlier maps revealed distinct spatial patterns (Fig 1, Panel A). At baseline, more regions exceeded the expected 2.5% outlier rate  (based on the Z < -1.96 threshold) in the solanezumab group (63) than placebo (47), with the right precentral gyrus showing the highest prevalence (5%) in the solanezumab arm. After 240 weeks, both groups showed an increase in the total number of affected regions (placebo 47 to 83; solanezumab 63 to 108), with the right superior temporal sulcus showing the highest outlier prevalence (12%) in the solanezumab arm. 

During the trial, tOC increased over time (β = 0.002, SE = 0.0003, p < 10⁻⁸) and with age (β = 0.035, SE = 0.0088, p < 10⁻⁴). No main treatment effect was observed, but a significant time × treatment interaction (β = 0.0007, SE = 0.00025, p = 0.008) indicated an increase in tOC in the solanezumab arm (Fig 1, Panel B).

Conclusion

Our analysis explored the use of neuroanatomical normative modelling for quantifying individual variability in preclinical Alzheimer’s disease trials. As expected, higher neuroanatomical outliers were associated with poorer cognitive performance,2,3 and summary outlier measure (tOC) was balanced between treatment arms at baseline. 

Z-score brain maps showed that individuals’ spatial patterns of outliers only partially overlap in the trial participants; with some moderate differences in the temporal-parietal regions between the trial duration and treatment arms. Here, the solanezumab group showed a higher proportion of outliers in these regions which indicate a pattern of tissue loss in some individuals assigned to the treatment group over the trial course. Furthermore, the solanezumab group exhibited a greater longitudinal increase in total outlier count compared to placebo – this is unexpected (since the trial had failed) and warrants further exploration to assess if the participants randomly assigned to the solanezumab arm have a higher preclinical risk of Alzheimer’s, perhaps due to sampling bias. Future work could assess whether an individuals’ rate of change in outliers over a one-year pretrial run-in period could better stratify treatment assignment. Combining outlier patterns with cognitive trajectories and fluid biomarkers in future analyses could clarify whether there are any associations between our normative quantification of heterogeneity and treatment response.

Overall, our findings outline how an individualised measure of brain structure can be used to analyse retrospective clinical trial data for Alzheimer’s to assess patterns of neuroanatomical heterogeneity. A key step for future work will be to use trial data where the treatment effects were positive, to compare normative modelling outlier measures with more commonly used neuroimaging readouts such as hippocampal volume or brain volume.
