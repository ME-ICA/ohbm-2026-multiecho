Introduction

Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is the second most common dementia syndrome1. Visual hallucinations are encountered in up to 80% of patients and can occur anytime, but often earlier, during the course of disease1,2. They are associated with broader cognitive impairments, more severe disease trajectory, earlier institutionalization, higher healthcare costs and greater burden on caregivers3. MRI and ¹⁸F-FDG PET are routinely performed in clinical settings to evaluate DLB to provide insights into structural brain changes and alterations in glucose metabolism. Visual hallucinations have been associated with distinct alterations in brain structure and metabolism in DLB4, and we hypothesize that these alterations can be used to predict visual hallucinations. Machine learning approaches have been applied to distinguish DLB from other dementias5. However, predictive models leveraging structural and metabolic data to identify patients at risk for visual hallucinations are largely lacking. Developing such models could inform prognosis, facilitate early interventions and care planning for patients likely to develop visual hallucinations.

Methods

After IRB approval, structural MRI, ¹⁸F-FDG PET and clinical data from 25 patients with working diagnosis of DLB2 were retrospectively obtained from a clinical database. Patients were grouped into those who experienced complex visual hallucinations (VH; n=15, M:F = 9: 14) and those who did not (non-VH; n=10, M:F = 6:4). No significant age difference was found between VH (mean = 72.2, SD = 1.43) and non-VH (mean = 73.8, SD = 2.89), as assessed by an independent t-test (p = 0.60). Each individual’s T1-MPRAGE images were segmented (FreeSurfer v7.4.1) to extract gray matter (GM) volumes from neocortex (Desikan-Killiany-Tourville classifier atlas), hippocampus and pons from each subject and corrected by estimated Total Intracranial Volume. Then, each individual’s F18-FDG-PET images were co-registered to the T1 MPRAGE images (SPM12), intensity normalized by the mean FDG uptake in the pons to generate mean SUV ratios (mean SUVr) of the brain regions for each individual and projected to the MNI space. Mean SUVr of each ROI was extracted and corrected by GM and pons volume. Pairwise correlations of volume-corrected mean SUVr of the brain regions were computed using Pearson’s r and organized into correlation matrices for VH and non-VH groups (Figure 1). The data were mean-centered (mean = 0) without scaling the standard deviation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was utilized to reduce the number of variables to predict visual hallucinations. Principal components (PC) were retained until more than 75% of the total variance was explained; this resulted in the selection of five PCs. Logistic regression was applied to the 5 PCs to classify VH vs. non-VH.

Results

Brain metabolic features corrected by volume measures discriminated between VH and non-VH groups with an AUC of 0.807 (SE = 0.052, 95% CI = 0.705–0.909, p = 0.0069) (Figure 2). The mean predicted probability of visual hallucinations was 0.714 (SD = 0.26) for the VH group and 0.430 (SD = 0.18) for the non-VH group. The large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.212) supports a robust difference between the groups.

Conclusion

Our analysis suggests that brain metabolic features have the potential to predict the likelihood of experiencing visual hallucinations in Dementia with Lewy Bodies.
