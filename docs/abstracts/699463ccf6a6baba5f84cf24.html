Introduction

Post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC), or Long COVID, is increasingly recognized in children and adolescents, with cognitive impairment (“brain fog”) among the most common central nervous system (CNS) symptoms.1 These persistent cognitive complaints raise concern for cerebrovascular or neurovascular unit dysfunction. Adult neuroimaging and cell culture studies suggest SARS-CoV-2 may interact with the blood-brain barrier (BBB),2–4 potentially altering permeability and cerebral physiology; however, pediatric data remain sparse. Here, we present multimodal MRI findings assessing BBB permeability and cerebral oxygen metabolism in children with PASC-associated cognitive symptoms compared to healthy adolescents.

Methods

Participants: Data from adolescents with PASC-associated brain fog (BF; n = 46) and healthy controls (n = 22) were analyzed. Groups were comparable in age (Control: 16.3 ± 5.5 years; BF: 17.4 ± 4.4 years), hemoglobin concentration (12.95 ± 1.35 vs 13.33 ± 1.06 g/dL), hematocrit (39.2 ± 3.3 vs 40.1 ± 3.3%), and sex distribution (Female/Male: 14/8 vs 29/17). Hematological values were measured by phlebotomy. 

Neuroimaging/ Statistical Analysis: MRI data were acquired on a 3.0T Philips Achieva (32-channel head coil) at Children’s Hospital Los Angeles.Brain volumes were derived from a T1w image using BrainSuite23a. Global cerebral blood flow (CBF) was quantified using 2D phase-contrast MRI of the carotid and vertebral arteries. Whole-brain oxygen extraction fraction (OEF) and cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen (CMRO2) were estimated using T2-relaxation-under-spin-tagging (TRUST) combined with pulse oximetry. BBB permeability–surface area product (PS) was quantified using WEPCAST.5 Between-group differences were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and associations between PS and CMRO2 were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation.

Results

Results. PS was significantly reduced in the BF group compared with controls (Wilcoxon p = 0.016), consistent with altered BBB permeability or reduced functional vascular surface area. In contrast, CMRO2 did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.34), although BF participants showed greater inter-individual variability and a nonsignificant trend toward lower values. Across all participants, PS was positively associated with CMRO2 (Spearman ρ = 0.321, p = 0.0076), indicating that lower BBB permeability–surface area was associated with reduced cerebral oxygen metabolism. Notably, this relationship was observed despite preserved group-level CMRO₂.

Conclusion

Conclusion: BBB permeability-surface area was lower in children with PASC-associated brain fog than in controls, and lower permeability was associated with reduced cerebral oxygen metabolism across participants. This pattern contrasts with prior dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI findings in adults, which report increased BBB permeability in long COVID.4 Because both permeability and microvascular surface area influence substrate exchange at the BBB, these data suggest that reduced functional vascular surface area may contribute to variability in cerebral metabolic capacity in pediatric PASC, potentially reflecting mechanisms distinct from those described in adults. Larger, longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these observations and to clarify the mechanisms linking BBB dysfunction and neurocognitive sequelae following SARS-CoV-2 infection.
