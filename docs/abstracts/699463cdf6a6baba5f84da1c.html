Introduction

Chronic aphasia after left-hemisphere stroke causes persistent language impairment with highly variable response to speech-language therapy. Lesion-symptom mapping links damaged loci to deficits but does not fully explain severity or recovery,1 suggesting that the condition of spared tissue also matters. ‘Brain age’ modeling estimates how old tissue appears relative to chronological age; higher brain age gaps (BAGs) index poorer tissue health and are associated with vascular risk and cognitive decline. Next-generation, region-specific brain age tools permit spatially resolved assessment of tissue integrity2,3 that may capture variability in spared cortex not evident from lesion maps or gross volumetry.

We tested whether right-hemisphere regional brain aging (i.e., intact-hemisphere BAGs) provides incremental information about aphasia severity and treatment response beyond lesion topology and demographics. We hypothesized that (1) parcel-wise BAGs would organize into stable spatial gradients, (2) expression of specific aging gradients would relate to baseline aphasia severity above lesion features, and (3) pre-treatment expression of these gradients would predict individual naming gains following speech therapy with adjunctive anodal tDCS.

Methods

Participants and behavioral measures: Retrospective cohort of 188 chronic left-hemisphere stroke survivors (67 female) with available T1/T2 MRI and language assessment. Language was measured with the WAB-R Aphasia Quotient (AQ) for all participants (range 0–100) and the Philadelphia Naming Test (PNT) for 165 participants (baseline average of two sessions). A subset (N = 34) completed a clinical trial4 of 3 weeks of speech therapy with adjunctive anodal tDCS and had PNT at six-month follow-up. Exclusions: prior stroke, other neurological disease, severe medical/psychiatric illness. Institutional approvals and informed consent were obtained.

Imaging and lesion processing: T1- and T2-weighted images were acquired on Siemens 3T scanners with 1 mm isotropic MPRAGE and 3D T2 SPACE sequences. Lesions were manually traced on T2, co-registered to T1, and normalized to MNI space using enantiomorphic repair to create ‘healed’ T1s for processing. Each left-hemisphere was parcellated using volBrain’s atlas into 66 parcels; lesion-proportion scores (0–1) were computed per parcel. Parcels with rare damage were excluded prior to dimensionality reduction.

Regional brain age estimation: Right-hemisphere parcel-wise BAGs were computed using volBrain’s BrainStructureAges2 pipeline applied to enantiomorphically repaired T1 images. The method combines an AssemblyNet parcellation ensemble (3D U-Nets) and an ensemble of voxel-wise U-Nets that predict chronological age from T1 subvolumes; overlapping predictions are averaged to create a 3D age map which is aggregated to parcel-level ages. BAGs = parcel predicted age − chronological age. Analyses were restricted to right-hemisphere parcels to avoid lesion-driven artifacts.

Dimensionality reduction and stability: Two independent exploratory factor analyses (EFA) reduced dimensionality: one on left-hemisphere lesion-proportion vectors (after logit transform and lesion-volume residualization) and one on right-hemisphere BAG vectors. Principal axis factoring with varimax rotation was used. Stability was assessed with 1,000 split-half resamples and Tucker’s congruence; stable factors were retained (six lesion factors; four brain age factors). Participant-level factor scores (regression method) indexed expression of each spatial motif.

Statistical modeling: Hierarchical regression tested whether right-hemisphere brain age factors explained additional variance in WAB-R AQ beyond demographics and lesion factors (Block 1: age, sex, total lesion volume; Block 2: lesion factors; Block 3: brain age factors). Models were bootstrapped (1,000 BCa iterations) and inspected for collinearity. For treatment prediction, a split-sample procedure minimized bias: baseline PNT predictors were identified in the non-tDCS group (N = 131) using the same three-block approach; significant predictors (chronological age, lesion volume, right BAF1) were then used to predict 6-month naming change in the independent A-tDCS cohort (N = 34), evaluated with BCa bootstrap; one-tailed tests were applied for directional hypotheses. Additional controls included testing right-hemisphere GMV (residualized on ICV) in place of brain age factors.

Results

EFA results: Lesion EFA (after preprocessing) yielded six stable lesion factors resembling vascular topologies (inferior frontal, occipital, temporal, parietal, dorsomedial frontal, subcortical) explaining 82.8% of variance. Brain age EFA on right-hemisphere BAGs produced four stable gradients: medial prefrontal (BAF1, 35%), fronto-temporo-parietal language homologues (BAF2, 30.9%), cerebellar/ventral (BAF3, 20.7%), and dorsal (BAF4, 9.2%); factors were orthogonal and robust across split-half resamples.

Aphasia severity: Hierarchical regression in the full cohort (N = 188) showed Block 1 (age, sex, lesion volume) explained significant variance in WAB-R AQ (Adj. R² = .33, p < .001). Adding lesion factors improved fit (ΔR² = .05, p = .037). Critically, adding right-hemisphere brain age factors further improved prediction (ΔR² = .07, p < .001). In the final model (R² = .46, Adj. R² = .42), significant predictors were chronological age (β = −.30, p < .001), lesion volume (β = −.57, p < .001), lesion factor 3 (temporal; β = −.10, p = .05), and right-hemisphere BAF1 (medial prefrontal; β = −.31, p < .001) and BAF3 (cerebellar/ventral; β = −.18, p = .009). Results generalized to major WAB-R subtests.

Baseline naming model (non-tDCS group): In N = 131, Block 1 explained significant variance in baseline PNT (Adj. R² = .23, p < .001). Lesion factors did not significantly add variance (ΔR² = .06, p = .15), whereas brain age factors did (ΔR² = .07, p = .01). Significant predictors were age (β = −.35, p = .003), lesion volume (β = −.42, p < .001), and right-hemisphere BAF1 (β = −.30, p = .008). These three predictors were retained for treatment analyses.

Treatment prediction (A-tDCS cohort, N = 34): Group-level naming improved at 6 months (baseline M = 60.2, SD = 41.5; follow-up M = 68.2, SD = 46.3; t(33) = −2.7, p = .012) with large inter-individual variability. Using the preselected predictors, a single-block regression explained 23% of variance in naming change (R² = .23, Adj. R² = .15, p = .051). Directional tests showed significant coefficients for chronological age (β = −.72, p = .003), lesion volume (β = −.31, p = .014), and right-hemisphere BAF1 (β = −.47, p = .03). Inclusion of baseline severity (WAB-R AQ or baseline PNT) did not materially alter results; BAF1 remained significant. A model substituting gross right-hemisphere GMV (residualized on ICV) was not significant, indicating that the brain age pattern provided specific predictive information beyond volumetry.

Conclusion

Regional brain aging in the intact (right) hemisphere explains meaningful variance in aphasia severity and contributes incremental predictive value for treatment-related naming gains beyond lesion topology, lesion volume, age, and gross volumetric measures. Specifically, a right-hemisphere medial prefrontal aging gradient (BAF1) and a cerebellar/ventral gradient (BAF3) related to poorer language outcomes; BAF1 additionally predicted individual naming improvement after adjunctive A-tDCS and behavioral therapy. These effects persisted when controlling for lesion features and baseline severity and were not recapitulated by gross right-hemisphere GMV, indicating that multivariate, spatially organized signatures of tissue health (as captured by regional BAGs) provide information that conventional lesion mapping and volumetry miss.

Applying regionally resolved brain age to the intact hemisphere is a scalable, MRI-based approach to quantify spared-tissue health and heterogeneity in recovery potential. These findings suggest that incorporating right-hemisphere brain age gradients into prognostic models may improve individualized outcome prediction and patient stratification for restorative interventions. Future work should validate these results in larger, prospective treatment trials and examine mechanistic links between regional tissue aging, network function, and responsiveness to neuromodulation.
