Introduction

Brain networks exhibit complex connectivity patterns underpinning behaviour and cognition. While the emergence and development of connectomics have been shaped by advances in network science and computational methods, integrating directionality into large-scale brain networks remains a critical challenge due to the lack of both data and methods. First, existing approaches that estimate directionality in the human brain are often constrained by modality- or method-specific limitations and are rarely validated against invasive datasets (e.g., electrophysiology). Second, the interpretation of such models is usually restricted by the absence of reliable frameworks for analyzing directed brain connectivity. However, recent attention has shifted towards the directional flow of neural signals, highlighting asymmetry as a defining feature of large-scale brain communication1. Advances in network science, combined with the availability of richer data, bring a new type of directed connectomics forward as a timely and pivotal opportunity for the field. In this work, we propose a framework to derive a whole-brain directed structural connectome of the human solely based on non-invasive imaging methods; i.e., diffusion and functional MRI. We leverage a novel framework for detecting directed graph communities, named bicommunities2, to identify the hierarchical and directional organization of the brain. We present a set of anatomical bundles that exhibit directional bias and may provide hints of asymmetrical communication along specific white matter fibers. Finally, the observed asymmetry is validated by comparison with an external dataset of intracranial cortical recordings that capture delays in electrical conduction (www.f-tract.eu)3.

Methods

Undirected edge weights from a population-level atlas of white matter bundles4 are informed with a directed asymmetry level estimated by whole-brain effective connectivity from regression Dynamical Causal Modelling5. A non-linear mapping (ANLin=Aγ) is used to adjust the directionality of communication between pairs of regions. Through our recent bimodularity2 framework, we then extract directed brain communities defined as clusters of white matter bundles. The stability of k-means clustering is evaluated using consensus and hierarchical clustering to mitigate methodological biases associated with specific clustering algorithms or parameter choices and to select a suitable number of edge clusters (Fig. 1). Then, the degree of asymmetry of each bicommunity is assessed and validated by comparison against a null distribution estimated by permuting the weights of half of the connectome edges with their corresponding edge going in the opposite direction. This approach identifies the bicommunities with statistically significant asymmetry levels that reject the null hypothesis of “edge directionality does not matter”. We then compare the asymmetry levels of the directed connectome with the one in directed cortico-cortical evoked potentials (CCEP) from stereo-encephalographic responses to a direct electrical stimulation3. The conduction delays measured in CCEPs are thresholded at a maximum of 50ms to avoid the contribution of indirect communication (axonal propagation through multiple regions) to the asymmetry. The Spearman correlation is used to assess the agreement between the asymmetry in both modalities at the level of the graph edges, graph bicommunities, and when shuffling the cluster assignment of graph edges. This shuffling creates a null distribution of correlations between the connectome and F-Tract when considering random clusters of graph edges (shuffled bicommunities) and it is used to evaluate the statistical significance of this correspondence.

Results

Stable consensus clustering is achieved for a number of bicommunities that gradually increases from coarse (K = 11) to fine (K = 52) separation of the graph edges (Fig. 1). Large-scale white matter pathways such as commissural and association fibers are identified within distinct bicommunities regardless of the number of clusters. Then, fixing K = 28 for further analyses, 16 bicommunities (57%) have asymmetry levels that differ significantly from 0.5 (bidirectional) with p-values < 0.05 (Bonferroni-corrected for multiple tests, Ntests = K). As shown in Fig. 2, bicommunities with higher asymmetry levels tend to capture directed communication along association fibers, whereas transmission on the commissural fibers remains bidirectional. Furthermore, we observe hemispheric similarities, as, for example, bicommunities 2 and 14 both capture the same temporal to occipital communication within the right and left hemispheres, respectively. Finally, we found no agreement between our directed connectome, as estimated from effective connectivity, and the one estimated from CCEPs when comparing asymmetry at the level of graph edges. There is, however, an agreement (Spearman’s >0.4) between the modalities when comparing the median asymmetry within bicommunities of the connectome and the median onset delay in CCEPs. Although the correlation remains moderate, we show that it is significantly higher within the bicommunities than within shuffled edge clusters.

Conclusion

This work redefines for the first time how directed brain networks can be observed and interpreted. While white matter pathways provide the anatomical scaffold for signal transmission, effective connectivity estimates the asymmetry. By leveraging directionality rather than ignoring it, the bimodularity framework and brain bicommunities offer insights that are concealed from traditional methods. Known bundles of white matter fibers are identified, and some are associated with preferred directionality, providing a more realistic picture of brain connectivity that extends beyond bidirectional interactions. While the connectome asymmetry and the one captured by the invasive F-Tract measurement do not coincide at the edge level, we demonstrate that the directionality captured by the bicommunities is consistent across modalities, suggesting that the intrinsic asymmetry in communication within the brain is reflected at different temporal scales and interaction types. The empirical application of bimodularity on the wiring of the human brain highlights the relevance of the approach for characterizing hierarchical organization in the brain and potentially for functional specialization of white matter pathways. This work thus helps set the stage for the field of directed connectomics.
