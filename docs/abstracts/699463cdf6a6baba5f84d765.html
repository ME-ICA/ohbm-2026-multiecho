Introduction

Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a heterogeneous, multifactorial psychiatric disorder, that is characterized by positive, negative and cognitive symptoms [1]. An interplay of multiple risk factors, like genetics, environmental stressors, and neurodevelopmental disturbances is necessary for disease onset with the disorders heterogeneity being evident across multiple levels of the disorder [1].

Antipsychotic treatment targets primarily dopamine D2 receptors, however not addressing negative symptoms[2]. Recently, the first non-dopaminergic antipsychotic, a muscarinic receptor agonist-antagonist combination was approved after clinical trials showed reductions in both PANSS positive and negative symptom scores, suggesting broader efficacy than traditional treatments[2]. These findings highlight the potential role of the cholinergic system in the pathophysiology of SCZ. Furthermore, studies on basal forebrain cholinergic nuclei (BFCN) voxel-based morphometry (VBM) analyses of T1-weighted MR images revealed reduced volume of the anterior BFCN in SCZ patients, supporting a BFCN involvement in SCZ[3].

However, typical case-control analyses face challenges in SCZ, since these assume homogeneous group averages within the cohort[4]. SCZ, however, is a highly heterogeneous disorder, which reduces effectiveness of group averages and may lead to inconsistent results across studies[4]. Therefore, the question arises how many schizophrenia patients truly exhibit altered BFCN VBM volumes compared to the norm. Such normative comparisons can be generated using normative models that show the distribution of certain metrics in a reference population[5].

In this study, a normative approach was used to demonstrate whether and to what extent individual patients deviate from the norm in terms of their BFCN VBM volume. It was hypothesized that there will be a deviation below the norm in patients with schizophrenia and that the affected individuals exhibit lower cognitive performance.

Methods

T1-weighted MR images for the patient cohort are derived from a multi-site dataset (data from Munich, Zurich, Basel, and the COBRE dataset), which includes a total of 140 SCZ patients and 169 healthy controls. Images from the publicly available Human Connectome Project (HCP) are used for the healthy reference cohort. This includes 1,113 subjects from the Young Adult Study and a further 725 subjects from the Aging HCP Study, resulting in a total of 2007 subjects in the control cohort.

The structural images are preprocessed separately for the different datasets using cat12 and spm12, implemented in MATLAB R2019b (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA), resulting in segmentation into grey matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) maps in 1.5 mm isotropic voxel size. All subjects are registered to a stereotactic standard space (Montreal Neurological Institute space) and modulated with the Jacobian determinant to preserve the gray matter volume within each voxel. The modulated GM maps are masked with a probabilistic BFCN atlas, and the GM voxels within the BFCN are summed to obtain the anterior and posterior BFCN VBM values of the subjects and subsequently normalized based on the total intracranial volume (TIV) of the individual subjects (Fig. 1). Quality control was performed based on interquartile range (IQR), with images exhibiting an IQR outside the range of Q1 – 1.5*standard deviation to Q3 + 1.5*standard deviation being excluded.

Group differences between healthy controls and SCZ patients in anterior and posterior BFCN VBM were evaluated using univariate analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), controlling for the influence of sex and age.

A generalized additive model for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) model, embedded in R 4.5.1, was used to determine the percentile curves of the BFCN-VBM values of the reference cohort as a function of age. Subsequently, for each region (anterior and posterior) of the BFCN, individuals from the schizophrenia spectrum were positioned on the normative percentile charts and categorized as: (i) within the normative range of variation for healthy adults of the same age and sex, (ii) significantly exceeding the normative range, labeled as supra-normal, or (iii) significantly below the normative range, labeled as infra-normal. This classification was accompanied by calculating individual z-scores, which indicate how much an individual deviates from the norm.

Results

Group comparison was performed on the SCZ datasets, including Munich, Zurich and COBRE. ANCOVA revealed significantly reduced TIV-normalized VBM in schizophrenia patients in the anterior BFCN (p=0.019), controlled for sex and age. No significant difference was observed for the posterior BFCN.

Subsequently, the percentile distribution of TIV-normalized BFCN-VBM for the healthy cohort was created for both sexes across age groups, including 5, 25, 50, 75 and 95% ranges (Fig. 2). Validity of the percentile curves was assessed using a randomly selected subset of healthy controls (n=236), whereby more than 90% of the subjects resided within the normative range.

After validating the normative model, the individual position of schizophrenia patients relative to the norm were determined. A distinction was made between infra-normal (<5% percentile) and supra-normal (>95% percentile). In contrast to the group comparison, the majority of schizophrenia patients remained within the normal distribution and only 8% fell out, with two-thirds of these being in the infra-normal range.

Conclusion

The results suggest structural alterations in the anterior BFCN in patients with schizophrenia. These changes, however, can only be observed among a small number of patients, when compared to the norm. This highlights the complexity of heterogeneity in schizophrenia in combination with typical case-control analyses, since these assume representative group averages, which are representative of all individuals within the group.
