Introduction

Autism is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by social interaction and communication difficulties, as well as restrictive and/or repetitive behaviors or interests (RRBI)1. These behavioral manifestations have been linked in a reward-based theoretical framework of autism that centers constructs related to motivation and affect2. In line with this framework, a recent study found that the habenula (Hb), a small epithalamic structure that plays a crucial role in motivation and emotion3, is enlarged structurally among autistic individuals4. Beyond structural alterations, the potential functional disruptions of this small dopamine-regulating region remains unexplored among autistic individuals largely due to technical limitations. Specifically, the habenula is barely larger than a single voxel in many neuroimaging studies (i.e., ~27mm3 per hemisphere), and few precision fMRI studies (i.e., with sufficient spatial resolution) have been done with autistic individuals. In a high-resolution 3T resting state fMRI study, Ely et al.5 overcame these limitations by defining multiple subject-level Hb connectivity seeds that maximized BOLD signal sensitivity, allowing them to map Hb resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) among neurotypical (NT) participants. Detailed mapping of the Hb’s rs-FC underscores its central involvement in reward processing; and as such, we propose that the Hb may play a substantive role in ASD-related behaviors. To address this gap in the literature, we explored potential Hb FC alterations underlying ASD by using manual, subject-level and probabilistic, atlas-based Hb seeds with 3T rs-fMRI data from over 1,000 individuals included in the large-scale, multi-site Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) I and II repositories.

Methods

We conducted two separate whole-brain rs-FC analyses using different approaches for defining Hb seed regions of interest (ROIs). First, for each participant, we manually delineated the Hb to produce subject-specific binary ROIs (each 32-48 mm3 in volume), representing a gold standard approach for ROI definition. Second, we used a probabilistic atlas-based ROI (523 mm3 in volume), representing a simple, but potentially less accurate, approach for defining the Hb (Fig. 1, A). In the ABIDE MRI data, we used MRIQC to assess quality and fMRIPrep for preprocessing, prior to analyzing Hb FC. The atlas-based Hb analysis included 1,586 participants, with 705 diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder (ASD; mean age = 13.88 ± 8.15 years). The manually-delineated, subject-specific Hb analysis included 1,060 participants (N = 475 ASD; mean age = 15.97 ± 8.40 years). For both analyses, we performed the participant-level FC analyses in AFNI, where we averaged the voxelwise Hb ROI time series and entered them into a generalized linear model to generate Z-transformed correlation maps (Fig. 1, B). Then, group-level regression models were used to identify group-averaged and group-difference (ASD>NT, Fig. 1, C) correlation z-maps, including diagnosis (ASD, NT) as a categorical factor and age as a continuous factor, controlling for sex and scanning site. Finally, we performed functional decoding on each thresholded z-map to provide additional information regarding the functional role of the Hb among ASD participants.

Results

Group-averaged connectivity results for both ROI approaches yielded similar strong patterns of positive subcortical and midbrain connectivity (Fig. 2, A). Specifically, positive Hb connectivity was most pronounced in the thalamus and extended through the substantia nigra (SN), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC, BA 31), and striatal regions, which functional decoding suggests are associated with fear, reward, and emotion processing. Despite yielding similar functional decoding results, the atlas-based Hb group-averaged maps showed weak positive connectivity with a more extensive network of cortical regions that weren’t significant for the subject-specific Hb. For the atlas-based and subject-specific Hb ROIs, the group-difference maps included significantly increased Hb connectivity in ASD with the middle temporal (MTG) and superior temporal (STG) gyri (Fig. 2B). Notably, the atlas-based Hb showed a less distributed pattern, with increased connectivity constrained to the right MTG (BA 22), right STG (BA 41), and left STG (BA 21/22). In contrast, the subject-specific Hb showed increased connectivity across the  bilateral MTG (BA 21, 22) and STG (BA 22, 41), as well as the left supramarginal gyrus (BA 39, 40). Functional decoding revealed that both Hb connectivity patterns are associated with speech and language, with the atlas-based pattern being mostly associated with speech and subject-specific pattern being mostly associated with auditory comprehension. 

Conclusion

Overall, despite the large volume difference between the atlas-based and subject-specific Hb seed ROIs, the two whole-brain resting-state FC analyses revealed similar group-averaged and group-difference results. Across all participants, the group-averaged results highlighted Hb FC with the dopaminergic reward system, in agreement with prior work5. Compared to NTs, autistic participants exhibited significantly increased Hb connectivity within the auditory cortex. These results provide new insight into a potential underlying neurobiological mechanism of ASD that links alterations in reward-based circuitry to known deficits in sensory processing and adaptive behavior. Future work will further evaluate age-related Hb connectivity differences, as well as link Hb alterations with ASD symptomatology and severity.
