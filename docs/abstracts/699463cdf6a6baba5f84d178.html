Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a primary cause of long-term neurological disability, increasingly recognized as involving diffuse injury to both axons and cerebral blood vessels. Specifically, impairment of cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR), the ability of vessels to dilate in response to vasoactive stimuli, may underlie persistent neurological deficits. CVR imaging has been applied to neurological disorders such as stroke, dementia, and moyamoya disease. Although traditional CVR mapping using carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation or breath-hold challenges provides a quantitative measure of vascular reserve, these techniques are often cumbersome and impractical for routine clinical use. Consequently, there is a critical need for more accessible approaches to assess cerebrovascular health in neurologic disorders. Recently, resting state CVR (RS-CVR) has emerged as a promising task-free alternative for CVR mapping, that leveraging spontaneous fluctuations in arterial CO₂ during natural respiration. Previous studies have demonstrated that RS-CVR yields results comparable to standard CO₂-inhalation CVR (CO2-CVR)1. However, its validity in TBI remains unproven. Given that CVR deficits after TBI are characteristically multifocal and stochastic2, it remains unknown whether spontaneous resting fluctuations can reliably capture the spatial heterogeneity of vascular impairments associated with traumatic injury. In this study, we present the first validation of RS-CVR in TBI patients, comparing RS-CVR maps against the reference standard CO2-CVR maps. We hypothesized that RS-CVR would exhibit concordance with CO₂-CVR in terms of both spatial distribution and sensitivity to TBI-related vascular deficits. Ultimately, by establishing this concordance, we aimed to confirm the utility of RS-CVR as a viable tool for assessing post-traumatic cerebrovascular dysfunction.

Methods

MRI acquisition: Data was acquired from 36 participants (23 chronic TBI, 13 age- and gender-matched healthy controls (HC)), who consented and were enrolled under an IRB-approved protocol (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01762475), using a 3T MRI scanner. Structural imaging included T1 weighted Magnetization Prepared Rapid Gradient Echo (T1w MPRAGE) for anatomical reference and T2 weighted Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2w FLAIR) to generate lesion masks. The imaging parameters were as follows. T1w MPRAGE images: repetition time (TR) = 2530 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, inversion time (TI) = 1200 ms, flip angle (FA) = 7°, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm2, matrix size = 256 × 256, 176 slices, and slice thickness = 1.0 mm. T2w FLAIR images: TR = 9,090 ms, TE = 112 ms, TI = 2450 ms, FA = 120°, matrix size = 256 × 256, 45 slices, and slice thickness = 3.0 mm.

Blood-oxygen level dependent (BOLD) functional MRI (fMRI) scans were performed during both resting state and hypercapnia challenge. For CO2-CVR, Hypercapnia was induced using a Douglas bag alternating between room air and a 5% CO2 gas mixture with room air every minute for a total of 7 min3. The hypercapnia BOLD fMRI sequence parameters were TR = 2,000, TE = 25 ms, FA = 80°, FOV = 220x220 mm2, matrix size = 64x64, 36 slices, thickness = 3.6 mm, and 210 volumes. For RS-CVR, resting state BOLD fMRI scan was acquired without external stimuli using TR = 2000 ms, TE = 27 ms, FA = 90°, FOV = 220 × 220 mm2, matrix size = 64 × 64, 36 slices, slice thickness = 3.6 mm, 206 volumes.

Data processing: To generate CO₂-CVR maps, the hypercapnia BOLD data underwent realignment, normalization, and spatial smoothing. Simultaneously, end-tidal CO₂ (EtCO₂) traces were extracted and corrected for sampling delays. To synchronize the physiological data with the hemodynamic response, we performed a cross-correlation analysis to determine the optimal temporal lag. This optimal delay was then applied to align the EtCO2 regressor with the BOLD time series. Using voxel-wise general linear model (GLM) with shifted EtCO₂ signal as the independent variable and the BOLD signals as the dependent variable, we computed CO2-CVR values by normalizing the EtCO2 regression slope against a baseline composed of the intercept and the slope-weighted minimum EtCO2.

RS-CVR maps were computed based on established methods1. Preprocessing for resting state BOLD data included motion correction, spatial smoothing, and linear detrending. To identify the optimal frequency band for RS-CVR mapping, we evaluated 120 bandpass filters (range: 0 to 227.4 mHz with an interval of 1.25 times of previous frequency). For each filter, a voxel-wise GLM analysis was performed using the whole-brain average BOLD signals as the independent variable and BOLD signal time courses as the dependent variable. The resulting voxel-wise beta coefficients were normalized by the global mean beta value to generate relative RS-CVR maps.

In this study, all analyses were conducted in each subject’s native T1w space. To compute z-score maps, CO₂-CVR and RS-CVR maps were spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. For each patient, voxel-wise z-score maps of CO2-CVR (CO2-Z) and RS-CVR (RS-Z) were computed referencing the mean and standard deviation established by the HC group. This normalization enabled subject-specific identification of abnormal CVR and facilitated lesion-based analyses at the individual level. Finally, these z-score maps were inversely transformed back to each subject’s native T1w space. The image processing pipeline of this study is shown in Figure 1.

Statistical Analysis: To determine the optimal frequency range for RS-CVR computation, voxel-wise Pearson correlation coefficients (r values) were calculated to assess the spatial similarity between RS-CVR and CO₂-CVR maps in both HC and TBI. We identified the optimal bandpass filter as the frequency range that yielded the highest average correlation coefficient.

Results

We evaluated the group-average r-values between CO₂-CVR and RS-CVR maps across various bandpass filter combinations. In the HC group, the highest r-value was observed at frequency band of [0-116.4 mHz] (r = 0.5239 ± 0.1107). In the TBI group, the peak correlation was found at a slightly narrower frequency band of [0–74.5 mHz] (r = 0.5217 ± 0.1108). When the [0–116.4 mHz] band was applied to the TBI group, it yielded a comparable r-value (r = 0.5093 ± 0.1263). A paired t-test comparing these two bands showed a trend toward significance but did not reach the statistical threshold (p = 0.068).

Subject-level variability in the spatial correlation between RS-CVR and CO₂-CVR was assessed by inspecting the distribution of individual r-values at [0–116.4 mHz] frequency band. The median r-value was 0.530 (IQR = 0.146) in HC and 0.509 (IQR = 0.179) in TBI. These results demonstrate consistent spatial correspondence with modest variability across participants in both cohorts.

Figure 2 presents representative examples of CVR alterations associated with different types and locations of TBI-related lesions. In the area of encephalomalacia involved the left hippocampus (Figure 2A), reduced CVR was evident in both CO₂-CVR (0.0832 ± 0.052) and RS-CVR (0.4890 ± 0.3953) maps. This reduction was mirrored by decreased z-scores (CO2-Z = -0.8948 ± 0.6631 and RS-Z = -1.182 ± 0.7804), indicating impaired vascular reactivity within the structurally damaged tissue.

Hyperintense lesions similarly exhibited patterns of vascular compromise. In regions such as the left medial temporal lobe including the hippocampus (Figure 2B), CO2-CVR was 0.0297 ± 0.0334 (CO2-Z = -1.517 ± 0.4269) and RS-CVR was 0.4744 ± 0.1164 (RS-Z = -1.254 ± 0.5949). Likewise, the right cerebellum (Figure 2C) showed diminished reactivity on both maps, with CO2-CVR of 0.0477 ± 0.0809 (CO2-Z = -2.525 ± 0.802) and RS-CVR of 0.5012 ± 0.4174 (RS-Z = -1.273 ± 0.7932).

In contrast, the lesion in the right medial temporal region (Figure 2D) exhibited a distinct pattern. RS-CVR was reduced to 0.6226 ± 0.2565 (RS-Z = -0.6654 ± 0.6348), whereas CO₂-CVR demonstrated preserved vascular function with a value of 0.2766 ± 0.1270 (CO2-Z = 0.8981 ± 0.1697). 

Importantly, CVR impairments were observed in normal-appearing brain tissue where structural imaging showed no abnormalities (Figure 2E). Both CO2- and RS-CVR maps, along with their z-scores, revealed consistent vascular deficits. These results demonstrate the ability of CVR mapping to detect microvascular dysfunction missed by conventional imaging, reinforcing its role as a crucial complementary tool for assessing TBI.

Conclusion

In this study, we present the first validation of RS-CVR mapping as an effective and reliable method for assessing cerebrovascular reactivity in chronic moderate-to-severe TBI patients. We demonstrated that the spatial patterns and magnitude of RS-CVR closely mirror those of the conventional CO₂-CVR maps. This strong concordance suggests that resting state BOLD signals possess the necessary physiological information to identify TBI-related cerebrovascular impairment. This finding is consistent with prior validations in other conditions, such as Moyamoya disease1, and extends those observations to the TBI context. The robust correlation supports the premise that RS-CVR can serve as a viable surrogate for hypercapnia-based CVR, even in the presence of diffuse injury and altered hemodynamics characteristic of TBI.

Interestingly, our analysis revealed a distinct shift in the optimal frequency band for RS-CVR in TBI patients. In HC, the [0-116.4 mHz] band used for RS-CVR analysis yielded robust correlation with CO₂-CVR, whereas the TBI group exhibited a shift toward a slightly lower frequency band. This shift reflects altered cerebrovascular hemodynamics following trauma. Impairments in neurovascular coupling following TBI often manifest as delayed or prolonged vasomotor responses4. Consequently, the coherence between RS-CVR and CO₂-CVR may be enhanced at lower frequencies, where these slower hemodynamic fluctuations are more prominent, effectively capturing the altered hemodynamics.

Both RS-CVR and CO₂-CVR methods converged in identifying regions of compromised vascular reactivity corresponding to structural lesions. Areas of encephalomalacia, reflecting tissue loss and scarring, exhibited markedly reduced CVR on both maps, indicating severely impaired vasodilatory capacity. Similarly, focal FLAIR hyperintense lesions, typically indicative of microvascular damage, showed lower CVR compared to surrounding normal-appearing tissue. The ability of RS-CVR to capture subtle lesion-related deficits parallels the sensitivity of the CO₂ method, confirming that significant cerebrovascular dysfunction can exist even in the absence of gross tissue loss5. CVR mapping uncovers functional deficits in lesioned areas, providing insights that go beyond static anatomy, thereby offering a potential explanation for neurological deficits that are disproportionate to structural MRI findings.

Our study confirms the feasibility and scalability of RS-CVR, highlighting its potential as a practical tool for widespread clinical application. As a task-free technique requiring no special gas delivery, RS-CVR can be seamlessly incorporated into standard MRI protocols with minimal burden on patients or technical staff. This applicability is particularly critical for moderate-to-severe TBI patients, pediatric cases, or patients with reduced consciousness, for whom hypercapnic challenges are often impractical. These findings highlight the RS-CVR as a robust biomarker capable of reflecting the severity of injury.
