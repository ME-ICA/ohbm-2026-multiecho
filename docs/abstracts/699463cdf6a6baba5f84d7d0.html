Introduction

Modafinil is increasingly attracting scientific attention beyond its clinical indication for sleep–wake disorders because of its reported ability to enhance alertness and cognition. Although several neurotransmitter systems have been implicated in its pharmacological profile - including dopaminergic, noradrenergic, serotonergic, glutamatergic, and GABAergic pathways1 - the large-scale neural mechanisms through which modafinil reshapes intrinsic brain activity remain insufficiently characterized. Resting-state functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (rs-fMRI) provides a powerful, task-independent window on spontaneous brain dynamics, allowing the detection of drug-related modulations in both regional and global network organization. Previous studies have shown that modafinil alters connectivity within attention and control networks, yet these findings mostly relied on region-of-interest approaches and did not consider the molecular architecture that may constrain functional reorganization.2,3 A critical unanswered question is whether modafinil-induced changes in neocortical connectivity follow the spatial distribution of specific neurotransmitter receptors or transcriptomic markers linked to synaptic plasticity. Establishing such correspondences would offer insight into the neurochemical gradients that shape the brain’s response to pharmacological perturbation. Here, we investigated the acute effects of modafinil on voxel-wise global functional connectivity and evaluated whether these changes align with cortical maps of receptor density and the expression of neuroplasticity-related genes.

Methods

We conducted a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pharmacological fMRI study enrolling fifty healthy adults, who were equally allocated to two parallel arms: 25 participants received 100 mg modafinil, whereas 25 participants received a visually indistinguishable placebo. On the same day, all subjects underwent two MRI sessions (anatomical MRI and rs-fMRI): before drug administration and again approximately three hours afterward, corresponding to the expected peak plasma concentration of modafinil. For each session, scanning was performed on a 3T MRI system using T1-weighted anatomical images (sagittal orientation, field of view (FOV) 180 × 256 × 256 mm, 1 mm slice thickness without inter-slice gaps, and an in-plane voxel resolution of 1 mm × 1 mm; flip angle of 12°, repetition time (TR) = 9.7 ms, and echo time (TE) = 4 ms) and T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequences optimized for spontaneous BOLD (Blood-Oxygenation-Level-Dependent) fluctuations (3 runs; FOV=256 mm, 64 × 64 matrix, voxel size = 4 mm × 4 mm in-plane, 4 mm slice thickness, and a flip angle of 75°, 30 axial slices, TR=1.7 seconds). Data preprocessing and rs-fMRI analysis were performed using the CONN functional connectivity toolbox (version 22). Preprocessing steps included slice-timing and motion correction, normalization to MNI space, segmentation of gray matter, white matter, and CSF, and identification of outlier scans. Nuisance regressors for reduction of physiological noise were obtained using CONN’s default CompCor implementation, which extracts principal components from white matter and CSF signals. Outlier volumes, motion parameters and their derivatives were also included as confounds. A temporal filter (0.008–0.09 Hz) was applied to retain low-frequency intrinsic fluctuations, and spatial smoothing was intentionally omitted to preserve voxel-level specificity for global synchrony analysis. Intrinsic connectivity was quantified using Global Coherence (GCOR), implemented within CONN as a voxel-wise measure reflecting the average correlation of each voxel with all other voxels in the brain. GCOR was estimated using singular value decomposition on z‐normalized time series, providing an operator-independent metric of large-scale cortical integration. For each participant, GCOR difference maps (post–pre) were computed and compound-specific effects were obtained separately within the modafinil and placebo groups. To investigate the biological gradients constraining modafinil-induced changes, GCOR values were extracted for each node of the Schaefer 100-region parcellation and correlated with PET-derived cortical distributions of 19 neurotransmitter receptors and transporters across dopaminergic, dopaminergic, serotonergic, noradrenergic, glutamatergic, GABAergic, histaminergic, and endocannabinoid systems.4 In parallel, GCOR changes were compared with cortical expression of plasticity-related genes (ARC, GRIA1, GRIA2, BDNF, NTRK2) from the MAGICC repository.5 Given the well-established spatial autocorrelation of cortical maps, receptor–GCOR and gene–GCOR correlations were first evaluated using BrainSMASH surrogate maps to obtain spatial-autocorrelation–corrected (SA) p-values. To control for multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was then applied across receptor and gene maps, resulting in significance thresholds of p = 0.0026 (0.05/19) for PET-derived receptors and p = 0.01 (0.05/5) for MAGICC genes.

Results

 The analysis revealed that both modafinil and placebo produced measurable alterations in cortical connectivity across the two scanning sessions; however, the spatial distribution, direction, and magnitude of these effects differed markedly between groups. In the modafinil arm, the most prominent changes emerged as significant (p-FDR = 0.002) increases in GCOR within medial prefrontal regions (Figure 1, panel A). These territories, which include higher-order associative cortices implicated in executive regulation, memory integration, and decision processes, exhibited enhanced large-scale synchrony following modafinil intake. In contrast, the placebo group demonstrated connectivity fluctuations primarily restricted to posterior visual areas - namely occipital and lingual regions - patterns consistent with nonspecific expectancy-related variability rather than true pharmacological modulation. To determine whether these functional effects aligned with neurochemical architecture, GCOR modulations extracted from the Schaefer parcellation were correlated with PET-derived receptor distributions (Figure 1, panels B-E). A clear pattern emerged in the left hemisphere: GCOR changes showed a strong negative association with D2 receptor density (Spatial Autocorrelation–corrected (SA-corrected) p = 0.001; R = -0.50), indicating that modafinil reduced global synchronization in regions enriched in dopaminergic inhibitory signaling. Additional negative associations were observed between GCOR and CB1 receptor distribution (SA-corrected p< 0.001; R = -0.60), indicating that modafinil-related decreases in network integration emerged within territories characterized by strong endocannabinoid signaling. Conversely, GCOR exhibited a trend toward significance correlation with GABAa receptor density (SA-corrected p = 0.02), suggesting that increases in global connectivity preferentially occurred in areas where inhibitory microcircuit activity is physiologically prominent. These multimodal receptor correlations confirm that modafinil does not act through a single neurotransmitter system; rather, its network-level consequences reflect the interplay of dopaminergic, GABAergic, and endocannabinoid mechanisms. Transcriptomic analyses provided additional mechanistic insight. GCOR modulation demonstrated a robust positive correlation with ARC expression (SA-corrected p = 0.01; R = 0.50), indicating that modafinil-sensitive regions are also those with elevated capacity for activity-dependent plasticity. The drug’s functional impact therefore appears preferentially expressed in territories predisposed to rapid synaptic remodeling. A significant negative association with GRIA1 (SA-corrected p = 0.05; R = -0.40) further suggested that areas with high expression of AMPA receptor–related transcripts tended to exhibit reductions in global coupling, consistent with a rebalancing of excitatory–inhibitory dynamics. Although correlations with BDNF and NTRK2 did not surpass SA-corrected thresholds, their directionality suggested coordinated engagement of neurotrophic mechanisms. Together, these results show that modafinil induces structured, biologically constrained reconfiguration of cortical network synchrony. Rather than exerting diffuse, uniform effects, the drug reshapes connectivity in ways that reflect underlying receptor distributions and gene expression profiles—revealing that the cortex’s molecular architecture provides a scaffold that determines where and how modafinil modifies intrinsic functional communication.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that the acute impact of modafinil on intrinsic cortical communication is not uniformly distributed but instead follows a biologically structured pattern tightly aligned with the cortex’s, underlying neurochemical and transcriptomic architecture. By integrating voxel-wise measures of global synchronization with PET-derived receptor landscapes and gene expression gradients, we provide evidence that modafinil’s effects arise from coordinated modulation across inhibitory, excitatory, and neuromodulatory systems. The positive correspondence between GCOR increases and GABAa receptor density suggests that enhanced global synchronization is supported by inhibitory regulatory mechanisms that stabilize network dynamics while facilitating efficient information exchange. Concurrent negative associations with D2 and CB1 receptor distributions indicate that modafinil reduces connectivity in regions where dopaminergic and endocannabinoid inhibitory signaling exert strong modulatory control, pointing to a nuanced recalibration of excitation–inhibition balance. The alignment between GCOR changes and ARC expression supports the hypothesis that modafinil transiently promotes conditions conducive to synaptic remodeling, linking large-scale network modulation to cellular mechanisms of plasticity. The additional relationship with GRIA1 suggests that excitatory receptor dynamics further constrain the direction of functional reorganization. Although the reliance on population-level molecular atlases limits conclusions about individual neurochemical variability, the spatial convergence across modalities underscores that cortical molecular gradients impose meaningful constraints on pharmacological modulation of resting-state networks. These findings open new avenues for predicting differential responsiveness to neuromodulatory agents based on receptor architecture and genetic expression profiles. They also provide a conceptual framework for leveraging modafinil or related compounds to optimize network-level function in clinical populations characterized by dysregulated connectivity. Future work incorporating subject-specific molecular imaging or longitudinal interventions will be essential to clarify the durability and cognitive implications of these modafinil-induced network adjustments.
