Introduction

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic pain disorder characterized by widespread musculoskeletal pain, fatigue and cognitive difficulties, affecting approximately 2-4% of the population (Häuser and Fitzcharles, 2018). Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) shares overlapping symptomatology with FM, including fatigue and cognitive impairment, but is additionally characterized by anhedonia and persistent low mood (Hards et al., 2020). Both conditions exhibit altered brain network dynamics and bidirectional relationships (Yepez et al., 2022), yet the temporal organization of these networks during resting-state remains poorly understood. Traditional resting-state fMRI analyses assume static connectivity across the entire scan duration, potentially missing critical dynamic reconfigurations that characterize brain function.

Traditional resting-state fMRI analyses often assume static connectivity, potentially missing critical temporal fluctuations associated with pathology (Lee et al., 2022). Recent network neuroscience research highlights the necessity of dynamic approaches. For example, dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) analysis has demonstrated that the temporal variance of connectivity captures essential information about depressive traits, such as rumination, that static measures fail to detect (Kim et al., 2023) . Similarly, the experience of sustained pain is supported by a dynamic reconfiguration of whole-brain networks, where somatomotor and cognitive systems integrate and segregate over time to maintain the pain state (Lee et al., 2022).

We hypothesize that FM and MDD, despite symptom overlap, exhibit distinct dynamic brain connectivity profiles, reflecting fundamental differences in how attention, emotion, and somatosensory processing unfold over time

Methods

Participants. We analyzed resting-state fMRI data from 138 female participants: 58 with fibromyalgia (FM), 53 healthy controls (HC), and 27 with major depressive disorder (MDD). Participants completed multiple fMRI acquisitions including resting-state and task-based runs (vicarious pain, multisensory, thermal pain, self-referential processing, negative affect).

Data acquisition. Functional images were acquired using a 3T MRI scanner with multiband echo-planar imaging (TR=0.8s). High-resolution T1-weighted structural images were acquired for anatomical reference and registration to MNI152 space (2mm resolution).

Preprocessing and denoising. Functional data was preprocessed using fMRIPrep, including motion correction, distortion correction, coregistration to anatomical images, and normalization to MNI152NLin2009cAsym space. We applied spatial smoothing (8mm FWHM) and denoising using anatomical aCompCor to extract white matter, CSF, and motion regressors. For task-based acquisitions, we additionally regressed out task-evoked activity by including hemodynamic response regressors (convolved with canonical HRF). Resting-state data were bandpass filtered (0.008-0.09 Hz). Each acquisition was independently denoised and demeaned before concatenation in a fixed order. Then, to increase the total usable time-series length, we created a concatenated multi-sequence dataset (Strategy-T)(Elliott et al., 2019; Cho et al., 2021).

Network Parcellation. BOLD time series were extracted from 246 regions of interest defined by the Brainnetome Atlas. These regions were mapped to the Yeo 7-network parcellation: Visual, Somatomotor, Dorsal Attention, Ventral Attention, Limbic, Frontoparietal, and Default Mode Network. Regional time series were averaged within each network to create seven network-level time courses for each participant. 

Dynamic Network Analysis. We applied a sliding window approach to characterize temporal network dynamics. Window size (32 seconds, 40 TRs) and step size (10 seconds, 12 TRs) were empirically optimized through split-half reliability analysis across window sizes ranging from 30-100 seconds, which revealed peak stability at 32 seconds. For each window, we computed network strength as the mean absolute BOLD signal across all ROIs within each network. The "dominant network" at each timepoint was defined as the network exhibiting maximum strength.

Statistical Analysis. We examined three main aspects of network dynamics: (1) Network Dominance (percentage of windows each network dominated), persistence (median consecutive windows), switching rate, entropy (Shannon entropy), and temporal trajectories (early vs late scan periods); (2) Hierarchical Network Organization, transition probability matrices quantifying network-to-network switching patterns and identifying preferred transition pathways; (3) Directional Asymmetry, asymmetric transition patterns revealing source networks (high outflow) versus receiver networks (high inflow). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons.

Results

Network Dominance. Brain dynamics were dominated by a dual-attractor system: Somatomotor (26.1%) and Dorsal Attention (23.1%) networks collectively accounted for 49.2% of all time windows. Visual network occupied 16.6%, Default Mode 14.7%, Frontoparietal 11.8%, Ventral Attention 7.0%, and Limbic showed minimal dominance (0.7%). All groups exhibited preserved switching rates (FM: 44.1±4.3%, HC: 43.1±4.1%, MDD: 43.1±4.9%; p=0.16) and entropy (FM: 2.28±0.15 bits, HC: 2.27±0.14 bits, MDD: 2.26±0.14 bits; p>0.5). However, dominance distributions differed significantly across groups (χ²=136.14, p<0.001). FM showed enhanced Dorsal Attention dominance (26.7%) compared to HC (19.7%). MDD exhibited elevated Visual dominance (18.8%) versus FM (14.0%) and reduced Somatomotor engagement (23.3%) relative to HC (27.5%). These preference differences occurred without alterations in transition dynamics. This means that while patients spent different amounts of time in specific networks (e.g., fibromyalgia patients showed 4% more dorsal attention dominance), the underlying mechanism controlling how often they switch between networks, and the randomness of those switches, remained intact. In essence, the brain's switching "engine" functions normally in patients, but the "destination preferences" are altered (Fig. a).

Hierarchical Network Organization. Transition probability matrices revealed preferential pathways structuring network dynamics. The strongest coupling occurred between Somatomotor↔Dorsal Attention (bidirectional), followed by Visual→Dorsal Attention and Default→Frontoparietal transitions. Networks assumed distinct hierarchical roles: Somatomotor and Dorsal Attention functioned as stable attractors with high self-persistence, Visual and Ventral Attention served as transient hubs enabling rapid switching, and Limbic acted as a fleeting state with sustained dominance <1% of time. Transition matrices were highly correlated across groups (FM-HC r=0.94, MDD-HC r=0.92, FM-MDD r=0.96), confirming preserved hierarchical organization despite dominance preference shifts. The top transition pathways remained consistent: Somatomotor→Dorsal Attention, Dorsal Attention→Visual, Visual→Somatomotor in all groups, indicating a conserved structural backbone governing network reconfigurations independent of clinical status (Fig. b).

Directional Asymmetry. Networks exhibited asymmetric transition patterns revealing source-receiver roles. Dorsal Attention demonstrated source characteristics in FM, with greater outflow than inflow (asymmetry index +0.15, p<0.01), driving transitions to other networks. Visual network showed receiver properties in MDD (asymmetry index -0.12, p<0.01), preferentially accepting transitions from Somatomotor and Dorsal Attention rather than initiating them. Limbic consistently functioned as a receiver across all groups, accepting brief transitions from Ventral Attention (p<0.01) but rarely initiating outbound transitions. Specific asymmetries differed by group: FM showed enhanced Dorsal Attention→Somatomotor outflow, MDD exhibited increased Visual inflow from multiple networks, and HC maintained balanced bidirectional flow. These directional biases reveal disorder-specific "push-pull" dynamics: FM networks push toward attentional states, MDD networks pull toward visual-perceptual states, while limbic engagement remains input-driven across populations (Fig. c).

Conclusion

We identified a dual-attractor system dominated by Somatomotor and Dorsal Attention networks that characterizes spontaneous brain dynamics. Fibromyalgia and major depressive disorder show distinct network preferences while preserving normal transition dynamics. Clinical groups differ in which networks dominate, not in how networks transition. FM showed enhanced Dorsal Attention dominance (+4.0% vs HC), consistent with attentional hypervigilance in chronic pain.

MDD exhibited elevated Visual dominance (+4.5% vs FM) and reduced Somatomotor engagement (-2.6% vs HC), suggesting that depression involves overactivation of visual and sensory processing networks, potentially reflecting a cognitive pattern where patients focus excessively on external perceptual information rather than internal mental states.

Directional asymmetry revealed disorder-specific mechanisms: FM networks actively push toward attentional states (source characteristics), while MDD networks passively pull toward visual states (receiver characteristics). 

These findings establish that chronic pain and depression share preserved temporal brain architecture but diverge in preferred network configurations. The dissociation between intact switching mechanisms and altered attractor landscapes suggests clinical symptoms arise from biased network engagement rather than fundamental disruptions in dynamic flexibility.
