Introduction

Brain encoding (BE) is widely used to model brain dynamics during naturalistic tasks. Recent studies1,2,3 using videogame paradigms suggest that the training objective of artificial neural networks (ANNs) strongly influences how well their representations align with brain activity. However, results across studies remain inconsistent, likely due to differences in architectures, training data, and task environments, which hinder direct comparisons.

Here, we investigate how training objectives affect an ANN’s ability to encode functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data, while carefully controlling for architecture and training data. We compare three objectives (reinforcement learning (RL), imitation learning, and a vision-based objective) using matched convolutional architectures trained on identical videogame inputs. Models were evaluated on fMRI data acquired while participants played Super Mario Bros.

Methods

This study uses the Mario dataset from the Courtois NeuroMod (CNeuroMod) project. Five participants (two females, three males) played 22 levels of Super Mario Bros during fMRI acquisition (Fig. 1a). Approximately 15 h of data per participant were collected on a 3T Siemens Prisma Fit scanner (TR = 1.49 s, 2 mm isotropic) and preprocessed with fMRIPrep. After spatial smoothing (FWHM = 5 mm), time series were denoised for motion, global signal, low-frequency drifts, and white-matter and CSF confounds, then projected onto the MIST atlas at its highest resolution (1097 parcels; Figure 1b).

Models shared the same convolutional backbone with four convolutional layers followed by a fully connected layer (see Fig. 1c), except for the pretrained ResNet baseline model.  Models were trained from scratch on gameplay frames, differing only in training objective: RL using Proximal Policy Optimisation, imitation learning from individual subjects’ actions, or a vision proxy task predicting features from a pretrained ResNet model. Two baselines were included: an untrained model and a pretrained ResNet152v2 vision model.

Brain encoding was performed by convolving model activations with a canonical hemodynamic response function and fitting ridge regression models to predict parcel-wise fMRI activity from the MIST atlas (Fig. 1d). Encoding performance was assessed both within distribution and on out-of-distribution (OOD) levels unseen during training (Fig. 1e). Specifically, the data from  20 of the 22 levels were divided into training, validation, and test sets. The training, validation, and test sets consisted of 80%, 10%, and 10% of the game runs, respectively. The remaining 2 held-out levels were used as an out-of-distribution (OOD) set to assess model generalizability.

Results

Within distribution, reinforcement learning models achieved the highest mean brain-encoding performance, followed by imitation learning and the vision proxy, although differences were small (<1% R²; Fig. 2a). The untrained model performed well above chance and comparably to trained models. Encoding maps were highly consistent across models, primarily capturing visual and dorsal attention networks (Fig. 2b). Encoding accuracy increased during training and tracked task performance within individual models (Fig. 2c). In contrast, all models showed a marked drop in encoding accuracy on out-of-distribution levels, revealing substantial brittleness; under these conditions, differences between objectives became more pronounced, with reinforcement learning generalizing best on average and the vision proxy collapsing to near-chance performance (Fig. 2d).

Conclusion

These results show that within-distribution brain encoding alone is insufficient to distinguish between training objectives. Videogame-based encoding models can be brittle, with poor generalization to novel environments. Robust evaluation of brain alignment therefore requires diverse test conditions and out-of-distribution assessment. Our findings suggest that small models trained from scratch are limited in their capacity to support robust brain encoding, and that more generalist or foundation-style vision–action models may be required for reliable alignment.
