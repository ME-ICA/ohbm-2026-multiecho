Introduction

A central objective in systems neuroscience is to elucidate the causal mapping between high-dimensional input stimulation patterns and their induced specific functional outcomes. While recent advances in connectomics and optogenetics have provided detailed structural maps and precise actuation tools, this inverse problem remains computationally intractable: determining the optimal input stimulation pattern required to drive a neural circuit into a desired state is a challenge necessitating exhaustive experimentation 1. This issue is further exacerbated in biological systems characterized by stochastic dynamics and high-dimensional input manifold, where the search space for effective control parameters is vast.

To address this, we propose a paradigm shift from conventional empirical trial-and-error to an in silico computational framework. We leverage the FlyWire connectome—specifically a stochastic simulation of Drosophila sensory-to-motor circuits—as a biological testbed to compare two computational approaches: (1) an online sampling-based approach utilizing from the basic random sampling to advanced sampling methods like evolutionary algorithms (e.g., CMA-ES) to actively traverse the input manifold, and (2) an offline model-based approach that employs a differentiable surrogate model to computationally invert the system's stochastic dynamics for input generation. We demonstrate that while naive random exploration fails to control the system, these advanced computational strategies successfully discover optimal stimulation patterns, establishing a proof-of-principle for the in silico resolution of the inverse problem.

Methods

Stochastic Simulation of the Drosophila Sensory-to-Motor Connectomes

We utilized the sensory-to-motor circuits derived from the FlyWire Drosophila connectome simulation2 as our primary biological testbed. To reduce computational complexity while retaining biological relevance, we restricted the network architecture to a subsystem linking 42 distinct taste-responding input neurons to 4 downstream motor neurons via intermediate interneurons. Crucially, while the original simulation operates as a deterministic system governed by fixed synaptic weights, we introduced a scalar offset parameter (variable c; see Fig. 1) into the neuronal dynamics. This modification induces bifurcation regimes and injects stochasticity, effectively transforming the network into a non-linear stochastic dynamical system. This ensures the control problem captures the inherent variability and noise characteristic of biological neural networks.

Baseline Characterization and Decoding Rules

To establish a ground-truth decoding rule for motor output, we first characterized the network's baseline dynamics. We subjected all 42 input neurons to uniform synchronous stimulation across discrete frequency intensities (0–200 Hz, 20 Hz intervals). We recorded the resulting mean firing rates of the four downstream motor neurons (MN6, MN8, MN9, and MN11), establishing these empirical means as activation thresholds. We defined a binary classification rule wherein a "target state" (Class 1) required motor neuron firing rates to exceed their respective baseline means; failure to meet these criteria was categorized as the "inactive state" (Class 0).

Computational Strategies for Optimal Input Discovery

We compared two distinct computational approaches for solving the inverse problem of mapping desired motor outputs to optimal sensory input stimulation patterns.

A. Online Sampling-Based Discovery

We first assessed the feasibility of an online, evolutionary approach (Fig. 1). The search space comprised 1142 combinatorial frequency options. Sampling continued until the system reached the active state (Class 1) or a maximum of 1,000 trials.

  • Stochastic Random Sampling: As a baseline, we employed uniform random sampling to search the input manifold.
  • Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES): To navigate the sparsity of valid solutions within this high-dimensional space, we implemented CMA-ES 3. This evolutionary algorithm actively optimizes input patterns by minimizing the Euclidean distance between observed motor firing rates and the pre-defined target thresholds.

B. Offline Model-Based Generation

Complementing the online approach, we developed a generative framework to solve the inverse problem in an offline manner (Fig. 2 # Workflow). This pipeline proceeded in three stages:

  • Surrogate Model Training: We trained two differentiable neural networks as surrogate forward models to predict (i) continuous motor activity and (ii) binary state classification from input patterns. Simultaneously, inverse encoders were trained to approximate the mapping from output states back to the input space.
  • Latent Input Optimization: To generate inputs for specific targets (e.g., Class 1), we performed gradient-based optimization within the latent space of the differentiable surrogate. By fixing the output layer to the desired target firing vector, we backpropagated error gradients to optimize input reconstruction, minimizing the discrepancy between the surrogate prediction and the target state.
  • Closed-Loop Empirical Validation: Input patterns generated via latent optimization were validated by applying them to the ground-truth stochastic FlyWire simulation testbed (100 independent trials) to quantify control fidelity.

Results

Results on Sampling-based Input Discovery

We first established the difficulty of the inverse problem by evaluating a brute-force random sampling baseline. Across 1,000 independent trials, where stimulation patterns were drawn uniformly from 1142 combinatorial options, the system failed to yield a single instance of the targeted active state (Class 1). Specifically, unguided exploration consistently failed to drive the four downstream motor neurons above baseline activation thresholds (Fig. 1, Result #1), confirming that the solution manifold within this high-dimensional input space is vanishingly sparse.

 In stark contrast, the evolutionary algorithm (e.g., CMA-ES) demonstrated rapid and robust convergence. By actively updating stimulation parameters to minimize the error between observed and target firing rates, the algorithm successfully identified optimal patterns within 160 trials. As illustrated in the optimization trajectory (Fig. 1, Result #2), CMA-ES produced a monotonic descent in the penalty metric, systematically reducing the distance to the target state while the random baseline fluctuated persistently in high-error regimes. This confirms that active search is sufficient to discover input vectors capable of driving specific motor targets (e.g., the firing vector of 6, 23, 8, and 15 Hz).

Results on Model-based Input Generation

For model-based input generation, we trained a differentiable surrogate model on 1000 curated trials including 1000 times stimulation of 42 taste-responding sensory neurons to 4 motor neurons. We tasked this model with generating 100 input patterns optimized to elicit specific target network states, i.e., active state (Class 1). As shown in Fig. 2 (# Generated Input Stimulation Patterns over 100 trials), our surrogate model is capable of generating structured input stimulation patterns. This organized structure contrasts sharply with the unstructured in the randomly generated input patterns.

We confirmed the efficacy of these generated input stimulation through closed-loop validation, re-injecting the model-optimized input patterns into the ground-truth FlyWire simulation. Quantitative analysis revealed high control fidelity: model-generated inputs consistently drove motor neurons to their precise target frequencies (e.g., MN8 reached 35Hz, aligning closely with the target), whereas random inputs failed to cross activation thresholds. Furthermore, distributional analysis highlighted the stability of the model-based generation: output firing rates were tightly clustered around target values with low variance, whereas random outputs exhibited high variance and low accuracy. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the generative surrogate successfully solves the inverse problem, identifying input stimulation patterns that reliably lead to desired motor targets.  

Conclusion

We have demonstrated that the connectomic inverse problem—mapping specific motor targets back to their required sensory inputs—can be effectively solved through computational optimization. Our results highlight a critical dichotomy in neural modulation: while high-dimensional stochastic systems are intractable under naive random interrogation, they become highly navigable under systematic optimization. The failure of random sampling to identify effective input stimulation patterns confirms the curse of dimensionality inherent in neural circuits, suggesting that the manifold of inputs capable of driving high-threshold motor outputs is exceptionally sparse. By successfully navigating this input manifold via CMA-ES, we demonstrate that such active evolutionary search strategy can identify optimal input stimulation patterns even in this sparse manifold.

Another key finding of this study is the promising capability of the differentiable surrogate model in solving this inverse problem. The distinct, structured patterns observed in the model-generated inputs contrasted sharply with the noise-like random patterns. Rather than simply memorizing training examples, the surrogate models effectively inverted the system dynamics, allowing for the generation of input vectors that are robust to the system's intrinsic stochasticity. This validates the feasibility of using a model-based computational approach to approximate the inverse dynamics of biological networks.

The most significant implication of this work is a methodological shift for experimental neuroscience: lowering the burden of in vivo exploration via a computational testbed. Conventionally, identifying optimal input stimulation patterns relies on in vivo trial-and-error experimentation—a process severely limited by subject viability, fatigue, and ethical constraints. We demonstrate that targeted stimulation patterns inducing desired functional (or behavior) outcomes can be derived entirely in silico, allowing researchers to reserve biological trials strictly for validation rather than exploration.

In summary, this study validates that the connectomics inverse problem is computationally solvable. We show that both online evolutionary sampling and offline generative modeling can navigate sparse, high-dimensional input manifold to elicit precise motor outputs. However, limitations persist. The sampling-based approach (CMA-ES), while effective, requires iterative real-time interaction; in biological settings, this could lead to experimental fatigue or the drift issue in neural recording. The model-based approach offers efficient offline generation but is constrained by the fidelity of the surrogate. If the surrogate fails to capture full system dynamics, the input generation quality may degrade. Future work should explore hybrid protocols that minimize biological queries while maximizing the robustness of the inverse mapping.
