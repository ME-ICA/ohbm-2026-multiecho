Introduction

Disease modifying therapies targeting specific molecular disease processes are becoming increasingly available for neurodegenerative disorders. However, profiling molecular features of neurodegeneration in living patients is challenging due to limited accessibility to brain tissue. In this work, we combine in-vivo ultra-high resolution 7T quantitative MRI (qMRI) metrics with molecular information from ex-vivo spatially resolved transcriptomics (SRT) data across cortical depths. In healthy controls, we find strong associations between qMRI and transcriptomic data and demonstrate that we can use qMRI to infer cell type presence and gene expression associated with specific molecular pathways across cortical depths. We also demonstrate the applicability of our approach to characterising disease by predicting molecular features in a Parkinson’s disease cohort. From qMRI data we predict disruption to differentiation of oligodendrocytes in the grey matter cortex alongside alterations in glycolipid binding and find corresponding changes to molecular pathways in independent bulk RNA sequencing and lipidomic analysis datasets.

Methods

MRI acquisition

27 people with Parkinson’s disease (PD) aged 50-80 years and within 10 years’ diagnosis and 21 age-matched controls underwent 0.6mm isotropic multi-parameter mapping (MPM)1, alongside MP2RAGE anatomical imaging on a 7T MRI system.

qMRI map creation

Quantitative MPM maps of longitudinal relaxation rate (R1), effective transverse relaxation rate (R2*), proton density (PrD) and magnetization transfer saturation (MTsat) were calculated using the hMRI toolbox v0.5.02. Quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) maps were generated by applying a custom QSM pipeline (including ROMEO phase unwrapping, PDF background field removal, and STAR-QSM dipole inversion) to MPM data before averaging across acquisitions.

qMRI cortical depth segmentation

MP2RAGE images were processed with Freesurfer v7.1.0s recon-all pipeline. LayNii was used to define 4 cortical depth spatial domains (SpD 1-4) between the pial and white matter surfaces. The Brodmann area 46 (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, DLPFC) mask from the HCP-MMP1.0 atlas was brought into participants' native space. This mask was divided into anterior, middle and posterior subregions and intersected with the 4 SpD masks to create 12 individual regions of interest (ROIs) from which median QSM, R1, R2*, PrD, and MTsat values were extracted (Fig 1A).

SRT data collection and segmentation

Publicly available SRT data from the DPLFC (10 controls) were used3. Anterior, middle and posterior samples of Brodmann area 46 (DLPFC) were dissected to capture cortical layers 1-6. The 10x Visium protocol was performed using manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with minimum depth of 50,000 reads per Visium spot (Fig 1A). Data were processed using previously described steps3. The SRT spots were partitioned to align with the qMRI data, using BayesSpace clustering to give 4 cortical depth SpDs (Fig 1A).

SRT cell-type and pathway analysis

Cell-type composition was inferred via cell2location and matched snRNA-seq data. For each SRT spot, the three cell types with the highest assignment likelihood were retained. Glial cells were chosen as they can have high myelin and iron content which are substantial determinants of qMRI metrics (Fig 1B). Biological process pathways were identified by searching Gene Ontology (GO) for the terms: myelin, iron, mineral, and lipids. Matched pathways and their hierarchical offspring were retrieved and filtered, producing a set of GO pathways related to qMRI biology (Fig 1E). Pathway expression was quantified per spot using scanpy.

qMRI and SRT statistics

Significant associations between age- and sex-adjusted median qMRI and SRT values across the 12 ROIs were assessed at pFDR<0.05. Leave-one-out cross validation (LOO-CV) for controls was completed across ROIs with the following model: SRT~QSM+ R1+R2*+PrD+MTsat, and correlations between predicted and actual values were assessed at pFDR<0.05. Differences in qMRI metrics between PD and controls were assessed for the whole DLPFC and individual SpDs using ANOVAs adjusting for age and sex. For PD SRT projections, PD qMRI data were input to the control LOO-CV model (Fig 2B).

Bulk RNA sequencing

RNA was extracted from frozen tissue (18 PD, 20 controls), quantified, and used to generate stranded mRNA libraries. Libraries were sequenced, demultiplexed, quality-filtered, and aligned. Low-quality samples were removed. Differential expression was modelled by brain region and disease status. Genes with pFDR<0.05 were considered significant. Gene set enrichment was run on ranked fold changes using curated myelin- and oligodendrocyte-related gene sets.

 

Lipidomic analysis
 Glycosphingolipids and glucosylceramide (GlcCer) were quantified from frozen brain tissue homogenates (5 PD, 5 controls). Lipids were extracted and purified, then split for digestion. Released glycans and glucose were analysed by chromatography with fluorescence detection. GSL and GlcCer species were identified and quantified.

Results

qMRI metrics correlate with and predict glial cell counts in controls

We found several significant associations between qMRI metrics and SRT-derived glial cell counts across ROIs (Fig 1B, C). Oligodendrocyte count had the most significant relationships, being positively associated with all qMRI metrics (0.43<R2<0.62, 1.6x10-2 <pFDR<2.7x10-2), except PrD, with which it was negatively associated (R=0.48, pFDR=0.023). Given that oligodendrocytes have the highest myelin and iron content and these features are key determinants of qMRI values, the observed correlations were biologically coherent. Microglia and astrocytes had significant negative correlations with all metrics except PrD with which they were positively correlated, and QSM with which they did not correlate. As they have lower myelin and iron content than oligodendrocytes, and their decreasing density from pial surface to white matter co-occurs with increasing oligodendrocyte density, this would be expected. Using LOO-CV analysis to predict cell-type counts from qMRI data, we found that qMRI-predicted cell counts for the withheld ROIs were significantly positively correlated with SRT-derived cell counts for all cell types (Fig 1D). We found that predictions for oligodendrocytes were not dependent upon any one SpD, whereas other predictions were highly dependent on SpD1 (closest to the pial surface).

 

qMRI metrics correlate with and predict molecular pathways in controls

After filtering, 1303 GO biological pathways relating the myelin, lipid, iron and inflammation were included (Fig 1E). A total of 14 pathways were significantly correlated with at least one qMRI metric, of which one related to inflammation (inflammatory response, correlation with R2*), one related to myelin (regulation of oligodendrocyte differentiation, correlation with PrD, R2* and MTsat) and 12 related to lipid metabolism (including glycolipid binding and paraspeckles, Fig 1F). We used the same LOO-CV approach as for cell types, and found that for all pathways, the qMRI-predicted scores significantly positively correlated with SRT-derived pathway scores, (0.55<R2<0.92, 1.3 x10-5<pFDR< 5.8x10-3, Fig 1G). We found that these predictions were not dependent on any one spatial domain.

qMRI predicts alterations to myelin- and lipid-related pathways in PD

We found that PD had significantly lower qMRI MTsat (pFDR=1.2x10-6) and R1 (pFDR=3.6x10-2) compared to controls in SpD1 of the DLPFC specifically (closest to the pial surface), but found no differences in any other qMRI metrics, or when examining the DLPFC as a whole (Fig 2A). As these are predominantly myelin- and lipid-sensitive metrics, we thought this may indicate an alteration to myelin- and lipid-related pathways in PD. We applied the SRT-based LOO-CV model in combination with PD qMRI metrics to project molecular pathway scores in PD for the 14 pathways where we found significant associations for controls (Fig 2B). Comparing the PD-projected scores for these pathways to control SRT pathway scores, we found that the PD-projected scores were significantly lower for “regulation of oligodendrocyte differentiation” (pFDR=0.04, Fig 2C), and “glycolipid binding” (pFDR=0.037, Fig 2E).

PD pathway differences predicted by qMRI are mirrored in independent datasets

Intriguingly, gene set enrichment analysis of an independent bulk RNA-sequencing dataset showed that genes associated with oligodendrocyte maturation were significantly less expressed in PD relative to controls, mirroring the qMRI-predicted decrease in “regulation of oligodendrocyte differentiation” (Fig 2D). To test the predicted decrease in “glycolipid binding” score, we performed lipidomic analysis in an independent cohort. We found decreases in GlcCer (a glycolipid precursor) concentration in superficial cortex in PD, mirroring the qMRI-predicted decrease in glycolipid binding, and the decrease observed in lipid-sensitive qMRI metrics in superficial cortex (Fig 2F).

Conclusion

With the emergence of disease modifying therapies for neurodegenerative disorders, new tools are needed to non-invasively stratify patients based on dynamic disease processes. In this study we explored the untapped potential of SRT to create inference models of cellular and molecular features of the living human brain from qMRI. Understanding how the molecular entities captured in qMRI imaging relate to biological entities is especially valuable from a therapeutic perspective.

We integrated MRI and SRT data, both spatially resolved across the cortical depth, for the first time in humans. Not only did this shed light on changes which otherwise would remain undetected when focusing only on macrostructure, but it also demonstrated the value of this approach for linking qMRI metrics with specific cell types and biological processes.

In controls, we found that qMRI metrics were strongly associated with glial-cell type counts and GO biological pathway scores across the cortical depth. Using LOO-CV, we also demonstrated that these SRT measures could be reliably predicted from a linear combination of qMRI metrics. We found that oligodendrocytes, and pathways associated with lipid metabolism could be the most robustly and reliably predicted.

We additionally applied this framework to a PD cohort, finding decreases in myelin- and lipid-sensitive qMRI metrics in PD relative to controls, and a projected decrease in oligodendrocyte differentiation and glycolipid binding pathway scores. Importantly, we found corresponding decreases in oligodendrocyte maturation in glycosphingolipid metabolism in independent post-mortem PD cohorts.

In conclusion, our proof-of-concept study shows an approach which allows predictive profiling of molecular and cellular disease features in living patients. While we demonstrate this in the context of Parkinson’s disease, we expect this approach to be relevant to any neurological or neurodegenerative disease. We believe this work demonstrates the potential of this type of predictive modelling to transform neurodegeneration research, drug trials and clinical practice.
