Introduction

It is well-accepted that cortical thickness decreases with age, demonstrated by both cross-sectional and longitudinal research.1 However, there is a high level of inter-individual variability in cortical thickness in healthy adults and the effect of age differs between regions. Specifically, research on cortical thickness of the visual cortex reports varying locations as well as varying effects of age, possibly due to methodological differences between studies.   

In subjects with retinal diseases such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and glaucoma, visual deprivation could lead to accelerated neurodegeneration of the visual cortex. Indeed, most studies report cortical thinning of central visual areas in subjects with bilateral AMD compared to control subjects, and thinning of peripheral visual areas in subjects with bilateral glaucoma. However, similar to studies in healthy subjects, the exact locations and amount of thinning differ between studies, possibly due to small sample sizes, or, to a limited understanding of cortical thickness of visual cortex in healthy subjects.

We, therefore, used the large, population-based, UKBiobank dataset to construct age-dependent normative ranges of cortical thickness in middle-aged and older healthy individuals. These can be used to study degenerative diseases of the visual system by creating predictive value on an individual level.

Methods

We used the large UK Biobank dataset (2020 release), with participants in the age range of 40-69 years at recruitment.2 Access to this dataset was obtained under approved research projects 23668 and 705478. Participants with a T1-weighted structural scan were included (age range of 44-82 years at imaging assessment). Participants without any eye disease other than cataract, based on self-reported measures and ICD10 records, were included as healthy. Participants were excluded in case of self-reported presence of one or more neurological diseases, psychiatric diseases and in case of any disease affecting the visual system.

The T1-weighted MRI was acquired by the UK Biobank team on a 3T MR scanner with a standard 32-channel head coil (Skyra, Siemens, Germany) using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TI = 880 ms, field of view = 208x256x256 mm, voxel size = 1 mm isotropic, in-plane acceleration = 2, acquisition time = 4 min 54 sec). Minimal preprocessing of the T1-weighted imaging scan was performed by the UK Biobank team. 

Cortical thickness estimates were derived from the T1-weighted imaging data with a surface-based analysis using Freesurfer 6.0, using the distance between the white matter and pial surfaces. The Freesurfer outputs were quality-controlled by the UK Biobank team using Qoala-T, a supervised learning tool for quality control of segmented MRI data.

Regions of interest (ROIs), cortical visual areas V1, V2, and V3, were derived by predicting anatomically-based retinotopic maps using the Benson atlas.3 The Neuropythy python toolbox anatomically aligns individual surface-based data to the fsaverage surface in FreeSurfer, where the fsaverage retinotopic maps, as defined by Benson and colleagues, are projected onto individual data, generating the subject-specific maps of the visual areas, eccentricity and polar angle. This method has been validated to reliably predict subject-specific retinotopic maps in V1, V2 and V3, based on cortical anatomy, without acquiring retinotopic mapping data using fMRI experiments.3

The ROI of V1 was further divided into foveal, parafoveal, near-periphery and far-periphery (0-1, 1-5, 5-30 and 30+ degrees of eccentricity). Cortical thickness was calculated for the entire brain and in each of these ROIs, averaged over left and right hemisphere. 

Normative models of cortical thickness as a function of age were created using the GAMLSS (generalized additive models for location, scale and shape) package in R (version 4.3.3).4 Within GAMLSS, we used the LMS (lambda, mu, sigma) method by Cole and Green, extended by Rigby and Stasinopoulos to model location, skewness and kurtosis. This method has been specifically developed for creating centile curves, and is used by the World Health Organization to create growth reference curves. The automated LMS function in R uses the RS (Rigby and Stanisopoulos) algorithm, iteratively fitting each of the four distributional parameters until convergence, selecting the best fit using the BIC. In addition, we assessed whether the parameter estimates capture the distribution of the response accurately, by visually checking several diagnostic plots of the residuals, including normal QQ-plots, detrended QQ-plots, also known as worm plots, quantile residuals against fitted values and a density estimate. Further, we used Q-statistics to determine any unexplained structure in the residuals due to inadequate fit in one of the four distribution parameters. Residuals with absolute z-scores greater than 3.5 are considered outliers and are removed, after which the normative model is fit again.

Results

In the 2020 release of the UK Biobank dataset, 42803 participants participated in the first imaging assessment and had a T1-weighted structural scan. For 33233 participants, a freesurfer analysis was available, of which 278 were excluded by the Qoala-T quality control performed by the UK Biobank team, leading to a remaining sample of 32945 participants.

Of the 42803 participants with a T1-weighted structural scan, 37255 participants reported, at all visits, not having any eye disease other than cataract. After excluding self-reported diseases impacting the visual system and self-reported neurological and psychiatric diseases, a sample size of 27082 participants remained. Combining these two subsets, we reached a final sample of 20814 participants with 52% females, in the age range of 44-82 years old (mean ± SD: 63.2 ± 7.5 years old).

Using normative modelling we defined normative ranges of cortical thickness across age for the entire brain, for visual areas V1, V2 and V3, and V1 subdivided into four areas of eccentricity, in healthy participants, without any visual disorders, neurological disease, and psychiatric disease.

Cortical thickness of the entire brain shows an age-related decline after the age of approximately 55 years old (figure 1A). These results confirm previous research using the large, population-based dataset of the UK Biobank.1 However, a large inter-individual variation in cortical thickness of healthy participants is visible.

The subjects show a large variation in cortical thickness of the visual cortex, with a median cortical thickness of approximately 1.73 mm in V1, 2.02 mm in V2, and 2.33 mm in V3 at age 50. At age 80, the 50th percentile is located at 1.76 mm in V1, 2.00 mm in V2 and 2.25 mm in V3, showing a slight age-related thinning in V3 (figure 1B-D). All visual areas show a large inter-individual variation, with approximately 0.4 mm difference in cortical thickness between the 5th and 95th percentile for all visual areas.

V1 was split in four different eccentricity bins, 0-1 degrees (foveal), 1-5 degrees (parafoveal), 5-30 degrees (near-periphery) and 30 degrees and higher (far-periphery). While V1 as a whole has a lower cortical thickness than V2 and V3, as shown previously (figure 1B-D), figure 1E shows a higher cortical thickness in foveal V1, comparable to V2.

Conclusion

Using a large, middle-aged to older sample, we aimed to establish normative ranges of cortical thickness in the visual areas of V1, V2, and V3. The normative ranges of entire brain cortical thickness confirm previous studies by showing an age-related decline with a large inter-individual variation.1 In contrast however, no age-related decline of cortical thickness was detected in cortical visual areas. While several studies have shown that the cortical thickness of the visual cortex is largely preserved, some studies find an age-related decline in cortical thickness in specific areas such as the pericalcarine region and peripheral V1 and V2. These inconsistencies could possibly be due to methodological differences, such as ROI selection, which, as illustrated by Burge et al. (2016) impacts cortical thickness measurements. Additionally, this impedes comparison between studies.5 

The ROI selection used here is one of the main methodological strengths of the current research. We used the Benson atlas to determine V1, V2 and V3 on an individual level, based on an anatomically defined retinotopic map. This gave us a very precise estimate of the visual cortex for each individual, with additional information on eccentricity and polar angle, without needing any functional MRI retinotopic mapping experiment.3 Additionally, our work benefits from the large sample, of a wide age range, acquired by the UK Biobank. Even though our research partly relies on self-reported information for exclusion criteria of diseases influencing the visual system as well as neurological and psychiatric diseases, this approach contributes to a healthy sample that adequately represents the population at large. A factor that should be kept in mind when interpreting cortical thickness estimates across age, is a possible loss of white matter directly adjacent to the cortical visual areas. As cortical thickness measurements rely on contrast differences to establish the boundary between white matter and grey matter, any changes in white matter microstructure can affect cortical thickness measurements. Future work might extend the current analyses by assessing any changes in white matter microstructure at the white matter boundary directly adjacent to the visual areas of interest to make sure the cortical thickness measurement were not affected by any white matter changes. 

The normative ranges presented here, give insight into the large inter-individual variation in healthy visual cortex and could be leveraged in further clinical research. Clinical trials, for example, aiming to restore vision at the level of the retina, show a large variability in treatment outcome. This variation could be due to methodological factors such as differences in outcome measures and follow-up time, but might also be due to individual factors; these treatments would be less likely to benefit subjects with neurodegeneration of the visual cortex, that could be identified on an individual level using the normative ranges established in this research.
