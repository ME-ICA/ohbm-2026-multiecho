Introduction

Multi-agent systems (MAS) represent a core idea in contemporary Artificial Intelligence, consisting of autonomous computational agents that interact within a shared environment to achieve defined objectives.1 In functional MRI (fMRI), established analytical approaches such as the general linear model (GLM), functional connectivity analysis, and regression-based methods have been applied for task-based studies. However, these approaches are limited in their ability to model nonlinear relationships across brain regions.

Machine learning and deep learning methods have increasingly been adopted in neuroimaging to address these limitations, including applications of MAS to structural MRI segmentation.2 In fMRI, data are high dimensional and brain activations exhibit nonlinear relationships, motivating the integration of statistical modelling with machine learning–based approaches. This approach enables the separation of distinct analytical functions, such as statistical modelling, feature extraction, and literature-based interpretation, while allowing the outputs to be combined within a unified framework.

In this work, we propose an analytical framework that integrates statistical modelling with deep learning architectures to analyse fMRI data, enabling the joint characterization of interpretable effects and latent neural patterns. The framework combines a statistical modelling stream, a dimensionality reduction stream, and a literature-analysis stream, coordinated by a large language model (LLM) that synthesizes their complementary outputs. 

Methods

The framework was developed and evaluated on a large-scale anonymized task-based fMRI dataset comprising high-dimensional voxel-wise time-series data from a large cohort of healthy adult participants. The experimental paradigm followed an event-related design with pseudo-randomized stimulus presentation and interleaved rest periods.

An extensive panel of odor stimuli was evaluated. Stimuli order was pseudo-randomized to avoid consecutive repetition of an odor. Depending on the mission, odor presentation lasted between 4 and 6 seconds, followed by rest periods ranging from 14 to 18 seconds. Odorants were delivered via a cotton-pellet perch to preserve an ecological experience in the MRI environment. Each stimulus was presented between 3 and 6 times per acquisition to ensure statistical power and limit olfactory fatigue. 

Functional images were acquired using a T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging sequence, together with a T1-weighted structural scan. Preprocessing was performed in BrainVoyager. Structural images were corrected for intensity inhomogeneity and normalized to MNI space. Functional data were spatially smoothed and normalized to 2 × 2 × 2 mm³ MNI space using the anatomical transformation matrix.

Voxel-wise GLMs were computed at the subject level. Group-level ROI-based GLMs were then performed using the Brainnetome Atlas. Contrast maps (stimulus > baseline) produced ROI β- and t-maps, which served as inputs to the analysis framework.

The analysis framework was implemented using Python and DSPy.3 The machine learning and latent feature extraction agent performs dimensionality reduction on the ROI-based β-maps.  Each statistical map was encoded as a feature vector indexed by Brainnetome regions.  β-values data from all missions were jointly used to estimate the latent space.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was first applied to identify the main axes of variance across the dataset. Component loadings quantify the contribution of individual brain regions, providing a description of large-scale activation patterns.
Independent component analysis (ICA) was subsequently performed using the FastICA algorithm (scikit-learn), with the number of components fixed to ten.4 ICA was used to identify statistically independent latent sources rather than variance-maximizing axes. Because ICA components are neither orthogonal nor ordered by explained variance, they were ranked according to the variance of their projections. Each component was interpreted through its mixing matrix, linking components to brain regions showing shared modulation. 

To characterize a stimulus, its group-level β-maps were projected onto the learned ICA components. The obtained coordinate components each reflected the strength of association between the stimulus and a specific independent component. Components with the largest absolute coordinates were considered most strongly engaged by the stimulus, and their associated brain regions were identified through the corresponding mixing weights.

In addition, a deep learning–based ICA module was implemented to capture nonlinear mixing effects. This approach extends classical ICA by using neural networks to estimate a nonlinear demixing function that recovers independent components from observed data.5

A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) agent was implemented in DSPy and operated on a curated neuroscience literature local database composed of publications linking regional activation patterns to functional interpretations. Activation patterns, derived from the dimensionality reduction agents, were used as queries to retrieve relevant studies and generate evidence-based summaries. Finally, a coordinator agent, implemented as a function-calling large language model (LLM) via Azure API, interpreted user queries, coordinated inter-agent communication, and synthesized the final outputs.

Results

Group-level voxel-wise GLM analysis produced β-values and corresponding t-statistics across participants. PCA applied to ROI-based β-maps showed that the first two principal components explained 60.33% of the total variance. The first component most strongly associated with the middle temporal gyrus, thalamus, inferior parietal lobule, superior temporal gyrus, and postcentral gyrus. The second component was mainly associated with the inferior and middle temporal gyrui, orbital gyrus, parahippocampal gyrus, and fusiform gyrus. Extending PCA to ten components increased the cumulative explained variance to 81.66%.

Ten-component ICA performed on the β-maps identified three dominant components in terms of explained projection variance: component 8 (C8, 21.20%), component 2 (C2, 13.56%), and component 9 (C9, 9.86%). C8’s highest contributing regions are the superior and inferior frontal gyri, middle frontal gyrus, lateral occipital cortex, and postcentral gyrus. C2’s highest contributing regions are the inferior and middle temporal gyri, lateral occipital cortex, inferior parietal lobule, and postcentral gyrus. C9 was characterized by contributions from the inferior temporal gyrus, medioventral occipital cortex, fusiform gyrus, amygdala, thalamus, and basal ganglia.

Preliminary experiments using a Deep Learning–based ICA approach are ongoing.

The RAG module was tested on a subset of the curated literature database using multiple queries and repeated trials. Retrieved references were consistently relevant to the queried activation patterns, and citation attribution was stable across runs, validation was performed by a human with a neuroscientific background. However, quantitative evaluation of semantic relevance remains challenging and represents a current limitation, particularly for large-scale deployment.

The coordinator agent successfully summarized statistical results, dimensionality reduction outputs, and retrieved literature evidence, producing synthesized summaries consistent with the outputs obtained from individual agents.

Conclusion

This study introduces a multi-agent framework for the structured analysis and interpretation of task-based fMRI data, evaluated on a large olfactory dataset. The framework integrates ROI-based statistical modelling with machine learning–based dimensionality reduction using PCA and ICA, enabling systematic identification of stimulus-specific activation patterns and latent components. Interpretation is supported by a RAG agent operating on curated literature, while overall coordination and synthesis are handled by an LLM agent.

A key limitation is the reliance on linear decomposition methods. Although PCA and ICA effectively capture dominant variance and independent sources, they are limited in modelling nonlinear relationships. Consequently, neural patterns arising from nonlinear interactions between brain regions may remain unresolved. To address this limitation, preliminary experiments using deep learning–based ICA were initiated to model nonlinear mixing effects.

At its current stage, the framework establishes a scalable foundation that combines statistical modelling, latent space modelling, and literature-informed interpretation through a RAG agent. This integration supports both exploratory and hypothesis-driven fMRI analyses. Future work will focus on advancing nonlinear methods and on the quantitative evaluation of literature retrieval and interpretability.
