Introduction

Visual working memory (VWM) is the ability to retain and manipulate a limited amount of visual information over brief periods of time. It is supported by the coordinated interaction of neural oscillations across multiple frequency bands. Fast oscillations in the high-beta and gamma ranges have been proposed to represent memory content itself,^1 whereas lower-frequency oscillations are thought to support top-down control and executive processes.^2 Among these, theta oscillations (4–8 Hz) have been shown to play a particularly prominent role in VWM, encoding memory load and task difficulty in frontomedial sources,^3 and representing ordinal position information during sequential VWM tasks in medial temporal lobe circuits.^4

Despite these advances, the precise mechanistic roles of distinct oscillatory frequencies—and how they jointly support VWM—remain poorly understood. A key limitation of the existing literature is that multi-item VWM has typically been studied using either simultaneous presentation paradigms, such as delayed-match-to-sample (DMS) tasks, or sequential presentation paradigms, such as Sternberg tasks, in isolation. This separation makes it difficult to determine whether observed oscillatory signatures reflect fundamental memory mechanisms or task-specific demands related to stimulus presentation, temporal structure, or control requirements.

In this study, we investigated whether sequential and simultaneous working memory differ in their spectral fingerprints, to uncover the oscillatory mechanisms supporting ordered sequence memorization and temporal integration in VWM. We recorded concurrent magneto- and electroencephalography (M/EEG) during parametrically controlled delayed-match-to-sample and Sternberg-like VWM tasks, enabling a direct comparison of task-specific and shared oscillatory dynamics.

Methods

Sample description. 28 healthy volunteers (17 males, age: 28.61+5.70) took part in the study. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no recent history of neurological or neuropsychological illness and were physically eligible to undergo both MRI and M/EEG recordings. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to the experiment. The volunteers received monetary compensation for their participation.

Task. We used a parametrically modulated VWM paradigm that included two types of trials: simultaneous (SIM) and sequential (SEQ) that appeared inteleaved in a pseudorandomized manner (Figure 1). In each trial, subjects viewed a set of 2, 4, or 6 visual objects (S1). In the simultaneous condition, the objects were presented concurrently whereas in the sequential condition, they appeared sequentially. After a 2000-ms retention period, subjects indicated whether a probe stimulus (S2) matched S1 with respect to the feature (object order or identity) they were cued to retain at trial onset.

Recordings. We recorded concurrent M/EEG data with 304-channel MEG and 64 channel system (MEGIN Ltd) at the  BioMag laboratory in HUS Medical Imaging Centre in Helsinki, Finland. We also collected T1-weighted anatomical MRI scans for cortical surface reconstruction models. 

Preprocessing. We recorded concurrent magnetoencephalography (MEG) and electroencephalography (EEG) as participants performed the task. MEG-EEG data was preprocessed, source modelled with minimum-norm estimate. Source time series were parcellated into 200 brain parcels with fidelity weighting.

Time–frequency analysis. For each parcel time series, we applied a Morlet wavelet transform with 38 logarithmically spaced wavelets spanning 3–102 Hz (7 cycles) and computed instantaneous amplitudes in sliding 100 ms windows with 50 ms overlap for each frequency. Time-resolved oscillations amplitudes  were averaged across trials separately for each time-frequency bin, parcel, condition (see below) and subject, and baseline-corrected using the mean pre-stimulus amplitude (−0.5 to −0.2 s relative to S1 onset). Statistical differences between SEQ and SIM working memory tasks were assessed separately for each memory load using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Load-dependent effects and associations with behavioral performance (hit rates) were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. Multiple comparisons across time-frequency bins were FDR-corrected. Whole-brain results across parcels were summarized with the fraction of parcels with significant effects (P).

Results

Participants performed the task accurately (overall hit rate: 0.706 ± 0.058) A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA to analyze the effects of task type and load on hit rates did not find a significant difference in hit rate between the SEQ and SIM conditions (F(1,27) = 3.43, p = 0.075). In contrast, the effect of load was highly significant (F(2,54) = 358.20, p <  0.01) ; paired t-tests showed a systematic significant decrease of hit rate with increasing load levels (p<0.01). Additionally, there was a significant interaction effect between task type and load level (F(2,54)=7.83, p<0.01).

Simultaneous and sequential VWM exhibited a shared pattern of oscillatory dynamics (Figure 2A), marked by alpha synchronization during encoding and alpha–theta desynchronization during the retention period. Beta-band activity showed a time-dependent profile, with early desynchronization followed by transient synchronization during the first half of the retention period.

Direct comparisons between SEQ vs. SIM VWM task conditions (Figure 2B) revealed stronger alpha synchronization during encoding in SIM, whereas alpha–theta activity during retention was enhanced in SEQ (i.e., alpha-theta desynchronization was stronger in SIM than in SEQ task conditions). These differences scaled with memory load.

A cortical visualization (Figure 2C) of the theta activity at a TF region with a high degree of modulation by load shows that at the lowest load level, 2, the cortical activity not very widespread and peaks in amplitude at the left precuneus, at the intraparietal lobule on both hemispheres, and on the right prefrontal cortex. At load 4, the activity is more widespread, but also shows amplitude peaking at the precuneus and prefrontal cortex, with the addition of the temporal lobe parcels. At load level 6, the highest amplitudes are found in the somatomotor, prefrontal and frontomedial corticesm as well as in the temporal lobe.

Correlations with hit rates (Figure 2D) showed that most oscillatory signatures were behaviorally relevant, including alpha during encoding, beta during early retention, gamma during late retention, and sustained theta during late retention.

Conclusion

Simultaneous and sequential VWM tasks exhibited a largely shared pattern of oscillatory dynamics, consistent with prior evidence in VWM.^5 This common temporal and spectral structure likely reflects general mechanisms of sensory gating and memory maintenance. In particular, a beta-band desynchronization followed by resynchronization during the early retention period is consistent with dynamic reorganization of task-relevant representations and with the established role of beta oscillations in cognitive control.

Despite these shared dynamics, direct comparisons revealed robust task-dependent differences. Specifically, theta and alpha oscillations were selectively enhanced during the retention period of the sequential VWM task, consistent with theta supporting the organization of information by ordinal position and alpha desynchronization reflecting a release of inhibition over task-relevant cortical regions.

Most oscillatory signatures were behaviorally relevant. However, we observed no consistent association between behavioral performance and sustained alpha activity during the retention period. This dissociation suggests that some oscillatory dynamics primarily reflect task structure, whereas others directly contribute to optimizing behavioral performance.
