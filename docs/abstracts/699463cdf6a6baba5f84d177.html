Introduction

The discovery of the blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal transformed modern brain research. Because functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) cannot easily achieve high spatial and temporal resolution at the same time, most methodological advances have prioritized smaller voxel sizes, often at the expense of temporal resolution.

However, by strategically integrating advanced acquisition methods, optimized experimental designs, and sophisticated analytical approaches, it is possible to preserve both high spatial and temporal fidelity. Recently, Nagy and colleagues introduced a technique that achieves whole-brain temporal resolution below 100 ms across the entire brain by precisely synchronizing stimulus presentation with MRI slice acquisition.¹ In their study, each visual stimulus was timed to coincide with the acquisition of a specific slice. This alignment allowed them to retrospectively reorder slices across volumes, yielding an "effective" repetition time (TR) equal to the slice acquisition time (100 ms in their setup). This technique was also successfully applied to multiband data, reducing the effective TR to 75 ms.² These first two studies served as a proof of principle, involving only two participants and limiting analyses to predefined regions of interest without statistical testing.³ Thus far, no statistical analysis approach has been presented that fully leverages the temporal information available in high-resolution fMRI data, as most existing analyses focus mainly on spatial patterns.³,⁴ Therefore, in this study we formally assess the temporal dynamics of averaged time courses across participants, similar to electroencephalographic (EEG) grand averages, in order to draw group-level inferences.

Methods

In the present study, we applied the reordering method introduced by Nagy and colleagues in a somatosensory paradigm involving N = 31 participants. Precisely timed median nerve stimulation was synchronized with slice-wise multiband acquisition. 

Data were acquired on a 3 T MRI scanner (Magnetom Prisma, Siemens) equipped with a 64-channel head coil. The whole brain was scanned in 36 slices, with an interleaved acquisition order. Each run comprised 412 functional T2*-weighted images (TE = 30 ms; matrix size = 64 × 64; flip angle = 67°; 3 × 3 × 3 mm³, SMS-factor = 4, TR = 540 ms). Therefore, the time required to record one multiband slice package (here four slices at once) was 60 ms.

A somatosensory stimulation paradigm was used in which participants received clearly perceivable single-pulse electrical stimulation to the left median nerve. To enable reordering of the slices based on their delay relative to the stimulus onsets, the stimuli were presented in a time-precise fashion, with the sampling of each multiband “slice package” (slices acquired at the same time) once in randomized order, thus resulting in nine trials for each of the four runs. 

Conceptually, the reordering process can be viewed as combining multiple phase-shifted trials into a single trial with high temporal resolution (Figure 1). Specifically, the temporal gap between two volumes (or the "effective TR") is reduced to the time required to acquire one slice package (i.e., 60 ms). Assuming a relatively constant BOLD response across trials, this results in volumes where the slices are effectively acquired at the same time relative to the stimulus.

To assess the full spatiotemporal dynamics of the BOLD signal following stimulation, a first-level finite impulse response (FIR) model was computed based on the reordered data. In total, signal strength was estimated for each participant in 72 time-bins, each with a width of 180 ms, i.e., average of three ‘effective TRs’. This then allowed for the computation of 72 contrast images, with one contrast against the implicit baseline per bin. Based on these contrast images, a second-level, one-way, repeated measures ANOVA was constructed to test for significance across subjects.

To generate whole-brain statistical maps for each time bin, two one-sided, one-sample t-contrasts (one positive and one negative, respectively) were computed to test each bin against the implicit baseline, analogous to EEG event-related potential (ERP) analyses.

To inspect both the temporal and spatial patterns simultaneously, the t-maps resulting from the 2x72 contrasts were concatenated into two 4D files (one for the positive contrasts and one for the negative contrasts, respectively). After applying a cluster-forming threshold and filtering t-values exceeding p<0.001, 4D clusters of voxels and time points were extracted for negative and positive beta values separately.

To correct for multiple testing and obtain cluster-level statistics, we performed 4D nonparametric permutation tests. This approach allowed us to identify consistent, high-resolution spatiotemporal patterns of somatosensory evoked BOLD responses.

Results

To evaluate the second-level models with 72 time-bins spanning 180 ms each, a within-subject one-way ANOVA was performed for each bin against an implicit baseline. Then, these were tested for significance using a custom 4D permutation test with 1,000 permutations. The permutation tests revealed one significant positive cluster (a signal peak at x = 34, y = 24, z = 4 mm, and 3.78 s with a maximum t-score of 12.96) and four significant negative clusters.

Notably, the significant 4D cluster based on the positive t-test extends across multiple frontal and parietal regions, including the bilateral somatosensory cortices, and persists for over ten seconds after stimulus onset. The first subcluster after stimulus onset is located in the contralateral insular cortex and reflects an early above-mean signal. The 3D subclusters and peak voxels shift, merge, and split throughout the time course. This results in a 4D cluster spanning 555458 voxels/time points (on average, approximately 9000 voxels per time-bin). Figure 2 shows the spatiotemporal dynamics of this cluster as a short movie.

Beginning at 1.62 seconds, the significant 4D cluster encompasses the contralateral primary somatosensory cortex (SI). Starting at 2.16 s, 3D subclusters appear in the bilateral secondary somatosensory cortices (SII) and the ipsilateral SI. Sub-clusters are already present in the bilateral insular cortex (IC) and supplementary motor area. Next, there is activation in the right inferior parietal lobule (IPL), the bilateral frontal poles, and the left superior parietal lobule. These areas are significantly different from baseline at 2.5, 2.7, and 3.8 seconds, respectively. All of the aforementioned areas exhibit a peak signal increase between two and six seconds. The last 3D subcluster in the somatosensory cortices is observable up to 10.44 seconds after the onset of the stimulus in the right SI. However, the parietal and frontal areas are part of the significant 4D cluster up to 10.8 seconds after the onset of the stimulus.

Significant 4D clusters based on negative beta values closely circumvent deactivations in the left IPL, left frontal pole, right IPL, and posterior parietal cortex. Compared to the positive cluster, they appear and peak comparatively late following stimulus onset (at 4.86, 6.66, 6.3, and 4.86 seconds, respectively), and are spatially confined, as can be seen from the cluster sizes (10394, 9038, 5304, and 4640 voxels/time points, respectively). On average, they span approximately 100 to 200 voxels per time-bin. 

Region-of-interest (ROI) analyses within the somatosensory cortex revealed temporal variability in the hemodynamic response. For example, in SII subregions, a sharp signal peak usually appears 4–5 seconds after the stimulus was presented. In contrast, contralateral Brodmann area (BA) 2 shows a slower, more dispersed peak. Signal peaks in contralateral BA 3b and BA 1 occur even earlier, around two to three seconds post-stimulus.

Conclusion

This work presents a whole-brain analysis of the spatiotemporal dynamics of the hemodynamic response at ultra-high temporal resolution following somatosensory stimulation. This was accomplished by combining an enhanced version of the reordering method introduced by Nagy and colleagues (2023) with FIR model analysis and nonparametric 4D permutation tests.1 Finally, nonparametric permutation tests employing 4D clustering provided a feasible way to correct for multiple testing in higher dimensions. 

Permutation tests revealed one significant, extensive 4D cluster based on positive beta values, spanning multiple brain areas, including the somatosensory, frontal, and prefrontal regions. Consistent with hierarchical processing within the somatosensory system, an initial subcluster emerged 1.62 seconds after stimulus onset in the contralateral SI. Starting at 2.16 seconds, additional subclusters appeared in the bilateral SII, accompanied by clusters in the insula and medial frontal areas. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have shown the actual BOLD signal peak to precede the modelled peak of the canonical HRF (appearing at four to six seconds) by up to two seconds.³ Notably, despite the commonly held belief that the BOLD response is sluggish, the first subcluster in SI was detected as early as 1.62 seconds after stimulus onset, persisting for over eight seconds despite the brevity of the stimulus. Four clusters based on negative beta values were identified in the bilateral IPL, the left frontal pole, and the posterior parietal lobule. These clusters likely reflect the well-known deactivations of the default-mode network. ROI analyses within the somatosensory cortex revealed the FIR model's increased sensitivity, especially when there is variability in the hemodynamic response. The variability in the timing and shape of the response across regions violates the assumption of a fixed canonical HRF. In contrast, the FIR-based method is better equipped to accommodate such regional differences, capturing dynamic neural responses that would otherwise be obscured. Although Chinchani and colleagues caution that this data-driven approach may initially result in more false positives due to the increased information evaluated, they posit that the hypotheses regarding the findings will be iteratively refined, akin to the investigation of ERP components in EEG analyses.4 Therefore, the risk of false positives will be reduced in the long term. An alternative option is to adapt error correction to account for the increased risk of false positives introduced by the fourth dimension. This study achieved this goal using custom permutation tests involving 4D clustering. Although computationally expensive, this approach provides a simple solution when no implementations exist that apply random field theory, and therefore family-wise error (FWE) correction, to four-dimensional fMRI data. 

In summary, this study used a new combination of methods to examine the spatiotemporal dynamics of the somatosensory-evoked BOLD signal across the whole brain at a high temporal resolution. This approach offers a promising means of bridging the gap between methods traditionally optimized for spatial or temporal resolution. It does so by applying a time-resolved hemodynamic analysis analogous to EEG grand-averaging. Thus, it has considerable potential to advance our understanding of the temporal unfolding of cognitive processes in the human brain.
