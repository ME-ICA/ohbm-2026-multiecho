Introduction

Gestational age plays a crucial role in neurodevelopment, and very preterm (VPT) birth may disrupt typical cortical maturation and has been linked to an elevated risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. As such, VPT birth presents a model for investigating how early cortical disruptions shape subsequent cognitive, and behavioural outcomes, which can provide critical insights into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying neurodevelopmental disorders, enabling the identification of neuroimaging biomarkers and the development of targeted strategies for early intervention.

Methods

The cortical morphology used in this study include cortical thickness, sulcal depth and surface area. Surface reconstruction and registration [1-3] is shown in Figure 1. In this study, we first compared cortical morphology across different developmental stages between 357 very preterm (VPT) individuals born before 33 weeks of gestation and 717 full-term controls born at 37–42 weeks, using three cross-sectional datasets: 622 neonates from the Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) [4], 239 children from the Brain, Immunity, and Psychopathology following Very Preterm Birth (BIPP) study, and 213 adults from the University College London Hospital (UCLH) cohort. Second, in a fourth infant cohort of 414 VPT neonates who were recruited into the Evaluation of Preterm Imaging Study (ePrime) [5], we examined the relationships between cortical alterations and clinical risk factors, including gestational age, birth weight, duration of mechanical ventilation, duration of continuous positive airway pressure, and duration of total parenteral nutrition. This was done using sparse canonical correlation analysis. Furthermore, we assessed the associations between cortical alterations and cognitive, language, and motor outcomes at 18-24 months assessed using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development–Third Edition (Bayley-III) in ePrime.

Results

We identified a consistent cortical footprint in VPT individuals across development, characterized by smaller surface areas and reduced cortical folding in the frontal, temporal, parietal and insular regions (Figure 2a). Notably, group differences in surface area and sulcal depth showed a trend of amplification from the neonatal period to childhood and adulthood. In contrast, cortical thickness alterations varied across developmental stages (Figure 2b). Furthermore, the cortical footprint was associated with clinical risk factors, including gestational age, birth weight, and the duration of respiratory and nutritional support, and later motor development (Figure 2c).

Conclusion

The findings revealed a persistent VPT cortical footprint across development and suggested neonatal surface area and cortical folding as potential neuroimaging biomarkers for later motor development. Given the critical role of early motor development in shaping environmental interactions and supporting later cognitive and behavioural function, these findings also offer mechanistic insight into how very preterm birth may lead to long-term neurodevelopmental and psychiatric difficulties.
