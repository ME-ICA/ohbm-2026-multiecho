Introduction

Transcranial and intranasal photobiomodulation (tPBM, iPBM) are neuromodulation approaches that non-invasively deliver near-infrared (NIR) light to the brain to enhance neural activity. Both approaches act primarily via the photoexcitation of mitochondrial chromophores, up-regulating oxidative metabolism and ATP synthesis [1], but they use distinct optical entry routes. Although prior work has demonstrated that both routes of delivery can alter EEG dynamics, it remains unknown how efficiently the brain converts the delivered optical energy (dose) into measurable electrophysiological change. As tPBM and iPBM are often associated with markedly different irradiances, a dose-normalized metric is required to meaningfully compare their respective neuromodulatory impact.

    Here, we quantify the efficiency of energy deposition in terms of the percent-change in EEG power per joule/cm2 delivered, allowing a direct comparison of the brain's responsiveness to tPBM and iPBM across irradiances. 

Methods

        Healthy young adults (N=46, 20-32 years; 24M/22F) without neurological/physiological disorders or substance use were recruited from Baycrest’s Participant Database. Participants were randomly placed in one of three protocols, determining the parameters for each of their eight EEG-PBM recordings: two wavelengths, two pulsation frequencies, and three irradiances (Figure 1c), chosen based on tissue penetration, safety profiles, and prior effectiveness. Participants sat and watched naturalistic stimulus videos to minimize drowsiness and control brain state. Each recording had a PRE-DURING-POST block stimulus design (4 minutes each; Figure 1b). The PRE period served as a within-subject baseline in the absence of sham stimulation. During stimulation, NIR light was delivered either through a custom headpiece (tPBM) on the right forehead or a nosepiece clipped to the right nostril (iPBM) via a 400 µm fiber cable and MDL-III lasers (Vielight Inc., Toronto, Canada; Figure 1a), targeting the right prefrontal cortex (rPFC). MR thermometry and subject reports confirmed no intracranial heating. Parameters were regulated remotely so participants were blinded to the protocols, light delivery method, and timing.

        EEG was recorded at 1,000 Hz with a 256-channel HydroCel Geodesic Sensor Net Amp 400 amplifier (Magstim Inc., Roseville, MN, USA) using saline electrodes (reference: Cz, <50 kΩ impedance, 0.024 μV amplitude resolution, 10-20 international placement system). A 20-minute break separated recordings with the same stimulus to avoid carryover effects. The data pipeline involved preprocessing and calculating the dose-efficiency of tPBM and iPBM using MATLAB (Mathworks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) and EEGLAB 2023.1.

        Preprocessing included removing face electrodes, high-pass filtering (1 Hz), resampling (125 Hz), channel rejection (clean_rawdata; ChannelCriterion=0.8, FlatlineCriterion=5, LineNoiseCriterion=4), and Independent Component Analysis (extended infomax) to remove eye movement artifacts, muscle activity, or line noise with probability>0.9.

        Data were segmented into 1-minute, non-overlapping epochs. In each epoch, the power spectral density (PSD) was estimated using Welch’s method (pwelch; 4-s Hanning window, 50% overlap). Band-limited absolute power was then computed by integrating the PSD curve within each frequency range (trapz)  (delta: 1-4 Hz, theta: 4-8 Hz, alpha: 8-12 Hz, beta: 12-30 Hz, low gamma: 30-50 Hz). For each channel and epoch, band-limited power was expressed as a percent change relative to the mean power across the PRE baseline (Min 1-4). 

        In each dataset, EEG responses were summarized by averaging the percent-change power across all electrodes within the latter half of the DURING (Min 6-8) and POST (Min 10-12) windows. These responses were then converted into an energy-normalized metric, “efficiency”, defined as the percent-change in power divided by the energy delivered. Energy (J/cm2) was computed by multiplying the irradiance (mW/cm2) divided by 1000, the stimulation duration (240 s) and the duty cycle of pulsed delivery (50%).

  Efficiency therefore reflects the percent-change in EEG power per joule/cm2 delivered, enabling a direct, within-subject comparison of tPBM and iPBM even though they use different physical irradiances. These values were grouped by wavelength (808/1064 nm) and irradiance for tPBM and iPBM separately. For each band, paired boxplots displayed DURING and POST efficiency distributions at each irradiance level. Statistical testing assessed differences between irradiance levels within each window using two-sample t-tests on the efficiency distributions. P-values were corrected with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate control (q<0.05). Only FDR-significant differences are annotated and interpreted. 

Results

        Figure 2 summarizes the dose-efficiency of tPBM and iPBM on EEG band power. Across all wavelengths, frequency bands, and irradiances, iPBM was consistently at least an order of magnitude more efficient than tPBM, indicating substantially greater modulation of EEG power per joule/cm2 delivered. During 808-nm stimulation, the mean efficiency was 7.98 for iPBM versus 0.39 for tPBM; after 808-nm stimulation, 13.41 versus 0.65. During 1064-nm stimulation, mean efficiency was 6.78 for iPBM versus 0.40 for tPBM; after 1064-nm stimulation, 9.90 versus 0.65.

        Figure 2a details efficiency patterns at 808 nm. For tPBM, the highest irradiance (200 mW/cm2) was usually least efficient, in both DURING and POST windows, particularly in theta, alpha, beta, and gamma bands. Lower-frequency bands (theta, alpha) tended to show maximal efficiency at 100 mW/cm2, whereas the intermediate dose (150 mW/cm2) yielded the highest efficiency in higher-frequency beta and gamma bands, consistent with a biphasic dose-response. For iPBM at 808 nm, lower irradiances (5 and 7 mW/cm2) were more energy-efficient than 9 mW/cm² during and after stimulation across all bands.

        Figure 2b focuses on the efficiency at 1064 nm. For tPBM, 100 and 150 mW/cm2 achieved better efficiency than 200 mW/cm2 in most bands, although the contrast between 100 and 150 mW/cm2 was more modest, suggesting a broader plateau of near-optimal efficiency. For iPBM at 1064 nm, lower irradiances preserved their advantage, with 5 (and often 7) mW/cm2 showing higher efficiency than 9 mW/cm2 in most bands, except alpha, where irradiance differences were attenuated. Overall, the results reveal robust low-dose efficiency for iPBM and a mid-dose optimum for tPBM across both wavelengths.

Conclusion

        Our work shows that iPBM is at least an order of magnitude more energy-efficient than tPBM in modulating electrical activity in healthy brains. This advantage was robust across two commonly used PBM wavelengths (808 and 1064 nm) and for both online (Min 6-8) and offline (Min 10-12) time windows. In both delivery routes, the highest irradiance was not the most efficient at inducing EEG power changes.

        For tPBM, efficiency followed a biphasic pattern. In higher-frequency beta and gamma bands, 150 mW/cm2 produced the highest efficiency during and after stimulation, whereas 100 mW/cm2 was more efficient in lower-frequency bands. In contrast, iPBM showed a consistent low-irradiance advantage: 5, and often 7, mW/cm2 outperformed 9 mW/cm2, particularly in beta/gamma bands. These patterns suggest that tPBM operates on a mid-to-high segment of the dose-response curve, where too little energy is ineffective and too much energy suppresses photochemical responsiveness, while for iPBM, smaller amounts of energy yield large biological returns per joule, producing a descending efficiency curve.

        Wavelength did not qualitatively alter these efficiency patterns but modulated the contrast between irradiance levels. The biphasic behaviour was clearest at 808 nm, especially in beta and gamma bands, while 1064 nm showed a broader plateau between 100 and 150 mW/cm2 in those bands. Conversely, for iPBM, the low-dose efficiency advantage was comparably strong at both wavelengths, implying that delivery route primarily determines the appropriate irradiance range, while wavelength influences spectral sensitivity within that range. Thus, once wavelength is chosen for a given application, irradiance should be carefully calibrated when designing PBM protocols.

The efficiency gap between tPBM and iPBM likely reflects anatomical and optical advantages rather than parameter tuning alone. Intranasal light bypasses the thick cranial bone of the forehead and instead traverses the cribriform plate, a thin, perforated bony layer separating the nasal cavity from ventromedial prefrontal and olfactory regions [2]. Unlike typical skull thickness (~5-7 mm), the cribriform plate is fractions of a millimetre thick and contains channels for olfactory nerves, creating a relatively direct optical pathway with minimal attenuation [3]. These efficiency findings position iPBM as a safe, non-invasive, portable, easy-to-use, and markedly more energy-efficient modality for neuromodulation, which is especially relevant in clinical populations where PBM is being explored for stroke, depression, and neurodegeneration.
