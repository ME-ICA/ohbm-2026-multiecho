Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common type of dementia and one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide1. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI), which is considered a transitional stage between normal aging and AD, has been a subject of increasing research focus. A key feature of AD and its progression from MCI is the accumulation of tau protein, which plays a critical role in the pathological changes observed in the brain. Numerous studies have constructed network propagation models based on tau protein. The pattern of atrophy follows a similar trajectory to that of tau, starting in the medial temporal and fusiform regions, before spreading to the posterior temporal and parietal cortices, and ultimately to the frontal cortex2. Connectomes derived from functional connectivity have commonly been used as the foundation for disease propagation models3. Other studies have used structural white matter connectomes derived from MR tractography, which more directly model the substrate for transsynaptic spread4. However, despite the wealth of knowledge on tau protein and its propagation, much of the existing research has either focused on specific brain regions or has not examined network propagation pathways and propagation timing from the perspective of large-scale brain activation. In this study, we employed a linear threshold model5 to characterize the brain network propagation patterns and, based on this, obtained the spreading times between nodes. Subsequently, we used a normative model to quantify the heterogeneity of propagation deviations from normal aging in the MCI group and explored the abnormal propagation pathways in key brain networks within the MCI group.

Methods

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data from 1,016 participants were collected from two separate datasets. The first dataset, which comprised 527 healthy individuals, was utilized to create a normative model and was sourced from the Cambridge Centre for Aging and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN) repository (available at http://www.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/datasets/camcan/). The second dataset contained 241 cognitively normal (NC) individuals and 248 patients diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment retrieved from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database.

The fMRI data were preprocessed using SPM12 as implemented in the Neuroscience Information Toolbox (http://www.neuro.uestc.edu.cn/NIT.html). Functional connectivity (FC) was computed among the average time series of 400 cortical and 16 subcortical regions of interest covering the entire brain, resulting in a 416 × 416 FC matrix for each participant. We employed a linear threshold model (LTM) to represent how each node in the network adopts a state based on the states of its neighboring nodes. This model characterizes the propagation time from the activation of node i to the activation of node j. ComBat method was used to remove the effects of different site biases. Next, we developed normative models to predict the spreading time from node i to node j, incorporating sex and age as covariates, using gaussian process regression on a large healthy reference cohort from CamCAN. We applied the model trained on CamCAN to make predictions on the ADNI dataset and calculated the deviation between the predicted values and the observed values:

Z=(y_predicted-y_observed)/√(σ_1^2+σ_2^2 )

where σ_1 is the prediction uncertainty, and σ_2 is the variance obtained from the normal distribution. Finally, we compared the differences in the average deviation of each brain network between the MCI and NC groups, as well as the differences in spreading time. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate method. The analysis flowchart is shown in Figure 1.

Results

We first compared the average propagation deviations of each network between the MCI and NC groups using a two-sample T test (Figure.2A). We found that the average propagation deviation starting from the subcortical network was higher in the MCI group compared to the NC group (T=2.91, P=0.0037), whereas the average propagation deviation starting from the temporal network was lower in the MCI group than in the NC group (T=-2.62, P=0.0090). In addition, within the MCI group, the average propagation deviation toward the sensorimotor network was significantly higher than in the NC group (T = 2.44, P = 0.0149), while the average propagation deviation toward the ventral attention network was significantly lower (T = −2.68, P = 0.0075).

Next, we analyzed the differences in propagation times across the networks that showed significant deviations between the MCI and NC groups. Specifically, with respect to network outflow times, we found that, compared to the NC group, the MCI group exhibited reduced propagation times from the subcortical network to the frontoparietal network (control network) and the default mode network (Figure.2B). Conversely, propagation times from the temporal network to the dorsal attention network, ventral attention network, as well as parts of the frontoparietal network and default mode network were increased in the MCI group (Figure.2C). Regarding network inflow times, we observed that, compared to the NC group, the spreading time to the sensorimotor network within the network itself was increased in the MCI group. However, the inflow time to the sensorimotor network from the frontoparietal and attention networks was reduced (Figure.2D). Similarly, we found that the propagation time within the ventral attention network was increased, while the inflow time from the frontoparietal and temporal networks to the ventral attention network was also increased. In contrast, the inflow time from deep brain regions to the ventral attention network was decreased in the MCI group (Figure.2E).

Conclusion

This study identified the heterogeneity of network propagation in the MCI group compared to the NC group by analyzing the deviation patterns from normative models of network propagation. The results revealed significant changes in brain network propagation patterns in the MCI group. Further analysis revealed that changes in both network outflow and inflow times were especially pronounced in the MCI group. These findings suggest that MCI not only affects the internal propagation patterns within brain regions but also influences the directional changes in information transfer between different networks. Our study not only offers new insights into the early diagnosis of MCI but also provides a theoretical foundation for future strategies aimed at intervening and restoring brain network function.
