Introduction

Recent neuroimaging studies indicate that cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) exhibits rhythmic fluctuations coupled with neural activity, potentially facilitating metabolic waste clearance via the glymphatic system during sleep and rest1. CSF dynamics are closely linked to neural low-frequency oscillations (0.01–0.1 Hz) associated with slow-wave activity in resting-state networks4. This suggests a close relationship between brain function and physiological maintenance through activity-driven CSF pulsations. Mindfulness meditation, characterized by nonjudgmental present-moment awareness, engages neural systems involved in attention, interoception, and self-referential processing. Despite extensive research on meditation-related neural changes, its influence on brain fluid dynamics remains largely unexplored. As meditation involves altered respiration and enhanced interoceptive awareness—both affecting intracranial pressure and CSF circulation—it offers a framework for examining voluntary neuro-fluid modulation. In this study, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) was used to examine temporal coupling between regional brain activity (BOLD) and CSF flow at the fourth ventricle across four physiological conditions: eyes-closed, eyes-open, breath meditation, and sound meditation. We characterized state-dependent temporal relationships between cortical activity and CSF oscillations and assessed whether systematic phase relationships emerge in given mental states. We hypothesized that meditation, particularly breath-focused practice, would show enhanced and more spatially consistent brain–CSF coupling relative to passive rest, especially in attention- and interoception-related regions. These findings may provide insight into the physiological correlates of meditative practice and large-scale brain dynamics.

Methods

2.1 Participants

Twenty-four right-handed healthy adults (14 females, age 24.6 ± 3.2 years) were recruited for the study, with written consents under local IRB approval.
Before MRI scans, participants were pretrained to perform the breath and sound mindfulness according to experimental design. Among them, 11 were beginners (less than 20 hours experience), and 13 were experienced mindfulness practitioners (above 20 hours). 

2.2 MRI Data Acquisition

Imaging was conducted on a 3T uMR 790 UIH scanner with either a 32-channel or 24-channel head coil (mainly used). T1-weighted structural images were acquired at resolution of 0.8*0.8*0.8mm^3.(TI/TR/TE = 1000/7.3/3.1 ms, flip angle = 8°, matrix = 208 × 320 × 320)

Functional MRI data were collected using multiband echo-planar imaging sequence (TR/TE = 528/30 ms, multiband factor = 7, flip angle = 30°, resolution = 2.5 × 2.5 mm^2, slice thickness = 2.5 mm) during six separate  runs, which were performed in a fixed order as illustrated in FigureA. Note that the last slice of fMRI acquisition was placed at the fourth ventricle. 

2.3 Data Preprocessing

Structural MRI parcellation was performed using FreeSurfer version 7.0 with the Desikan-Killiany (DK) atlas. Preprocessing steps included slice-timing correction (FSL) on Raw fMRI for interleaved acquisition, realignment for motion correction (FSL or SPM), and linear detrending to remove low-frequency drifts. Volumes were optionally smoothed with a 4 × 4 × 4 mm Gaussian kernel. Functional images were then coregistered to individual T1-weighted structural images using SPM.

BOLD time series were extracted by averaging voxel signals within each atlas-defined ROI. CSF signals were extracted from the bottom slice of fMRI data with manually drawn ROI in the fourth ventricle. A temporal bandpass filter (0.01–0.075 Hz) was applied to isolate low-frequency fluctuations. The time derivative (-dx/dt) of these low-frequency BOLD signals was computed for dynamic analysis (written as -BOLD').

2.4 CSF Signal Spectral Analysis

Ventricular CSF time series were subjected to power spectral density (PSD) estimation under 4 conditions. One-tailed paired t-tests were performed between conditions for CSF PSD at pre-defined frequency band. Significance threshold was set at α=0.05 with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

2.5 Time-Shifted CSF–BOLD Correlation and Peak-Lag Selection

Time-shifted correlation analysis was conducted between the time derivative of low-frequency regional -BOLD' signals (−dx/dt) and the ventricular CSF time series.

For each participant and each of the four states, Pearson correlations were computed across lags of −19 to +19 time points (±10.03 s, TR = 0.528 s), resulting in a 39-lag × 108-ROI matrix per run (shift_list = −19:19).

A group-averaged correlation matrix (108 × 39) was computed for each condition by averaging across all 24 participants. For each of the 108 regions, the lag yielding the maximum absolute correlation coefficient was identified as the peak lag.

Regions were retained only if they fulfilled both of the following stringent criteria: 

(1) at least 18/24 participants exhibited a significant correlation (p < 0.05, uncorrected) at that specific peak lag;  

(2) the group-averaged absolute correlation coefficient at the peak lag exceeded |r| > 0.18.

2.6 Comparison based on Mindfulness Training Experience

For each of the four experimental conditions, the total average power of the ventricular CSF time series was calculated separately for beginners and experienced groups illustrated in section 2.1.

Between-Group Differences in CSF-BOLD Coupling Strength

Using only the brain regions and their corresponding group-level peak lags identified as significant in Section 2.5, the mean CSF-BOLD correlation coefficient was calculated separately for the beginners and experienced subgroups to assess whether the strength of CSF–BOLD coupling in previously identified key regions differs significantly based on training experience.

Results

3.1 Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Power Spectral Density Across States

Group-averaged CSF power spectral density (PSD) curves for the four experimental conditions are shown in Figure c . 

The 0–0.01Hz band peak fall substantially below typical adult respiration frequencies and cardiac-related mechanical noise, aligning closely with intrinsic low-frequency neural fluctuations. The frequency band of 0.18-0.25Hz is considered close to adult respiration frequency, suggesting that these CSF spectral components predominantly reflect neurogenic and breathing rhythm rather than purely  mechanical physiological sources. For T-test result, see Figure d.

3.2 Time-Shifted Coupling Between CSF Flow and -BOLD’ Signal Dynamics

This procedure yielded a small but highly consistent set of regions exhibiting robust CSF-BOLD coupling (Figure e). Notably, resting states (EC and EO) showed no consistent coupling. Both breath meditation and sound meditation showed increased coupling between CSF flow and the paracentral gyrus (ctx-rh-paracentral or ctx-lh-paracentral). The breath meditation uniquely exhibited coupling in the right insula (ctx-rh-insula), a region critically associated with interoception (the sense of the body's internal state) and breathing awareness. The sound meditation showed four other CSF-coupled brain regions: left postcentral (ctx-lh-postcentral), left precentral (ctx-lh-precentral), and left/right paracentral ( ctx-lh-paracentral and ctx-rh-paracentral).

Furthermore, the time point of maximum absolute correlation coefficient was identified as the peak lag, and for all observed coupled cortical regions, the lag was consistently negative (ranging from -7 to -9 time points, as shown in the original data). This indicates that the CSF signal consistently lagged behind the -BOLD’ signal negative derivative by approximately 7 to 9 time points (approximately 3.7 - 4.8 s).

3.3 Group Comparison between beginner and experienced subgroups

The experienced group showed slightly lower CSF average power than the beginner group, but this difference was not statistically significant across any condition (one-tail t-test,p > 0.05). 

The mean CSF-BOLD correlation coefficients in all retained brain regions were numerically lower in the experienced group compared to the beginner group. However, this difference in coupling strength was not statistically significant for any condition or region (one-tail t-test,p > 0.05).

Conclusion

Our study reveals state-dependent patterns in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics through spectral analysis of CSF pulsations. In the low-frequency band (0–0.1 Hz), spectral power during eyes-open (EO) rest was significantly lower than in the other three conditions. Given that meditation states are also performed with eyes closed, the current observations at low frequency band suggest that EC condition with or without mindfulness state, may shift the brain toward a more "sleep-like" state, elevating low-frequency CSF fluctuation pattern to promote the clearance of metabolic waste described in prior research¹,². On the other hand, the breath condition showed markedly higher spectral power than the other three conditions in the respiratory band (0.18–0.25 Hz), which overlaps with typical adult breathing rates, indicating that mindful breathing—to fulfill guided respiratory tasks—generates CSF pulsations synchronized with respiratory rhythms, suggesting respiration-driven enhancement of CSF flow.

The differences between meditation states and eyes-closed resting state arise at the CSF-BOLD coupling analysis. While neither eyes-open nor eyes-closed states show consistent brain regions with CSF-BOLD coupling, in mindfulness states several regions become consistent: the right paracentral lobule and insula for breath meditation, and bilateral paracentral lobule with left postcentral and precentral gyri for sound meditation. These areas overlap with mindfulness neuroimaging findings³. These results suggest that mindfulness meditation may focus time-lagged synchronization between CSF pulsations and activity in attention-related networks, thus influencing the activities of brain regions related to mindfulness, self-perception, and sensorimotor areas through the attention mechanism.

Future studies with larger samples and greater contrasts in practice duration could test  whether lower correlation magnitudes indicate more efficient neural processing and reduced energetic demands—a pattern consistent with the "neural efficiency hypothesis" observed in experienced meditators.
