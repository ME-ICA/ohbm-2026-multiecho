Introduction

MRI harmonisation is the process by which technical variation is removed from different batches of data (e.g., from different scanners or sites) to make the images or imaging-derived measures more comparable with each other. The overall efficacy of MRI harmonisation should be measured in two ways, through the removal of the batch effect and through the preservation of biological signal within the data, such as consistent age effects pre- and post-harmonisation. A batch effect can manifest as an additive effect on the mean, a multiplicative effect on the variance, or differences in the distributions between batches that are not driven by biological factors1.

Many methods exist for harmonising measures derived from MRI images (e.g. imaging derived phenotypes - IDPs, organised in tabular format), such as classic regression-based approaches (e.g., General linear or mixed effects model), ComBat2,3 and its variants, as well as a range of machine learning techniques4. However, choosing the most appropriate method for specific scenarios is difficult, as each method has underlying assumptions as well as advantages and limitations. The choice is further complicated by inconsistent reporting of harmonisation efficacy both when harmonisation methods are developed or applied. Currently, no standard guidelines exist to help researchers assess the effect of batch on the MRI measures of interest (to guide which harmonisation method is more suitable) or assess whether harmonisation has succeeded4. To address this gap, we present here an open-source software tool for the streamlined analysis and reporting of harmonisation efficacy for a variety of research applications.

Methods

The software was developed as a Python library for the automated diagnosis of batch effects in tabular MRI measures. We selected a comprehensive set of tests to characterise the batch effect (additive, multiplicative, and distributional). The tests included are widely used, although often in isolation, hence not necessarily providing a good description of the batch effect on the data. The tool is designed for use both before and after harmonisation, providing a clear indication of the nature and severity of batch effects across IDPs, identifying the batch pairs showing the largest difference, and offering standardised metrics to evaluate how effectively harmonisation has preserved biological signal. The library integrates easily into existing analysis workflows, offering reproducible diagnostics for different harmonisation methods across research applications (Figure 1).

Two modules are included: one for cross-sectional datasets, where each participant is unique, and one for longitudinal datasets which assumes that within-subject changes across timepoints must be preserved. Within the cross-sectional module, batch effects are assessed via IDP-wise comparisons of mean and variance using Cohen’s d, Levene’s test, and linear modelling, alongside distributional comparisons based on the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Multivariate batch effects are further evaluated using the Mahalanobis distance between batch pairs and principal component analysis. The preservation of biological information is assessed by examining the consistency of covariate effects (e.g., age effects) before and after harmonisation. For longitudinal data, the tool incorporates mixed-effects modelling with subject-specific random terms to ensure that individual trajectories are maintained, while still assessing batch differences using comparable statistical and visual diagnostics.

Additionally, while the novelty of this project largely comes from improving harmonisation workflows through ease of analysis and standardised reporting, we also include several widely used and well-validated harmonisation methods, such as ComBat and its extensions (Long-, M- and GAM variants) as well as regression approaches. Their inclusion in the library enables users to perform complete end-to-end harmonisation, while still giving the option to use in conjunction with other harmonisation methods, allowing direct comparison and validation by using the diagnostic reports from different methods.

As part of the package, we also provide an easy-to-use module that generates simulated datasets with controlled batch effect severity levels, enabling users to test harmonisation methods across different sample and batch sizes. We used this tool, in combination with real data from the UK Biobank and ON-Harmony study5 to validate the diagnostic module's ability to detect known batch effects with varying characteristics and severities.

Results

We have published version 1.0.0 of the package on GitHub (https://github.com/Jake-Turnbull/HarmonizationDiagnostics) and provided example outputs of the cross-sectional report in a simulated scenario before and after applying harmonisation using ComBat. In this simulated example, we generated five unique batches, each comprising 200 subjects and 50 IDP measures, with added covariate effects (age, sex and diagnosis) and a simulated additive and multiplicative batch effect which increase progressively in severity with batch number. The results of a few selected diagnostic tests are shown in Figure 2A (pre-harmonisation) and in Figure 2B (post-harmonisation).   In Figure 2A, strong correlations between batch with the first principal component are observed, alongside large multivariate differences in batch means as indicated by the magnitude of the Mahalanobis distance. Batch differences are also evident from distinct clustering by batch in the PCA projection and from differences in the multivariate z-score distributions. In contrast, Figure 2B shows substantially weaker correlations of batch with the first principal component, stronger associations with covariates and decreased Mahalanobis distance between batches. Additionally, batches no longer form distinct clusters in the principal component projection and exhibit nearly identical Z-score distributions, indicating successful removal of the batch effect and preservation of the underlying structure and biological information of the data.

Conclusion

Harmonisation is often an essential step when using multisite MRI data, but careful analysis, both pre- and post-harmonisation, is needed to understand the severity and nature of batch differences. We present an open-source library that streamlines this process by providing standardised diagnostics and clear, reproducible reporting using well-established analytical methods. These tools allow researchers to directly evaluate harmonisation efficacy, compare different approaches and avoid potentially inappropriate methods. By ensuring the choice in harmonisation method is guided by the characteristics of the dataset, we aim to support more reproducible, transparent and robust workflows and improve the overall quality of multisite MRI research.
