Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) involves intrusion, avoidance, negative cognitions/mood, and hyperarousal. Neuroimaging findings are heterogeneous, partly because studies often mix trauma types and chronicity.1 Combat-related PTSD is a clinically important subtype with high cumulative trauma burden and comorbidity. While previous studies have shown reduced hippocampal volumes in PTSD, microstructural mechanisms across gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) remain unclear.

Diffusion MRI indexes WM microstructure, most commonly fractional anisotropy (FA), yet prior PTSD studies report cohort heterogeneity and analytic variability.2 These mixed findings raise the possibility that WM alterations in PTSD do not occur in isolation, but rather in relation to cortical microstructural changes within connected networks. T1-weighted MRI also enables GM-to-WM signal intensity contrast (GWC), a putative proxy for microstructure near the gray–white boundary including intracortical myelination and boundary integrity, that may detect subtle cortical alterations beyond conventional volumetric measures. The gray–white interface represents a critical transitional zone linking cortical processing with distributed WM pathways, yet its role in PTSD has received limited attention. 

Despite the conceptual relevance of both diffusion- and T1-derived measures to network-level pathology, these modalities have typically been examined separately, leaving it unclear whether cortical boundary alterations and WM microstructure are coordinated or independent processes in PTSD. This study aimed to identify convergent structural signatures of combat-related PTSD spanning the gray–white boundary and distributed WM circuitry using GWC and FA in.

Methods

Data were obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative–Department of Defense (ADNI-DOD), a multicenter observational cohort of Vietnam War Veterans (PTSD: 52 subjects, controls: 40 subjects; IRB: 1041849-202502-BM-024-01). We analyzed combat-exposed participants with PTSD (without TBI) and combat-exposed controls without PTSD/TBI. Standard ADNI-DOD eligibility criteria were applied. Additional quality-control exclusions were applied for major processing failures and non-harmonized T1 acquisitions to ensure sequence homogeneity for GWC estimation.

High-resolution 3D T1-weighted images were acquired on 3T GE scanners using an inversion-recovery spoiled gradient-recalled echo (IR-SPGR) sequence (repetition time 6.98–7.70 ms; echo time 2.84–3.19 ms; inversion time 400 ms; 256 × 256; resolution 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.2 mm; flip angle 11°). T1-weighted images were preprocessed with the automated cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentation pipeline implemented in FreeSurfer version 7.4.1. This process included intensity normalization, skull stripping, segmentation of GM and WM using geometric features derived from intensity gradients, followed by vertex-wise cortical surface reconstruction to delineate both the WM and pial surfaces.2 GM intensity (GMI) was sampled at each vertex from a surface positioned at 30% depth into the cortical ribbon from the white surface toward the pial surface, and WM intensity (WMI) was sampled 1.0 mm below the white surface. Vertex-wise GWC was computed as ((WMI-GMI)/((WMI+GMI)/2) × 100(%)) and resampled to the fsaverage template.

Group differences in GWC were tested using a vertex-wise GLM in FreeSurfer. The model included group (PTSD group = 1, control group = 0) as the primary effect of interest; age served as a covariate. Interaction term between group and age was included to adjust for the possibility that group differences in GWC varied by participant age. This approach allowed evaluation of group effects while considering potential moderating influences of demographic variables. The GLM was defined by the following equation:

GWC = β0 + β1∗Group + β2∗Age + β3∗(Group×Age)

Multiple comparisons were controlled using cluster-wise correction based on precomputed Monte Carlo simulation (10,000 iterations; cluster-forming threshold 1.3). Significant clusters were labeled using the Desikan–Killiany atlas.3 Partial eta-squared (ηp²) for group effects was computed from cluster summaries in SPSS v28.0 using the same model specification.

FA maps were obtained directly from ADNI-DOD. To skull-strip FA, a diffusion-derived brain mask was generated from the axial DWI using MRtrix3 and applied to the FA map to produce a brain-extracted FA image.4 The brain-extracted FA image was registered to MNI space using FSL v6.0.7.1, with affine initialization (FLIRT) followed by nonlinear registration (FNIRT). Mean FA was extracted in standard space within JHU atlas-defined WM ROIs (JHU ICBM-DTI-81 labels) by averaging FA across voxels in each label, and ROI overlays were visually inspected for plausible alignment.

For each significant GWC group-difference cluster, subject-level mean GWC was correlated with JHU ROI mean FA values using Pearson correlations in SPSS v28.0 within the PTSD group. P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) across ROIs tested per cluster. Participants without FA data in the PTSD group (n = 4) were excluded from correlation analyses.

Results

Vertex-wise GLM analyses revealed five clusters in which PTSD group showed significantly higher GWC than the control group after cluster-wise multiple comparison correction (cluster-wise p-value < 0.05). These clusters were located in the left supramarginal and pars triangularis regions and in the right caudal middle frontal, supramarginal, and superior parietal regions. Effect sizes for these clusters were large (ηp2 > 0.14), suggesting that the observed group differences in GWC are meaningful beyond mere statistical significance (Figure 1 and Table 1).

Within the PTSD group, significant positive GWC–FA associations (FDR corrected) were observed for the left pars triangularis cluster with FA in the genu (r = 0.422, p = 0.029) and splenium (r = 0.357, p = 0.043) of the corpus callosum, left anterior limb of the internal capsule (r = 0.426, p = 0.029), left anterior corona radiata (r = 0.415, p = 0.029), left corticospinal tract (r = 0.389, p = 0.033), left cerebral peduncle (r = 0.393, p = 0.033), left superior cerebellar peduncle (r = 0.370, p = 0.037), and left fornix/stria terminalis (r = 0.373, p = 0.037). In addition, the right caudal middle frontal cluster showed significant correlations with FA in the right retrolenticular part of the internal capsule (r = 0.420, p = 0.039) and sagittal stratum (r = 0.427, p = 0.039). No significant correlations were detected for the remaining clusters, indicating that cross-modality coupling was selective rather than widespread (Table 2).

Conclusion

In the combat-enriched ADNI-DOD cohort, we identified robust cortical GWC abnormalities in PTSD. Compared to controls, PTSD showed significantly higher GWC across five clusters spanning left supramarginal and pars triangularis, and right caudal middle frontal, supramarginal, and superior parietal cortex after cluster-wise correction. The large effect sizes (ηp² > 0.14) indicate that these GWC differences are not only statistically reliable but also meaningful in magnitude. Because GWC is derived from intensity sampling across the gray–white boundary, these findings suggest that combat-related PTSD is associated with systematic alterations at the cortical interface—potentially reflecting changes in microstructural properties near the boundary (e.g., intracortical myelination, boundary sharpness, or tissue composition) that are not fully captured by conventional volumetric measures. These results underscore GWC as a sensitive cortical marker for detecting boundary-level alterations in combat-related PTSD.

Importantly, cross-modality associations between cortical GWC and FA were not widespread but instead selective within the PTSD group. Significant positive GWC–FA coupling (BH-FDR corrected) was observed only for the left pars triangularis cluster and the right caudal middle frontal cluster, and only with a limited subset of JHU-defined tracts. In contrast, other GWC group-difference clusters, despite large between-group effects did not show detectable coupling with FA. This pattern suggests that cortical GWC alterations in PTSD can occur relatively independently of broad WM microstructural variation, and when coupling is present, it may reflect more specific structural relationships between localized cortical boundary changes and particular WM pathways rather than a global, uniform gray–white disruption. The presence of GWC–FA coupling in the left pars triangularis, a region central to prefrontal control and emotion regulation, suggests that cross-modality structural coordination may be most evident in cortical hubs with a key role in fronto-limbic network regulation. These findings support a model of compartment-specific structural reorganization in PTSD, in which cortical boundary alterations and WM microstructure may follow partially dissociable yet selectively coordinated trajectories rather than a single, uniform pathological process. Such selectivity is consistent with prior reports of heterogeneity in PTSD neuroimaging findings and supports the rationale for focusing on more homogeneous trauma subtypes when seeking reproducible biomarkers. By integrating surface-based cortical boundary measures with tract-level diffusion metrics, this study advances a network-oriented framework for characterizing structural brain alterations in combat-related PTSD.

Future work should test the reproducibility of these findings in independent combat-exposed cohorts, evaluate longitudinal trajectories to distinguish vulnerability from consequence, and refine multimodal models that integrate cortical boundary measures with tract-level diffusion features to better characterize the structural circuitry most relevant to PTSD symptomatology and neurobiological mechanisms.
