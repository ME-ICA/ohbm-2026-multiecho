Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) produces motor dysfunction through progressive nigrostriatal degeneration and pathological cortical hyperexcitability. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus is clinically effective but invasive and accessible to only a small fraction of patients. Emerging evidence indicates that classical DBS influences cortical circuits by recruiting inhibitory interneurons in primary motor cortex (M1), notably parvalbumin (PV) and somatostatin (SST) subtypes, to suppress pyramidal hyperactivity. While spiking-neuron studies have demonstrated this mechanism, population-level conductance-based models capable of reproducing its whole-brain consequences are lacking.

Here, we extend the Larter–Breakspear neural mass model to include distinct PV and SST inhibitory populations and implement this formulation within The Virtual Brain using the Allen Institute 314-region mouse connectome with distance-dependent delays. We validate the model against healthy mouse resting-state LFP and show that targeted stimulation of PV/SST in M1 reduces pyramidal firing to physiological ranges, reproducing the therapeutic signature of STN-DBS in-silico. Grid-based combined-stimulation analyses reveal continuous, tunable control of pyramidal rates, and noise-augmented  The Virtual Brain (TVB) simulations establish robust network-level behavior suitable for data-driven fitting.

This framework bridges microscale inhibitory mechanisms and macroscale network dynamics, enabling systematic exploration of cortical stimulation strategies, understanding DBS mechanisms in M1 and providing a principled platform for fitting healthy and parkinsonian mouse LFP data.

Methods

Model architecture and implementation
We extended the conductance-based Larter–Breakspear neural mass to include three interacting populations per cortical node: pyramidal (excitatory), PV and SST (inhibitory). Each population is represented by membrane potential states and ion-channel activation functions (Ca, Na, K) and an open‑channel fraction W, as in the Larter–Breakspear formalism (Larter et al. 1999; Breakspear et al. 2003; Breakspear et al. 2005) PV and SST subpopulations have distinct coupling weights and time‑scale parameters to capture subtype-specific dynamics. The model was implemented in Python and embedded in The Virtual Brain (TVB) simulation platform for network simulations (TVB; http://www.thevirtualbrain.org/, Schirner et al. (2022); Sanz-Leon et al. (2015); Ritter et al. (2013)).

 Network topology and delays
Whole‑brain simulations used the Allen Institute 314‑region mouse structural connectome (weights and tract lengths) as the coupling scaffold (Oh et al. 2014) Interregional conduction delays were computed from tract lengths assuming constant conduction velocity; delays were applied as distance‑dependent time lags in TVB.

Numerical integration and noise
Deterministic ODEs and stochastic SDEs were integrated using fixed‑step schemes: Euler–Maruyama for exploratory runs and the stochastic Heun method for production SDE simulations to improve accuracy of noise terms. Additive Gaussian white noise of configurable standard deviation (σ) was injected per node (global, population‑weighted, or pyramidal‑specific variants) to assess robustness.

Stimulation protocols
Virtual DBS used step inputs applied to pyramidal, PV and SST populations. Protocols included single‑population stimulation, combined PV+SST, and multi‑population fine‑tuned stimulation identified via grid sweeps. In combined‑stimulation grid experiments, pyramidal input was held at 0.01 (unitless) while PV and SST inputs were varied as percentages of this level to map percent change in pyramidal firing (pre → during stimulus).

 Data and fitting targets
Empirical healthy mouse LFP recordings (resting state) provided fitting data. Preprocessing included band‑pass filtering and segmentation; static functional connectivity (Pearson correlation matrices) and per‑region power spectral density (PSD; Welch method) were computed as primary fitting targets. Model tuning prioritized alignment of simulated and empirical static FC.

 Parameter search and validation

We performed grid searches across key parameters (recurrent excitation, inhibitory input currents and global coupling C) to identify regimes replicating healthy firing rates (≈0.5–1.3 Hz) and parkinsonian hyperactivity (≈3–4 Hz). Previous validation of the standard model obtained strong FC correspondence (ρ≈0.89) using similar procedures (Munoz Lagunas et al. 2025) the extended model was validated against the same criteria.

Analysis and metrics
Population firing rates, time‑series, PSDs, static FC, and functional connectivity dynamics (FCD) were extracted from simulated LFP proxies. Spike detection in noisy time series combined smoothing pre‑processing with peak‑finding routines tuned to avoid noise artifacts. Therapeutic efficacy was quantified as percentage reduction in pyramidal firing during stimulation and by changes in FC similarity to healthy recordings.

Results

Extended model and validation
We extended the conductance‑based Larter–Breakspear neural mass to include two heterogeneous inhibitory subpopulations (PV and SST) alongside the pyramidal population and embedded the extended node model within TVB on the Allen 314‑region mouse connectome with distance‑dependent delays. This modification preserves ionic conductance dynamics while enabling explicit, mechanistic treatment of interneuron‑specific coupling and timescales. We validated the framework at node and network scales against healthy mouse LFP benchmarks and established fitting targets (static FC and per‑region PSD) for data‑driven tuning. 

Quantitative validation
Using grid search across excitatory self‑coupling and inhibitory input parameters, we reproduced physiologically realistic regimes: healthy pyramidal firing in the 0.5–1.3 Hz range and parkinsonian hyperactivity near 3–4 Hz. Our prior standard‑model fitting achieved strong FC correspondence (ρ ≈ 0.89) (Munoz Lagunas et al. 2025); the extended model attains comparable network‑level realism while adding mechanistic inhibitory detail for causal experiments.

Reproducing DBS-like therapeutic effects
In virtual DBS experiments, targeted stimulation of PV or SST interneurons in M1 consistently reduced pyramidal firing into the healthy range, whereas direct pyramidal stimulation produced the expected excitatory increase. These effects were robust across parameter variations and noise conditions. Quantitatively, pre‑stimulation parkinsonian rates (3.7–3.8 Hz) dropped to healthy values during inhibitory stimulation (example reductions to ≈1.0–1.3 Hz), and optimized combined stimulation (pyramidal+PV+SST) produced the greatest suppression (down to ~0.5 Hz in our simulations).

Fine‑grained control via combined‑stimulation grids
Grid sweeps varying PV and SST stimulus intensities (relative to a fixed pyramidal input) revealed a continuous, bidirectional stimulus–response surface for pyramidal firing. This map shows that small adjustments to inhibitory inputs permit precise tuning of pyramidal rates, implying the possibility of patient‑specific stimulus profiles to match desired network targets.

Network simulations and stochastic robustness
Embedding the model on the 314‑node connectome enabled whole‑brain exploration of stimulation spread, delay‑dependent interactions, and emergent functional connectivity changes. We injected additive Gaussian white noise (global, population‑weighted, and pyramidal‑specific variants) and used stochastic Heun integration for accuracy. The therapeutic effects of PV/SST stimulation remained qualitatively preserved under realistic noise amplitudes, though noise increased baseline excitability and modulated the dose required for effective suppression.

Bursting and timescale heterogeneity
By varying inhibitory timescales and adding a slow adaptation state in extended variants, the model reproduced biologically relevant bursting patterns. Heterogeneous PV/SST timescales produced more complex burst morphologies and showed how interneuron kinetics shape macroscopic rhythmicity—important when interpreting stimulation effects in pathological regimes.

Conclusions and utility
The extended Larter–Breakspear model provides a principled, biophysically interpretable platform that links interneuron‑level mechanisms to whole‑brain network outcomes. It reproduces experimentally observed inhibitory‑mediated suppression of pyramidal hyperactivity and supports systematic exploration and fitting of healthy and parkinsonian dynamics, offering a computational testbed for designing less‑invasive cortical stimulation strategies.

Conclusion

This project delivers a mechanistic and scalable framework linking interneuron‑level inhibition to whole‑brain outcomes and demonstrates its utility for studying cortical mechanisms of deep brain stimulation (DBS). We extended the conductance‑based Larter–Breakspear neural mass to include heterogeneous PV and SST inhibitory subpopulations and embedded the model in The Virtual Brain (Schirner et al. (2022); Sanz-Leon et al. (2015); Ritter et al. (2013)) using the Allen 314‑region mouse connectome (Oh et al. 2014) with distance‑dependent delays. This architecture combines ionic‑level biophysics with anatomically constrained network dynamics, enabling causal interrogation of how targeted cortical stimulation propagates through structural connectivity and alters LFP and functional connectivity (FC) signatures.

Key findings are threefold. First, at the single‑node level the extended model reproduces physiological pyramidal firing regimes and demonstrates how PV/SST recruitment modulates excitatory output through subtype‑specific coupling and timescales. Second, virtual DBS experiments show that PV or SST stimulation in M1 robustly suppresses pyramidal hyperactivity (shifting 3–4 Hz pathological rates toward 0.5–1.3 Hz), whereas direct pyramidal stimulation increases excitatory activity—reproducing the inhibitory mediation mechanism suggested by experiments and spiking models. Third, grid‑based combined‑stimulation sweeps and whole‑brain TVB simulations reveal a continuous, tunable stimulus–response surface: small adjustments to inhibitory inputs enable precise tuning of pyramidal rates, and these effects persist under realistic additive Gaussian noise.

The model also captures richer dynamics: heterogeneous inhibitory timescales and optional slow adaptation support biologically plausible bursting and timescale‑dependent rhythms, showing how interneuron kinetics shape macroscopic oscillations. Methodological advances include stochastic Heun integration, noise‑aware spike detection, and multi‑target fitting (static FC and PSD), forming a practical pipeline for data‑driven calibration.

Limitations include the abstraction inherent to neural mass models (reduced single‑cell detail), simplified noise processes, and challenges of parameter identifiability, which require careful empirical constraints. Next steps are systematic fitting to healthy mouse LFP, perturbation of Parkinsonian parameters to reproduce pathology, and optimization of cortical stimulation protocols (including closed‑loop and plasticity effects). Packaging code and parameter sets for reproducible experiments will facilitate collaborative refinement and experimental translation.

Overall, the extended Larter–Breakspear model integrated with TVB provides a principled testbed linking interneuron mechanisms to whole‑brain outcomes, reproduces inhibitory‑mediated suppression of pyramidal hyperactivity, quantifies stimulus–response landscapes for cortical DBS, and supports the design and validation of less‑invasive neuromodulation strategies.
