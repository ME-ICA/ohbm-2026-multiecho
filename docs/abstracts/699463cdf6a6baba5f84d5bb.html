Introduction

Pooling neuroimaging data across sites and scanners is increasingly important to achieve adequate sample sizes and reproducible findings. Large initiatives such as the NIH Acute to Chronic Pain Signatures (A2CPS) program highlight the value of integrating multimodal information for biomarker discovery1. However, heterogeneous acquisition protocols introduce batch effects that can obscure biological signals and limit reproducibility, motivating the need for robust harmonization methods.

ComBat2 is widely used to correct site- and scanner-related mean and variance shifts and has been adapted for diffusion imaging, cortical thickness, functional connectivity, and PET radiomics. Extensions such as ComBat-GAM3, LongComBat4, and CovBat5 address nonlinear covariate effects, longitudinal designs, and batch effects in cross-feature covariance structure. These methods, however, operate on a single metric (e.g., cortical thickness, surface area, or volume) at a time, which we refer to collectively as the univariate ComBat framework (U-ComBat). Yet neuroimaging analyses often involve correlated features across modalities whose shared covariance reflects underlying biology. Applying U-ComBat separately ignores these dependencies, potentially leaving residual covariance-related batch effects or attenuating true multivariate structure.

We introduce M-ComBat, a multivariate extension that jointly harmonizes multiple metrics by leveraging shared covariance structure to remove batch effects in means, variances, and covariances while preserving biological associations. To further address covariance-level batch effects arising from shared low-rank structure, we propose M-CovBat, which applies an additional harmonization step in latent covariance space. Using A2CPS data and targeted simulations, we show that both M-ComBat and M-CovBat outperform their univariate counterparts, providing a principled framework for large-scale multimodal neuroimaging studies.

Methods

A2CPS dataset. We analyzed baseline T1-weighted MRI from 473 A2CPS participants across six sites and multiple scanner vendors (GE, Siemens, Philips), with standardized preprocessing via fMRIPrep and FreeSurfer. Cortical measures were extracted from the Destrieux parcellation, including eight ROI-level metrics (e.g., surface area, gray matter volume). Site and scanner differences constituted major batch effects, motivating harmonization to reduce technical variability while preserving biological signal.

Multivariate ComBat and CovBat. We developed M-ComBat to jointly harmonize multiple correlated metrics. After adjusting for covariates, residuals for each ROI are modeled as multivariate normal across metrics with batch-specific means and covariance matrices, enabling adjustment of batch effects in both marginal distributions and cross-metric covariance. We propose two estimation strategies: (1) an empirical Bayes (EB) approach with conjugate multivariate normal and inverse–Wishart (IW) priors with MOM hyperparameters, and (2) a fully Bayesian approach via MCMC implemented in Stan with multivariate normal priors for batch means, an LKJ prior on the correlation matrix, and independent half-t priors for marginal standard deviations. Harmonization is achieved by removing estimated batch shifts, rescaling covariance, and restoring fixed effects.

Since M-ComBat treats batch effects as independent across features, it may be limited when covariance-level differences arise from shared low-rank structure. To address this, we introduce M-CovBat, extending CovBat to the multivariate setting. After applying M-ComBat, we perform PCA separately within each metric and embed the scores in a shared low-dimensional latent space G that captures residual cross-feature batch variation. We then apply U-ComBat to the latent scores to remove batch effects in mean and scale, and map the adjusted scores back through the loading matrices. This two-stage approach targets covariance-level batch effects shared across metrics and features.

Simulation and Evaluation. We compared M-ComBat and M-CovBat with their univariate counterparts using both simulations and A2CPS data. The first simulation set introduced batch effects through ROI-level mean shifts and covariances under (i) model-concordant conditions aligned with EB assumptions and (ii) model-misspecified conditions with mixed structured covariance families. We evaluated two realistic sample-size settings and a severe batch-effect stress test to assess M-ComBat’s improvements and robustness to prior misspecification.

A second simulation set targeted multivariate covariance-level harmonization, using A2CPS-derived data with latent-space perturbations generating low-rank covariance distortions. These experiments assessed M-CovBat’s ability to remove structured covariance-level batch effects and further tested M-ComBat’s robustness under complex multivariate structure.

Harmonization performance was evaluated across three dimensions: batch removal, biological signal preservation, and correlation recovery. Feature-wise batch removal was assessed using univariate tests of mean (ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis) and variance (Levene’s, Bartlett’s, Fligner–Killeen) and multivariate tests (MANOVA, Box’s M), using Bonferroni-adjusted p-values. Global residual batch signals were quantified using 10-fold cross-validated random forest classifiers predicting batch. Biological signal preservation was assessed via feature-wise regressions on prespecified covariates, using Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control in A2CPS and empirical FDR in simulations. Correlation recovery was quantified by comparing within- and cross-metric correlations to ground truth and across batches using Frobenius norm, MSE, eigenvalue error, and spectral norm, with lower values indicating better recovery.

Results

In the A2CPS dataset, age, sex, and pre-surgical pain duration showed nonlinear associations with several cortical metrics, motivating the use of generalized additive models (GAMs) for fixed-effect estimation. Strong cross-metric correlations persisted and differed across sites and scanner types, indicating substantial batch effects retained in the covariance structure. Residual distributions exhibited both additive and multiplicative batch effects, and several metrics (notably FoldInd) contained influential outliers that posed challenges for ComBat-type methods.

Multivariate approaches provided substantially improved harmonization. Both M-ComBat and M-CovBat markedly reduced residual batch effects in cross-metric covariance, with the MCMC variant fully eliminating them across all ROIs (Figure 1A). In contrast, U-ComBat and U-CovBat left clear covariance-level batch structure unresolved. Random-forest classifiers trained to predict batch confirmed these improvements: multivariate harmonization methods produced much weaker within-metric batch signals, with M-CovBat achieving the lowest AUCs (Figure 1B). Although U-CovBat generally outperformed U-ComBat, it remained sensitive to outliers, further highlighting the advantage of multivariate modeling. Both M-ComBat and M-CovBat also preserved nonlinear age and pain-duration effects and retained as many or more significant associations as the univariate methods, demonstrating better preservation of biological variation.

Simulation studies reinforced these empirical findings. When batch effects were introduced through feature-specific mean shifts and covariances, both M-ComBat variants consistently mitigated cross-metric covariance batch effects more effectively than U-ComBat. The MCMC variant provided the strongest correction in small samples and under severe batch effects, while the EB variant showed greater stability under prior misspecification. Both approaches improved correlation recovery and better preserved biological associations (especially moderate-to-strong signals), with FDR control comparable to univariate methods. Notably, the MCMC variant more effectively prevented within-metric correlation distortion (particularly in small samples) and better recovered cross-metric correlations under strong batch effects when priors were correctly specified. 

Under simulations with low-rank latent-space covariance distortions, M-CovBat provided the most effective removal of structured covariance-level batch effects, especially across metrics (Figure 1C). In contrast, U-CovBat exhibited substantial confounding in the latent space, reflected by strong directional variation within clusters in the PCA of the G space, indicating a failure to disentangle batch structure from unmodeled variation (Figure 1D). Both multivariate methods reduced these distortions in the leading principal components, with M-CovBat showing superior performance in higher-order components. These results highlight M-CovBat’s particular strength in correcting global covariance distortions driven by cross-metric dependencies, as well as the robustness of M-ComBat to low-rank structured batch effects due to its joint multivariate modeling.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight the importance of robust harmonization for multi-site and multimodal neuroimaging data. While univariate methods effectively mitigate site effects within individual metrics, they do not address covariance across metrics, which is crucial for multimodal analyses where biological processes are inherently correlated. To overcome this limitation, we developed two complementary multivariate frameworks: M-ComBat, which jointly harmonizes correlated metrics to adjust for batch effects in means, variances, and covariances, and M-CovBat, which extends this framework through latent-space harmonization to remove residual covariance-related batch effects. Together, these methods enhance preservation of biologically meaningful variation and recovery of inter-metric relationships.

In the A2CPS data, substantial batch effects were observed in means, variances, and covariances across metrics. Severe outliers, particularly in FoldInd and CurvInd, posed challenges for ComBat-type methods. U-ComBat and U-CovBat failed to correct cross-metric batch effects and were sensitive to measurement error, whereas M-ComBat and M-CovBat effectively reduced covariance-related signals and preserved more biological associations. Among all methods, M-CovBat achieved the best overall performance, with the EB variant showing greater robustness to measurement error.

Simulation studies further demonstrated the strengths of the MCMC approach in removing cross-metric covariance batch effects, preserving moderate-to-strong fixed effects, and recovering correlations under small sample sizes or severe batch effects with well-specified priors. The MCMC variant was also robust to prior misspecification under mild batch effects but struggled with structured covariance patterns under stress, where the EB variant was more stable. In simulations with latent-space batch effects, M-CovBat showed superior performance in removing within- and cross-metric batch effects in correlations, improving recovery of biologically meaningful signals. Although M-ComBat performed slightly worse, it remained robust even when the assumed batch-effect distribution was violated, benefiting from its multivariate modeling and cross-feature information sharing. In contrast, U-CovBat performed poorly under strong confounding between batch and covariate effects due to its independent treatment of each metric.

Despite these advantages, several limitations remain. Like U-ComBat, M-ComBat requires pre-specified covariates to preserve biological signals, which may inadvertently remove unmodeled variation of interest. The current framework handles only one batch variable at a time, limiting its use in hierarchical designs such as nested scanner effects. While pairwise combinations and sequential adjustments have been proposed, they may distort dependencies across batch factors. Extending M-ComBat to incorporate hierarchical batch structures is an important direction for future work. 

In addition, the current two-stage implementation, while computationally efficient, may bias fixed-effect estimates under model misspecification. Finally, the MCMC variant relies on weakly informative priors that do not fully capture structured covariance. Fully Bayesian approaches that jointly estimate fixed and batch effects, using more flexible priors capable of modeling both unstructured and structured covariance, could further enhance robustness in complex, real-world datasets.
