Introduction

Associative learning and memory evoke widespread brain network interactions¹, which are known to be disrupted in schizophrenia². Typical learning paradigms oscillate between periods of Encoding (when associations between items are memorized), Retrieval (when memory for those associations is tested, usually via cued recall)³, and post-task rest. Each phase of memory formation has distinct task characteristics and is likely to evoke contextually-specific patterns of network dysfunction in SCZ. The present study aimed to characterize functional connectivity (FC) alterations across four task phases (Encoding, Encoding Rest, Retrieval, and Retrieval Rest) and to investigate whether stable FC pathways remain consistent across all phases or vary with task demands.

Methods

We acquired fMRI data from 78 participants (45 stabilized SCZ patients and 33 healthy controls (HC)) during an object–location associative learning paradigm in which participants learned nine object–location associations⁴. Each of the eight task cycles included an Encoding phase, where objects were presented in their associated locations, followed by a rest, and a Retrieval phase, during which locations were cued and participants named the associated objects, followed again by a rest. We extracted time series from 246 regions defined using the Brainnetome Atlas⁵. For each of the Encoding, Encoding Rest, Retrieval, and Retrieval Rest conditions, we computed zero-lag bivariate correlations to estimate undirected FC across the full 246-region matrix. All resulting images were compared across task phases to identify significant inter-group differences (pFDR < .05). From these, we isolated phase-specific patterns of reduced FC in schizophrenia and, through an intersection-based model, tried to identify a stable dysconnectivity pattern reflecting alterations consistently present across all task phases.

Results

Analyses within specific phases have shown different dysconnectivity profiles. During Encoding, FC reductions were mild and distributed across the cortex, primarily involving frontal, temporal, parietal, and visual regions. During Encoding Rest, reductions were more pronounced, mainly affecting the temporal lobe, followed by parietal and occipital regions. In the Retrieval phase, the most affected areas were visuo-parietal cortices, followed by the frontal lobe. During Retrieval Rest, FC loss was highly widespread and pronounced across all cortical lobes, including temporal, frontal, parietal, and visual cortices, as well as subcortical, insular, and limbic structures.
 Although the images show variable phase-specific patterns of dysconnectivity, an integrated analysis of all radial bar plots revealed a relatively stable pattern of FC loss, mainly involving occipito-temporo-parietal and frontal regions.

Conclusion

These findings show that brain network dysconnectivity in schizophrenia is strongly phase-dependent, with relatively circumscribed deficits during Encoding and widespread disruptions during post-retrieval rest. Nevertheless, intersection-based analyses reveal a stable baseline pattern of functional disconnection that persists across phases and may represent a core network vulnerability underlying associative learning and memory impairments in SCZ.
