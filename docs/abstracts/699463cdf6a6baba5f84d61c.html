Introduction

Blood oxygenation level–dependent (BOLD) fMRI provides a powerful window into human brain function; however, the BOLD signal reflects not only neural activity but also autonomic physiological processes such as respiration, cardiac dynamics, and sympathetic vascular tone. These processes introduce structured and temporally delayed fluctuations that can mimic or obscure neural signals and shape large-scale functional patterns (Birn et al., 2008; Chang et al., 2009; Özbay et al., 2018). Converging evidence shows that fluctuations in EEG power co-occur with widespread fMRI signal changes and peripheral autonomic measures, including respiratory variability and sympathetic-mediated vascular tone, and often precede or follow BOLD responses with distinct temporal delays. These coordinated neural and autonomic dynamics have been linked to large-scale propagating fMRI patterns and global signal fluctuations, highlighting a shared brain–body arousal axis (Gu et al., 2022).

While autonomic influences on fMRI and EEG–fMRI coupling are well characterized during rest, their role during cognitively demanding tasks remains less well understood. Tasks such as mental arithmetic reliably engage arousal systems, evoking changes in respiration and sympathetic activity that unfold across multiple timescales and may overlap with, or confound, task-evoked neural responses.

Methods

Ten healthy adults participated in a block-designed mental arithmetic task during simultaneous multimodal recording. Each run consisted of three task blocks (45 TR per block; TR = 3 s), with alternating ON (12 TR) and OFF (3 TR) periods, preceded by a 15 TR baseline (See Fig.1). Task onset times were synchronized across all modalities using scanner volume triggers.

Whole-brain fMRI data were acquired on a Siemens 3T MR scanner using gradient-echo EPI with a 20-channel head/neck coil (TR = 3s, TE = 36 ms, voxel size = 2.5 mm isotropic). Respiration was recorded using a respiratory belt (400 Hz), and cardiac activity was measured using a finger photoplethysmogram (PPG; 400 Hz).  Simultaneous EEG was recorded using a Brain Products MR-compatible 32-channel cap (5000 Hz). 

 fMRI data were preprocessed using afni_proc.py, including despiking, slice-timing correction, motion correction, alignment to anatomy, spatial normalization, blurring (6mm FWMH), masking, and scaling. High-frequency physiological noise correction was performed using RETROICOR (8 regressors per slice). Respiratory signals were band-pass filtered and peak-detected to compute respiration volume per time (RVT) for each TR with the RetroTS function in AFNI MATLAB library. PPG signals were filtered and processed to extract pulse amplitude (PPG-AMP) per TR. EEG data underwent gradient and cardioballistic artifact correction followed by independent component analysis (ICA), downsampled to 250Hz and re-referenced to Fz. Cleaned EEG signals were first transformed into time–frequency representations using a 15-cycle Morlet wavelet; spectral power (in frontal channels F3,F4,F7,F8) was then averaged within 10 frequency bins to obtain band-limited power time series downsampled to the TR resolution. 

Task-locked responses were computed by aligning standardized multimodal signals (BOLD, EEG power, RVT, and PPG-AMP) to task onset and averaging across blocks and subjects. Group-level responses were visualized as mean ± SEM, with task periods indicated graphically.

Lagged cross-correlations were computed between autonomic measures (RVT, PPG-AMP), EEG band power, and regional fMRI signals across lags ranging from −21 to +21 s. Positive lags corresponded to autonomic/EEG signals preceding fMRI. Statistical significance was assessed using circular time-shift permutation to generate empirical null distributions (99% confidence intervals).From the cross-correlations with autonomic signals, three dominant physiological lags associated with global fMRI changes were identified for each autonomic measure (RVT: 15, 18, and 21 s; PPG amplitude: 6, 9, and 12 s).

To isolate autonomic contributions, nuisance regression models were constructed using RVT and/or PPG-AMP regressors time-shifted according to the dominant physiological lags. Linear regression was then applied to remove RVT, PPG-AMP, or their combined effects from the fMRI time series using AFNI’s 3dDeconvolve function, yielding autonomic-corrected fMRI residuals for subsequent analyses.

Cross-correlations between the band-limited frontal EEG power time series and the fMRI signal were computed for each frequency bin, yielding heatmaps in which rows correspond to frequency bins and the x-axis denotes cross-correlation lags. These heatmaps were used to examine frequency-resolved EEG–fMRI coupling before and after autonomic correction. To quantify the impact of autonomic influences, changes in coupling strength across frequencies and lags were assessed by computing difference maps (Δr), defined as the pointwise difference in cross-correlation coefficients between the uncorrected and autonomic-corrected conditions.

Results

In Figure 2A, group-level (n = 10) event-locked responses are presented. Event-locked averaging revealed consistent, task-associated changes across neural, hemodynamic, and autonomic modalities. In the left panel, voxelwise event-locked maps illustrate the spatiotemporal evolution of BOLD signals within each task event, averaged across events and subjects. In the middle panel, block-averaged autonomic measures and region-specific BOLD signals are shown, with lines representing the standardized mean signal and shaded areas indicating the standard error of the mean (SEM). Following task onset, PPG amplitude showed a robust decrease across participants, indicating reliable sympathetic activation. Respiratory responses (RVT) were more heterogeneous, showing subject-specific variability. In parallel, BOLD signals exhibited region-specific task responses, with positive deflections in the intraparietal sulcus (IPS), thalamus, and insula, and negative deflections in the pre- and postcentral gyri (sensorimotor cortex) and visual regions, with region-dependent timing. Similarly, as shown in the right panel, EEG alpha and theta power decreased after task onset, reflecting increased cortical arousal, and recovered during inter-block periods.

In Figure 2B, lagged correlations between autonomic signals and both global and region-specific BOLD signals are shown. Significant coupling between autonomic measures and BOLD activity was observed at the global (gray-matter–averaged; left panel) and regional (middle and right panels) levels. Region-specific analyses revealed near-zero-lag positive correlations between RVT and BOLD signals in the thalamus and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), suggesting close temporal alignment between task-related respiratory changes and BOLD responses. Autonomic measures also exhibited delayed associations with BOLD signals. RVT–BOLD correlations peaked predominantly at longer positive lags (~15–21 s), with sensorimotor regions showing significant negative correlations at these later lags, consistent with delayed respiratory influences.

PPG amplitude showed a complementary biphasic pattern, with an early negative correlation near zero lag followed by delayed positive correlations (~6–15 s). Near-zero-lag negative ppgAmp–BOLD correlations were observed across multiple regions, including IPS, visual cortex, precuneus, and thalamus, indicating close temporal coupling between task-evoked sympathetic vasoconstriction and BOLD responses. At longer positive lags, ppgAmp–BOLD correlations became positive in visual, sensorimotor, and precuneus regions, as well as at the global gray-matter level, indicating delayed autonomic effects.

In Figure 2C, frequency-resolved EEG–fMRI cross-correlation heatmaps reveal both global and region-specific effects of autonomic arousal. At the global gray-matter level, low-frequency EEG power exhibited positive coupling at early lags and negative coupling at longer positive lags (~6–12 s) with BOLD signals, indicating that EEG fluctuations preceded subsequent fMRI changes. Following autonomic correction, this delayed negative coupling became less strong, while shorter-lag positive coupling in higher-frequency bands became slightly more prominent. Region-specific analyses revealed heterogeneity in autonomic sensitivity. The sensorimotor cortex closely mirrored the global pattern and showed the largest attenuation after correction, suggesting strong autonomic modulation. The visual cortex exhibited mixed effects, while the intraparietal sulcus (IPS) showed weaker delayed coupling and smaller correction effects, indicating greater neural specificity. Together, these findings suggest that task-evoked EEG–fMRI coupling reflects a combination of global arousal-related dynamics and regionally specific neural processes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that task-evoked brain activity reflects coordinated neural and autonomic dynamics operating across multiple timescales. During a cognitively demanding task, autonomic arousal was reliably engaged, producing structured respiratory and sympathetic responses that temporally overlapped with regional BOLD and EEG power changes. Lagged analyses revealed that autonomic signals contribute delayed, spatially widespread influences on BOLD activity and EEG–fMRI coupling that extend beyond canonical neurovascular response windows.

Importantly, autonomic correction selectively attenuated delayed, physiologically driven EEG–fMRI coupling—particularly negative coupling in lower-frequency EEG bands at longer lags—while preserving or enhancing faster, near-zero-lag relationships consistent with neural activity. These effects were regionally heterogeneous, with sensorimotor and global signals showing stronger autonomic modulation than task-positive regions such as intraparietal sulcus. These findings support a framework in which task-evoked EEG–fMRI coupling reflects a mixture of fast neural responses and slower autonomic and vascular influences linked to arousal regulation.

Together, our results highlight autonomic arousal as an integral component of task-evoked brain–body interaction rather than a purely nuisance source of variance. Explicitly modeling autonomic dynamics improves the interpretability of multimodal neuroimaging data and is essential for dissociating neural activity from delayed physiological contributions in EEG–fMRI studies.
