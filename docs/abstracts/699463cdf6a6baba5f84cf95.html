Introduction

Using predictive models and different neuroimaging modalities, multiple studies have attempted to investigate sex-related structural and functional brain features and relate them to behavioural differences between the two sexes1,2. Although many of these studies have achieved high sex-classification accuracies, there is ongoing debate about whether these performances reflect genuine sex-dependent neural differences or are merely driven by general factors such as brain size differences between males and females3.

To control for size effects, some studies have statistically adjusted for estimated intracranial volume (eTIV) and found that model performance drops substantially after this adjustment2,3. However, because brain size and sex are highly intercorrelated, adjusting for brain size also removes sex-related variance. Further, it remains unclear to what extent high sex-classification accuracies reflect task-evoked neural differences versus more global properties of the brain, such as grey matter or average BOLD.  

To clarify these issues, we used multiple structural and functional MRI modalities from a large-scale neuroimaging study to predict sex. Prediction was first done in the whole sample, and then in two subsamples with matched eTIV distributions and the same number of participants in each sex group to reduce the influence of brain size. Moreover, for fMRI data, we used task-related contrasted and non-contrasted images as well as mean brain activity in separate models to better understand the source of the predictive signal and whether there are task-related brain activity features predictive of sex. Finally, we validated our findings using Human Connectome Project (HCP) fMRI data as an independent dataset.

Methods

Molecular and Cognitive Neuroscience (MCN) Dataset

Data from 1163 healthy adults (38% male; 22.5 ± 3.4) were collected on the same 3T scanner with a 12-channel head coil. Structural T1-weighted images were acquired using an MPRAGE sequence. Preprocessing was performed in SPM12 (MATLAB R2016b), with DARTEL used for normalisation to MNI space. For task-related fMRI, we used data from a verbal N-back working memory task (0-back and 2-back; six blocks of 14 stimuli per condition). To control for brain size, two eTIV-matched subsamples consisting of the same number of participants in each sex category (MCN-S1: N = 152; MCN-S2: N = 218) were created. More information about MCN data can be found in prior publications4.

Human Connectome Project (HCP) Dataset

To validate the findings, we used preprocessed fMRI data from the emotional faces task in the HCP S1200 release. In total, 1045 subjects (45.6% males) were included in the model. To ensure generalisability, we used a different fMRI task and retained the original HCP preprocessing pipeline, which differs from the MCN preprocessing pipeline. Two eTIV-matched subsamples consisting of the same number of participants in each sex category were created (HCP-S1: N = 247; HCP-S2: N = 303) to control for the possible head size effects in classification performance.

Modalities

The following modalities were used in separate models to predict sex:

  • Grey matter (GM) maps: Smoothed, warped probabilistic GM maps, each voxel value representing the probability of the voxel being grey matter.
  • Beta maps: First-level fMRI GLM models for both datasets were estimated. Task conditions and movement parameters were included as regressors, and for each subject, voxel-wise beta coefficients for the 2-back condition (MCN dataset) and face condition (HCP dataset) were used as the beta maps modality.
  • Contrast maps: For each subject, voxel-wise contrasted values for 2-back > 0-back (MCN dataset) and face > shape (HCP dataset) were used as contrast map modality.
  • Average BOLD signal: In the MCN dataset, after performing first-level fMRI GLM analysis, a voxel-wise map of intercept values was created. The intercept values of the GLM model can be considered as an indirect measure of the average BOLD signal and were used as a task-independent modality in our analysis.
     As intercept values from the HCP dataset were not directly available, we calculated the mean of each voxel’s preprocessed BOLD signal over time and used the resulting voxel-wise map as the average BOLD signal modality. 

Model

Sex prediction was conducted using a linear support vector machine implemented in the PRoNTo toolbox 5 (SPM12, MATLAB R2021b). All models employed a nested cross-validation scheme with 5 inner folds for hyperparameter tuning and 5 outer folds for performance evaluation. Balanced accuracy (BAC) and area under the curve (AUC) were used as evaluation metrics. Feature weights were calculated per voxel, and the AAL atlas parcellation was used to summarise the relative weight contribution of each brain region by averaging the absolute weights of the voxels within the region.

Results

Using the MCN dataset, the GM modality predicted sex with a balanced accuracy (BA) of 93.2% (AUC = 0.98). We could also predict sex using functional MRI modalities (Beta maps: BA = 79.2%, AUC = 0.88; Contrast maps: BA = 75.3%, AUC = 0.83). Notably, average BOLD signal predicted sex nearly as well as GM (BA = 92.6%, AUC = 0.98). After matching for brain size, using two eTIV-matched subsamples, all models remained above chance, with GM and average BOLD signal modalities outperforming beta and contrast maps.

Using HCP data, we replicated the same results as in the MCN dataset (Beta maps: BA = 77%, AUC = 0.86; Contrast maps: BA = 75%, AUC = 0.84; Average BOLD signal maps: BA = 97%, AUC = 0.99). The same pattern of results was also observed in both HCP eTIV-matched subsamples (Figure 1).

Feature-weight analyses revealed broadly distributed contributions across the brain, with no region contributing more than ~3 % of the total model weight (Figure 2).

Conclusion

Our findings show that sex can be accurately predicted from both structural and functional MRI data. Although models using task-related modalities (beta and contrast maps) performed well, models using average BOLD signal that are not tied to task-related neural responses performed better. This suggests that the biggest sex-related differences arise from basic, task-independent properties of the brain rather than specific patterns of task-evoked activity. 

This interpretation is further supported by the feature-weight analyses in which no specific regions contributed strongly to classification. Using the 116-region AAL atlas, a uniform weight would correspond to 0.86% per region. Across all models, no region exceeded 3% contribution, and many regions showed comparable weights, indicating a diffuse, non-region-specific basis for prediction.

Brain size and its strong correlation with sex are frequently discussed as a major confounding factor. Leveraging large-scale fMRI data from two datasets, we controlled for brain size effects by subsetting the data into smaller, eTIV-matched samples. Although accuracy decreased, sex could still be predicted above chance in these subsamples, indicating that brain size alone does not account for the observed differences. The reduction in accuracy likely reflects both the removal of size-related variance and the smaller sample sizes.

Other basic factors may also contribute, such as sex differences in head shape affecting image normalisation or differences in vascularisation influencing the BOLD signal. We expect such effects to be reduced in modalities like contrast maps, which include a within-subject control by subtracting two conditions. The lower prediction accuracy observed for contrast maps may reflect this reduction in general confounding effects.

We replicated fMRI findings from the MCN dataset in the HCP dataset. This replication in an independent sample—with a different fMRI task paradigm and preprocessing pipeline—supports the generalisability of our results and suggests they are not driven by dataset-specific factors. The HCP dataset, particularly its resting-state fMRI data, has frequently been used in sex-prediction studies. Our findings reinforce the idea that modalities capturing more general brain activity, such as resting-state fMRI, may be more susceptible to global confounders and that caution is warranted when interpreting such results in relation to behaviour.

In summary, sex can be decoded with high accuracy from structural GM and average BOLD, which systematically outperform task-evoked activity. These findings suggest that high sex-prediction accuracy in neuroimaging mainly reflects broad structural and/or physiological differences rather than specific differences in task-evoked processes.
