Introduction

Late childhood and adolescence are critical neurodevelopmental period characterized by structural and functional brain changes, alongside an increased vulnerability to psychiatric disorders. Recent shifts in clinical and research paradigms have embraced a dimensional approach to psychopathology. The Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) proposes a hierarchical framework, with a general susceptibility to psychiatric disorders at the top, branching into progressively specific dimensions1. While previous research has identified structural and functional brain differences associated with psychopathology dimensions, much remains unclear, hindering the development of effective intervention and prevention strategies2. Moreover, research on sex-specific neural correlates remains sparse despite emerging evidence of distinct neurodevelopmental trajectories between males and females3. Changes in whole-brain functional dynamics can be used as markers of mental illnesses and to improve our understanding of psychopathology and its subgrouping and treatment effectiveness. Dynamic characteristics in brain states can be assessed by their dwell time, how much time is spent in any given state, the fractional occupancy (FO), the proportion of time spent in each state, appearance rate, how often a state is transitioned into, and number of transitions (NT).

While most research has focused separately on structural and functional MRI data, emerging evidence underscores the importance of structure-function coupling in understanding brain-behavior relationships. To link functional states and structure, we will compute transition energies between said states using tools from network control theory (NCT), which models the diffusion of activity through the structural connectome4.

Here in this study, we aim to determine whether the functional activity dynamics and the energy landscapes are associated with different dimensions of psychopathology in a sex-specific manner.

Methods

We utilized baseline imaging and behavioral data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study, including 1,227 girls and 1,073 boys aged 9-11 years, with high-quality resting state-fMRI (rs-fMRI). We conducted a principal component analysis on the six DSM-V oriented scales Childhood Behavior Checklist (CBCL) subscales to obtain transdiagnostic dimensions of psychopathology (Figure 1). 

Pre-processed rs-fMRI data were parcellated into 86 regions using the Desikan-Killiany atlas combined with subcortical structures. We applied k-means clustering to the concatenated time series to identify recurring brain activity states. We computed multiple temporal-dynamics metrics, including fractional occupancy (FO), defined as the number of TRs in each state divided by the total number of TRs. The Appearance Rate was calculated as the number of first TRs divided by the total number of TRs. Dwell time was computed by counting the number of consecutive TRs assigned to the same state after transitioning into it. We also computed the number of transitions by summing the number of consecutive TRs that differ in state.

Diffusion-weighted imaging was processed using probabilistic tractography to construct an average structural connectome. NCT was applied to calculate the regional TEs required to move between each pair of activity states, based on a linear time-invariant model. Global TEs for each pairwise transition were computed by summing regional TEs,yielding a 4x4 global TE matrix per participant.

Associations between psychopathology (PC1–PC3) and temporal dynamics/TEs were assessed using linear mixed-effects models (LMEMs) with fixed effects for psychopathology, sex, and their interaction, and random intercepts for imaging site. Covariates included age, handedness, socioeconomic status, puberty, and framewise displacement. Sex-stratified follow-up models were run when significant psychopathology × sex interactions were detected. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across states and transitions.

Results

The PCA on the subscales revealed three psychopathology dimensions: PC1 (general psychopathology, PC2 (internalizing vs. externalizing symptoms), and PC3 (somatic complaints; Figure 1). Four brain states were identified via k-means clustering, and, for ease of interpretation, regional activity was compared with the Yeo canonical resting-state networks (RSNs) and a subcortical network. This revealed the following four brain states: Default mode network (DMN)-, DMN+, Visual (VIS)-, VIS+. 

PC2 showed the strongest and most consistent associations with temporal dynamics, and these effects were moderated by sex (Figure 2A-B). High internalizing scores are associated with lower VIS- appearance rate in females but not in males (Figure 2A). Furthermore, higher internalizing symptoms were associated with longer dwell times in VIS-/+ for females, but trended toward shorter dwell times in males, indicating opposing effects of internalizing–externalizing symptoms on state stability across sexes (Figure 2B).

Additionally, general psychopathology was negatively associated with the global number of transitions per minute in females (more psychopathology = fewer transitions), with an opposite trend in males, suggesting sex-specific reductions or increases in overall dynamical flexibility as symptom severity increased.

Global TE analyses revealed complementary findings (Figure 2C). TE matrices demonstrated distinct psychopathology-related patterns, with especially pronounced sex-specific effects for PC2. 

General psychopathology showed a trend of widespread positive associations with TE, indicating globally higher energetic demands with increasing general symptom severity (Figure 2C). In contrast, PC2 effects were highly state-specific and moderated by sex (Figure 2C). Females with higher internalizing symptoms exhibited substantially higher TE for transitions involving the DMN, including transitions from DMN− to DMN+ and sustained engagement of DMN+, suggesting increased energetic cost of accessing internally oriented brain configurations. Males showed the opposite pattern, with high internalizing symptoms associated with reduced TE across these same transitions, reflecting a more energetically permissive landscape. Similar sex-dependent effects appeared in VIS–DMN transitions, further reinforcing a broad divergence in the energetic architecture of brain-state switching between boys and girls.

Together, these results show that internalizing–externalizing symptoms are linked to sex-specific alterations in both temporal dynamics and transition energies, revealing distinct dynamical and energetic pathways through which psychopathology manifests during late childhood.

Conclusion

Our findings show that distinct dimensions of psychopathology are differently related to both temporal functional brain states dynamics and transition energies in a sex-dependent manner. Specifically, the internalizing vs externalizing symptoms were found to interact with sex to influence how long states persist, how frequently they are entered, global switching behavior, and the energetic cost of transitioning between states. 

Our results converge with prior work reporting sex-specific changes in energy landscapes in youth with a history of family substance abuse disorder (SUB), where girls exhibited globally elevated transition energies and boys showed reduced TE4. Our findings extend this literature by showing that sex-related differences in energetic cost are not restricted to familial risk populations but emerge transdiagnostically as a function of internalizing symptom severity. Moreover, our temporal dynamics results are consistent with previous observations that girls and boys differ in state stability and engagement in both typically developing and neurodevelopmental cohorts. Girls with and without ADHD have been shown to exhibit shorter dwell times in higher-order association states relative to boys5. These findings suggest distinct neurodevelopmental mechanisms linking symptoms to brain dynamics in males and females and underscore the value of sex-specific modeling of both temporal and energetic features to understand adolescent psychopathology.
