Introduction

Cognitive neuroscience is increasingly prioritizing naturalistic tasks to capture the complexity of real-world behavior. Autobiographical memory (ABM) narratives recruit a complex interplay of episodic memory, semantic knowledge, speech production and executive control. While healthy aging alters narrative production, shifting from episodic to semantic content, there remains substantial inter-individual variability in narrative style among cognitively unimpaired older adults. It remains unknown how this variability maps onto structural brain differences, particularly when excluding preclinical Alzheimer’s disease pathology. 

Methods

Study population: We analyzed data from 64 cognitively unimpaired (CU) older adults (mean age=72.1±6.8 years; 56.3% female) from the Flemish Prevent Alzheimer’s Disease Cohort KU Leuven (F-PACK). Recruitment in F-PACK followed a genetic stratification scheme balancing age, sex, education, APOE-ε4, and BDNF Val66Met status (Genetic data for the present cohort: APOE (ε4 carriers: n = 23; 36%) and BDNF Val66Met (Met carriers: n = 34; 53.1%). Inclusion in the current study required confirmed amyloid-negative status via amyloid PET (Centiloid threshold <23.5) or CSF biomarkers, alongside normal neuropsychological performance (CDR=0; MMSE>27) and absence of neurological or psychiatric disorders. 

Connected speech pipeline: Participants completed a semi-structured autobiographical interview based on the Levine et al. framework.^1 They were instructed to recall specific, personally experienced events from five distinct time periods: early childhood (5–11y), adolescence (12–17y), early adulthood (18–35y), midlife (36–55y), and the past year. Audio responses were transcribed using a finetuned automatic speech recognition (ASR) model. Subsequently, we employed a finetuned Dutch transformer-based language model (RobBERT) to derive an aggregated, high-dimensional vectorized representation per interview part.

Neuroimaging pipeline: High-resolution T1-weighted structural MRI was acquired on a 3T Philips Achieva system (3D TFE; TR=9.6ms, TE=4.6ms, flip angle=8°, voxel size=0.98×0.98×1.2mm³). We used FreeSurfer (v7.4.1) to extract cortical thickness (CT) measures across 64 regions (Desikan-Killiany atlas) and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV)-adjusted hippocampal volumes.^2

Statistical analysis: We employed representational similarity analysis (RSA) to determine if inter-individual variability in speech aligns with inter-individual variability in brain structure.^3 For each interview period, we constructed a 64×64 subject-by-subject speech representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM) using cosine distances between embeddings. Similarly, a subject-by-subject 64x64 cortical-thickness-based RDM was constructed from cosine distances between subject-level regional cortical thickness vectors. The primary outcome was the Spearman rank correlation (ρ) between the upper triangles of the speech and brain RDMs, partialing out covariate RDMs for age, sex, and education. Statistical significance was established via permutation testing (rows of the speech-based RDM were randomly permuted 10 000 times), and to control for multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied within each family (five tests per family). Finally, to gain further insight into the underlying anatomical basis, we applied principal component analysis (PCA) to the cortical-thickness data after scaling the regional values and correlated the resulting component-based RDMs with speech representations.

Results

In amyloid-negative, CU healthy older adults, we found significant positive correlations between inter-individual similarity in speech embeddings and inter-individual similarity in regional cortical thickness, particularly for remote memories. This brain-language coupling exhibited a significant temporal gradient (Spearman’s ρ=-0.9, p=0.03), systematically waning as autobiographical memories became more recent.

Specifically, the coupling was strongest for early childhood narratives, where speech similarity significantly predicted cortical thickness similarity (ρ=0.315, p=0.0018; Bonferroni-adjusted p=0.009). A similar but non-significant trend was observed for adolescence narratives (ρ=0.223), while narratives from early adulthood, midlife, and the past year showed no significant Spearman correlations. Analyses restricted to hippocampal volume (adjusted for eTIV) yielded no significant correlations after multiple comparison correction (childhood ρ=0.175), suggesting the observed coupling is driven primarily by neocortical patterns.

PCA revealed that the association between speech RDMs and regional cortical thickness RDM was mainly driven by lateral inferior frontal and lateral temporal regions without clear hemispheric lateralization (cfr. Results figure). This anatomical signature partially overlaps with the established core autobiographical memory network but lacks key parietal nodes (e.g., precuneus). 

Conclusion

In cognitively unimpaired, amyloid-negative older adults, inter-individual similarity in connected speech representations partly aligns with inter-individual similarity in cortical integrity. This brain-language coupling is most pronounced for autobiographical narratives from remote memories, specifically childhood and adolescence, and is driven by frontotemporal cortical thickness patterns consistent with the core autobiographical memory network. This work supports connected speech as a low-burden window on brain health.
