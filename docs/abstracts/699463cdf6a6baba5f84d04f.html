Introduction

Emotion and action readiness are intertwined.1 For some clinical populations, their defensive motor system may be hyperactivated, leading to maladaptive behavior. This notion is relevant to Functional Neurological Disorder (FND), a condition commonly involving motor impairments such as immobility.2 Importantly, patients with FND often have stressful life events before symptom onset, particularly those with an escape potential (i.e., the type of stressors that could be ameliorated by becoming ill).3 While it is postulated that these high-escape stressors play a key role in the pathology of FND,4 it remains unclear how they may be linked to motor behavior. In the current study, we examined this link in healthy individuals, by testing whether vividly recalling high-escape stressors may elicit defensive freezing-like behavior, with the neural underpinnings explored.

Methods

Twenty-seven healthy adult participants completed a novel autobiographical motor task in an ultra-high-field (7-Telsa) MRI scanner. All participants were interviewed to identify stressful and neutral events which had occurred within the preceding 12 months. During the task, in each trial, participants were asked to maintain an isometric pinch force at 10% of maximum voluntary contraction with visual feedback on how accurate they were (the baseline phase). Subsequently, the feedback was replaced by statements related to their personal events, namely stressful events with high or low escape potential, as well as neutral events. Participants were instructed to maintain the same level of force while viewing the statements (the maintenance phase). Imaging data was pre-processed and analyzed using SPM12. A generalized linear model was used to examine the neural responses in the contrasts between the conditions. 

Results

Participants could maintain the target level of force (i.e., 10%) during the baseline phase. In the maintenance phase, without visual feedback, they showed a decay in force across all conditions. However, this effect was attenuated in the high-escape condition, but not the low-escape condition, compared to the neutral condition. At a whole-brain level, participants showed increased activity in medial prefrontal cortex, inferior frontal gyrus, angular gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, precentral gyrus, and supplementary motor area in both the high-escape and the low-escape conditions, compared to the neutral condition (p < 0.05, FDR cluster-level corrected). However, increased activity was observed in posterior cingulate cortex (Fig. 1A), superior temporal sulcus (Fig. 1B), right temporal pole (Fig. 1C), and right crus I (Fig. 1D) in the high-escape-versus-neutral contrast (p < 0.05, FDR cluster-level corrected), but not the low-escape-versus-neutral contrast.

Conclusion

The results demonstrate diminished force decay induced by recall of high-escape stressors, potentially mediated by temporal and cerebellar regions. The results reveal that these high-escape stressors considered important for the development of FND could elicit transient freezing-like behavior even in a non-clinical population. The process of how such freezing-like behavior is maintained could be the target of future investigation in FND.
