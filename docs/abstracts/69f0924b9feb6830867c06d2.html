Introduction

Accurate subtyping and prediction of clinical outcomes in dementia remain major challenges due to inter-individual heterogeneity in brain phenotypes and disease progression1,2. Population-based modelling has emerged as a powerful framework3–5 for quantifying individual brain deviation from healthy ageing trajectories, with the clinical utility of these deviation patterns for early dementia risk stratification being an important area of research. BrainCharts, derived from >170,000 MRI scans spanning the human lifespan5,6, provide age- and sex-referenced centiles that quantify each individual's regional brain measures compared to a typical population, allowing us to get interpretable and standardised quantification of neuroanatomical changes. 

In this work, we hypothesised that unsupervised clustering of Braincharts-derived multi-regional centile profiles in Alzheimer's Disease (AD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) would reveal distinct neuroanatomical subtypes with differential clinical phenotypes, and that applying these learned patterns to cognitively healthy individuals (HC) could identify those exhibiting dementia-like brain phenotypes despite not showing any cognitive deficits at baseline assessment. We also predicted that such a subgroup would present worse cognitive outcomes over time. We therefore combined cross-sectional and longitudinal data to test whether brain-based risk stratification adds prognostic value beyond established risk factors (age, education, APOE genotype), and examined variables associated with cognitive resilience. This approach addresses a fundamental gap in precision medicine: the need for objective, population-referenced biomarkers that can identify individuals at elevated dementia risk before any clinical symptoms emerge. 

Methods

Participants and data: We analysed data from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC)7, including a total of N=6,039 participants with T1-weighted MRI. We used a large baseline cohort for subtype discovery (AD/MCI N=1,925; HC N=2,647) and a longitudinal validation cohort with ≥2 post-MRI cognitive/clinical assessments (AD/MCI N=1,228; HC N=2,117). 

BrainCharts Calculations: T1w scans were processed with FreeSurfer 7.3, and the data was harmonised with CombatLS8 to minimise site-differences. We excluded scans with an Euler Index (which quantifies topological defects/holes in the surface reconstruction of both hemispheres) of over N=2 median absolute deviations (MAD). The Freesurfer outputs were then used to calculate population-reference centile scores using the BrainCharts framework, which employs GAMLSS (Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape) to estimate age- and sex-stratified normative trajectories from a reference dataset of >170,000 scans5,6. We extracted 358 centile features: 68 cortical thickness (CT), 68 surface area (SA), 68 grey matter volume (GM), 3 global features (total cortical GM volume, total subcortical GM volume, and White Matter Volume), 37 subcortical volumes, and 114 asymmetry indices (see Dorfschmidt et al.6 for further details) derived from the Desikan-Killiany atlas.

Subtype Discovery: In line with robust methodology from existing literature9, we performed dimensionality reduction using UMAP10,11 with systematic grid search optimization on the baseline scans of AD/MCI participants (N=1,925). K-means clustering was applied to the 2-dimensional UMAP embedding. Optimal cluster number (k) was determined by composite evaluation of different scores (Davies-Bouldin index12, gap statistic, silhouette score,and bootstrap stability). Robustness was validated by comparing implementations with and without scaling of the input centiles.

Classifier Development and HC Risk Stratification: We trained a Random Forest classifier on AD/MCI cohorts using a pipeline with univariate feature selection (SelectKBest, f-test) and a Random Forest, with hyperparameters optimised by 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set (70% of AD/MCI). The best model was subsequently wrapped in a calibrated classifier. Performance was assessed on the held out 30% test set, and then the calibrated pipeline was refitted on all AD/MCI baseline participants for subtype-discovery on HC.

Longitudinal Analyses: For the longitudinal analyses, cognitive outcomes were assessed using CDR Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB)13, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)14, and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)15, with strict temporal filtering to only account for all the cognitive assessments occurring after the baseline MRI assessment. Main analyses included: (1) Linear mixed-effects models examining group × time interactions on cognitive trajectories; (2) Covariate-adjusted comparisons controlling for age, education, and APOE4 status. Among high-risk CN with follow-up, participants were classified as 'Resilient' (stable cognition despite AD-like brain pattern) or 'Declining' (progressive cognitive worsening) based on their follow-up cognitive data, with logistic regression examining predictors of resilience.

Results

Subtype Discovery and Validation: Composite cluster evaluation converged on k=2 as optimal, with strong bootstrap stability (ARI=0.96) and balanced cluster sizes (Cluster 1: 54.5%, N=1,050; Cluster 2: 45.5%, N=875). Scaled vs unscaled feature comparison demonstrated excellent reproducibility (ARI=0.805, 94.9% participant overlap). Cross-metric analysis revealed regional consistency, with high correlations across CT, SA, and GM features (mean r=0.71), indicating clusters capture patterns of regional vulnerability rather than just reflecting metric-specific effects.

Clinical Characterisation of Subtypes: Our analysis converged on a robust binary distinction: a more impaired subtype 1 enriched for AD diagnoses, and a less impaired subtype 2 with milder neuroanatomical deviations. Subtype 1 (more impaired) showed higher prevalence of AD individuals (51.0% vs 28.8%), worse cognition (MMSE: p<0.001; MoCA: p<0.001; CDR-SB: p<0.001), and lower education (14.6 vs 15.4 years, p<0.001) than Subtype 2. Diagnosis distribution also differed significantly (χ²=101.44, df=2, p<0.001). APOE4 carrier frequency was slightly higher in Subtype 1 (χ²=4.32, p=0.038). Regional centile profiles revealed Subtype 1 had globally lower centiles, with pronounced reductions in medial temporal areas (hippocampus, entorhinal cortex), subcortical regions, precuneus, and lateral temporal ones. This pattern is consistent with typical AD neurodegeneration (Figure 1 A,B). 

High-Risk Identification in Healthy Individuals: Applying the trained classifier to N=2,647 HC participants, identified N=1,069 (40.4%) as high-risk (i.e., predicted Subtype 1 membership), and N=2284 as Low-Risk (Subtype 2 membership). High-risk HC exhibited significantly lower mean centiles across all brain regions compared to low-risk HC (Cohen's d=−2.23, t=−58.43, p<0.001), and as shown in Figure 1 (D,F), the HC individuals in the at-risk cluster showed generally lower centiles across the whole brain in terms of cortical and subcortical GM volume, alongside SA. This confirmed that our classifier captured meaningful neuroanatomical differences even within the cognitively normal range.

Longitudinal Cognitive Outcomes: Among high-risk HC, N=932 had >2 post-MRI cognitive visits. Of these, N=329 (35.3%) showed clinically meaningful decline (conversion to MCI/dementia), whereas N=603 (64.7%) remained cognitively stable over time. Linear mixed-effects models demonstrated significant group × time interactions for CDR-SB (β=0.038, p=0.024) and MMSE (β=−0.113, p=0.029), indicating faster functional and global cognitive decline in high-risk HC after adjustment for age, education, and APOE4. MoCA trajectories were similar between groups, but high-risk HC started from lower baseline MoCA scores. In Cox models controlling for age, sex, education, and APOE4, high-risk HC had a 60% increased risk of incident dementia than low-risk HC (HR=1.60, 95% CI 1.13–2.27, p=0.008). 

Resilience Factors: Within the high-risk HC group, those who declined were substantially older at baseline than resilient high-risk HC (73.1 vs 65.0 years, p<0.01), and were more often APOE4 carriers (33.7% vs 25.8%, p=0.0009), whereas education levels were comparable. Therefore, among those individuals with dementia-like brain phenotypes at baseline, older age and APOE4 status markedly increase the likelihood of future cognitive decline, while a proportion of high-risk HC still remain clinically resilient over follow-up despite elevated structural brain risk.

Conclusion

Population-referenced brain centile maps derived from BrainCharts can be used to identify robust neuroanatomical subtypes in AD and MCI, and a centile-based classifier trained on these subtypes generalises with high accuracy to held-out patients. Critically, when projected into the space of cognitively healthy individuals, the more impaired subtype defines a group of high-risk HC with globally lower centiles and AD-like regional patterns, stratifying cognitively normal individuals by future decline risk. These individuals experience faster functional and cognitive decline over multi-year follow-up, even after adjusting for age, education, and APOE4 status, indicating that centile-based subtype membership carries prognostic information beyond classical risk factors. The ability to identify these at-risk individuals during a preclinical window represents a crucial advance for secondary prevention trials and personalised risk stratification. Future directions include validation in diverse community-based cohorts and integration with fluid and PET biomarkers to determine pathological substrates of subtypes.

In conclusion, population-reference brain centile profiles provide a scalable, interpretable framework for not just dementia subtyping, but also for preclinical risk stratification. By identifying cognitively healthy individuals with dementia-like brain phenotypes and who subsequently decline, alongside characterising factors potentially protecting others from such decline, this approach advances precision neurology toward the goal of intervening before irreversible cognitive loss occurs.
