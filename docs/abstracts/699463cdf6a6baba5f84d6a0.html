Introduction

Advancements in network neuroscience have redefined the brain as a complex, interconnected system, leading to the discovery of “brain fingerprinting”: the observation that individual functional connectivity patterns are unique, stable, and predictive of behavioral traits [1, 2]. While robust in healthy populations and emerging in neurodegenerative diseases [4, 5], the application of brain fingerprinting to stroke remains underexplored.

Stroke is inherently a network disorder; focal lesions trigger widespread disruption via mechanisms like diaschisis, altering global topology and impairing function. However, functional recovery is highly heterogeneous, driven by dynamic large-scale reorganization involving both ipsilesional and contralesional hemispheres. The distinction between adaptive and maladaptive plasticity is often obscured in group-level or cross-sectional studies, leaving the longitudinal evolution of individual brain fingerprints from the acute to the chronic phase poorly understood.

In this work, we address this gap by leveraging the TiMeS project [3], a longitudinal fMRI dataset tracking stroke patients at four key time points during the first year after stroke. We aim to characterize the temporal reconfiguration of the functional connectome, assessing the stability of individual-specific connectivity patterns and their relationship to functional outcomes. By mapping these individual trajectories, we aim to identify personalized network biomarkers predictive of recovery.

Methods

We leveraged the TiMeS dataset [3], an ongoing longitudinal observational study following up to 100 stroke patients at four timepoints over 1 year after the ictal event, assessed at four key stages (T1: 1 week, T2: 3 weeks, T3: 3 months, T4: 12 months). Resting-state fMRI scans were acquired at 3T (multi-band EPI, TR=1250ms, 2mm isotropic resolution) and preprocessed to derive individual functional connectomes. In the present analyses, n = 64 stroke patients were included. A normative baseline was established using two healthy control cohorts (ECONS, n = 31; TrainStim, n = 10), matched for age and sex. Individual functional connectomes were constructed by computing Pearson’s correlation coefficients between regional mean BOLD time series extracted from N = 377 regions, comprising 360 cortical areas from the Glasser parcellation and 17 subcortical regions from the HCP release. To facilitate network-specific analyses, nodes were assigned to the seven canonical resting-state networks (RSNs): Visual, Somatomotor, Dorsal Attention, Ventral Attention, Limbic, Fronto-Parietal, and Default Mode. To quantify patient deviations from normative functional architecture, we calculated a clinical similarity index (IClinical), adapted from Sorrentino et al. [4]. For each patient at every time point, this metric represents the average correlation between the patient’s connectome and the connectomes of the entire healthy control cohort. As a benchmark for normative healthy variability, we computed the pairwise similarity among the two healthy control groups (i.e., TrainStim with ECONS) [1]. Building on recent frameworks for brain identification [1, 2, 5], we then assessed the longitudinal stability of individual functional architectures using the self-similarity measure (ISelf). This is defined as the Pearson correlation between a subject’s flattened functional connectivity (FC) matrix at a specific time point (Ti) and their own FC matrix at a distinct time point (Tj). To characterize the evolution of these fingerprints during the critical early recovery window (1 week to 3 weeks), we utilized the chronic stage (T4) as a stable reference target. We compared the distributions of acute-to-chronic similarities (ISelf(T1, T4)) against subacute-to-chronic similarities (ISelf(T2, T4)) using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (FDR-corrected, q < 0.05). To investigate local alterations, we performed this comparison both at the whole-brain level and for specific pairs of functional resting-state networks. Finally, for pairs of networks showing significant evolution (p < 0.05), we computed a fingerprint recovery index, defined as the percentage increase in median self-similarity from T1 to T2 relative to the chronic baseline at T4.

Results

Our analysis of the TiMeS cohort revealed distinct longitudinal trajectories in the restoration of individual functional architectures. First, using the clinical similarity index (IClinical), we observed that the acute stroke phase (T1) is characterized by a significant deviation of patient connectomes from the normative healthy template compared to controls (p < 0.01). While these clinical similarity scores progressively improved over the first year of recovery, they remained significantly distinct from healthy control levels even at the chronic stage (T4), indicating a persistent, albeit reduced, alteration of the functional landscape. Examining individual stability through longitudinal self-similarity (ISelf), we found that patient connectomes were significantly less stable between the acute and chronic phases (T1-T4) compared to the sub-acute and chronic phases (T2-T4). This marked increase in stability after T1 suggests that the most substantial functional reorganization of the individual brain fingerprint occurs within the critical window between the first and third weeks post-stroke.

Network-specific analyses further pinpointed the topography of this reorganization. By comparing the T1-T4 and T2-T4 self-similarity matrices, we identified specific subnetworks that drive this stabilization. By analyzing the relative percentage increase in self-similarity from the acute to the sub-acute stage, we found statistically significant reconfiguration (FDR-corrected Mann-Whitney U test, q < 0.05) primarily in the Ventral Attention (VA), Limbic (L), and Somatomotor (SM) networks. This suggests that the re-establishment of individual-specific connectivity patterns is spatially heterogeneous, with higher-order attentional and limbic systems showing the most rapid functional consolidation from the acute to sub-acute phase.

Conclusion

This study provides the first longitudinal characterization of functional brain fingerprinting in stroke recovery. We demonstrate that stroke induces an acute disruption of individual functional signatures, which undergo a rapid, network-specific reconfiguration during the first three weeks before stabilizing into a chronic, yet distinct, functional architecture. By mapping these trajectories, we highlight the critical role of early sub-acute plasticity in re-establishing individual brain identity, particularly within attention and limbic networks. These findings establish brain fingerprinting as a sensitive, personalized metric for tracking neural reorganization, offering new potential biomarkers for predicting recovery outcomes and tailoring rehabilitation windows.
