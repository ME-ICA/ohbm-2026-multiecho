Introduction

Structural covariance networks (SCNs) capture spatial patterns of covariation in brain morphology, commonly as inter-regional correlations in grey matter volume or cortical thickness across a group of participants' structural MRI data.^1 SCNs are hoped to reveal between-group differences in structural brain network organisation, and often these differences are sought in graph-theoretic measures such as the degree of clustering, segregation into distinct modules, or the characteristic path length between nodes.^2 A typical practice is to compute SCN graph measures for two groups (e.g., case-control design), and use permutation testing to determine if the samples are significantly different in the graph measure of interest.^3^4 However, the statistical validity of group comparisons using SCN-derived graph measures remains poorly understood. We address this gap, using Monte Carlo simulations and resampling data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) to study how sample size (n) and atlas resolution (number of regions) influence the distribution of graph measures and error rates in downstream SCN analyses.

Methods

We analyzed cortical thickness data from 1,096 HCP participants, parcellated using 10 atlases spanning 14–360 nodes. Structural covariance networks (SCNs) were constructed by computing interregional correlation matrices, ensuring connectivity via minimum spanning tree, then binarizing at the top 10% of remaining correlations to produce the SCN adjacency matrix. Reliability analysis: We simulated sample correlation matrices from known ground-truth matrices (either empirical HCP or randomly generated) across a 10×10 grid of sample sizes (n=30–300) and atlas resolutions (14–360 nodes). Reliability of correlation matrices was quantified as the mean log-Euclidean distance from ground-truth. Reliability of derived SCNs was quantified as edge recall: the probability of correctly detecting a ground-truth SCN edge. Sampling distributions of graph measures: Using the Desikan-Killiany atlas (68 nodes), we generated bootstrap sampling distributions of five graph measures—clustering coefficient, characteristic path length, small-worldness, modularity, and global efficiency—at both large (n≈1096) and small (n=30) sample sizes. Simulation of group comparisons: To assess sensitivity and specificity of group comparisons, we selected exemplar correlation matrices yielding SCNs with high or low small-worldness. Paired samples were then simulated under either the null (identical ground-truth matrices) or alternative (distinct matrices) hypotheses. Small-worldness was compared using permutation testing (1000 permutations, α=0.05). We report sensitivity, specificity, and false discovery rate across sample sizes n=30–300.

Results

Simulation results demonstrated that sample correlation matrix reliability and SCN edge recall probability is inversely proportional to atlas resolution and directly proportional to sample size. At n=30 with commonly used atlases (68–360 nodes), recall of true edges was below 0.5, indicating that network topology is poorly preserved in small sample SCNs. Bootstrapped graph measure distributions revealed increased variance and systematic biases at small n: small-worldness and global efficiency were biased upwards, while modularity, path length, and clustering were biased downwards. The simulated graph-theoretic comparisons showed that, while false positive rate remains constant across sample sizes under permutation testing, false discovery rate was extremely high at low sample sizes (n≤30) due to low sensitivity, meaning that statistically significant group differences at this sample size and comparable atlas resolution (68 regions) were the result of random chance.

Conclusion

Simulation results indicates that at low sample sizes, SCNs cannot be reliably estimated (low edge recall probability), especially for high-dimensional parcellations. Studies with smaller sample sizes should restrict themselves to estimating networks with fewer nodes. Small-sample noise in the estimation of correlation matrices leads to bias in SCN graph measures, most notably by reducing characteristic path lengths, clustering, and modularity, because of the addition of random edges. This may lead to false positives in studies where sample sizes are not matched between groups. False discovery rate for graph-theoretic comparisons of SCNs is very high at commonly used sample sizes, casting doubt on a number of published research findings. We recommend: (1) restricting analyses to coarse parcellations or subsets of regions when n<100; (2) matching sample sizes between groups; (3) treating published small-sample SCN findings with appropriate skepticism. As a heuristic, researchers should consider that a p-node correlation matrix requires estimating (p²-p)/2 parameters, demanding substantially larger samples than typical practice. Future work should evaluate regularization approaches and alternative null models for SCNs.
