Introduction

Aging and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) both alter resting-state functional connectivity (FC), yet region-based studies report mixed hyper- and hypoconnectivity patterns without a unified model.¹ This may partly be explained by non-linear FC trajectories observed across the AD spectrum, suggesting stage-dependent shifts in network demands. Furthermore, because aging and AD disproportionately affect executive and memory functioning, respectively, differences in cognitive demands may contribute to differential effects on FC. Recent work2,3 suggests that these heterogeneous findings can be understood within the framework of functional axes or ‘gradients’ of brain organization (see Fig. 1).4 AD pathology and aging show dissociable FC reconfigurations aligned with two major axes: a sensory–association (SA) axis for AD and a representational–executive (RE) axis for normal aging. What drives this systematic alignment, and whether and how it relates to cognitive change in both conditions, are unanswered questions. Here, we illustrate robust effect patterns of aging and AD that align with these functional axes and aim to elucidate underlying factors that may be associated with these alignments. We jointly model age, AD pathology and other age related factors; evaluate whether memory and executive performance map differentially onto the gradients; and use parcel-wise mediation to determine whether gradient-aligned FC reconfiguration mediates the effects of age and AD on memory vs. executive function.

Methods

Data came from the BioFINDER-2 study (cross-sectional N=973; longitudinal N=378) and ADNI (N=129). All cognitively impaired individuals were Aβ-positive. Pathology scores [0,1] were derived through a non-linear mapping of CSF amyloid-β 42/40 and tau PET uptake quantified across three Braak regions. Resting-state fMRI was preprocessed using a modified version of the Configurable Pipeline for the Analysis of Connectomes (C-PAC). Parcelwise connectivity matrices (Schaefer-1000) were computed as Pearson correlations, and functional connectivity similarity (FCS or nodal affinity) was derived from cosine similarity of each parcel’s connectivity profile (Fig. 1A). Parcel-wise models (linear and mixed effects, Fig. 1C–E) estimated associations of FCS with age, AD pathology, and their longitudinal change. Resulting t-maps were correlated with the SA- and RE axis, derived as principal components from the average connectome of healthy controls (Fig. 1B). Non-linearities in t-map-to-gradient association were examined using sliding windows across baseline age and pathology (Fig. 1F,G). Cognition was decomposed using a Schmid–Leiman bifactor model into global cognition (g) and domain specific components (Fig. 2B). Parcel-wise models related FCS to each domain while adjusting for age, pathology, sex, motion, and g (Fig. 2C). Finally, parcel-wise mediation analyses tested indirect effects of age and pathology on memory and executive function (orthogonalized to g) through FCS (Fig. 2E).

Results

AD pathology–related FCS effects more strongly aligned with the SA axis, whereas age-related effects significantly aligned with the RE axis (Fig. 1C–D). Within-person increases in AD pathology (Δpathology) were associated with concurrent SA alignment in FCS (Fig. 1E), and non-linear analyses showed that this relationship weakened at higher baseline pathology (Fig. 1F). To test whether age alignments could be explained by other age- and risk-related factors, we jointly modelled age, AD pathology, atrophy, cerebrovascular burden, α-synuclein, and APOE ε4 status (Fig. 2A). Age- and pathology-related alignment remained robust, and all variables except ε4 status showed independent alignment with at least one of the gradients. Decomposing cognition into global, memory, and executive components (Fig. 2B) revealed that global cognition showed an inverse alignment with the SA axis, whereas memory and executive performance showed positive alignment with both the SA and RE axes (Fig. 2C). Aging disproportionately affected the executive component, and AD pathology the memory component (Fig. 2D). Parcel-wise mediation analyses showed that the indirect effects of age and AD on cognitive performance were spatially distributed along the corresponding gradients - SA for pathology and RE for aging (Fig. 2E). Notably, these relationships had opposite signs for the two different domains.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings show that aging- and AD-related FC alterations consistently align with large-scale functional gradients. These signatures are robust yet non-specific. They appear for other biological factors that may strain the neural system and vary with AD disease stage, reflecting the complex ways in which multiple biological forces interact with the brain’s intrinsic organization. Some of these forces exert differential cognitive pressures (e.g. aging vs AD), which may partly explain their alignment with separate axes. Although higher memory or executive performance did not show dissociable associations with the gradients, both showed positive alignments, suggesting that gradient-aligned FC may support cognitive functioning. The mediation analyses added nuance to this interpretation. They showed that indirect positive effects on cognition on one side of the gradient were associated with indirect negative effects on cognition on the other side. These effects were reversed between the two cognitive domains – such that patterns seemingly beneficial for one domain tended to be detrimental for the other – consistent with the notion that cognitive processes draw on shared, limited neural resources.5 Together, these results highlight the relevance of functional gradients for characterizing how aging and AD relate to FC and cognition, while underscoring the complexity and non-specificity of these relationships.
