Introduction

Recent studies have revealed a robust link between enhanced attentional ability and resilience against alcohol use disorder (AUD) in at-risk young adults. Therefore, enhancing attentional capacity in individuals with AUD that have attentional deficits may represent an effective therapeutic target for such individuals. One mechanism by which attention may protect against AUD is through “top-down” regulation of alcohol cue reactivity, as exposure to alcohol cues can capture attention and induce craving and motivation to drink alcohol in individuals with AUD. Leveraging the high rates of comorbidity of AUD and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), we sought to demonstrate whether experimentally enhancing attentional processing in individuals with AUD and attentional deficits (i.e., comorbid ADHD) reduces neural reactivity to alcohol cues. Methylphenidate is a medication commonly prescribed for ADHD to improve attention. We hypothesized that methylphenidate-associated attentional enhancement would engage brain mechanisms involved in top-down attentional control in support of reduced alcohol cue reactivity in young adults with AUD and ADHD. 

Methods

Thirty-three young adults (ages 18-24, 16 females) with co-occurring AUD and ADHD were recruited for a double-blind, placebo-controlled, within-subjects experiment to test the attentional effects of a single 20 mg dose of methylphenidate on alcohol cue reactivity measured with 3T fMRI. The 15-minute fMRI paradigm included six interleaved blocks of validated images of alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages separated by rating periods and rest blocks. Preprocessing and first-level analyses modeling BOLD responses to the two beverage conditions were completed in SPM12 software. A second-level linear mixed effects model including beverage condition (alcohol or neutral), pill (methylphenidate or placebo), and their interaction was run in AFNI’s 3dLME. Craving ratings were also obtained with the Alcohol Craving Questionnaire at the beginning of each session, prior to the scan (~45 minutes after taking the pill), and at the end of the session (~2 hours after taking the pill). A repeated-measures analysis of variance tested effects of pill and time on self-reported craving.

Results

Self-report data indicated that methylphenidate reduced experiences of craving as a function of (F(1.79,53.7)=3.29, p=0.05). FMRI analyses indicated that methylphenidate was associated with reduced neural activity in several cortical clusters, with the largest (249 voxels, p<0.001) spanning the rostral anterior cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal cortex (Figure 1; peak MNI coordinate: -4, 38, 12).

Conclusion

Although we did not find enhanced activation in attentional control regions with methylphenidate, we did identify reduced activation of the rostral anterior cingulate cortex, which has previously been positively associated with subjective craving during exposure to drug cues (Kühn and Gallinat, 2011). Activation in this region is also a target of addiction treatments, in which cue-related activation decreases during active treatment relative to placebo (Evohr et al., 2025). Combined with self-report evidence that methylphenidate reduced subjective alcohol craving, the fMRI data indicate that methylphenidate decreases craving-associated neural activation in young adults with comorbid AUD and ADHD. Given that these effects were not specific to the alcohol condition, the findings suggest that methylphenidate effects on craving may generally function through modulation of attention to salient stimuli.
