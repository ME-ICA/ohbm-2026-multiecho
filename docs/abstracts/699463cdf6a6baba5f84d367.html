Introduction

A central question in systems neuroscience is how the hippocampus transforms overlapping inputs into distinct episodic representations after a single encounter, a computational process defined as pattern separation. Computational models and animal electrophysiology attribute this computation to the dentate gyrus (DG) and CA3. However, whether the same is indeed the case for one-shot learning in humans is unclear, because prior studies either employed repetition-based paradigms, obscuring single-event memory reorganisation; or they relied on univariate signals that cannot distinguish true pattern separation via decorrelation of neuronal patterns from activation-based novelty responses. We therefore combined uni- and multivariate analysis approaches to 1) determine whether greater feature overlap results in a relative increase of hippocampal pattern separation relative to MTL cortical involvement as predicted by the pattern separation account, and 2) whether the mechanisms supporting resolution of feature interference are domain-general (e.g. across object-level and spatial features) or whether different regions along the anterior-posterior and transversal axes of the hippocampus are involved in a domain-specific manner during single-event pattern separation is unclear. 

Methods

Thirty-four healthy younger adults performed object and scene mnemonic discrimination tests for similar targets and lures in a 7-Tesla MRI scanner with 1mm isotropic resolution for echo-planar imaging. Participants were presented with two novel stimuli, followed by either exact repetitions or a lure with features similar to those of the target. We varied target-lure similarity to determine how different MTL cortical regions and hippocampal subfields overcome different degrees of feature overlap. We used the following approaches to obtain evidence for pattern separation-like neural signatures: 1) univariate activation strength during successful mnemonic discrimination (correct > incorrect lures) and novelty detection (correct lures > repeats) and 2) multivoxel pattern similarity for these same contrasts, where more pattern dissimilarity is indicative of orthogonalisation of similar neural representations in line with the predictions of the pattern separation account. We carried out analyses on a voxel-wise level across all MTL regions and on the level of regions of interest defined through either manual segmentation of subfields (CA1, CA3, subiculum, dentate gyrus)1 and most other MTL regions (Brodmann Area 35, BA36, parahippocampal cortex), with the exception of the amygdala, which was segmented using Automated Segmentation of Hippocampal Subfields algorithm for T1-weighted images2,3, and the entorhinal cortex which was split into anterolateral and posterior-medial subregions based on prior evidence that these regions belong to partially separable networks.4,5 In order to determine the response shape of a given region of interest as a function of trial type (first target, low, moderate and high similarity lure, repeated target) we conducted a curve fitting approach on univariate activity, fitting different power functions (example in Figure 1). As argued by prior studies, a region involved in pattern separation should have similar response levels across first targets and lures even at higher levels of feature overlap.

Results

All MTL regions had greater univariate activity during correctly identified lures averaged across all similarity levels compared to targets, showing clear novelty signals despite feature overlap. However, greater activity during correct vs. incorrect lures was more commonly found in hippocampal subfields, underscoring the behavioural relevance of hippocampal signals for successful mnemonic discrimination. BA35, anterolateral entorhinal cortex, posterior DG and the hippocampal tail maintained similar overall activity levels during novel targets and low, moderate, and even high-similarity object (for regions in the left hemisphere) and scene lures (regions in the right hemisphere). This is indicative of these regions treating lures like entirely novel stimuli despite feature overlap – in line with what prior univariate fMRI studies interpreted to be pattern separation (Figure 1). In contrast, all anterior hippocampal subfields showed suppressed activity already at low levels of feature overlap, revealing hippocampal long-axis heterogeneity. In our multivariate analysis, both anterior and posterior DG and CA3 pattern similarity between first object targets and correct lures was lower than similarity between firsts and repeats. This suggests that these regions actively decorrelate target and lure representations to overcome feature overlap - again in line with a pattern separation account despite univariate activity in anterior subfields having not met the criteria for pattern separation defined by prior univariate studies. This seemingly contradictory finding highlights that similar univariate response profiles can be accompanied by different multivariate representational patterns. Crucially, under conditions of high feature overlap for object lures, the proportion of hippocampal compared to cortical regions with more dissimilar neural patterns increased (Figure 2A), suggesting that hippocampal processes take over when cortical representations are insufficient to distinguish highly similar stimuli. Interestingly, brain responses during the scene task showed a different profile. While posterior CA3 exhibited multivariate signatures of pattern separation, anterior CA3 showed the opposite during lure discrimination: at high degrees of feature overlap, relatively fewer hippocampal than cortical voxels had greater pattern dissimilarity (Figure 2B). Instead, especially anterior CA3 and DG showed greater pattern similarity between targets and high-similarity lures. This may indicate different strategies for object and scene mnemonic discrimination: the former engages pattern separation, while the latter may require pattern reinstatement to successively compare features of the lure to detect a mismatch with the reinstated target representation. In line with this suggestion, neural indicators of pattern separation predicted trial-by-trial and inter-individual variability in object memory, whereas indicators of reinstatement predicted scene performance, underscoring their behavioral relevance.

Conclusion

We provide an unprecedented, fine-grained view of hippocampal subfield and long-axis heterogeneity needed to understand the fundamental organisation of MTL circuits and their role in memory fidelity during one-shot learning. While all MTL regions carry uni- and/or multivariate signals indicative of differentiating stimuli at low similarity levels, hippocampal CA3 and DG signals are more prominent under conditions of high feature overlap and more consistently correlate with behavioural outcomes. This is in line with the predictions of a pattern separation account of hippocampal involvement in episodic memory. Importantly though, especially anterior CA3 and DG showed striking flexibility in supporting pattern separation or reinstatement depending on feature overlap and stimulus domain, whereas posterior subfields were more dominated by pattern separation signals across conditions. We also show that identical univariate novelty responses can reflect opposing neural representational geometries, explaining prior inconsistencies in interpreting fMRI signatures of pattern separation and highlighting the need for multivariate approaches to gain mechanistic insight.
