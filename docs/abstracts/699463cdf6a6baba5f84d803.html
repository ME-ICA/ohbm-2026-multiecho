Introduction

Aging is often associated with a decline in higher-level cognitive functions – functions that are linked to coordinated interactions between large-scale functional brain networks. Connector hubs, or brain regions that strongly interact with multiple brain networks, serve as integration zones allowing for efficient information flow across networks and are critically involved in cognitive functioning. A comprehensive account of how connector hubs differ across the adult lifespan may provide important insights into cognitive aging and identify targets for brain-based intervention. Critically, however, the few previous studies on this topic used small amounts of fMRI data per person (5-10 min.) and a priori networks, which impact the reliability of functional connectivity and bias measurement of connector hubs.1 These confounds likely particularly impact lifespan findings, given age-related changes in brain networks and physiological biases. Here, in a sample of 144 adults spanning 18 to 75 years we characterized individual-specific functional networks and connector hubs to assess how connector hub location and strength differ across the adult lifespan.

Methods

A cross-sectional dataset of 144 subjects were used for these analyses. This sample includes N=87 young adults aged 18-35 years, N=31 middle adults aged 36-59 years, N=26 older adults aged 60-75 years. Young adults completed four 2.5 hour MRI sessions, while middle and older adults completed five 1.5 hour MRI sessions. Each individual collected more than 2 hours of resting state data across their MRI sessions. Functional MRI data were processed using current gold standard practices, including robust efforts to reduce the impact of head motion. After processing, each individual had at least 60 minutes of low motion resting state data remaining. For each individual, we identified an individualized set of 400 brain regions using Multi-session Hierarchical Bayesian Modeling.2 Low-motion resting state data were concatenated across runs and timeseries of blood oxygen level-dependent signal were extracted from each region. Functional connectivity matrices (400x400) were constructed by estimating Pearson correlations between the timeseries of each pair of regions. Then, we implemented the Infomap community detection algorithm to identify individual-specific networks.3 Finally, we calculated participation coefficient, a graph metric indexing the diversity of a region’s connections across brain networks, to assess hubness for each region.4 Regions in the top 20% of participation coefficient values for each individual were then labeled as connector hubs.

Results

First, we tested the stability of regional participation coefficient values between young adults and older adults with a spearman’s rank order correlation. We found strong correspondence in average participation coefficient values between YA and OA (rho=.92, p<.001). Additionally, we observed that regions with the highest participation coefficient values tended to be assigned to association networks, including networks involved in cognitive control and attention (e.g., fronto-parietal, cingulo-opercular, dorsal attention). Regions with the lowest participation coefficient values tended to be assigned to sensorimotor networks, including the dorsal and ventral somatomotor, auditory, and visual networks. Next, we tested for differences in the spatial location of connector hubs between younger and older adults using permutation testing. We found that the spatial correlation of hub locations across the cortex is very high (r=0.85) yet significantly lower than would be expected by chance (p=.001), indicating that nuanced but significant differences in hub spatial organization are seen with age. Additional analyses demonstrated age-related differences related to how connector hubs differ based on their position along the sensorimotor to association axis.

Conclusion

Overall, precision fMRI revealed a strong correspondence in the strength and location of connector hubs between young and older adults. These results suggest a largely stable hub architecture in functional brain networks across the adult lifespan, rather than a dramatic reorganization of connector hubs across age. Importantly, the precision approaches employed here, including collecting large amounts of low-motion resting state data from each individual and using techniques to assess individualized brain networks, address confounds present in prior reports and enable insights into connector hubs on an individual level. We plan to extend this work to explore variability in specific hub patterns within each age group. Findings from this work contribute to an understanding of age-related differences in brain network structure related to cognitive decline.
