Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe and chronic mental disorder characterised by recurrent psychotic episodes. It typically emerges in late adolescence or early adulthood, with a slightly earlier onset in males, and diagnosis is most often established after the first episode of psychosis (FEP). Accumulating evidence indicates that biological sex influences the incidence, clinical presentation and severity of schizophrenia1. Compared with females, males show an earlier age of onset limited to early- and middle-onset schizophrenia, poorer premorbid functioning, and more severe negative symptoms2.

Neuroanatomically, schizophrenia is associated with widespread alterations in both grey and white matter (WM). WM abnormalities, reflecting disrupted tissue organisation and connectivity, can be assessed using diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). Previous DWI studies have reported WM microstructural alterations across early- and late-onset schizophrenia, including in individuals experiencing their FEP. However, most investigations have relied on diffusion tensor imaging metrics, such as fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity, which provide voxel-averaged measures and cannot disentangle multiple fibre populations within a single voxel.

To address these limitations, Fixel-Based Analysis3 (FBA) was introduced as a framework for quantifying fibre-specific WM properties, offering a more anatomically specific characterisation of WM organisation. Afixel represents an individual fibre population within a voxel.

In this study, we performed sex-stratified analyses to examine WM microstructure in 79 male individuals with first-episode schizophrenia (FES) and 29 healthy male controls. Additionally, a female cohort (FES n=48, HC n=46) recruited during the same period was analysed. Using FBA, we aimed to identify fibre-specific abnormalities associated with early-stage illness. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate WM alterations at fixel level in individuals during their FEP.

Methods

Participants: All individuals were recruited as part of the Early-Stage Schizophrenia Outcome (ESO) study, a large prospective programme covering Prague and the Central Bohemia region.
For the FES groups, diagnoses were confirmed by two senior psychiatrists using ICD-10 Diagnostic Criteria for Research. Inclusion criteria required: (1) first psychiatric hospitalisation; (2) ICD-10 diagnosis of schizophrenia, acute/transient psychotic disorder, or schizoaffective disorder confirmed with the MINI; (3) <24 months of untreated psychosis; and (4) age ≥18 years. Individuals with psychotic mood disorders were excluded.
Healthy controls (HC) were recruited through local advertisements and excluded if they had any psychiatric disorder, substance abuse, or family history of psychosis in first- or second-degree relatives.
All participants were scanned using the same MRI protocol at the National Institute of Mental Health (Prague). FES individuals (males and females) were receiving antipsychotic treatment and were scanned within the first month of hospitalisation.

MRI Acquisition: MRI data were acquired using a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner.
DWI: spin-echo EPI; TR/TE = 8300/84 ms; voxel size 2 mm isotropic; flip angle 90°; six b0 volumes in each phase-encoding direction; two shells (b = 1000, 2500 s/mm²) with 64 directions each. T1-weighted MPRAGE: TR/TE/TI = 2300/4.63/900 ms; flip angle 10°; voxel size 1 mm isotropic; FOV 256 mm; GRAPPA 2.

MRI Preprocessing: All data modalities underwent visual quality control. Toolboxes and commands from MRtrix3 and FSL software packages were used. 

DWI preprocessing consisted of denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, susceptibility correction (TOPUP), eddy-current/head-motion correction (EDDY), and intensity inhomogeneity correction (N4, ANTs). WM and CSF intensities were normalised across participants. 

T1w images were coregistered to DWI using boundary-based registration, skull-stripped with BET, and segmented using FAST (FSL).

Fixel-based Analysis (FBA): FBA enables the assessment of three metrics: fibre density (FD), fibre-bundle cross-section, and their combined measure, providing a more anatomically precise characterisation of WM architecture. 

FBA was applied to assess WM microstructural alterations in FES in both sex-based cohorts separately. Connectivity-based fixel enhancement (CFE) was performed using 4 million streamlines from a 20-million-streamline tractogram template filtered with SIFT. Default CFE parameters were used (10 mm FWHM smoothing; C = 0.5; E = 2; H = 3). Non-parametric permutation testing (5000 permutations) produced family-wise error–corrected P-values. Absolute motion (RMS displacement from the first volume) obtained from EDDY was included as a nuisance covariate.

Fixels to Voxels Analysis: To test voxel-level generalisability of FBA results, a binary voxel mask was created from significant fixels (P < 0.05). Mean FD within this mask was extracted for each participant. Normality and variance were assessed, followed by a Student’s t-test (when criteria were met). Effect size was quantified using Cohen’s d.

The binary voxel mask was moved from male-template space to female-template space by applying transformations implemented in MRTrix3 software.

Results

The male-FES group showed significantly reduced fibre density (FD) compared with male healthy controls. These differences were primarily located in frontal WM regions of both hemispheres and included a thin segment of the anterior corpus callosum. All effects survived FWE correction, with p-values ranging from 0.05 to 0.03.

Figure 1A illustrates the streamlines corresponding to significant fixels, coloured by principal fibre orientation (RGB-XYZ convention). In Figure 1B, the same streamlines are displayed using a colour scale that represents the percentage FD reduction relative to the HC mean. Yellowish tones indicate regions where the FES group shows approximately 50% lower FD than controls.

To characterise the magnitude of these effects, we extracted the standardised effect sizes (Cohen’s d) for all significant fixels. Values ranged from 0.50 to 1.16, indicating moderate effects in darker blue fixels and strong group differences in lighter blue regions (Figure 1C).

We further computed each participant’s mean FD across all voxels corresponding to significant fixels (using the binary voxel mask). This analysis confirmed lower FD at the voxel level in the male-FES group (HC: 1.10 ± 0.11; FES: 0.97 ± 0.09). After verifying normality and homogeneity of variance, a two-sample t-test revealed a highly significant group difference (p = 3.46 × 10⁻⁸, d = 1.29). Figure 1D displays the voxel mask derived from significant fixels, along with the corresponding distributions of mean FD values for each group.

When the analysis was repeated in the female cohort, no significant group differences were observed in any of the FBA-derived metrics. Additionally, FD was examined in the WM region defined by the male cohort results (Figure 1), and no significant differences (p < 0.05) were detected between female groups (HC: 0.89 ± 0.079; FES: 0.88 ± 0.079; p = 0.535, d = 0.12) within this region.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified WM microstructural abnormalities in males experiencing a first episode of schizophrenia (male-FES) relative to male healthy controls (male-HC). Specifically, the male-FES group showed reduced fibre density (FD)—a proxy for the intra-axonal volume of a fibre population—indicating a lower apparent axonal content within affected bundles compared with controls.

The FD reductions were predominantly located in bilateral frontal WM, adjacent to cortical regions including the superior frontal gyrus, rostral anterior cingulate, and orbitofrontal cortex. The frontal cortex is consistently implicated in schizophrenia and has been linked to early symptom emergence, structural alterations, and impairments in social and cognitive functioning4,5.

In contrast, the corresponding female cohort did not show significant group differences in any of the three FBA-derived metrics. Moreover, restricting the analysis to the male-defined FD-altered region also revealed no FD differences between female-FES and female-HC. Together, these findings are consistent with the possibility of sex-specific timing in the emergence of WM changes, aligning with evidence for an earlier clinical onset in males. However, replication in larger, well-powered samples is essential to confirm the robustness and specificity of this pattern, to disentangle potential confounds related to biological sex, illness stage, and treatment exposure, and to explicitly consider the role of gender in addition to biological sex. Ultimately, clarifying how early white-matter alterations unfold—and whether they differ according to sex and gender—may support the development of more targeted and stage-sensitive interventions.
