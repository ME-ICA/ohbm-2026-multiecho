Introduction

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) tractography estimates the brain's white matter structural connectivity (SC) in vivo, but it cannot resolve the directionality of white matter pathways. While much work has developed methods for measuring directed functional connectivity^1. estimating a directed SC remains a significant gap in connectivity research. Here, we introduce a computational framework to infer directional SC using dMRI-derived SC with functional MRI (fMRI) in a physics-informed structure-function model.

Methods

Theory:

We introduce asymmetry to the SC matrix (C, Figure 1a) via a similarity transform ($\tilde{C}=ACA-1$), where $A = diag(e^{α})$ is a learnable diagonal matrix that encodes region-specific asymmetry by amplifying or dampening each region’s overall signal flow. Parameterizing directionality in this way is not only parsimonious, but it enforces detailed balance in the structural network. We then learn asymmetry parameters by entering the directed SC into the Higher Order Network Diffusion (HONeD) model, a generalization of the previously described Network Diffusion Model^2. HONeD is a physics-informed generative model for fMRI covariance ($F = 1/T \sum_t^T x(t) x(t)T$) as a multi-step diffusive processes along the SC. The HONeD model for a directed SC can be written as: $\hat{F}(β, ξ, α) = e^{(-βL + ξL2)T} e^{(-βL + ξL2)}$, where $L = I - Dout-1 \tilde{C}$ is the random walk graph Laplacian with Dout as the diagonal matrix of out-degrees (Figure 1b). Our model has one asymmetry parameter for each region (αi), and two global parameters determining the amount of signal diffusion through the network (β and ξ).

Model Fitting:

We fit the model parameters via hybrid gradient descent and genetic optimization to predict the empirical symmetric functional covariance in resting state fMRI time series. We optimized model parameters by minimizing the Frobenius norm of the difference between our model’s prediction and the empirical covariance. 

Datasets:

Our datasets included (i) 9 macaques (8 Macaca mulatta, 1 Macaca fascicularis) using a symmetric dMRI-derived SC and fMRI in the macaque RM atlas^3 with ground truth directionality measured from CoCoMac tracer-based studies^4 and (ii) 770 Human Connectome Project (HCP) subjects with 100 cortical regions in the Schaefer atlas and 14 subcortical regions^5, of which 34 subjects additionally had test-retest data. Note that macaque MRIs were acquired in a different study from tracer estimates, and even tracer estimates represent an aggregate across multiple studies with many different individual macaques.

Validation Analyses:

We first validated our theoretical approach in simulation with pre-defined “ground-truth” asymmetry and evaluated how well our model can estimate the induced asymmetry from a symmetric covariance under varying levels of noise. We then investigated how well out predictions compared to empirical tracer-based directionality in macaques. We computed the Spearman's correlation between empirical and predicted node skew out-degrees, then verified significance with a spin-test using 100,000 permutations. We evaluated our method's test-retest reliability using the HCP dataset, measuring both the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, one-way) and overall discriminability for each region’s asymmetry parameter across subjects.

Human Explorative Analyses: 

We inferred SC directionality across all 770 HCP subjects and quantified the total degree asymmetry for each region ($Dasym = log(Dout) - log(Din)$. We assessed the population-level distribution of regional degree asymmetry with a student's t-test with Bonferroni correction. As an additional analysis, we tested how signal seeded in the right thalamus (which had no significant directional bias across subjects) propagated through the directed network. We hypothesized that signal seeded in the thalamus in the directed SC would travel preferentially toward visual cortices compared to the undirected SC. 

Results

In simulation, we found that our model prediction significantly correlated with our ground-truth directionality (mean r=0.96; Figure 2a), and that our model retained good predictions under noise in the structural connectome (r ≈ 0.75) and in time series (r ≈ 0.70; Figure 2a). Moreover, our model accurately predicted the scale of simulated directionality, and it did not hallucinate directionality when the simulated “ground-truth” was undirected (Figure 2a, inset). 

Next, we estimated the directionality in the macaque dataset and compared our consensus prediction across all 9 macaques to the directionality estimated from CoCoMac tracer data. We found a significant correlation in skew out-degree (Spearman’s Rho=0.42, pspin<10-4; Figure 2b) which supports our model's biological plausibility. 

In humans, our directionality estimates showed fair-to-good reliability across regions (mean ICC=0.51; Figure 2c) and high subject discriminability (0.92, ppermutation=0; Figure 2c), suggesting that our directionality parameters are both replicable and discriminative in humans. 

To test how directed SC changes signal movement, we seeded signal in the thalamus of a consensus directed SC network from 770 HCP subjects, finding that our predicted directionality moves signal from the thalamus to visual areas, whereas undirected SC does not show such a thalamus-to-visual cortex flow (Figure 2d). 

In our consensus network, we observed that the caudate, putamen, temporal pole, inferior temporal gyri, and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex were the structural network's major sources; meanwhile, the amygdala, nucleus accumbens, pallidum, and the salience network formed major network sinks (Figure 2e). 

Conclusion

Overall, our HONeD-Asymmetry model to infer directed SC from an SC-FC model in fMRI 1) shows tractable and identifiable parameters, 2) corresponds with empirical tracer data in macaques, 3) shows good test-retest statistics. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of subject-specific white-matter directionality estimation with successful validation. Although our model's predictions in humans should be interpreted cautiously, our explorational analysis in human directionality suggests that the insula and cingulate regions represent the strongest network sinks while inferior temporal regions are the strongest network sources.

This method has the potential to provide many new avenues for studying brain networks across both basic neuroscience and clinical applications. Possible applications include modeling brain disease progression, evaluating directionality changes in therapeutic interventions or development, and computing novel metrics of directed functional connectivity.
