Introduction

Sound symbolism refers to the non-arbitrary relationship between sound and meaning (e.g., the bouba-kiki effect^1). Sound symbolic impressions of sounds can be various impressions such as size, sharpness, and hardness. For example, voiced obstruents (e.g., /g/, /z/) are perceived as hard whereas voiceless sonorants (e.g., /r/, /m/) are perceived as soft^2, obstruents are perceived as spiky whereas sonorants as round^3, and the back vowels (/a/, /o/) are perceived as big whereas the front vowels (/i/, /e/) are perceived as small^4. While some previous studies have identified isolated brain regions for specific sound symbolic types, it remains unclear whether a common neural mechanism underlies various sound symbolic impressions. We hypothesized that all sound symbolic judgments recruit a shared embodied network, specifically involving articulatory motor simulation and cross-modal integration and further that brain regions associated with articulation would contribute broadly to the semantic processing of sound symbolism. To test this, we investigated brain activity during the judgment of three distinct sound symbolic types: Sharpness, Hardness, and Size.

Methods

Thirty-three healthy native Japanese speakers participated in the study. This study was approved by the ethical committee of the National Institute for Physiological Sciences. All participants provided written informed consent. We utilized 3T fMRI to measure brain activity while participants listened to 67 different types of Japanese 1-mora syllables. Participants were required to answer their impression of the syllable using a 7-point Likert-scale (e.g., ‘most rounded’ = 0 and ‘most pointed’ = 7). Participants provided one impression (hardness, size, sharpness) per each one run. Additionally, as a control condition, they answered which sound consisted only of vowels or consisted of vowels and consonants. This condition served to control for low-level auditory processing, attention, phonological retention, and motor response. Participants completed the control condition run first and then completed the rest of the run of the impression condition. The order of the three runs of impression condition was random. Data were preprocessed and analyzed using the HCP (Human connectome project) pipelines and FSL (FMRIB Software Library). We performed a conjunction analysis to identify common neural substrates across the three conditions and a parametric modulation analysis to identify regions correlated with the intensity of subjective impressions. For the group-level analysis, statistical maps were thresholded at Z > 1.64, with a peak-level FDR correction of p < 0.05. 

Results

In the size condition, the results indicate that vowels influence the impression of size; specifically, front vowels (/i/, /e/) tended to be perceived as small, whereas back vowels (/a/, /o/) are likely to be perceived as big (Figure 1a). There were similar tendencies observed for sharpness and hardness. (Figure 1b,c) Sharpness and Hardness ratings were primarily driven by consonant types (obstruent/sonorant), Obstruent (e.g., /z/, /d/) were perceived as hard and spiky, whereas sonorant (e.g., /m/, /r/) were soft and round. The fMRI conjunction analysis revealed significant common activation in the left premotor cortex (PMC), inferior parietal cortex (IPC), and lateral temporal cortex across all three conditions compared to the control task. Finally, parametric modulation analysis demonstrated that activity in left inferior frontal gyrus (Broca’s area) and IPC positively correlated with the subjective intensity of the sound symbolic impressions across the type of impressions (Figure 1d).

Conclusion

Our findings suggested that sound symbolism relies on a distributed network involving the motor system and multimodal integration areas, regardless of the impression type. Behavioral results, consonant-driven hardness/sharpness versus vowel driven size impression, support the view of that articulatory features of 1-mora syllable shape perceived impressions. We also observed some exceptions consistent with articulatory influence. The back vowel /u/ was perceived as small, likely because the Japanese /u/ is unrounded (compressed). Additionally, the consonant /s/ showed a pattern distinct from other obstruents (sharp yet soft), suggesting that factor beyond voicing, such as the manner of articulation, influence the perception of hardness. Taken together, the involvement of the PMC and Broca’s area, which are linked to the articulation process, along with the IPC, associated with cross-modal binding, supports the hypothesis that the brain utilizes articulatory circuits to extract sound symbolic meaning from nonsense syllables. The present study has shed light on the role of the embodied neural network in translating auditory input into sound symbolic meaning.
