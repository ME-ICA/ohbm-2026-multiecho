Introduction

Quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) is a non-invasive MRI technique that provides an in vivo proxy for brain tissue iron content1. Iron is essential for oxygen transportation, mitochondrial ATP production, and neurotransmitter synthesis, yet excessive or poorly regulated iron exacerbates oxidative stress through reactive oxygen species formation. Brain iron homeostasis, therefore, requires tight regulation, maintaining sufficient iron for metabolic demands while preventing toxic accumulation.

QSM studies have consistently shown that brain iron increases with age and is linked to cognitive performance and brain health2–4. However, this literature has largely relied on region-of-interest approaches, focusing on a limited set of a priori selected structures such as the basal ganglia or hippocampus. While informative, this strategy implicitly assumes that iron-related vulnerability is spatially localized, and overlooks the possibility that iron dysregulation manifests as coordinated, distributed patterns across cortical and subcortical systems rather than isolated regional effects. This limitation is particularly problematic given that iron metabolism is governed by global physiological processes which are unlikely to respect anatomical boundaries. 

Here, we address this gap using a whole-brain, data-driven approach in a population of healthy individuals aged 30-40. First, we apply principal component analysis (PCA) to extract dominant between-subject spatial patterns of QSM values across the entire brain. We then relate these patterns to physiological, hematological, and cognitive markers linked to iron metabolism. This approach allows us to identify not only whether such markers are associated with brain iron, but which large-scale spatial susceptibility patterns are involved, providing a systems-level perspective on brain iron regulation.

Methods

Quantitative susceptibility maps were acquired using a multi-echo gradient echo sequence (0.85 mm isotropic) in a 3T scanner and reconstructed with a custom MATLAB pipeline incorporating SEPIA, ARLO, FANSI, and MEDI. QSM maps were registered to individual MP2RAGE scans to improve segmentation accuracy5. After visual quality control, we included data from 256 healthy participants (147 females) aged 30-40 years (mean = 34.0, SD = 2.9).

Cortical and subcortical segmentation was performed using FreeSurfer, with cortical regions defined by the Desikan-Killiany–Tourville (DKT) atlas and subcortical regions by the aseg atlas. For cortical gray matter, QSM values were sampled from the middle of the cortical layer; subcortical values were averaged within each structure. PCA was applied to region-wise QSM values, with component retention determined by Horn’s parallel analysis.

Component scores were entered into multivariable linear regression models, including age, sex, body fat mass index (BFMI), red blood cell count, burnout exhaustion (Utrecht Burnout Scale), average fatigue during the previous week assessed via ecological momentary assessment, working memory performance (backward digit span), and reaction time measures.

Results

Horn’s parallel analysis supports retention of five QSM components, which together explained 68.6% of the total variance (PC1 = 46.9%; PCs 2–5 explaining 3.1–8.5%). The first component (PC1; Figure 1) reflects a whole-brain susceptibility pattern and is positively associated with age (β = 0.20, p = 0.004) and burnout exhaustion (β = 0.18, p = 0.015), indicating a dominant global influence on brain susceptibility. PC2 selectively associates with EMA-derived state fatigue (β = 0.20, p = 0.014) and involves primarily caudal–lateral cortical regions. PC3 (Figure 1) associates with working memory performance (β = 0.16, p = 0.040) and includes frontal regions and subcortical structures. PC4 shows a specific association with red blood cell count (β = 0.22, p = 0.027), consistent with hematological contributions to susceptibility. PC5 exhibits strong sex differences (β = −0.79, p < 0.001) and additional associations with body fat mass index (β = 0.22, p = 0.016) and red blood cell count (β = −0.23, p = 0.010).

Conclusion

In a large sample of healthy adults in their thirties, whole-brain QSM revealed multiple dissociable susceptibility patterns linked to aging, subjective burnout scores, subjective fatigue, cognitive performance, hematological and metabolic markers. Importantly, global and regionally specific susceptibility effects coexisted and could be empirically separated. The dominant component captured a brain-wide pattern associated with age and burnout score, consistent with cumulative or systemic influences on iron regulation. In contrast, other components exhibited more spatially constrained patterns related to subjective fatigue, working memory performance, and red blood cell count. These findings indicate that different aspects of iron metabolism and related physiological processes map onto distinct large-scale susceptibility configurations, even within a relatively narrow and healthy age range.

Methodologically, this work demonstrates the value of whole-brain, multivariate analyses for studying brain iron. By modeling spatial patterns rather than isolated regions, this approach avoids misattributing global effects to local structures and provides a systems-level view of iron-related susceptibility. Together, these findings establish whole-brain QSM pattern analysis as a sensitive framework for characterizing heterogeneity in brain iron regulation. Future work will assess the reproducibility of these spatial patterns in independent datasets and extend the analysis to white matter by integrating diffusion tensor imaging with QSM, allowing us to disentangle contributions of iron and myelination to susceptibility signals.
