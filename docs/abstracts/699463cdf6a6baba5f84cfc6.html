Introduction

Panic disorder (PD) is characterized by recurrent unexpected panic attacks accompanied by persistent concern about future attacks and maladaptive behavioral changes.¹ While pharmacological and psychotherapeutic interventions demonstrate efficacy for many patients, a substantial proportion fail to achieve adequate treatment response, highlighting the need to identify neural markers distinguishing responders from non-responders.

Resting-state functional connectivity (FC) has emerged as a valuable tool for investigating the neural substrates of anxiety disorders.² Previous studies have implicated alterations in the default mode network (DMN), salience network, and frontoparietal network in PD pathophysiology.³ The insula, as a key hub of interoceptive processing, plays a central role in translating bodily sensations into fear responses—a core mechanism underlying panic attacks.⁴

However, longitudinal studies examining how FC patterns change differentially based on treatment outcome remain limited. Understanding the distinct neural trajectories of treatment responders versus non-responders could provide insights into mechanisms of therapeutic change and identify potential biomarkers for treatment prediction.

This study investigated whether patients with PD who respond to treatment show different patterns of FC change compared to non-responders over a 6-month treatment period, using a 2×2 mixed-design analysis examining group-by-time interactions.

Methods

Participants: Twenty-five patients with PD completed both baseline and 6-month follow-up resting-state fMRI assessments. Patients were classified as treatment responders (n=19) or non-responders (n=6) based on clinical improvement criteria at 6-month follow-up. Treatment included standard pharmacotherapy.

Image Acquisition: Resting-state fMRI data were acquired using a 3T MRI scanner. Participants were instructed to remain awake with eyes closed during the scan.

Preprocessing: Standard preprocessing steps were applied, including slice-timing correction, realignment, normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing. Nuisance regression was used to get rid of signals from white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and motion parameters.

FC Analysis: Whole-brain FC matrices were computed using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas comprising 116 regions of interest (ROIs). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the mean time series of all ROI pairs, yielding 6,670 unique connections per subject. Correlation coefficients were Fisher z-transformed for statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis: A 2×2 mixed-effects ANCOVA was conducted for each FC pair with the group (responder vs. non-responder) as a between-subject factor and the session (baseline vs. 6-month) as a within-subject factor. Age and sex were included as covariates. The primary outcome of interest was the group×session interaction, indicating differential FC changes between groups over time. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons across all FC pairs, with the significance threshold set at q<0.05.

Results

Three functional connections showed significant group×session interactions after FDR correction, all demonstrating opposite directional changes between responders and non-responders.

First, the connection between right precuneus and left superior temporal gyrus (STG) showed a significant interaction (F=17.18, p-FDR=0.023). Treatment responders exhibited increased FC from baseline to 6-month follow-up, while non-responders showed decreased connectivity. 

Second, the connection between left orbital middle frontal gyrus (OFC) and right inferior parietal lobule (IPL) demonstrated a significant interaction (F=16.99, p-FDR=0.023). Responders maintained stable connectivity across time points, whereas non-responders showed a marked increase. 

Third, the connection between left insula and left cerebellar lobule 9 showed a significant interaction (F=14.96, p-FDR=0.045). Responders demonstrated decreased FC, while non-responders exhibited increased connectivity.

Across all three significant connections, responders and non-responders showed opposing trajectories, indicating fundamentally different neural reorganization patterns associated with treatment outcome

Conclusion

This study reveals distinct functional connectivity trajectories distinguishing treatment responders from non-responders in PD. Three key findings emerge from the analysis.

First, successful treatment is associated with enhanced DMN-temporal connectivity. The increased precuneus-STG connectivity in responders suggests improved integration between self-referential processing and external information processing, potentially reflecting enhanced cognitive flexibility and reduced self-focused rumination characteristic of anxiety disorders.

Second, treatment non-response is characterized by maladaptive network hyperconnectivity. The excessive OFC-IPL connectivity increase observed exclusively in non-responders may represent a failed compensatory mechanism, with heightened but ineffective emotion-attention coupling maintaining a state of chronic hypervigilance.

Third, interoceptive circuit normalization differentiates treatment outcomes. The insula-cerebellum connectivity pattern provides direct evidence for the role of body-emotion integration in PD.⁵ Responders showed reduced connectivity, suggesting decreased reliance on cerebellar prediction error mechanisms to regulate interoceptive signals. Conversely, the dramatic connectivity increase in non-responders indicates persistent dysregulation of the body-to-fear translation process central to panic pathophysiology.

These findings suggest that treatment response in PD involves coordinated network reorganization across three functional domains: self-awareness and language integration, emotion-attention balance, and interoceptive normalization. The consistent pattern of opposing changes between groups indicates that treatment non-response is not simply an absence of change but involves distinct maladaptive neural adaptations.

Limitations include the relatively small sample size, particularly for the non-responder group, and the naturalistic treatment design. Future studies with larger samples and randomized controlled designs are needed to validate these biomarkers and explore their predictive utility for treatment selection.
