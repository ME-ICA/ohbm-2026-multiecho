Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder marked by degeneration of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons and widespread alterations of large-scale brain dynamics. Beyond its classical motor symptoms, PD is increasingly understood as a network disorder, characterized by pathological synchronization within cortico–basal ganglia–thalamic circuits. In particular, excessive beta-band (13–30 Hz) oscillations and hyperconnectivity have been consistently linked to motor impairment and clinical disability. Although dopaminergic medication and deep brain stimulation (DBS) are effective in alleviating symptoms, the network-level mechanisms through which these interventions reshape pathological dynamics remain poorly understood. Bridging empirical electrophysiological biomarkers with mechanistic, biophysically grounded models is therefore critical for advancing personalized neuromodulation strategies.

In recent work, we demonstrated that whole-brain computational modeling, when constrained by individual anatomy and physiology, can provide mechanistic insight into disease-specific brain states and therapeutic responses.^1,2 In parallel, next-generation neural mass models derived from exact mean-field reductions of spiking networks offer a principled way to incorporate neuromodulatory processes—such as dopamine-dependent synaptic modulation—into large-scale brain simulations. Here, we combine source-reconstructed EEG connectivity analysis with a dopamine-sensitive neural mass modeling framework to investigate how pharmacological (L-Dopa) and electrical (DBS) neuromodulation reorganize large-scale brain dynamics in PD.

Methods

We analyzed high-density resting-state EEG recordings from patients with Parkinson’s disease acquired preoperatively (ON and OFF L-Dopa medication) and postoperatively, six months after DBS implantation (ON and OFF stimulation, ON and OFF L-Dopa). EEG signals were source-reconstructed using individual structural MRI, enabling cortical-level analysis of large-scale functional networks. Functional connectivity was quantified using amplitude envelope correlation (AEC) and phase-locking value (PLV) across canonical frequency bands (delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma). Statistical significance of connectivity changes across medication and stimulation conditions was assessed using permutation-based analyses.

In parallel, we developed a biophysically grounded neural mass model incorporating dopamine-dependent neuromodulation, based on a next-generation mean-field formalism derived from adaptive quadratic integrate-and-fire neuron networks. The model explicitly accounts for dopamine dynamics through a slow-timescale variable governed by Michaelis–Menten reuptake kinetics and receptor-mediated modulation of excitatory synaptic conductances, consistent with D1-type dopaminergic effects. Bifurcation and numerical analyses were used to characterize how changes in dopaminergic tone and excitatory drive reshape the dynamical repertoire of neural populations, including transitions between asynchronous activity, oscillatory regimes, and bistability. This modeling framework was designed for integration into The Virtual Brain (TVB) platform, enabling patient-specific whole-brain simulations.

Results

EEG connectivity analysis revealed a robust and selective reduction of beta-band functional connectivity following DBS, representing the largest and most consistent effect across all frequency bands. Both AEC and PLV measures showed significant decreases in beta-band coupling, particularly among frontal and temporal cortical regions. In contrast, changes in delta, theta, alpha, and gamma bands were comparatively modest and spatially limited. Importantly, beta-band connectivity was systematically modulated by both DBS and dopaminergic medication, supporting its role as a sensitive biomarker of pathological network synchronization in PD.

Computational modeling revealed that increasing dopaminergic modulation shifts neural populations away from pathological oscillatory and bistable regimes toward more stable dynamical states. Bifurcation analysis demonstrated that dopamine acts as a control parameter regulating the emergence and suppression of synchronized oscillations, particularly in frequency ranges compatible with beta-band activity observed empirically. Simulations mimicking dopaminergic enhancement and DBS-related parameter changes reproduced the attenuation of pathological oscillations observed in EEG data, providing a mechanistic link between neuromodulatory processes and large-scale connectivity changes. These results support the interpretation that DBS and L-Dopa converge on a common dynamical mechanism: suppression of excessive beta synchronization through modulation of population excitability and synaptic gain.

Conclusion

By integrating source-resolved EEG connectivity measures with a dopamine-sensitive neural mass model, we move beyond descriptive biomarkers toward a mechanistic understanding of how neuromodulation reshapes large-scale brain dynamics. The modeling results suggest that dopaminergic tone governs transitions between qualitatively distinct dynamical regimes, offering a unifying explanation for the effects of both pharmacological treatment and DBS.

Embedding these models within The Virtual Brain framework will enable the construction of individualized digital twins of Parkinson’s disease patients, combining anatomy, electrophysiology, and neuromodulatory mechanisms. Such personalized models provide a powerful platform for predicting patient-specific responses to DBS and dopaminergic therapies, optimizing stimulation parameters, and advancing precision medicine in network neurology.^3 More broadly, this work illustrates how next-generation neural mass models can bridge molecular neuromodulation, electrophysiological biomarkers, and whole-brain dynamics, for clinical conditions such as Parkinson’s Disease.
