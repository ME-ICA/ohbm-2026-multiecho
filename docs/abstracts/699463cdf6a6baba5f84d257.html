Introduction

For the brain to function appropriately, it is necessary not only to activate regions relevant to a given task but also to effectively suppress regions irrelevant to the task. Recent accumulating evidence suggests that negative BOLD (NB) responses—often observed as regional deactivation—reflect such neural inhibition1,2. However, it has never been causally demonstrated that suppressing irrelevant regions enhances a brain function. One representative form of NB response is cross-modal inhibition, a phenomenon in which the activity of a sensory cortex decreases when information from other sensory modalities is being processed. For example, visual cortices usually show deactivation during purely auditory tasks. We have previously demonstrated that auditory hand motor performance is better when the visual cortices are more deactivated during an auditory hand motor task3. In the current study, we used transcranial static magnetic field stimulation (tSMS), a non-invasive method known to reduce cortical excitability4, to test whether suppressing activity in the visual cortex can causally improve auditory hand motor performance. We conducted two experiments. In Experiment 1, participants performed the same auditory hand motor task while receiving either real tSMS or sham stimulation over the visual cortex. We expected that real tSMS would improve task performance. In a separate cohort (Experiment 2), we acquired fMRI data before and after real or sham tSMS to investigate changes in neural activity and functional connectivity underling the expected behavioral improvement.

Methods

In Experiment 1, we conducted a randomized, sham-controlled, single-blind behavioral study with 22 healthy right-handed young adults, who were randomly assigned to either a tSMS or sham group. The participants sat on a chair with their heads stabilized and wore a tSMS-specific helmet (MAGdpv1.1, Neurek) to ensure accurate placement over Oz. With their eyes closed and covered, they completed two sessions of the auditory hand motor task before stimulation, eight sessions during 20-min stimulation, and two sessions after stimulation. During the stimulation, either a real magnet (tSMS; Mag60r, Neurek SL) or a nonmagnetic cylinder (sham; Mag60s) was positioned over Oz (Figure 1A). Each session comprised three 15-s task epochs alternating with three 15-s rest epochs, starting with the rest. During task epochs, participants pressed a button in synchronization with a 1-Hz tone while keeping their finger on the button. The same tone was presented during rest epochs. Task performance was quantified as the absolute timing discrepancy between tone and button press.

In Experiment 2, 44 healthy right-handed young adults participated and were randomly assigned to either a tSMS or sham group under a double-blind procedure. As in Experiment 1, they performed eight sessions of the auditory hand motor task during stimulation. In addition, brain activity was measured with fMRI while the participants performed the same task before (pre) and after (post) the stimulation. Functional images were collected using T2*-weighted gradient echo-planar imaging with a 3.0-Tesla MRI scanner (Trio Tim; SIEMENS, Germany) and a 64-channel array coil for each participant. For the fMRI data analysis, we identified brain regions exhibiting tSMS-specific pre–post changes of task-related activity and task-related functional connectivity.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Institute of Information and Communications Technology and the MRI Safety Committee of the Center for Information and Neural Networks (CiNet; no. 2003260010). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after full explanation of the study procedures.

Results

In Experiment 1, the timing discrepancy between tone and button press decreased over the course of the stimulation in the tSMS group (Figure 1B red). In contrast, no such change was observed in the sham group (Figure 1B green). Statistical analysis using a linear mixed-effects model revealed a significant time × group interaction (F(1,152) = 6.28, p = 0.01), indicating that the temporal patterns of performance change differed between the two groups. Post-hoc analyses showed a significant effect of time in the tSMS group (Coefficient = -0.42; t = -2.75, p = 0.007), whereas no significant time effect was found in the sham group. These results indicate that tSMS applied to the visual cortex improves auditory hand motor task performance. We could replicate this tSMS effect in an independent group of other healthy younger adults (Figure 1B blue, n=11, Coefficient = -0.38; t = -2.17, p = 0.03), suggesting replicability of the tSMS effect.

In Experiment 2, we again confirmed the same tSMS effect: the timing discrepancy gradually decreased only in the tSMS group (interaction, F(1,306) = 5.19, p = 0.02). On the other hand, no significant between-group (real or sham) differences in either neural activity or functional connectivity were observed. One possible reason for the absence of significant group-level effect was inter-individual variability. Therefore, we divided the participants of the tSMS group into those who showed greater improvement and those who showed smaller improvement, and compared their pre–post neuronal changes (interaction: greater or smaller x pre or post). After tSMS, the participants with greater behavioral improvement exhibited greater deactivation in the early visual cortices (V1 and V2) and in default mode network regions (precuneus and medial prefrontal cortex), when compared with those who showed smaller improvement (Figure 2A, B). Notably, in the former participants, the visual cortices reduced functional connectivity with the hand section of the medial-wall motor region, i.e., cingulate motor area (CMA; Figure 2C, D)—rather than with the hand area of the lateral motor cortex, i.e., primary motor cortex.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that applying tSMS to the visual cortex can causally improve performance on the auditory hand-motor task, and its underlying neural mechanisms are suppression of activity in the visual cortices along with medial regions and decrease in functional coupling between the visual cortices and the medial-wall hand motor region (CMA), which may lead to reduction of interference from visual system to motor system. This study provides the first compelling behavioral and neuroimaging evidence from cross-modal inhibition that suppressing brain regions irrelevant to a task can causally enhance performance on that task.
