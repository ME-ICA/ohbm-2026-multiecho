Introduction

Age-related modifications of the brain have been largely described based on either morphometric MRI parameters for grey matter part or diffusion microstructural parameters for white matter part. The underlying cellular events invoked to explain these modifications are very different physiopathological process. Indeed, whether these age-related microstructural changes are due to dysmyelination, axonal degeneration, or gliosis from persistent low-level inflammation remains unclear. Quantitative mapping has been developed to improve reproducibility and histological interpretation of these tissue properties changes. Among these techniques, quantitative Magnetization Transfert (qMT) is sensitive to macromolecular density of the tissue (Sled, 2018). Classicaly, qMT is based on the exchange between two pools of hydrogen nuclei, those in free water and those bound to macromolecules to extract parameters such as F, the pool size ratio, which is efficient to assess myelin quantity in the white matter part of the brain (Plank et al., 2024). Another parameter arising from the qMT is the rate of magnetization transfer from the restricted pool to the free pool (krf); this last one appears to be more sensitive to mictrostructural changes of the grey matter and could be related to glia and metabolic activity of the tissue. A such, it is increased in the insular cortex after vaccine induced acute inflammation (Harrison, et al., 2015) and decreased in posterior cingulate cortex and in the hippocampus during aging (Metzler-Baddeley, et al., 2019) and in Alzheimer’s disease condition (Giulletti et al., 2012). The first aim of this study was to describe these quantitative magnetization parameters of the brain tissue in a large cohort (Bcube) of aging subjects (n=295). In a second step, the impact of these microstructural states of the tissue on functional connectivity of the 7 major resting state networks was assessed.  

Methods

Methods

A subsample of 295 volunteers (mean age: 65+4 yo; 207 women) of the Bcube cohort underwent an MRI examination at the bioimaging platform of the University of Bordeaux.

MRI acquisition

MRI scans were acquired on a 3T MRI Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthcare) equipped with a 64-channel head/neck coil. A T1-weighted (T1w) structural scan was acquired using a 3D MPRAGE sequence (TR = 2120 ms; TE = 2.35 ms; FA = 9°; Inversion Time = 1070 ms; FOV of 256x256x192 mm3 with a voxel size of 0.8 mm3). Magnetization transfer acquisition was performed with ten 3D MT-prepared spoiled gradient recalled echo sequences with the following parameters: TR 25ms, TE 2,30ms, Resolution 2mm3 with different flip angles (100°, 140°, 180°, 340°, 350°, 630°) and offset frequencies (1000hz, 2700hz, 2750hz, 12350hz, 25000hz). This acquisition is associated with B0 and B1 maps to correct for spatial inhomogeneities and a T1 map to constrain the qMT model. Six-hundred resting-state functional scans were acquired using a single-shot multi-band gradient-echo EPI sequence with a whole-brain resolution of 2.5 mm3 (TR=1000ms, TE=30ms, MB factor=4) with two spin echo EPI images acquired in opposite phase-encoding direction to correct for geometric distortions. 

During the fMRI session, participants will be instructed to remain eyes closed and not to fall asleep.

 MRI analysis

MT-weighted images processing has been performed with house-written pipelines and the qMRLAB toolbox. The first step involves realignment using a rigid-body transformation between the 10 MT volumes acquired per subject to correct for interscan motion. Then, a two-pool fitted model from Ramani (Ramani et al., 2002) was performed including B0 and B1 maps to correct for magnetic field and RF pulse variations in the volume and using the T1 map to constrain the value of R1 in the Ramani model. The output results in two metrics: the pool-size ratio F, the magnetization exchange rate from restricted to free pool Krf. Finally, the images were normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI152) template and smoothed at 4 mm3.

The functional MRI data were quality-controlled using mriqc and preprocessed as follow: corrected for gradient non-linearity effects using gradunwarp (version 1.0.3), corrected for susceptibility-induced distortions using the top-up method of FSL5.0.11 (FMRIB, Oxford, UK). The images were then motion-corrected using linear transformations with MCFLIRT in FSL and intensity-scaled to 1000. ICA-AROMA was then applied to remove motion-induced signal variations that could not be suppressed with rigid-body registration. Finally, physiological noise correction was applied using the PhysIO toolbox in MATLAB v9.2. No global signal regression was applied. The images were normalized to the MNI152 template. The Nilearn package (Abraham et al., 2014) was used to bandpass the time series (0.009-0.15Hz) and build a functional connectivity matrix based on cross-correlation and the 432 regions from the Tian-Schaefer atlas for each participant.

Results

First whole brain analysis was conducted to address the effect of age on global brain tissue; As expected, the older the subjects are and the is the F in an extended cluster located in the white matter (p<0.05, FWE corrected, fig A orange cluster). For the krf, the association with age is also significant in various clusters in white matter surrounding ventricular system, and in grey matter part of the brain including, insulo-temporal cortex, medial temporal cortex, left caudate (p<0.05, FWE corrected, fig A blue clusters).  It is noteworthy that clusters observed in these 2 analyses are not overlapping.

In a second step, mean F value of the white matter part of the brain and mean krf value for the grey matter part of the brain were extracted to assess the impact of this microstructure variability of the tissue onto the functional connectivity of the brain. According to this analysis, subjects presenting lower mean F value in their white matter also present lower connectivity inside the somatomotor resting state network (p<0.05, FWE corrected, fig B). In contrast, neither mean krf grey matter value nor mean krf of the insular granular cortex were associated with any functional connectivity variability in our sample.

Conclusion

Quantitative mapping allows to ascertain the loss of myelin quantity in the white matter part of the brain in aging population. This loss of myelination is associated to a loss of connectivity inside the somatomotor network at rest in our population. Whereas krf parameters analysis revealed age-related changes of propriety in the grey matter, these changes are not associated to modifications in resting state network connectivity. In future analysis, these qMT measurements will be contrasted with MRI estimates of neurite microstructure from NODDI.
