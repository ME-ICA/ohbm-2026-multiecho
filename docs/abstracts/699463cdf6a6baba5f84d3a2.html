Introduction

The hippocampus plays a critical role in distinguishing between similar experiences, yet the mechanisms by which it organizes information at different levels of granularity remain unclear. Everyday memory demands range from recognizing coarse high level information, such as that a context is a beach versus kitchen, to recalling precise details of individual scenes. Competing theories propose that the hippocampus supports multiple scales of representation organized along either its long- or transverse- axis across subfields1,2, but few studies have tested hippocampal representations at high enough resolutions to resolve both axes. In this experiment, using ultra-high field 7Tesla fMRI, we asked: 1) How does the hippocampus support this spectrum of representations?, and 2) how do these representations change with experience?

Methods

We scanned 24 healthy young adults with ultra-high resolution 7 Tesla fMRI as they repeatedly encoded unique objects paired with one of four scenes: two visually similar beaches, and two visually similar kitchens. At retrieval, they were presented with each object and asked to retrieve which specific scene it had been paired with. To assess hippocampal representations, we applied searchlight analyses across the unfolded hippocampal surface3, comparing neural pattern similarity to predefined models of memory granularity. These models captured both coarse distinctions (beach vs kitchen) and fine-grained details (beach1 vs beach2; kitchen1 vs kitchen2) at each exposure.

Results

We found evidence of coarse representations in anterior regions of the hippocampus and detailed representations in posterior dentate gyrus across exposures. With repeated exposure, distinctions between coarse kitchen and beach representations was lost in anterior hippocampus, while specific scene representations emerged in the posterior hippocampus, becoming integrated and then separated from highly overlapping scene stimuli. Representations of individual scenes became anticorrelated with themselves in the posterior dentate gyrus with repeated exposure.

Conclusion

Our findings support a multi-scale view of hippocampal organization, pointing to a distinction in representational granularity along the long- and transverse-axes of the hippocampus. While anterior regions represented coarse information, the posterior dentate gyrus represented specific details distinguishing similar scenes. Importantly, we also showed that hippocampal representations are dynamic and evolve with experience. We observed that representations become increasingly distinct with repeated exposure, particularly in posterior regions of the hippocampus, as initially overlapping patterns diverge. This process may serve as a mechanism for resolving interference among highly similar experiences.
