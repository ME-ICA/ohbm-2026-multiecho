Introduction

Recent neuroimaging work increasingly aims to explain macroscale patterns of cortical organization across modalities, rather than attributing task contributions to isolated regions. Cutting-edge methods in DTI analysis extend anatomical findings beyond white matter integrity measures. For example, we can investigate how white matter projects to gray matter via surface tract projections¹. This shift is particularly important for complex behavior, such as social cognition, with widely distributed neural correlates of its underlying cognitive processes. Macaque societies are socially complex, dynamic, and hierarchical, making them a powerful model for studying the neural organization of social cognition2. 

Sallet and colleagues demonstrated robust associations between social group size, social rank, and gray matter morphology in frontal and temporal regions implicated in social cognition³. We analyzed macaque diffusion MRI data from the same cohort of monkeys to examine whether variation in social group size and social rank is reflected in the anatomy and organization of cortical white matter. Focusing on long-range association pathways implicated in social and socio-cognitive processing, we explored expression of social variation in tract anatomy and surface projections to gray matter. The current framework offers a novel approach to investigate the relationship between white matter tracts and social cognitive variables in macaque monkeys, extending our understanding of where social cognition is afforded in the macaque brain. 

Methods

We analyzed diffusion-weighted MRI data from eight macaque monkeys originally reported by Sallet and colleagues³. The original dataset encompasses 23 animals, and we are currently working toward analyses of the full sample. The monkeys were housed in groups of differing sizes, spanning variation in social group size and social rank. The former was achieved by housing the monkeys in sets of different group sizes; the latter emerged from the monkeys’ behavior within those groups and reflects success in social settings. We performed probabilistic tractography, computed white matter integrity measures (volume, length, and mean FA, MD, and MO over samples), and derived tract projections using the XTRACT ecosystem⁴. 

For each tract, cortical surface projection maps were generated by estimating streamline terminations across the cortical surface. Projection maps were normalized and registered to a common surface space. As an exploratory step, across-subject variance maps were computed to identify cortical regions showing high inter-individual variability in tract projection strength. 

We then related vertex-wise variance in tract projection profiles to social group size and social rank. Analyses focused on identifying spatially consistent patterns of variability across the cortical surface rather than formal tract-level inference. 

Results

We conducted a preliminary white matter tract projection strength analysis on a subset of 8 macaques out of the 23 in the original dataset³. No uniform effects of social variables (social rank and group size) were observed on tract-level white matter integrity measures, suggesting that social influences on white matter organization are spatially specific rather than global. To test this hypothesis, we adopted an analysis strategy well suited for investigating spatially distinct differences in white matter, namely projection strength to the cortical surface. 

We illustrate findings in the uncinate fasciculus and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, due to their involvement in social cognitive functions¹ and their temporal lobe terminations (Figure 1A). Examining localized variance revealed distinct hotspots of projection variability in both tracts that resembled gray matter patterns reported previously³. In the uncinate fasciculus, variance associated with social rank was specific to inferior temporal regions, albeit more frontal than variability in gray matter associated with the same measure (Figure 1B, left, blue). We found another hotspot of variance associated with group size in the medial prefrontal cortex, resembling gray matter findings (Figure 1B, left, blue). Variance in polar and superior temporal sulcus was associated with group size, mirroring gray matter effects³ (Figure 1B, left, red). High variance related to both group size and rank was observed in the lateral prefrontal cortex. 

In the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, social rank was selectively associated with a more dorsal and posterior hotspot, including the superior temporal sulcus and medial prefrontal regions (Figure 1B, right, blue). These rank-related projection patterns overlapped with gray matter variability³, albeit being more widespread. Group size–selective variance was observed in the middle superior temporal sulcus, extending posteriorly from superior temporal polar regions (Figure 1B, right, red). Again, these patterns resembled gray matter variability³, while tract projection variance was more spatially extensive. Our initial findings indicate partial convergence, between cortical morphology and white matter organization. 

Additional variance related to both social group size and rank was observed in lateral prefrontal cortex (Figure 1B, left). Similar patterns were also observed in the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (Figure 1B, right), suggesting that social variation is expressed at shared cortical territories across multiple tracts. 

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that variations in macaque social rank and group size are not reflected in general white matter integrity measure, but rather in spatially specific onto tracts projections. This reframing provides an explanation for why robust social effects are consistently observed in gray matter³, while tract-level white matter findings  remained inconsistent. 

Focusing on tract–cortex coupling revealed spatially specific, socially meaningful patterns of variability that closely mirrored gray matter effects. Social group size and social rank were associated with partially dissociable projection hotspots in temporal and prefrontal regions implicated in social cognition1. Variance peaked in similar cortical locations across different tracts, indicating that social effects reflect shared cortical embedding rather than tract-specific anatomy. 

These findings highlight a key limitation of tract-centric approaches: in a densely interconnected system, multiple tracts converge onto the same cortical territories, making it difficult to attribute social effects to any single tract in isolation. This motivates a shift toward gradient-based analyses, which capture shared patterns of cortical organization across pathways and better reflect the distributed nature of social cognition¹.
