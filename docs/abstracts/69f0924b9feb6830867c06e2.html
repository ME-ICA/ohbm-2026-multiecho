Introduction

Neural stimulation can directly modulate brain activity and influence behavior. Intracranial electrical stimulation (iES) precisely and reversibly alters neural excitability in targeted areas and is used to treat neuropsychiatric disorders and study cognition. Studies suggest iES affects both local and distant regions, resulting in spatially heterogeneous activation patterns. Understanding the neurobiological mechanisms underlying spatial variability is critical for clarifying how targeted stimulation modulates brain function and behavior.

Prior work has begun to address this problem by combining iES with intracranial EEG (iEEG) recordings. These showed that activation depends on functional connectivity to the stimulation site and its network hierarchy. Yet, the brain is a complex biological system influenced by interactions across macro-, meso-, and micro-scales, including the distribution of neurotransmitter receptors and whole-brain gene expression. 

Most prior studies relied on iEEG, which has high temporal resolution but inherent limitations, making it difficult to clarify the causal effects of focal stimulation on brain function and behavior. Combining iES with whole-brain fMRI (es-fMRI) overcomes these limits and visualizes stimulation-induced hemodynamic effects, emerging as a promising method in primates and humans1,2.

We analyzed an es-fMRI dataset with medial temporal lobe (MTL) stimulation3. MTL stimulation induced local and distal BOLD changes. BOLD responses correlated positively with functional connectivity and ion-transport genes, but negatively with receptor diversity. These features predicted the spatial distribution of BOLD changes. Comparing with CCEP data showed distinct spatial profiles; CCEP amplitudes correlated positively with receptor diversity and ion transport/action potential genes. BOLD and electrophysiological responses reflect different facets of neural reactivity, and may be shaped by partly different underlying biological mechanisms.

Methods

fMRI Data acquisition and analysis

The data supporting the findings of this study were derived from the es-fMRI dataset created by Thompson et al, which is publicly available on the OpenNeuro platform (https://github.com/OpenNeuroDatasets/ds002799). Sixteen epilepsy patients (11 males and 5 females, mean age 36.9±11.5) with MTL stimulation participated voluntarily under IRB-approved protocol. Pre-implantation resting-state fMRI (4.8 min, Siemens/GE scanners) and post-implantation es-fMRI were collected. Stimulation used interleaved 100ms silent intervals with biphasic pulses (5–9 pulses, 100 Hz, 9–12 mA) in 30s ON/OFF blocks. We directly used the preprocessed data for subsequent analyses.

Electrophysiological data acquisition

The electrophysiological data come from a cohort of 780 adult patients with pharmaco-resistant focal epilepsy, collected by the F-TRACT project (https://f-tract.eu). Amplitude of cortico-cortical evoked potentials (CCEPs) was measured: pulses averaged, z-scored vs. prestimulus baseline; |z|>5 deemed significant. Stimulations in parahippocampus, hippocampus, and amygdala were selected using Lausanne atlas; recorded amplitudes across the brain were used in analyses.

Brain Activation and Functional Connectivity Analyses

Neuroimaging data were processed using the SPM and DPABI toolboxes implemented in Matlab23.0. 33 valid runs of fMRI data following electrical stimulation were included. The general linear model (GLM) included 1 main regressor and 18 nuisance regressors. Group-level activation maps were generated separately for participants stimulated the parahippocampus, hippocampus, and amygdala, as well as for all participants combined. We treated each ROI as an independent sample to assess the spatial similarity between stimulation-evoked activation patterns in the subregions and the MTL, and quantified this similarity using Pearson correlation and spin permutation tests. We derived individual-level connectivity from epilepsy patients’ pre-op resting-state fMRI using 6-mm ROI seeds at stimulation sites, and group-level connectivity from 1,003 HCP subjects using Lausanne atlas and Fisher z-transformed correlations. Because negative activations and negative functional connectivity are generally considered to have ambiguous biological significance, only positive activations and positive connections were used in subsequent analyses.

Ionotropic and metabotropic receptor diversity ratio 

Receptor density used in this study were obtained from publicly available PET-derived receptor maps generated from more than 1,200 healthy individual. The 15 receptor maps were then classified into metabotropic and ionotropic types. We parcellated the receptor maps into 234 regions using the Lausanne atlas and computed a receptor-diversity value for each region. Where N represents each region’s normalized receptor profile and M is the total number of receptors. The ionotropic-metabotropic ratio was computed by dividing 𝐻𝑖𝑜𝑛𝑜𝑡𝑟𝑜𝑝𝑖𝑐 by 𝐻𝑚𝑒𝑡𝑎𝑏𝑜𝑡𝑟𝑜𝑝𝑖𝑐.

Identifying genes associated with stimulation induced brain activation

Regional gene expression data were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas. Analyses were restricted to the left hemisphere (111 regions) using a 111×15,633 matrix processed with abagen. PLS analyzed the relationship between gene expression and brain responses. Spin permutation (10,000 iterations) assessed significance. Gene loadings identified positive/negative subsets, generating region-wise gene scores by multiplying expression by loadings and summing, for association with functional patterns.

Gene enrichment analysis

Genes with valid Entrez IDs were split into positive/negative sets; top 10% positive genes underwent enrichGO analysis using clusterProfiler, with other valid genes as background.

Results

iES to MTL elicited widespread brain activations both locally and distally

We analyzed es-fMRI data from 16 patients (33 runs) with MTL stimulation. The results revealed that iES evoked widespread activation (iES-BR) across the brain, including the left insula, right middle cingulate cortex, and left Rolandic operculum (GRF correction with a voxel-wise threshold of P < 0.001 and a cluster-level threshold of P < 0.02；Fig. 2B). Stimulation sites in three MTL regions (parahippocampus, hippocampus, amygdala) produced similar whole-brain activation patterns (Fig. 2A). Correlation with the whole-MTL pattern was strong for all regions (all ps < 0.05, Fig. 2C-E). To maximize sample size and statistical power, all MTL electrodes were retained for analysis.

Spatial variations in BOLD responses were shaped by multiscale biological factors

After understanding the whole-brain distribution pattern of iES-BR, we examined whether these patterns are constrained by biological factors, focusing on functional connectivity, receptor diversity, and transcriptomics. A linear model showed that iES-BR was positively associated with resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) independent of Euclidean distance (t = 2.60, p = 0.010; Fig. 2F), indicating stronger responses in regions more connected to the stimulation site. Using the ionotropic-metabotropic (IM) ratio as an index of receptor diversity, we found a negative association with iES-BR (t = −2.41, p = 0.018; Fig. 2G), suggesting regions with lower IM ratios are more sensitive to stimulation. PLS analysis of AHBA gene expression data revealed a significant association between regional transcriptomic profiles and iES-BR. GO enrichment of positively weighted genes showed overrepresentation of transmembrane ion transport pathways, indicating a genetic constraint on iES-BR spatial patterns (Fig. 2H).

Predicting whole-brain BOLD responses from functional connectivity, receptor diversity, and gene expression

We assessed the predictive value of these features using leave-one-ROI-out cross-validated models. A Linear Mixed-Effects Model (LMM) incorporating functional connectivity, receptor diversity, and gene scores accurately predicted iES-BR (r = 0.63, p < 0.001; Fig. 2I). Prediction remained significant after regressing out Euclidean distance (ED), with all predictors retaining consistent effect directions (r = 0.28, p = 0.003; Fig. 2J). Together, these results indicate that functional connectivity, receptor diversity, and gene expression account for variance in the spatial distribution of iES-BR that is not attributable to ED.

Electrophysiological responses exhibited a distinct spatial organization and shaped by partially different biological factors

We next analyzed stimulation-evoked electrophysiological responses (iES-ER) using CCEP data from 780 patients. Comparing iES-ER with iES-BR across the same regions (parahippocampus, hippocampus, amygdala), linear mixed-effects modeling revealed no significant association between CCEP amplitude and BOLD activation, suggesting that the whole-brain responses evoked by iES differ between BOLD and electrophysiological signals. In contrast to BOLD responses, iES-ER amplitude was positively associated with both functional connectivity(t = 2.08, p = 0.039) and receptor diversity (t = 2.12, p = 0.035).Gene enrichment analysis indicated that iES-ER–related genes were enriched in ion transport and action potential pathways, distinct from those linked to BOLD responses. Cross-validated prediction analyses demonstrated significant prediction of iES-ER (r = 0.43, p < 0.001), which remained significant after regressing out ED (r = 0.28, p < 0.001). These results collectively indicate that functional connectivity, receptor diversity, and gene expression offer explanatory power for the spatial distribution of iES-ER that is distinct from that of ED.

Conclusion

In this study, we aimed to determine whether and how intrinsic neurobiological properties shape stimulation evoked brain activation. Using es-fMRI, we found that MTL stimulation elicited widespread BOLD activity increases, including in regions such as the left insula, right middle cingulate cortex, and left Rolandic operculum. These responses were jointly shaped by multiscale neurobiological factors: at the macroscale, activation strength scaled positively with intrinsic functional connectivity; at the mesoscale, it decreased with increasing IM ratio; at the microscale, it was positively associated with the expression of genes enriched in transmembrane ion-transport pathways. To contextualize these findings, we compared BOLD responses with stimulation evoked electrophysiological responses derived from CCEP recordings. Unlike BOLD, CCEP amplitudes showed a distinct spatial pattern and were positively associated with functional connectivity, receptor diversity and genes related to potassium/sodium ion transport, transmembrane transport, and action potentials. Together, these findings indicate that BOLD and electrophysiological responses capture different facets of neural reactivity, and are shaped by different combinations of functional connectivity, receptor architecture, and molecular profiles.
