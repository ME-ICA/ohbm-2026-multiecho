Introduction

Multisite neuroimaging data from large consortia, such as the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development1 and Healthy Brain and Child Development2 initiatives, are increasingly being adopted in neurodevelopmental research to enhance statistical power and improve generalizability. Against this backdrop, there is a growing recognition of the need for population-based studies to expand further geographic representativeness. To address this, the Canadian Pediatric Imaging Platform (C-PIP), an ongoing longitudinal study, is acquiring advanced multimodal neuroimaging and phenotypical data in 820 children aged 0-18 years across three of Canada’s largest pediatric hospitals: (i) Alberta Children’s Hospital (ACH - Calgary), (ii) CHU Sainte-Justine (CHUSJ - Montreal), (iii) Hospital for Sick Children (SK - Toronto). C-PIP aims to accelerate the landscape of Canadian neuroscientific research through the provision of high-quality longitudinal brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data across a wide developmental range. In doing so, C-PIP offers increased precision in characterizing brain-behavioral development and identification of brain-based disruptors during childhood. To support broader adoption of the platform, we present a preliminary assessment of the stability of neuroimaging measures derived from the C-PIP MRI protocol using a travelling-phantom design. Here, we focus specifically on diffusion MRI given its high sensitivity to scanner-related differences3 and widespread use across the neurodevelopmental literature.

Methods

Data were acquired from three healthy, young adult human volunteers (30-33 years, two females). Participants were scanned twice at each site (six scans per person; 18 scans total) on research-dedicated 3T MRI systems: (i) GE SIGNA UHP (ACH), (ii) Siemens MAGNETOM Cima.X (CHUSJ), and (iii) Siemens PRISMAFIT (SK). The C-PIP diffusion acquisition consisted of a multi-shell sequence with 145 gradient directions (b500 = 15 directions, b1000 = 30 directions, b2000 = 40 directions, b3000 = 60 directions) and 12 non-diffusion-weighted (b0) volumes. Scans were acquired in the anterior–posterior direction with an isotropic voxel size of 1.7 mm3. An additional set of 12 b0 volumes were collected with opposite phase-encoding direction to correct for susceptibility distortions during processing. Whilst efforts were made to harmonize all parameters across sites, minor vendor-specific differences are present in TE (ACH: 65ms; CHUSJ: 50ms; SK: 84.6ms), TR (ACH & SK: 4 secs; CHUSJ: 4.3 secs), and number of slices (ACH & SK: 93; CHUSJ: 96). The data collection workflow is presented in Figure 1A.

Diffusion images were processed centrally using a standardized pipeline in MRtrix34 (Figure 1B), which included artefact correction, multi-shell, multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (MSMT-CSD), and template registration. Automated tractography was performed to delineate several major white matter bundles of interest spanning the major taxonomic classes (Figure 1C): Commissural: corpus callosum (CC), Projection: corticospinal tract (CST), Association: inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFO) and uncinate fasciculus (UF), Limbic: fornix (FX), and Cerebellar: middle cerebellar peduncles (MCP). Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and fixel-based analysis (FBA)5 were implemented to estimate tract-averaged measures of fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), fiber density (FD) and fiber cross-section (FC). Within-site test-retest stability of DTI and FBA metrics were quantified as a percentage difference between repeated sessions for each participant. Between-site effects were assessed via mixed-effects models predicting tract-averaged metrics as a function of site, with random effects to account for participant- and tract-level clustering. Standardized beta-coefficients (β^) were used to quantify the magnitude of differences between sites relative to a reference site (ACH).

Results

Tract-averaged DTI metrics demonstrated good test-retest stability in FA and MD (Figure 2A, top panels), with percentage differences largely within the 3% range and maxima up to 2.7% (FA), and 4.7% (MD) across all sites. The FX consistently showed lower between-session stability, whereas the CC, CST, IFO, and MCP were among the most stable. Among FBA measures (Figure 2A, bottom panels), FD displayed the greatest variability, with percent differences ranging from a minimum of 0.14%, to maxima of 10-11% in some tracts. In contrast, FC showed high stability across sites, with percent differences all within 3%. Compared to DTI measures, comparisons of test-retest stability across tracts did not reveal any clear anatomical patterns of variability. Site-level differences in mean DTI and FBA metrics (Figures 2B and 2C) revealed that scans at ACH generally exhibited higher FA values across most tracts compared to CHUSJ (β^ = -0.24) and SK (β^ = -0.04), although effects were small. Differences between sites for MD were negligible (CHUSJ: β^ = -0.002, SK: β^ = 0.004). For FBA metrics, higher FD and FC values were observed in scans from CHUSJ (FD β^ = 0.28; FC β^ = 0.08) and SK (FD β^ = 0.68; FC β^ = 0.22) relative to ACH.

Conclusion

Our preliminary analysis of the C-PIP diffusion MRI protocol indicate good within- and between-site stability in tract level metrics. Across repeated scans of the same individuals, we found relatively small percent differences between sessions across tracts and sites, while between-site models showed small-to-moderate differences across most metrics, except for FD. Overall, these findings offer early support for the robustness of the C-PIP diffusion sequence across sites. We aim to extend these analyses to other modalities within the C-PIP imaging protocol, thereby facilitating user adoption of the platform.
