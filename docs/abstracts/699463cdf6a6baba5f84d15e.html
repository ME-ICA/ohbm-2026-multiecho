Introduction

It is a long standing question at what stage of processing the human is able to report that a tactile stimulus is perceived consciously. Electroencephalography (EEG) studies applying a near-threshold electrical stimulus detection paradigm revealed that perceived stimuli elicit the mid-latency event-related potential (ERP) component N140, which is absent when the stimulus was not perceived.^1,2 In contrast, earlier ERP components such as the P50 seem to encode physical features of the stimulus.^1 In contrast to those findings, an earlier MEG study using mechanical instead of electrical stimulation, discovered differences between perceived and unperceived trials already at around 70ms after stimulus onset.^3 A recent EEG study suggests that indeed the stimulus modality plays a crucial role. In mechanical stimulation, the P50 already predicts stimulus perception, which is not the case in electrical stimulation.^4 To shine light on these conflicting results, we conducted an EEG study in which we look at the differences in ERP waveform between perceived and unperceived trials and directly compare mechanical and electrical stimulation in the same subjects.

Methods

We recruited 40 healthy subjects for our EEG study, (25 females) aged between 18 and 40 years. The study follows a within-subject EEG study in which each participant takes part in one mechanical session (stimulation via piezoelectric stimulation) and one electrical session (stimulation via medically certified constant current generator). Each session consists of 8 experimental blocks, that always start with a threshold assessment (4-5 min), followed by the main experimental block (9-10 min) in which 7 intensities and catch trials are presented. The main experimental block entails 198 trials, with 7 different intensities, assessed to correspond to 5%, 20%, 35%, 50%, 65%, 80%, 95% yes-responses, and 18 trials without any stimulation (catch trials). Both the threshold assessment and the experimental block follow the same trial sequence (see Figure 1C). After applying exclusion criteria (based on fit of psychometric curve per experimental block) we included 36 electrical sessions and 35 mechanical sessions in further analyses. We computed the event-related-potentials (ERPs) for both modalities for perceived and unperceived trials. For statistical analysis we used a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) and included the detection response, intensity, and force as fixed effects, and subjects and block as random effects. We included an interaction between modality, detection response and intensity in the model.

Results

To statistically analyse the difference between perceived and unperceived trials at the components of interested we defined time windows based on previous literature (P60: 50-60ms, N80: 80-90ms, N140: 140-150ms) and used the averaged amplitudes for the statistical analysis. We fit a linear mixed model (LMM) with the fixed factors modality (electrical/mechanical), intensity level (reordered post-hoc, depending on fitted response curves, 2-6) and perception response (yes/no), We also included a random intercept for subject, and random slope for experimental block. At P60 (at electrode CP4), modality showed no significant effect (β = -0.21, SE = 0.13, t = -1.64, p = 0.101), nor did detection response (β = 0.25, SE = 0.15, t = 1.64, p = 0.102). Stimulus intensity had a significant positive effect (β = 0.092, SE = 0.024, t = 3.85, p < 0.001) with a significant response × intensity interaction (β = -0.093, SE = 0.035, t = -2.64, p = 0.008). The P60 amplitude at CP4 increases linearly with intensity for mechanical stimulation (Figure 2B). For N80 (electrode C6), modality (β = -0.13, SE = 0.11, t = -1.23, p = 0.218), detection response (β = -0.098, SE = 0.13, t = -0.74, p = 0.458), and intensity (β = 0.028, SE = 0.021, t = 1.34, p = 0.180) showed no main effects. The interaction between detection response and intensity was significant (β = -0.066, SE = 0.031, t = -2.16, p = 0.031). Also at N140 (at electrode C6), modality was not significant (β = 0.18, SE = 0.12, t = 1.50, p = 0.134). Detection response (β = -0.40, SE = 0.14, t = -2.87, p = 0.004) and intensity (β = -0.065, SE = 0.022, t = -2.90, p = 0.004) showed significant main effects, with no significant interaction (β = -0.051, SE = 0.033, t = -1.55, p = 0.122). Conclusion

We could replicate the result that the N140 correlates with successful detection of somatosensory stimuli. The P60 shows an effect of intensity on the amplitude, but does not change significantly with stimulus detection. In contrast to recent findings on the P60, we did not find a difference between mechanical and electrical induced P60 component. We did not find that the N80 significantly correlates with stimulus detection, and no significant difference due to modality was found. Though, the topography and strengths of the N80 might differ for mechanical in comparison to electrical stimulation. Therefore, future analyses include source reconstruction of the mentioned ERP components using beamforming. This delivers us new insights about the neural correlates of conscious somatosensory perception.
