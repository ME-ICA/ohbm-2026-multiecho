Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is associated with social communication difficulties and atypical neurodevelopment affecting frontal and temporal brain systems1. The right inferior frontal gyrus (RIFG) is a key node of the social brain network, supporting action understanding, imitation, empathy, and theory of mind2. Functional neuroimaging studies have reported atypical engagement of inferior frontal regions in ASD during social perception and action observation tasks, motivating the RIFG as a neuromodulation target3.

Beyond functional alterations, ASD is characterized by atypical cortical morphometry, including alterations in cortical thickness, gyrification, and sulcal geometry1. Early sulcal-based studies reported abnormal folding patterns in frontal and opercular regions in ASD, while recent large-scale investigations demonstrate pronounced heterogeneity across development and cognitive ability4. These findings suggest that cortical folding reflects stable, individual-specific aspects of neurodevelopment that may contribute to variability in social-cognitive function.

If repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) targeting the RIFG engages social brain networks, individual differences in underlying cortical organization may therefore influence treatment responsiveness. Sulcal folding reflects long-term neurodevelopmental processes and may provide a trait-like marker of cortical organization.

The sulcal phenotype network (SPN) analysis enables sulcus-level characterization of cortical folding architecture by integrating local sulcal features within a network representation5. The eigen-fold index (EFI) of SPN provides a low-dimensional summary of sulcal patterning. The primary aim of our study was to test whether baseline sulcal folding organization, indexed by EFI, predicts individual response to RIFG-targeted rTMS in ASD. Secondary analyses examined longitudinal EFI changes and associations with social-cognitive outcomes. 

Methods

Study design and participants 
We recruited 60 intellectually able autistic children and young adults (age 8-30 years) into a double-blind, sham-controlled randomized trial. ASD diagnoses were established according to DSM-5 criteria and confirmed using the ADOS assessment. Participants were randomized (1:1) to receive either active or sham rTMS targeting the RIFG. Stimulation was delivered twice weekly for 8 weeks (16 sessions). MRI and clinical assessments were conducted at baseline, week 8, and week 12 (four weeks post-intervention). After quality control of MRI and behavioral data, 49 participants (active n=26; sham n=23) were included in the final analyses. (See Table 1 for baseline demographics.)

Frith-Happé Animations Test 
Social cognition was assessed using a shortened Frith-Happé Animations Test, including theory-of-mind and random motion conditions, yielding Total and Feeling scores.

rTMS intervention 
Intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) was delivered at 90% of individual active motor threshold. Sham stimulation used a matched sham coil producing comparable auditory and somatosensory sensations without effective cortical stimulation. The RIFG target was localized individually using MRI-guided neuronavigation. Per-subject electric-field estimates were computed using SimNIBS, and the ratio of normalized electric field strength at the RIFG relative to primary motor cortex (NormE) was used to account for inter-individual variability in stimulation dose.

MRI acquisition and sulcal analysis 
High-resolution structural T1-weighted MRI data were acquired at all three time points using a 3T scanner. Cortical surfaces were reconstructed and sulcal features extracted using the SPN analysis framework5. EFI was computed for each of 40 sulci per subject at each time point, providing sulcus-specific measures of folding organization.

Statistical analysis 
Statistical analyses were conducted in R using general linear models (GLMs). The primary analysis tested whether baseline EFI predicted treatment response, defined as Frith-Happé Animations Test outcomes (Total and Feeling scores) at week 8 and week 12, as well as changes from baseline, examined separately within the active and sham groups. Secondary analyses included inter-group comparisons of EFI at each time point, group differences in longitudinal EFI change, and dimensional associations between EFI change and behavioral change. All models included age, sex, intracranial volume, IQ, and NormE as covariates. Statistical significance was assessed using a false discovery rate (FDR) corrected p-value threshold of 0.05.

Results

Table 1 summarizes baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the active and sham groups. The two groups did not significantly differ in age, sex, IQ, clinical severity, comorbid psychiatric conditions, or concurrent medication use. The active group showed lower baseline Frith-Happé Animations Feeling scores (p=0.01). 

Figure 1 shows subject-level EFI distributions and variabilities across sulci at baseline. Sulci with the lowest and highest group-level EFI values exhibited the lowest inter-subject variability. Sulcal folding variability showed marked spatial heterogeneity, with sulci exhibiting high variability spatially adjacent to sulci with low variability.

Baseline EFI did not significantly predict Frith-Happé Total or Feeling scores at week 8 or week 12, nor changes from baseline, in either the active or sham groups; no sulcus showed a significant predictive association (FDR-corrected p>0.05).

Secondary analyses revealed no significant inter-group differences in EFI at baseline, week 8, or week 12 across the 40 sulci examined. Longitudinal analyses comparing changes in EFI from baseline to week 8 and from baseline to week 12 likewise showed no significant group differences. Dimensional analyses revealed no significant associations between EFI change and changes in social-cognitive performance within either group.

Conclusion

In this double-blind, sham-controlled trial, baseline sulcal folding organization, quantified using the EFI derived from the SPN framework, did not predict individual response to RIFG-targeted rTMS in our ASD cohort. Neither the primary predictive analyses nor secondary longitudinal and dimensional models showed significant associations between EFI and social-cognitive outcomes. 

These findings suggest that sulcal patterning, as measured with EFI, may not constitute a strong standalone predictor of short-term rTMS response in our ASD cohort. Sulcal folding metrics are thought to reflect relatively stable neurodevelopmental processes, particularly beyond early developmental windows, and may therefore capture trait-like features rather than the plastic mechanisms underlying rTMS efficacy. In addition, substantial heterogeneity in cortical morphology and developmental stage in ASD may further reduce predictive sensitivity at the group level4. 

Nevertheless, the absence of predictive effects does not preclude other neurobiological mechanisms of rTMS efficacy. Treatment response could be associated with functional network dynamics, microstructural properties, or interactions between stimulation and individual neurodevelopmental trajectories that are not captured by static folding metrics. Methodological factors, including sample size, rTMS protocol, and the use of a global summary metric, may also constrain sensitivity.

By assessing baseline sulcal folding organization as a predictor of neuromodulation response using a SPN-based framework, this study provides a reference point on the utility of cortical folding metrics as predictive biomarkers in ASD. Future work integrating sulcal organization with complementary functional and microstructural imaging may improve individualized prediction of treatment response in ASD.
