Introduction

Fetal biometry from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) serves as a pivotal quantitative basis for prenatal brain development monitoring, facilitating the assessment of various pathologies including germinal matrix-intraventricular hemorrhage (GMH-IVH), ventriculomegaly, cerebellar hypoplasia and vermian ageneses, and Dandy-Walker malformation1. However, in clinical practice, the manual localization of anatomical landmarks for biometry measurement and subsequent diagnosis demands substantial neuroanatomical expertise, proves exceedingly time-consuming for large volumes of repeatedly acquired MRI data, and remains susceptible to inter-observer variability2. Recently, deep learning-based methods offer great promise for automated biometry and diagnosis with improved consistency and accuracy3. However, automated biometry tools based on supervised training face substantial domain shift challenges due to the highly heterogeneous clinical fetal MRI data from multiple centers and varying pathologies, highlighting the need for domain adaptation4 (Figure 1A).

To address these challenges, this study proposes BioTTA, a two-stage unsupervised test-time adaptation (TTA) framework. BioTTA trains on source dataset and adapts during inference by jointly optimizing three self-supervised losses and constraining with atlas guidance. Extensive evaluations across five out-of-distribution (OoD) multi-center, multi-disease OoD datasets demonstrate BioTTA’s robustness and practical utility in both cross-domain biometry and multi-disease diagnosis.

Methods

Dataset Construction. With IRB approval, retrospective data from 332 pregnant women were collected across four centers. The cohort exhibits significant heterogeneity, spanning four sequence types, four SVR methods, three neurological pathologies, and wide gestational age (GA) ranges (Figure 1D). Data were stratified into a source domain training dataset (WCT dataset: center 1, 71 normal cases for training ,10 normal cases for validation) and four target domain testing datasets: i) WCT-GMH-IVH dataset (Center 1, 40 GMH-IVH cases); ii) SMCS-GMH-IVH dataset (Center 2, 31 normal cases and 6 GMH-IVH cases) and CWCT-GMH-IVH dataset (Center 3, 36 normal cases and 6 GMH-IVH cases), combined as an external OoD-GMH-IVH dataset; iii) FeTA (Center 4, 19 normal cases and 31 ventriculomegaly cases); and iv) WCB-CA (Center 1, 45 normal cases and 33 cases with cerebellar abnormalities, specifically 16 cerebellar hypoplasia cases and 20 vermian ageneses cases). Following quality control (Figure 1C), all volumes were preprocessed (downsampled, registered, normalized, and cropped) to unify resolution and dimensions

Biometric Measurements. Based on clinical guidelines5, 11 commonly used biometry measurements were selected as follows (Fig. 1B): Right/Left Lateral Ventricle-Axial (RLV-A, LLV-A), Right/Left Lateral Ventricle-Coronal (RLV-C, LLV-C), Bone Biparietal Diameter (BBD), Cerebral Biparietal Diameter (CBD), Transverse Cerebellar Diameter (TCD), Fronto-Occipital Diameter (FOD), Anteroposterior Vermis Diameter (AVD), Vermis Height (VH), and Corpus Callosum Length (CCL). Measurement labels for WCT, WCT-GMH-IVH, OoD-GMH-IVH, and FeTA datasets were manually annotated by senior radiologists (more than 10 years of clinical experience) using ITK-SNAP. Disease labels for the WCB-CA dataset were extracted from clinical reports based on corresponding keywords

Developmental Curves. Utilizing the labeled biometrics of 71 normal fetuses in the training dataset, developmental trajectory curves were fitted for each biometry measurement against GA. Second-order polynomial fitting was applied to the median (50th percentile) curve, and standard deviation were calculated linearly fitted. The 5th and 95th percentile curves were then derived based on the assumption of normality.

BioTTA Framework. BioTTA is a two-stage unsupervised TTA framework (Figure 1A). The first stage involves pre-training on source data. A unified encoder-decoder architecture with a flexible backbone (BioCNN or BioFormer) is trained, where a shared encoder feeds into two parallel decoders for landmark heatmaps prediction and direct biometry measurements regression, respectively. In the second stage at test-time, the model adapts to each unlabeled target sample by optimizing length consistency loss，entropy loss and boundary-gradient loss. Finally, atlas-informed anatomical priors are integrated via registration to refine the heatmaps, from which the final measurements are derived

Multi-Disease Diagnosis and Grading. To evaluate clinical applicability, BioTTA-predicted biometry measurements were applied to diagnose multi-disease across target domain testing datasets based on standard clinical criteria. GMH-IVH is GA-independently graded by axial and coronal average ventricular width (normal or Grade I: <10 mm, Grade II: 10–15 mm, and Grade III: >15 mm). Ventriculomegaly is similarly diagnosed by average ventricular width (Normal: <10 mm, Mild: 10–12 mm, Moderate: 12–15 mm, and Severe: >15 mm). Cerebellar abnormalities are diagnosed using GA-dependent developmental reference ranges, include cerebellar hypoplasia (TCD <5th percentile) and vermian ageneses (AVD or VH <5th percentile).

Results

Diagnostic Accuracy. BioTTA results were compared against independent and qualitative diagnosis of a senior doctor (more than 10 years’ experience), junior doctor (3 years’ experience), and a medical student. Metrics included binary accuracy, F1, and false negative rate for disease presence, plus classification accuracy and F1 for grading or subtypes. BioTTA demonstrated expert-level performance, comparable to or even surpassing the senior radiologist (Figure 2F). Specifically, in binary diagnosis, BioTTA achieved an accuracy of 82.1% on the WCT-GMH-IVH dataset (vs. the senior doctor’s 82.1%), 93.2% on OoD-GMH-IVH (vs. 95.9%), 95.0% on FeTA (vs. 92.5%), and 89.3% on WCB-CA (vs 74.6%).

Distributional Conformity on OoD Normal Cases. BioTTA-predicted biometric measurements for 92 normal cases from the OoD-GMH-IVH and WCB-CA datasets were plotted against GA-based reference curves to evaluate distributional conformity with the developmental curves (Figure 2A). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to evaluate the uniformity of prediction centiles (0–100%) at α=0.05. Results confirmed that 9 metrics followed a uniform distribution (p < 0.05). The deviations observed in RLV-A and RLV-C were likely driven by anatomical asymmetry, boundary ambiguity, or inherent model bias.

Biometric Deviations in Abnormal Cases. Further validation involved benchmarking BioTTA predictions from abnormal cases in four testing datasets against these developmental curves (Figure 2B-2E. The findings robustly affirm both the sensitivity of BioTTA and the clinical relevance of the curves. Notably, Grade II/III GMH-IVH cases and ventriculomegaly cases overwhelmingly exhibited measurements registered above the 95th percentile. Meanwhile, in WCB-CA, cerebellar hypoplasia cases with reduced TCD and vermian agenesis cases with reduced AVD or VH exhibited measurements consistently fell below the 5th percentile.

Conclusion

In this study, BioTTA was employed to diagnose multiple diseases in fetal brains across OoD datasets. By leveraging atlas-guided test-time adaptation, BioTTA overcomes distribution shifts problem to achieve better biometry and diagnosis accuracy than senior doctor, which provides technical support for advancing intelligent prenatal neurological monitoring integrating multi-center and multi-disease data, holding significant implications for clinical translation.
