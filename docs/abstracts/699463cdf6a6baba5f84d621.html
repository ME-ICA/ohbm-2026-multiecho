Introduction

Cognitive reserve is known to modulate the clinical expression of neurodegenerative diseases, with higher educational attainment often buffering the impact of Alzheimer’s pathology on cognitive performance.1,2 Education, one of the most robust proxies of reserve, has been consistently linked to preserved cognitive function in aging and Alzheimer’s disease, even in the presence of significant neuropathology.1,3 However, in individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or Alzheimer’s disease (AD), depressive symptoms are highly prevalent and may alter the extent to which cognitive reserve can be expressed at the neural level. Emerging evidence suggests that depression is not a unitary construct but comprises distinct symptom dimensions that engage partially dissociable neural pathways.4 Yet, it remains unclear whether specific depressive factors differentially interact with education to influence the relationship between cognition and gray-matter structure in MCI or AD. To address this gap, we examined how three depressive symptom dimensions, Life Dissatisfaction (LD), General Depressive Affect (GDA), and Withdrawal (W), modify education-dependent coupling between cognitive performance and regional gray-matter volume in MCI or AD.

Methods

This retrospective study analyzed 228 individuals diagnosed with MCI or AD, who completed the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD) neuropsychological battery and the Short Geriatric Depression Scale (SGDS). T1-weighted structural MRI, acquired within one year of cognitive testing, was processed using SPM12 and gray-matter regional volumes were extracted according to the Neuromorphometrics atlas. Three depressive-related factors, LD, GDA, and W, were derived from the SGDS. Linear regression models, adjusted for age, gender, intracranial volume, and medications, were used to test the Education × Cognition × Depressive-factor interactions across predefined regions of interest. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate. (FDR) For regions showing significant interaction effects after FDR correction, covariate-adjusted simple slope and Johnson–Neyman analyses were conducted.

Results

Three-way interaction effects (Education × Cognition × depressive factor) were identified in several regions prior to multiple-comparison correction. For LD, interaction effects were observed in the left middle temporal gyrus and the right middle frontal gyrus. For GDA, effects were found in the right superior temporal gyrus and the left superior frontal gyrus. For W, interaction effects were detected in the right hippocampus and the right ventral diencephalon. After FDR correction, the effects in the left middle temporal gyrus for LD and the right ventral diencephalon for W remained statistically significant, while the effect in the left superior frontal gyrus for GDA showed marginal significance.

Post-hoc analyses revealed differential education-dependent modulation across depressive profiles. For LD, cognition and grey matter volume associations became significant only at higher education levels, particularly in the left middle temporal and right middle frontal gyri. For GDA, significant cognition and grey matter volume slopes appeared across the full education range in the low GDA group but were absent in the high GDA group. For W, cognition and grey matter volume coupling in the hippocampus increased with higher education, especially in low withdrawal, whereas the ventral diencephalon showed significant slopes only within lower education levels in the high W group.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that depressive symptom dimensions differentially shape the expression of cognitive reserve in MCI and AD. Although higher education generally strengthened cognition and grey matter volume associations, this buffering effect varied markedly across depressive profiles. LD showed reserve-related enhancement primarily under high educational attainment. For GDA, education-dependent coupling between cognition and grey matter volume was evident only in individuals with low depressive affect and was not observed under high GDA. W exhibited the strongest modulation in subcortical regions, where elevated withdrawal restricted the educational range over which structural coupling emerged. Collectively, these findings indicate that depressive symptoms are heterogeneous in their neuroanatomical impact and can either facilitate or constrain the neural expression of cognitive reserve. Understanding how education interacts with specific depressive dimensions may inform more individualized intervention strategies and refine clinical risk stratification in MCI and AD.
