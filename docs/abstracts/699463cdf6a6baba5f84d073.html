Introduction

Investigations of symmetry and asymmetry are commonly performed in structural and functional MRI to help identify the differences between right and left-brain properties. These may be useful to help characterize disease, development and evolution. Here, we describe a new program in AFNI [Cox 1996] to quickly compute a number of useful quantities that include morphological differences in volume, surface area of regions and the surface area of the regions projected onto a common surface (e.g., cortex).

Methods

The new program, asymm_report.csh, computes the various shape-related quantities and outputs a clear report with the columns of values. To run the analysis, the user provides the program with lists of both right and left-side region indices, along with a volumetric dataset containing the regions. Optionally, surfaces can be provided for left-right queries, such as those generated with FreeSurfer [Fischl 2012] and SUMA [Saad  2012].  Alternatively, the surfaces can be automatically generated from the volumetric data as isosurfaces via the IsoSurface program in AFNI.

Figure 1 shows visualizations of the three types of asymmetries that are computed for a pair of regions, using the AFNI and SUMA GUIs. The purely volumetric differences are the most straightforward to evaluate, counting voxels for each of the left regions and the right regions with multiplication by the voxel size for the volume using 3dBrickStat. The ratio of right to left volumes are included in the output by default with options for laterality index (left-right)/(left+right). The surfaces of each region can be generated using an isosurface rendering of the labeled volume, and the surface areas are estimated from these for each region using SUMA's SurfaceMetrics; the ratios between right and left areas are then computed and output analogously to the volumetric measures. A typical way to compute surface area is to project volumetric parcellations onto a common surface like the cortex surface from FreeSurfer; the surface area is then computed as the area of the patch of surface the labelled parcellation covers with SUMA's SurfMeasures. 

We present an example here using this program to evaluate the asymmetries of the cerebellar structures were measured across three species - marmoset (MBMv3.0) [Liu, 2018], macaque (NMTv2.1) [Jung 2021] and human (MNI_2009c_asymmetric) [Fonov 2011] using the atlases we have recently developed for the cerebellum [Saleem 2021].

Results

Asymmetry was measured for the cerebellum across recently introduced atlases for different species with reports shown for each. An example report is shown in Fig. 2 for the macaque cerebellum in the NMT 2.1 template space, and the Sim_p (Posterior part of the simple lobule) region is highlighted. Here we can see the regions are nearly symmetric  in terms of volumetric and region isosurface areas (i.e., the R/L ratios are near 1), but they have much less symmetric patch surface areas, with a R/L ratio of 0.58.

Conclusion

The measurement of asymmetry is an important part of many protocols, but few tools exist to easily and directly assess this. This new AFNI program provides a simple interface for users to compute many aspects including the ROI isosurface areas and the ROI projections to a common surface. In order to assess the usefulness of surface patches, the shape of the region and its proximity to the surface need to be considered. Furthermore, errors and variability in surface generations and projections can lead to large differences in the surface area for any region. For example, labels crossing gyral and sulcal boundaries can provide less reliable results. Regions are not only superficial to the surface, but typical assessment is on a cortical surface without regard to the more interior folds, so surfaces assessed by the patch of surface they occupy are prone to a very particular assessment of the properties of surface area and asymmetry.
