Introduction

We have recently introduced velocity centiles in normative modelling (NM) (https://www.humanbrainmapping.org/i4a/pages/index.cfm?pageid=4319), which, unlike the standard cross-sectionally estimated population or ‘distance’ centiles, enable the quantification of the significance of longitudinal change at the level of the individual. That is, they capture movements up, down, or across centile trajectories as individuals are measured repeatedly over time (see Figure 1A)(van Buuren 2023; Cole 1994). We have also developed thrive lines, estimated from the correlation structure of repeated measurements, which define a cutoff for the expected normal change within a certain time period and generalise techniques used to declare ‘failure to thrive’ in pediatrics. When applied to neuroimaging phenotypes, deviations outside projected thrive bounds signify change more extreme than that observed in 97.5% of the reference population (Figure 1.B-D).

We present two fundamental methodological extensions to this longitudinal NM framework. First, we introduce ‘z-gain’, a statistic that provides formal quantification of change between two time points expressed as the corresponding z-score derived from the normative reference distribution. Second, we extend this score to account for an arbitrary number of past observations, resulting in a conditional z-gain that incorporates an individual’s prior trajectory to provide more precise predictions that account for each individual’s measured longitudinal history.

We evaluate these extensions in individuals across the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) spectrum in the ADNI cohort and independently replicate results in the OASIS-3 dataset (Petersen et al. 2010; Marcus et al. 2007). Our findings demonstrate that z-gain discriminates subjects transitioning to AD more accurately than conventional cross-sectional z-scores. Furthermore, incorporating multiple time points substantially improves senstitivity for identifying future diagnostic transition.

Methods

Data

Our full sample contains scans from 55995 healthy subjects (104 scanning sites). 45174 subjects provide one scan each and are processed with the standard cross-sectional Freesurfer pipeline (Desikan, Destrioux and subcortical atlases). The remaining 10821 subjects have longitudinal measurements spanning up to 9 follow up points and are processed using the longitudinal Freesurfer pipeline. 

For normative modelling, we created a training set containing 25567 subjects and a test set containing 29834 subjects. All longitudinally processed subjects are part of the test set. For clinical validation, we additionally used 327 scans (169 subjects) with mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer’s diseases from the OASIS2 and OASIS3 cohorts (LaMontagne et al. 2019; Marcus et al. 2007) and 1435 individuals with mild cognitive impairment, stages of Alzheimer’s diseases from the ADNI cohorts (Jack et al. 2008; Petersen et al. 2010). 

 Model

The Normative modelling approach employed here has been described elsewhere (de Boer et al. 2024). In brief, we estimated three models per ROI, one drawing from a normal likelihood (Normal) and two models drawing from a sin-arcsin likelihood (SHASHo and SHASHb_1)(de Boer et al. 2024). The SHASH models are ideally suited to fitting brain features following non-Gaussian and heteroscedastic distributions. The normative modelling covariate matrix included age, sex and a random intercept batch effect for site. Distance centiles were generated based on the training set, and z-score predictions were made for each subject from the test set.

Model evaluation

We evaluated the z-gain predictions in subjects that transition to Alzheimer’s disease in the ADNI (Jack et al. 2008; Petersen et al. 2010) and OASIS3 cohorts (LaMontagne et al. 2019; Marcus et al. 2007) (see data). Group wise z-gain distributions were calculated for the groups healthy controls (HC), early mild cognitive impairment (EMCI) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), as well as for groups marked by change between two time points (HC->HC, HC-> EMCI, HC->AD, EMCI->EMCI, EMCI-AD, AD->AD) (see Figure 2A-D). All evaluation data was part of the test set.

Results

We show that z-gain reflects disease status (Figure 2 A) more accurately than volumetric estimates and –more importantly– provides a sensitive measure for disease transitions (Fig 2C). For example, the z-gain measure shows that individuals who transition to dementia have faster volume loss in the hippocampi relative to both healthy individuals and individuals with a stable dementia diagnosis (Figure 2C). The same pattern was also observed in the OASIS dataset (Fig 2 B & D).

We also quantify the discriminative value of the z-gain score across multiple brain regions by computing the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve across all pairs of timepoints and regions in the ADNI dataset. The z-gain scores provided considerably better discrimination of disease transitions relative to the more conventional measure of the volume or cortical thickness score at each timepoint, especially lateral temporal regions (Fig 2E,F).

Next, we take this framework further towards precision diagnostics and show that our approach becomes more sensitive for detecting individual disease transitions with an increasing quantity of longitudinal data. In Fig 2G-L, we show trajectories for four illustrative subjects having more than 6 timepoints within the normative centiles of this region (Fig 2G, and zoomed in Fig 2H). Figures 2I and J illustrate how adding multiple time points can detect transition to Alzheimer’s as an outlier event.  For example, the subject shown in Figure 2I has seven usable follow up time points, the last of which is associated with transition to Alzheimer’s disease (orange). Calculating z-gain from the sixth to the seventh timepoint alone, results in a z-gain of 1.78, just within the non-significant range. Including the all six time points before the 7th to calculate z-gain for the seventh time point leads to an adjusted z-gain of 4.44, and marks this timepoint as highly significant. This shows that accounting for multiple timepoints can assist to identify transition to dementia by shrinking the cone of uncertainty for the future timepoint. Figures 2K and L demonstrate how including multiple time points of the past shrinks the windows of uncertainty, even for an event that lies already outside the range of non-extreme predicted range.

Conclusion

With this abstract we extend our velocity normative modelling approach and to include z-gain, a measure quantifying the significance of a change between two time points, and conditional z-gain, which conditions the prediction of the next measurement onto multiple time points. These results generalize to an external data set. 

 We demonstrate that z-gain offers a robust and interpretable measure of longitudinal deviation beyond cross-sectional z-scores, which can distinguish transition to Alzheimer’s disease. Beyond that, we take a step further towards precision diagnostics by showing that integrating multiple time points of a person’s personal history can improve the sensitivity of the prediction.
