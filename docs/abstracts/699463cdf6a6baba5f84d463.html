Introduction

Head motion is a serious concern in neuroimaging. Imaging derived phenotypes (IDPs), especially resting state fMRI (rsfMRI) IDPs, are often confounded to various degree, by head motion. A common approach to mitigate this confounding effect is to censor high-motion frames using framewise displacement (FD; Power et al., 2012), followed by removing the data of subjects with many missing time points. However, this strategy is often not enough as residual motion confound remains. In addition, as shown in a recent study (Peverill et al., 2024), censoring can lead to selection bias by disproportionately removing data from certain participant groups. To address these, we introduce Motion Insensitive Resting-State Analysis (MIRA), a new method to quantify and correct motion-related bias in resting-state derived correlation matrices.

Methods

Given subject-wise rsfMRI correlation matrices and mean FD values, the MIRA framework has five steps (Figure 1):

  1. divide mean FD into 25 percentiles (0-4th percentile, 4-8th percentile, etc.); identify low motion group (0-4th percentile)
  2. calculate the average of the correlation matrix for the lowest percentile group (“reference correlation matrix”)
  3. quantify, for each subject, the “bias” as the difference between the correlation matrix and the reference correlation matrix
  4. for each FD percentile group, calculate the average bias
  5. perform singular value decomposition (SVD) on the average bias and project the subject-wise bias matrices onto these components

This results in additional subject-wise covariates which can be used in any standard general linear model analyses; these additional covariates will account for motion-related biases.

As a concrete demonstration of the MIRA approach, we use data from 6.1 release of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development℠ (ABCD) Study. Using rsfMRI correlation matrices from 333 Gordon parcels (Hagler et al., 2019), we compute MIRA projection vectors across seven framewise displacement values. For each FD threshold value, we quantify the effectiveness of MIRA by examining the median QCFC (the partial correlation between motion and correlation matrices), distribution of QCFC, and distance-dependent QCFC with and without including MIRA components as covariates. Finally, we use MIRA components as covariates in an analysis of association between NIH toolbox total composite score and rsfMRI correlation matrices.

Results

Our results show that including six MIRA components as covariates provides a robust reduction in the QCFC values across all threshold values (Figure 2a) – this means that a far more liberal FD threshold could be used for censoring, increasing the temporal degrees of freedom, reducing the chance of eliminating study participants, and substantially reducing the chances of introducing selection biases. Further examination of the distribution of the QCFC results indicates that using MIRA components as covariates results in unbiased QCFC distribution (Figure 2b). A similar pattern is evident when examining the distance dependent QCFC, showing that including MIRA components eliminates distance-dependent motion effects on the correlation matrices (Figure 2c). Finally, using MIRA components as additional covariates when examining the association between NIH Toolbox total composite score and correlation matrices showed a dramatic reduction in the distribution of effect sizes and the number of significant connections, potentially highlighting that several of these connections might have been false positives due to motion confounds (Figure 2d).

Conclusion

We have proposed a new method, MIRA, for performing motion correction for rsfMRI correlation matrices that has the promise of removing motion-related confounds from these correlation matrices, reducing the chances of introducing selection biases due to scrubbing, and reducing the potential to discover spurious associations. We expect MIRA to have a significant impact on the resting state connectivity field by providing a mitigation strategy for a long-standing problem in the field.
