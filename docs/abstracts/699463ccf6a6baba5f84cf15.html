Introduction

Self-esteem refers to individuals’ subjective evaluation of their social worth. According to Sociometer Theory, state self-esteem functions as an internal monitor of perceived social acceptance, varying across different events.1 Recent computational work formalises this process by modelling state self-esteem as a learning signal driven by discrepancies between expected and received social feedback. Using reinforcement learning frameworks, these studies show that self-esteem is updated by social prediction errors, with positive evaluations exerting stronger effects when negative feedback is expected.2 Moreover, individuals with low trait self-esteem exhibit asymmetric learning, updating more strongly from negative than positive evaluations, consistent with heightened vulnerability to social threat.3

At the neural level, such computational signals have been linked to valuation circuits centred on the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC). The vmPFC integrates self-relevant value information and interacts with the ventral striatum, a key region for reward learning and prediction-error processing.4 Prior work demonstrated that vmPFC activity encodes social prediction errors and that its functional connectivity varies with trait self-esteem, providing a neural basis for individual differences in social evaluation.2, 3

However, little is known about how these neurocomputational processes operate during adolescence, a developmental period with heightened social sensitivity and developing maturation of the vmPFC. The present study investigates how adolescents update their state self-esteem in response to social evaluation, focusing on activations in the vmPFC and ventral striatum. By integrating computational modelling with neuroimaging, we aim to characterise developmental differences in social learning mechanisms and identify neural markers of vulnerability in adolescent self-esteem

Methods

The study recruited 34 healthy adolescents (16 males, mean age 16.71 ± 0.81 years, range 15-17 years) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. In the behavioural session, participants first created personal profiles that were evaluated by others. They then completed questionnaires assessing affective states, self-esteem, and social engagement constructs. One week later, they performed a social evaluation task adapted from previous research (See Figure 1A).2 All Participants were informed that evaluators differed in their probability of giving positive ('like') feedback (15%, 30%, 70%, or 85%). On each trial, they first predicted whether the evaluator would respond with a 'like' or 'dislike', followed by feedback ('like', 'dislike', or 'neutral'). Every 2-3 trials, they rated their momentary self-worth on a scale of 0 to 10 in response to the question, 'How good do you feel about yourself at this moment?'

Following previous modeling work, expectations of positive feedback were modeled using a dual learning-rate reinforcement learning model. Additionally, state self-esteem ratings were modelled as a function of prediction errors, with parameters including a self-esteem baseline term 'w0', a prediction-error weighting parameter 'w1', and a temporal decay parameter 'gamma' (Figure 1B). Four models were compared, differing in whether they included initial value parameters and/or a bias term. Initial values captured participants’ prior expectations about social feedback, whereas the bias term reflected a global response tendency independent of learning. Model comparison was based on a penalized joint score that combined the negative log-likelihood of the reinforcement learning model and the mean squared error of the self-esteem model (Figure 1C). 

Exploratory factor analysis was conducted on questionnaire measures using JASP (v0.18.3), employing parallel analysis and promax oblique rotation. Questionnaires with factor loadings greater than 0.6 were retained for further correlation analyses and interpretation. Spearman correlation analyses were performed to examine associations between model parameters and factor scores.

fMRI data preprocessing was performed using SPM12 (v7771) and included segmentation, realignment, coregistration, normalisation to MNI space, and spatial smoothing. Two general linear models (GLMs) were constructed using the Nilearn toolbox (v0.21.1), with regressors indicating the onsets of ‘like’ expectation, feedback, and self-esteem ratings, all with zero durations. Six motion regressors were added, and the volumes with a framewise displacement value over 0.9 were masked.

The first GLM included parametric modulators of the estimated subjective value of expectations and the corresponding social prediction errors, time-locked to the onsets of expectation and feedback, respectively. The second GLM included parametric modulators of z-scored inferred self-esteem ratings at both expectation and feedback onsets. Both GLMs also included modulators of z-scored actual self-esteem ratings, time-locked to rating onsets. Subsequent whole-brain analyses were cluster-level FWE-corrected at p < 0.05 (voxel-wise p < 0.001).

Results

Model comparison indicated that the model including initial value parameters but no explicit bias term provided the best fit (model performance score = 92.76), outperforming the initial bias-free model (97.34), the bias-only model (94.81), and the full model (94.21). Exploratory factor analysis revealed a two-factor structure with good model fit (χ² = 68.84, p = 0.071; Figure 2A). Factor 1 was primarily characterised by affective self-evaluative measures: the State Trait Anxiety Scale showed the highest positive loading (0.950), whereas the State Self-Esteem Scale showed the strongest negative loading (−0.885). Factor 2 was dominated by social engagement measures, with the Social Media Engagement Scale showing the highest positive loading (0.900). 

Correlation analyses revealed dissociable associations between modeling parameters and psychometric factors. The learning rate for negative (‘dislike’) feedback was negatively correlated with the total score of Social Media Engagement (r = −0.401, p = 0.034), particularly the positive cognitive engagement subscale (r = −0.490, p = 0.008; See Figure 2B). Notably, this scale loaded onto Factor 2, and neither learning rate was significantly correlated with scales loading onto Factor 1. For parameters of the self-esteem model, the baseline term w0 was positively correlated with both trait self-esteem (r = 0.452, p = 0.016) and state self-esteem (r = 0.424, p = 0.025), consistent with its interpretation as an individual baseline of self-evaluation. The prediction-error weighting parameter (w1) was positively correlated with affections, such as State Trait Anxiety (r = 0.470, p = 0.012), Depressed Mood (r = 0.532, p = 0.004), and Fear of Negative Evaluation (r = 0.504, p = 0.006). 

Neuroimaging analyses revealed no significant associations between estimated subjective value of ‘like’ expectation or social prediction errors and brain activity, in contrast to previous findings in young adults.2 However, the second GLM indicated that inferred state self-esteem significantly modulated activity in the previously proposed interpersonal vulnerability biomarker region2 (peak MNI coordinates: −46, 12, 10; Z = 4.53; k = 919; p < .001; See Figure 2C). In addition, inferred self-esteem was positively associated with activity in a primary motor cortex region that was previously implicated in low trait self-esteem5 (peak coordinates: −30, 0, −52; Z = 5.92; k = 18,222; p < .001; Figure 2D). All neuroimaging results were cluster-level family-wise error-corrected.

Conclusion

This study extends computational accounts of self-esteem by integrating behavioural modelling, neurobiological evidence, and a developmental perspective. At the behavioural level, the state self-esteem model captured core features of self-evaluation in adolescence. The baseline parameter (w0) showed robust associations with both state and trait self-esteem, supporting its interpretation as a stable individual reference point. In contrast, the prediction-error weighting parameter (w1) was associated with anxiety, depressive symptoms, and fear of negative evaluation, indicating that adolescents with greater affective vulnerability are more sensitive to discrepancies between expected and received social feedback when updating their self-esteem. Reinforcement learning results further suggested that greater positive cognitive engagement with social media was associated with reduced sensitivity to negative social feedback, consistent with the idea that positive online engagement may buffer against adverse social learning.

At the neural level, inferred state self-esteem emerged as a strong modulator of brain activity during social evaluation, particularly during the anticipation. This modulation was expressed in a large cluster overlapping regions previously implicated in interpersonal vulnerability and social pain processing. Notably, the peak coordinates of this activation closely aligned with the left premotor cortex, a region shown to exhibit heightened activation and connectivity in individuals with low trait self-esteem during social exclusion.5 This convergence suggests that adolescent self-esteem updating engages neural systems related to action preparation and embodied responses to social threat, consistent with heightened interpersonal sensitivity.

In addition, inferred self-esteem modulated activity in a region consistent with the interpersonal vulnerability biomarker proposed by a previous study,2 indicating that neural signatures of social sensitivity observed in adults are already present during adolescence. Importantly, unlike previous studies in young adults, no significant effects were observed in the ventral striatum or vmPFC. This absence may reflect developmental differences in reward processing and valuation circuitry in social state learning, as vmPFC-mediated integration of social value continues to mature throughout adolescence. Instead, adolescents appear to rely more strongly on neural systems associated with interpersonal vigilance and sensorimotor preparation, which is consistent with the heightened social sensitivity observed during this developmental period.

Together, these findings suggest that adolescent self-esteem is shaped by neurocomputational mechanisms that differ qualitatively from those observed in adulthood, with a greater emphasis on low self-esteem traits rather than social reward valuation. Future studies incorporating adult comparison samples and cross-cultural designs will be essential for determining how developmental stage and sociocultural context jointly shape the neural architecture of self-esteem. More broadly, these results suggest that negative social feedback may have a disproportionate impact on self-evaluation, even among high-performing students. This highlights the importance of considering developmental vulnerability in increasingly evaluative social media environments.
