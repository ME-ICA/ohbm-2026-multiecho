Introduction

The human primary sensorimotor (SM1) cortex exhibits a classical somatotopic organization, in which individual fingers are represented in distinct cortical regions. This organization has also been studied using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). In the primary somatosensory (SI) cortex, finger representations in response to tactile stimulation have been mapped in multiple studies, showing a lateral-to-medial and inferior-to-posterior organization from digit 1 to digit 5.1 Similarly, the finger representations in response to volitional movements in SI and the primary motor (M1) cortices revealed a detailed somatotopic organization, where digits were arranged from the thumb ventrolaterally to the little finger dorsomedially within the hand area.2 Previous research has also shown that tactile stimulation and voluntary finger movements follow somatotopic organization with similar spatial pattern in SI cortex.3

Proprioception refers to the sense of position and movement of the body. Unlike tactile and motor representations, cortical representations of proprioceptive input in the human SM1 cortex are incompletely understood. A recent 7T-fMRI study examined the laminar response of human M1 cortex to proprioceptive stimulation of a single finger and found that passive movement activates both superficial and deep layers of M1 cortex.4 However, it remains unknown how proprioceptive representations of multiple fingers are organized in both M1 and S1 cortex, and how they differ from voluntary finger movement representations.

The aim of this study was to use 3T-fMRI and a proprioceptive stimulator to investigate cortical representations of the index and ring fingers in SM1 cortex during passive and active finger movements. Specifically, we asked whether the index and ring fingers of the same hand show spatially distinct representations to proprioceptive stimulation in the SM1 cortex, and whether the SM1 cortex activation differs between passive and active conditions.

Methods

Twenty-four healthy participants, with no neuropsychiatric diseases and no contraindications for MRI, were recruited for the study. All participants except one were right-handed; one was ambidextrous.

During the MRI, participants lay in a supine position in the scanner, with their right fingers attached to a proprioceptive stimulator fixed to the scanner table (Fig. 1a). Each finger holder was attached to a separate pneumatic muscle able to evoke flexion-extension movement of the proximal interphalangeal joint. The index and ring finger movement was controlled using the Presentation software.

Participants attended a single imaging session, including an anatomical scan and two functional runs. Each functional run consisted of a pseudo-randomized block design with 15 blocks per condition across five conditions: passive and resisted stimulation of the index or ring finger, and rest (Fig. 1c). During passive stimulation, the stimulator moved the relaxed index or ring finger at 2 Hz. In the resisted stimulation, the movement was the same, but the participant slightly resisted the movement, i.e., applied a slight finger flexion. In the rest condition, the fingers were not moved. The blocks occurred in block lists of five, such that each condition was presented once per block list. The passive stimulation and rest blocks each lasted 15 seconds. At the beginning of each resisted stimulation block, a 5-second cue was first presented, and the participants started the active finger flexion at the onset of the 15-second movement stimulation. Each movement block comprised 30 finger movements at 2 Hz.

Imaging was performed with a Siemens 3 T MAGNETOM Vida scanner with a 64-channel head coil. Anatomical T1-weighted images were acquired with MPRAGE sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 2.41 ms, flip angle = 9°, resolution = 0.7x0.7x0.9 mm3, FOV = 216x230x187.2 mm3, orientation = axial oblique). Functional data were collected in two runs (1300 volumes each) using SMS EPI sequence (TR = 999 ms, TE = 31 ms, flip angle = 52°, resolution = 2.4x2.4x2.4 mm3, FOV = 216x216x144 mm3, orientation = axial oblique).

Data preprocessing and analysis were performed using FreeSurfer and Matlab and Python scripts. Anatomical images were used to generate the cortical reconstructions for each participant. Functional data preprocessing included motion correction, co-registration, intensity normalization, and 5-mm surface-based smoothing. Six participants were excluded for excessive head motion, leaving 18 for analysis.

Regions of interest (ROIs) were anatomically defined using Freesurfer’s probabilistic Brodmann area (BA) parcellation. The SI cortex ROI comprised BAs 1, 2, 3a, and 3b, while the M1 cortex ROI comprised BAs 4a and 4p. To focus the analysis closer to the hand region, both SI and M1 cortex ROIs were subdivided into seven components using principal component analysis, and subject-specific hand area ROIs were constructed.

First-level analysis was performed by fitting a general linear model (GLM) to each cortical vertex. The hemodynamic response function (HRF) was modeled using the canonical SPM HRF function and its temporal derivative. Motion parameters were included as nuisance regressors in the GLM. Contrasts included comparisons of all conditions versus rest and active versus passive conditions. Group-level analysis was also conducted to identify vertices for which contrast effects were consistent across participants.

At the individual-level, we compared the extent and strength of the activations between active and passive conditions for each finger on M1 and SI cortex. Furthermore, the overlap and geodesic distance were compared between fingers within passive and active conditions. Finally, it was examined whether the finger representations followed a somatotopic organization. Statistical comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Results

Figure 2a shows four metrics of cortical finger representations: the extent of activation, the strength of activation, the overlap between finger representations, and the geodesic distance between fingers. The extent of activation, defined as the number of statistically significant vertices, was larger in the active condition (resisted stimulation) compared to the passive condition for both fingers and ROIs. For the strength of activation, a mask was created by contrasting all movement conditions against the rest. This mask was applied to the percent signal change maps, and the average percent signal change was computed. The strength of the activation was higher in the active condition compared to the passive condition for both fingers and ROIs.

To assess overlap between finger activations, a modified Dice coefficient between fingers was calculated for the passive and active conditions across both ROIs. The modified Dice coefficient accounts for cases where one representation is fully contained within another but differs greatly in size.3 The coefficient ranges from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating no overlap and 1 indicating complete overlap. Finger representations were more overlapping in the active than passive condition in SI cortex. In M1 cortex, no significant difference was observed. The finger separation was quantified by computing the geodesic distance between vertices showing the maximum percent signal change for each finger. No significant difference in finger separation between active and passive conditions was found in either ROI. However, there was a trend toward larger geodesic distances in the passive condition. 

Finally, we tested whether the finger representations followed somatotopy by calculating the average MNI x-coordinate from the finger-versus-rest maps. If the representations followed a somatotopic organization, the index finger would be located more laterally, corresponding to a more negative MNI x-coordinate. In the active condition, somatotopic organization was observed in M1 and SI cortex for 11 out of 17 and 11 out of 18 participants, respectively. In the passive condition, somatotopy was observed in 7 out of 13 participants in M1 cortex and 7 out of 16 participants in SI cortex.

Figure 2b shows the group-level activation maps contrasting finger conditions with rest. Activations during the active condition were more overlapping in M1 cortex compared to SI cortex. In the passive condition, no significant activation was observed in M1 cortex. When comparing active and passive condition maps in S1 cortex, activation was larger in the active condition. Furthermore, when contrasting active versus passive conditions for each finger and ROI at the group level, activation in the active condition was stronger than in the passive condition in the same regions identified in the finger versus resting state contrast. The passive condition did not show stronger activation than the active condition in any ROI or finger.

Conclusion

The results indicate that cortical representations for proprioceptive stimulation in active condition are stronger and spatially more extensive than those elicited by the passive proprioceptive stimulation only for both fingers and both in SI and M1 cortices. The primary difference between the conditions is the active state of SM1 cortex to maintain finger flexion during the resisted condition, which may explain the stronger cortical activation. The difference between the active/passive condition was especially well demonstrated in the M1 cortex, but also in the SI cortex, which suggests that also the processing of the proprioceptive afference might be intensified during active tasks when compared to passive conditions. Although the finger movement was identical in both conditions, the active condition required motor planning and output from M1 cortex, whereas proprioceptive afference to M1 cortex in passive condition is likely suppressed or inhibited while the motor plan is to relax the muscles.

Our findings are also consistent with previous work demonstrating that voluntary movement elicits stronger BOLD responses compared to tactile stimulation.3 Similarly, stronger M1 cortex activation has been observed for active volitional movement than proprioceptive stimulation of one finger.4 The way activation strength was calculated may have amplified the difference: average percent signal change was computed within a mask derived from all finger movement conditions versus rest, meaning that when the extent of activation was larger in the active condition, the average strength was likely higher as well.

Finger representations overlapped more in the active compared to passive condition in SI cortex. This may be because individual fingers are used together, and they are anatomically linked by partially sharing the same parent muscles. Thus, it seems that also the proprioceptive afference activates more overlapping neuronal networks in the SI cortex during active than passive condition. In M1 cortex, a similar difference was not observed, which may be because the activation of M1 cortex was weak in passive condition with only 11 subjects showing significant activations in both passive and active conditions. 

The geodesic distance between peak cortical signal change of the index and ring fingers did not differ when comparing active and passive conditions. Nevertheless, a trend toward larger spatial finger separation in passive condition was observed, being in line with the more overlapping finger representations in the active than passive condition in SI cortex.

Furthermore, the group-level activation maps in the active condition were more overlapping in M1 cortex compared to SI cortex, which aligns with previous findings.5 In the passive condition, no significant activation was observed in M1 cortex, which can be expected given the absence of volitional motor output and lesser direct proprioceptive input compared to SI cortex. Despite the vast proprioceptive input to the SI cortex, its activation was clearly wider during active than passive condition. 

In conclusion, active motor state of the SM1 cortex appears to intensify its proprioceptive processing. However, future studies are needed to confirm cortical layer specific patterns of the processing of proprioceptive afference during passive and active conditions.
