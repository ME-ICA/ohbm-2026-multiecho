Introduction

Physiological noise is a term referring to variations in the functional imaging signal of not BOLD-mediated neural origin, thought to be generated by factors such as respiration, heartbeat and variation in its frequency, etc. Recently, we have shown that the average signal from cortex in resting state (often referred to as the ‘global signal’) is closely associated with the average signal from the skull bone in most individuals (Huber et al. 2024). This association was specific (it was not present in soft tissue) and persisted irrespective of attempts to filter or adjust for confounders such as head movements. This finding suggests that a source of signal in cortex is vascular and subject to forces that act irrespective of neural activity.

In the present study, we provide evidence that this signal is not independent of factors usually associated with central processes. In a sustained attention task, we reported that individual differences in self-regulation correlated with differences in activity in the basal forebrain during a sustained attention task, possibly indicating differences in cholinergic activity, after adjustment for physiological noise (Orsini et al. 2025). Here, we describe the existence of extensive cortical correlates of individual differences in self-regulation that progressively disappear depending on the aggressiveness of the adjustment for physiological noise.

Methods

The task was described in Viviani et al. (2020). Briefly, participants accumulate virtual rewards by responding to the appearance of a dot on the left or right side of the screen by clicking a button on the same side. Targets appeared at brief intervals averaging 1.23 sec., grouped in blocks of 12-13 trials. Before each block, a cue signaled the value accrued by executing the task during the block, i.e. 1 or 20 cents per detected target. Among the several contrasts that can be estimated in this task, we focus here on the effect of reward levels during the blocks, when participants had to sustain attention. The whole experiment lasted about 8 minutes. During this time, T2*-weighted EPI volumes were collected at TR/TE 2460/30 msec. After high-pass filtering (128 sec), realignment, and registration to a MNI template, images were smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (FWHM 8mm). Individual self-regulation scores (N=203) were collected with the SCS scale (short German version). The validity of the scores was verified by checking that they significantly correlated with the body mass index of participants, a known correlate of self-regulation (see Orsini et al. 2025 for details).

Adjustment for physiological noise consisted of six movement covariates and the signals from lateral ventricles, white matter, cranial bone, and soft tissue. These signals were extracted from segmentation maps on the images prior to registration and smoothing based on the intensity of the signal (we used the segmentation computed by SPM as part of the registration; this segmentation uses a spatial prior to identify the segments in MNI space). To further reduce partial volume effects, the segments were eroded by one voxel (for cranial bone, this ensures exclusion of subdural spaces). Bone was complemented with a derivative to account for delays relative to gray matter. The physiological noise signals were processed with a principal component analysis. We progressively increased the number of components used for the adjustment to verify their effect on reported individual differences in self-regulation. At the second level, self-regulation was modelled with age, sex, and acquisition site as confounding covariates. To assess the effect of the adjustment for physiological noise, we summed the voxels of clusters significant at p ≤ 0.05 (permutation test), cluster definition threshold p = 0.001, in the interaction reward level x self-regulation scores (see Figure 1B).

Results

Physiological noise adjustment has well known effects on fMRI inference, generally an improvement of significance levels. For brevity, however, we focus here only on its effects on neural correlates of individual differences.

Without adjustment for physiological noise (but adjusting for head movements and the scanner drift), individual self-regulation scores were associated with significant, extensive and distributed differences in cortical activity in the reward level contrast during the trial blocks (see Figure 1A). The effect formed one cluster of size k= 110044, p < 0.001. The main peak was in the left Middle Frontal Gyrus (MNI coordinates -39, 42.5, 15.5), t = 5.508 (significant peak level for the whole volume). 

Adding components from the extracortical segments selected for physiological noise extraction resulted in a progressive disappearance of this association (Figure 1B). White matter and cranial bone, when combined, were most effective in adjusting for the effect. The lateral ventricles (labelled ‘CSF’ in the figure) were the least effective. Soft tissue (not shown) was not effective.

Conclusion

The capacity of the physiological noise signal to adjust for individual differences in self-regulation for cortical activity suggests the existence of mechanisms affecting vascular regulation that are sensitive to contingencies of the task and individual traits. Here, the adjustment was effective on a contrast that implied processing of the differences between reward levels. The first implication of this finding is the obvious importance of these mechanisms in causing misattribution of differences of observed cortical activity to individual differences. The second is that it is becoming increasingly difficult to characterize this signal as “noise”. Instead, it appears to include signal related to central processing of task-related information probably emerging through vascular regulation (Turchi et al. 2018). We anticipate that understanding these mechanisms will advance our capacity to identify the sources of variation in the neuroimaging signal related to individual differences.
