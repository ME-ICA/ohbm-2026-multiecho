Introduction

Spatial navigation entails mapping the spatial environment, planning a path, and subsequently moving between locations based on this internal representation. Beyond the physical domain, navigation extends to the social domain, guiding individuals to form social relationships and make adaptive decisions. Navigation involves two kinds of spatial frameworks, egocentric and allocentric references. Previous studies suggested that the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) /precuneus represent the social distance between others and ourselves in social navigation process1. Notably, these regions have been shown to functionally interact with affective valuation systems (e.g., the amygdala and ventromedial prefrontal cortex) to track other’s popularity2. However, it remains unclear whether the brain relies only on the egocentric framework or combines both allocentric and egocentric frameworks in social networks to guide social behaviors3. Thus, we acquired pre- and post-task resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) scans, and calculated resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC). By comparing the rsFC between the pre- and post-task, we attempted to analyze how navigation in a social space shapes the resting state brain.

Methods

Subjects. We recruited 66 healthy adult subjects at the campus of South China Normal University (SCNU). Among them, 39 subjects (20 M/19 F, aged 21.89 ± 2.57 years old) who reached our accuracy criterion (selection task ≥ 85%, comparison task ≥ 90%) took part in the fMRI scanning. Two subjects were excluded due to their excessive head motion (mean FD > 0.2 mm) or technical errors. Eventually, the fMRI data from 37 subjects were included in the following analysis. This experiment was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of SCNU. All subjects provided written informed consent prior to the experiment and were paid for participating.

Data acquisition. All of the MRI data were acquired on a 3.0 T Siemens PrismaFit scanner with a 64-channel phased-array head coil. The BOLD-fMRI data were obtained using a single-shot gradient-echo multiband EPI sequence. Field map was acquired with a dual-echo gradient-echo sequence for distortion correction. High resolution brain structural images were acquired using a T1-weighted 3D MP-RAGE sequence.

Experimental design and procedures. The experiment was performed in two consecutive days, Day 1 for behavioral training, and Day 2 for fMRI scanning. On day 1, subjects completed behavioral training to learn the seven faces (six unfamiliar faces and a self-face) and their associated competence and popularity values. On Day 2, we acquired the task-fMRI data when subjects performing the 1-back and social navigation task inside the scanner. The rs-fMRI data, which were acquired before and after the task-fMRI, were used to assess the changes of brain spontaneous activity caused by the social navigation task.

MRI data analysis. All of the rs-fMRI data were quality controlled using MRIQC and preprocessed by fMRIPrep v25.2.24. All analyses were performed using the CONN toolbox (version 22.v2407)5 . We adopted three analytical approaches, ROI-to-ROI connectivity, large-scale network-level connectivity, and graph theory.

For the ROI-to-ROI connectivity, rsFC was compared between the pre- and post-task resting-state runs using the HCPex parcellation (426 parcels), with ROI time series computed as the mean BOLD signal within each parcel. For each of the two resting-state runs (pre- and post-task), we calculated Pearson’s r correlation between the time series of every pair of ROIs, and made Fischer’s r-to-Z transformation. At the group level, we used a two-tailed paired t-test for comparing the pre- and post-task rsFC maps. The significant level was determined by applying threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) with family-wise error correction (pFWE < 0.05).

A large-scale network-level analysis was performed by mapping parcel-wise rsFC onto the Yeo 7-network using the Schaefer 2018 parcellation with 400 parcels. For statistical inference, we considered only the unique off-diagonal entries of this symmetric matrix. Given that the distribution of these connectivity differences may not satisfy assumptions of normality, a two-sided sign-flip permutation test (nPerm = 5,000) was performed and controlled the false discovery rate at q < 0.05.

For graph theoretical analysis, we first constructed whole-brain functional networks using the HCPex template. The HCPex template includes 426 cortical and subcortical regions, with each region was defined a node. For each subject and each run, we computed a 426-by-426 rsFC matrix, where edges were given by Fisher’s r-to-Z transformed inter-regional correlations. A proportional cost threshold of 0.15 was applied to retain the strongest 15% of connections. For each subject, we estimated network global efficiency and eigenvector centrality for each node. For the group level analysis, the difference in global efficiency were assessed using a paired t-tests across subjects, and differences in eigenvector centrality were tested for each ROI with a paired t-tests followed by FDR correction across ROIs (q < 0.05).

Results

The ROI-to-ROI connectivity analysis

By comparing the post- to pre-task rs-fMRI, we observed primary reductions in rsFC within the visual, sensory-motor, and subcortical functional systems after the social navigation task. Specifically, the visual cortex (V1, V4) exhibited significantly reduced rsFC with the LGN and dorsal attention nodes (IPS1, FEF) in the post rs-fMRI. Both the ventral lateral thalamus (VLp/VLa) and the putamen showed decreased rsFC with the primary somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate, and posterior parietal region in the post rs-fMRI. 

Fig. 2 shows a pattern of increased rsFC (rsFCpost > rsFCpre) centered on two regions (L_52 and the PCC/precuneus) after the social navigation task. First, the left para-insular area (L_52) emerged as a critical node for social evaluation (Fig. 2A). L_52 showed an increased rsFC with the anterior prefrontal pole (L_a10p), a key region within the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), and the temporo-parietal junction (TPJ). L_52 also displayed increased rsFC with inferior frontal (p47r), auditory association (A4, STGa), and lateral temporal cortices (TE1p). Additionally, the rsFC between the parahippocampal area (PHA1) and auditory parabelt (PBelt) served as a functional bridge to the parietal operculum (OP1). Similarly, the left OP1 displayed an increased rsFC with the mPFC, TPJ, the frontal operculum (FOP5), and the inferior parietal lobule (PGi). We also observed a cluster in the PCC/precuneus, primarily involving the area 7Pm, dorsal posterior cingulate area (7m), and the precuneus visual area (PCV) (Fig. 2B). The significant increase in rsFC was observed between the amygdala and PCC/precuneus (POS1, 7m, 7Pm). Furthermore, the PCC/precuneus showed increased connectivity with the superior temporal visual area (STV).

Large-scale network-level analysis

Fig. 2C shows changes the map of ΔrsFC = rsFCpost - rsFCpre at the Yeo seven-network level, summarized as a 7×7 matrix using only the off-diagonal entries. We observed the increased rsFC for only two connections between these 7 distinct networks, the rsFC between the somatomotor network and the limbic network (t = 3.056, p = 0.046, Cohen’s d = 0.502), and the rsFC between the somatomotor network and default mode network (t = 3.014, p = 0.032, Cohen’s d = 0.496). No other significant between-network rsFC was found following FDR correction (all q > 0.05).

Graph theoretical analysis

From graph theoretical analysis, we revealed significant increased global efficiency for the L_STV, PCV, and R_7m (Fig. 2D) and increased eigenvector centrality in the bilateral PCC and precuneus (7m, PCV) after the social navigation task. We also observed increased eigenvector centrality in the right anterior cingulate and mPFC (a24, d32, p32, and 8BM), the orbitofrontal cortex (47s), and the amygdala (Fig. 2E).

Conclusion

The present study analyzed brain rsFC before and after the social navigation task and found that navigation in a social space caused the changes of brain functional connectivity pattern. Post-task rest showed a broad reduction in rsFC within the sensory–subcortical and sensorimotor–subcortical circuits, indicating decreased coordination between sensory processing and subcortical regions after social navigation. The connectivity between amygdala and PCC/precuneus was increased after the task, suggesting tighter integration between spatial representations and emotional relevance. At the large-scale network level, we found increased interactions between somatomotor network and the DMN and limbic networks after the social navigation task compared to the pre-task. The graph-theoretical results further suggested greater post-task network integration than the pre-task rsfMRI. Global efficiency and eigenvector centrality were increased in the PCC, precuneus, and amygdala, with additional eigenvector centrality increases in the cingulate and orbitofrontal cortex. Together, our findings suggest that social navigation is an emotionally engaged, self-referenced mapping process that may guide adaptive decisions.
