Introduction

Recent comparative neuroscience has identified a uniquely human region within the frontal cortex: the prefrontal extent of the frontal operculum (PFO), absent in non-human primates^1,2. With the expansion of the frontal cortex in primates, the lateral cortex folds over the insula. This buried portion, lateral to the insula and medial to Broca’s area, forms the frontal operculum and is thought to have emerged in the genus Homo, alongside auditory specializations and the onset of speech capacities. In humans, this operculum extends into both the prefrontal (PFO) and the premotor cortex (precentral operculum – PCO). Using resting-state functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging, our team demonstrated that the PFO can be subdivided into an anterior (aPFO) and a posterior (pPFO) region, each with distinct whole-brain functional connectivity profiles, with the language and cognitive control networks respectively^3. This evidence suggests a pivotal evolutionary role of the PFO in the emergence of modern speech abilities.

From an Evo-Devo perspective, brain regions that appeared most recently in phylogeny should also be among the last to develop in human ontogeny. They may be more susceptible to neurodevelopmental disruptions, particularly in the context of prematurity, since the last gestational trimester represents a critical window for cortical functional specialization, connectivity maturation, and network integration. Yet, the developmental timing and vulnerability of the PFO remain unexplored. The present study addresses this gap by leveraging the unique neuroimaging dataset of the new release of the developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP; https://www.developingconnectome.org) to characterize the in-utero maturation of PFO and its alterations in prematurity.

Methods

To understand if the PFO was the last cortical region to setup during brain development, we selected subjects from the dHCP database, from 21 to 44 gestational weeks post-menstrual age, all with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) anatomical scans.

First, we assessed the whole-brain sulcal pattern development in 148 in-utero fetuses (21-36 gestational weeks, gw) and 61 full-term (37-44 gw). We also included 44 preterm subjects from 26 to 36 gw to assess the impact of prematurity on sulcal emergence and PFO formation during the early pre-term period. Sulci were analyzed across the entire brain based on Petrides’ atlas on the pial cortical surfaces provided for each subject by the dHCP. For each subject, the presence of each sulcus was assessed in both hemispheres, on the lateral and medial surfaces of the brain. Sulcal development was classified into two categories: sulcus (a well-defined, long, and deep sulcus), and dimple (a slight indentation indicating an early stage of sulcus formation). We tested the influence of gestational age at scan and hemisphere on sulcal presence with a General Linear Mixed Model (GLMM), and ANOVA Type III Wald χ² tests as post-hoc analyses. To evaluate the impact of premature birth, we analyzed subjects between 26 and 36 gestational weeks, including both fetuses and preterm neonates. We added “birth status” (0 = fetus, 1 = preterm) as an additional fixed factor in the GLMM used previously, using the same post-hoc procedures.

Second, we assessed the percentage of covered insula (by the different opercular parts). Based on the segmentation and parcellation of the cortex of T2w images provided by the dHCP, we extracted the mask of the left and right insula for each subject and measured the areas of the lateral surface of the insula and of the uncovered insula. We calculated the ratio of the uncovered insula (relatively to the lateral surface), and by extension of the covered insula for each hemisphere of all subjects throughout typical brain development and prematurity. The percentage of insular coverage was modeled as a function of age and hemisphere using a linear mixed model (LMM), with subject as a random factor. To determine whether this coverage continues to evolve after birth, we compared late neonates (n = 10, 44 gw) with adults (n = 20).

Lastly, special attention was given to the paracingulate sulcus (PCGS), the most variable sulcus across individuals, to evaluate whether prematurity impacts its development and configuration. Infants were divided into four prematurity groups according to gestational age at birth and one full-term neonates/infant group to assess the PCGS configuration (presence in both hemispheres, only in the left hemisphere, only in the right hemisphere or in none). The same analysis was performed in adults, in a previous study^4.

Results

Our results indicate that all sulci are set by 34 gw, three weeks before the threshold defining full-term birth, which begins at 37 gw and extends to 41 gw. Examining the probability of sulcus emergence across the 21-44 gw window revealed a precise developmental sequence: sulci delineating lobe boundaries formed first (between 23 and 25 gw), followed by sulci separating gyri (between 26 and 28 gw), and finally sulci involved in finer gyral refinement (between 29 and 34 gw). The emergence of the sulcus limiting the PFO, the horizontal ramus of the lateral fissure (HALF), occurs rather late at 31 gw. 

Results reveal that the PFO is the final cortical region to emerge, reflecting the progressive coverage of the insula on a caudo-rostral axis. Specifically, at 21 gw, the insula is almost entirely exposed. Subject-by-subject analysis showed that the closure of the insula by the parietal and temporal opercula begins at 24 gw in the left hemisphere (LH) and 26 gw in the right hemisphere (RH), forming the parietal operculum. By 36 gw, when all sulci are established, anterior insula closure begins at the pars opercularis by pPFO, followed by aPFO at 42/43 gw. We also wondered if the ratio of insula coverage in the neonatal brain reaches adult levels, and we compared the last group of neonates (44 gw) with adult brains from the WU-Minn Human Connectome Project database. At 44 gw, PFO has not fully covered the insula with mean coverage increasing from 94% at this age to 97.5% in adults.

In this study, we also demonstrated that fetuses and preterm infants show differential brain development. We selected the 106 fetuses from the previous dataset (between 26 to 36 gw) and added 44 preterm infants scanned after birth during this same time window. Three main differences emerged between the two groups: 1) the formation of sulcal precursor is delayed by approximately one week in preterm infants; 2) the occipital lobe is more expanded in fetuses than in preterm infants; 3) we demonstrated an earlier closure of the insula in prematurity compared to typical development.

Altogether, these results reveal that the PFO is the last cortical region to be formed during brain development and is particularly sensitive to prematurity, exhibiting both delayed sulcal emergence and accelerated insular coverage by the operculum.

We also assessed the effect of prematurity on the PCGS sulcus, the most variable sulcus across individuals and the only cortical sulcus with a frequency of occurrence not approaching 100% in adults. Results reveal that at the end of a typical gestation, the PCGS is observed in at least one hemisphere in about 70% of full-term neonates, consistent with adult data. By contrast, infants born extremely preterm (before 28 gw), and especially those born at 23 and 24 gw, displayed a markedly lower presence of the PCGS after 37 gw (40-50%) and it was never observed in the right hemisphere.

Conclusion

Beyond providing a precise timeline of sulcal organization in typical (fetuses) and atypical development (prematurity), this study demonstrates that the PFO is the last cortical region to be formed during gestation. As it is also the last region to emerge in phylogeny, our findings strengthen the hypothesis of a close link between phylogenetic and ontogenetic timelines. By revealing how prematurity alters the dynamics of sulcal development and insular closure, this study underscores the need for future research linking these deviations to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.
