Introduction

Binge Eating Disorder (BED) is the most prevalent eating disorder1, yet its neural substrates remain poorly understood. BED has been substantially underdiagnosed in clinical settings, with fewer than half of affected individuals ever receiving a formal diagnosis. Relying solely on clinical records would thus introduce significant bias toward more severe or treatment-seeking cases. Structure-function coupling (SC-FC) provides a novel metric for assessing brain network organization. We investigate SC-FC coupling differences in BED using UK Biobank MRI data and assess whether group differences reflect altered structure, altered function, or a true disruption in their relationship.

Methods

Participants: We analyzed 167 UK Biobank participants (61 BED, 106 propensity score-matched controls) with complete diffusion MRI and resting-state fMRI data. BED cases were identified using a questionnaire-based approach operationalizing DSM-5 criteria from UK Biobank mental health items, capturing individuals with clinically significant binge eating who may lack formal diagnosis due to underdiagnosis. Controls were matched on age (BED: 56.3±7.8y; Control: 57.1±7.4y), sex (79%/83% female), and BMI (31.2±5.4/30.8±4.9 kg/m²). Participants with substance use disorders or other eating disorders were excluded. Brain Parcellation: We combined the Glasser HCP-MMP1.0 atlas (360 cortical parcels) with the Melbourne Subcortex Atlas Scale 4 (54 subcortical parcels), yielding 414 total parcels. Cortical parcels were assigned to 12 functional networks using the Cole-Anticevic partition. Connectivity Estimation: Structural connectivity (SC) was derived from probabilistic tractography (MRtrix3, 10M streamlines, SIFT2-weighted). Functional connectivity (FC) was computed as Pearson correlations between parcel BOLD time series, Fisher z-transformed. Both yielded 414×414 symmetric matrices. SC-FC Coupling: Whole-brain coupling was computed as the Spearman correlation between SC and FC matrix elements, restricted to edges with non-zero SC (~38% of possible edges). Network-level coupling was computed separately for within-network and between-network edges. Pathway Analysis (Primary Hypothesis): To directly test our hypothesis of reduced coupling in reward-related circuits (striatal–OFC–insula–ACC), we computed SC-FC coupling for specific inter-regional edges: NAc-OFC, NAc-vmPFC, NAc-Amygdala (reward), Insula-ACC, Insula-Amygdala (salience), Caudate-dlPFC (control), and Putamen-Premotor (habit). Supplementary regional ROI analyses examined each region's overall coupling profile. Statistical Analysis: Group differences were assessed using Welch's t-tests with Cohen's d effect sizes. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied across all analyses. Mass univariate testing compared all 414 parcels individually. Age-stratified analyses examined coupling in three tertiles (47-55y, 56-62y, 63-80y). Head motion was verified equivalent between groups (all p>0.05). 

Results

Whole-brain SC-FC coupling did not differ between groups (d=0.16, p=0.31; Figure 1A). Pathway-specific analyses revealed nominally reduced coupling in the NAc-Amygdala reward pathway (d=-0.44, p=0.025, p_FDR=0.22) and the Insula-ACC salience pathway in middle-aged participants (d=-0.63, p=0.058, p_FDR=0.52); neither survived FDR correction (Figure 1B,E). Network-level coupling showed no significant differences in the full sample (all p_FDR > 0.45). Supplementary regional ROI analyses showed no FDR-significant effects across 17 hypothesis-driven regions; the Insula showed the largest effect (d=0.33, p=0.045, p_FDR=0.27), notably in the opposite direction (BED>Control) to our hypothesis. Mass univariate testing of all 414 parcels yielded no significant effects after correction (mean d=0.03). Critically, this preserved neural coupling occurred alongside substantial psychological burden in the BED group (Figure 2A). Compared to controls, BED participants showed markedly elevated rates of lifetime depression (85% vs 60%, OR=3.7), loneliness (40% vs 12%, OR=5.0), guilty feelings (58% vs 32%, OR=2.9), and GP mental health consultations (70% vs 43%, OR=3.1) FDR-significant. Cognition was equivalent across Trail Making, Tower Rearranging, Numeric Memory, and Reaction Time tests (all p>0.1). Exploratory age-stratified analyses suggested possible developmental specificity: middle-aged BED participants (56-62y; 17 BED, 35 controls) showed elevated whole-brain coupling compared to controls (d=0.73, p=0.026; Figure 1C), an effect absent in younger (d=0.04, p=0.88) and older (d=-0.07, p=0.76) subgroups. Within this subgroup, Frontoparietal (d=0.80), Visual2 (d=0.68), and Ventral-Multimodal (d=0.67) between-network coupling showed the largest effects (Figure 1D). Parcel-level analysis revealed opposing effects in L-IP1 (d=+0.79) and R-d32 (d=-0.67), with L-IP1 coupling correlating with executive function (Figure 2B). However, given the post-hoc nature of age stratification, these findings require independent replication. 

Conclusion

Despite comprehensive testing across multiple scales of brain organization, SC-FC coupling appears largely preserved in BED. This suggests that SC-FC coupling alterations may be disorder-specific rather than universally present across psychiatric conditions2,3. The striking dissociation between preserved coupling and marked psychiatric burden indicates that BED pathophysiology may operate through alternative mechanisms4, focal circuit-specific alterations, or peripheral pathways such as gut-brain signaling. Age-specific effects observed in exploratory analyses warrant targeted investigation in independent samples.
