Introduction

Stroke recovery interplay between the location of structural injury and the brain’s capacity to reorganise functional networks over time1. Lesion volume alone explains a fraction of clinical variability, and structural disconnection maps provide an incomplete picture of the mechanisms that guide improvement or persistent impairment2,3. Disruptions to white-matter pathways alter communication within distributed systems, but the degree to which these disruptions reshape functional topology, or are compensated by network-level reorganization, remains poorly characterised.

Recent advances in lesion-network mapping and disconnectome have improved our ability to quantify structural impact, yet these approaches typically isolate the structural domain. In parallel, resting-state fMRI has revealed changes in network efficiency, integration, and modular nature that accompany recovery2. However, functional metrics are rarely integrated with subject-specific measures of structural, and the temporal evolution of this combined architecture across early, intermediate, and later recovery stages has not been systematically evaluated.

To address this gap, we developed a multimodal lesion network framework that pairs lesion load, probabilistic disconnectome estimates, and graph-theory measures derived from resting-state fMRI. This design allows us to track how structural disruption interacts with evolving functional topology and to determine which features most strongly relate to clinical outcomes at 1, 3, and 6 months after stroke. By examining peri-lesional, subcortical, and distributed cortical contributions across time, the framework captures both early local efficiency changes and later shifts toward broader network reorganization. Our goal was to test whether integrating these complementary metrics provides a more complete description of recovery trajectories and strengthens prediction of functional outcomes measured with the NIHSS and modified Rankin Scale.

Methods

Forty-nine individuals with first-ever ischemic or haemorrhagic stroke (mean age 49 ± 13 years; 42 men) were enrolled at either one or three months after onset and followed longitudinally for up to six months. A harmonized multimodal MRI protocol was implemented on two 3T systems (Philips Ingenia and Achieva), including 3D T1-weighted imaging, FLAIR and resting-state fMRI (TR = 2.0 s; 180 volumes). Scanner-specific geometric distortions and contrast differences were minimized through consistent acquisition and standardized preprocessing.

Structural preprocessing followed a lesion-aware pipeline designed in house. T1 and FLAIR weighted images were denoised, bias-field corrected, and aligned to a common subject space. Lesion masks were generated using a hybrid workflow combining LST-AI for initial lesion detection, and a modified PALS-based correction optimized for peri-lesional border fidelity4,5. All lesion masks were reviewed by two neuroradiologists. Lesion-load metrics were derived from volumetric estimates and voxel-level deviation maps.

Subject-specific disconnectome maps were computed using the BCBtoolkit6.To capture spatial gradients in peri-lesional physiology, three concentric rings (2-4 mm, 4-6 mm, 6-8 mm) were constructed around the lesion boundary. From these regions, we extracted disconnection extent, tract involvement, and peri-lesional structural load. Resting-state fMRI preprocessing was performed using fMRIPrep v23.0.2 with lesion cost-function masking to avoid distortion around lesioned areas7. Hemodynamic lag was corrected using a combined fmristroke and rapidtide approach, yielding voxelwise delay-corrected BOLD time series8. XCP-D was then applied to standardize temporal filtering, nuisance removal, and QC metrics across subjects. Graph-theory metrics were derived using CONN9 and C-PAC10,11. Whole-brain functional connectivity matrices were computed across a standardized multimodal atlas. For each subject, we quantified global efficiency, local efficiency, clustering coefficient, average path length, degree centrality, and lesion-region specific connectivity indices. All features were z-scored to enable integration across modalities.

To evaluate predictive value, structural and functional metrics were combined with demographic variables (age, sex, lesion type) and clinical severity scores. Clinical outcomes included the Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 1, 3, and 6 months. Two regression models were implemented for each outcome: a full model that incorporated admission NIHSS (NIHSS_adm) and a reduced model designed to predict recovery without early clinical data as shown in figure for NIHSS and mRS Clinical scales. Graph theory measures are included to equation in addition to estimate the effect of model improvement.

For NIHSS at time t (NIHSS_t), the full model took the form:

NIHSS_t = β₀ + β₁·Age + β₂·disc_NRC + β₃·disc_total + β₄·LesionLoad + β₅·NIHSS_adm +GT + ε.

The reduced model excluded the baseline severity term:

NIHSS_t = β₀ + β₁·Age + β₂·disc_NRC + β₃·disc_total + β₄·LesionLoad +GT + ε.

Here, disc_NRC denotes the number of disconnected regions derived from thresholded disconnectome maps, disc_total represents total disconnection load across all tracts, and Lesion Load corresponds to the volumetric lesion burden. This dual-model structure allowed us to test whether multimodal neuroimaging features retained predictive value even when clinical severity at presentation was unavailable a scenario common in delayed referral settings.

Model performance was quantified using R², ΔR² after the inclusion of functional metrics, and information-criterion change (AIC/BIC). Feature importance profiles were used to track how the relative contributions of lesion, disconnection, and functional-network metrics evolved over the recovery timeline

Results

Forty-nine patients imaging data contributed to the multimodal framework, out of which sixteen patient completed all three imaging timepoints and the remainder providing early-late or mid–late pairs. The lesion–disconnectome model alone explained a substantial proportion of clinical variability, yielding R² values of ~0.58–0.61 at 1 month and ~0.91–0.92 at 3–6 months. Adding graph-theory metrics provided a consistent ΔR² of 0.03–0.05 and lowered AIC/BIC values, demonstrating that functional topology contributed predictive information beyond structural damage.

Predictive features displayed a clear temporal evolution. At 1 month, the strongest correlates of NIHSS were derived from the 6–8 mm peri-lesional ring (“ring 8”), including degree (ρ ≈ 0.44), cost (ρ ≈ 0.44), and global efficiency (ρ ≈ 0.38) of core functional networks. major contribution to increase in R Value were dominated by peri-lesional efficiency and short-range structural-functional interactions. These results indicate that early recovery is shaped primarily by local network disruption and immediate compensatory efficiency near the lesion.

At 3 months, predictive contributors broadened. Ring 2 features increased in prominence, alongside subcortical hubs such as the Putamen and Accumbens. Average path length and global efficiency extracted from disconnected regions also emerged as influential. network interaction shift toward mid-level integration was observed, suggesting that structural disconnection and subcortical routing play a larger role during the peak reorganisation period.

By 6 months, the magnitude of correlations was lower (ρ ≈ 0.15-0.22), yet the spatial distribution of influential features shifted toward higher-order cortical and integrative networks. Parietal association cortex, thalamic nuclei, and bilateral insular regions appeared repeatedly in the top predictors, particularly through clustering coefficients and global-efficiency variations. feature impotence moved from peri-lesional dependence to distributed network compensation feature in comparison to 1M metrics.

A cumulative analysis across all timepoints demonstrated both stable and time-specific predictors. such as disconnection-derived path-length indices, ring-based cost metrics, and efficiency measures from insular and parietal nodes remain influential throughout the recovery trajectory, whereas others are restricted to early or late phases. 

Model validation using mRS confirmed robustness across outcomes. Even without admission NIHSS, predictive accuracy remained strong (R² ≈ 0.53-0.55), demonstrating that multimodal imaging features provide reliable estimates of recovery in settings where early clinical scores are missing.

Together, the results reveal a reproducible temporal migration of predictors: early peri-lesional efficiency → mid-phase subcortical integration → late distributed cortical reorganization. 

Overall, the results outline a coherent temporal pattern in how structural disconnection and functional network properties contribute to recovery across early, intermediate, and late phases.

Conclusion

The longitudinal patterns observed in this study indicate that stroke recovery is governed by a shifting balance between structural disconnection and functional network reorganization. Early after injury, predictive features were concentrated within peri-lesional zones, particularly in efficiency and degree-based metrics derived from the outer lesion rings. Earliest determinants of clinical status reflect residual local connectivity and the capacity of tissue immediately surrounding the lesion to maintain short-range communication. Such peri-lesional efficiency changes align with contemporary models of early plasticity, where local circuit stabilisation precedes broader network engagement.

By three months, the predictive landscape broadened toward subcortical hubs, Putamen and Accumbens, alongside tract-specific disconnection metrics. This stage captured the period where widespread structural disconnection and functional redistribution interface most strongly. The prominence of average path length and global efficiency in structurally compromised networks suggests that mid-phase recovery depends on the brain’s ability to reconfigure communication pathways beyond the immediate vicinity of the lesion. These findings align with network neuroscience frameworks in which subcortical structures act as intermediaries that redistribute load during structural perturbation.

At six months, predictive contributions shifted again, toward distributed cortical and integrative regions. Parietal association cortex, thalamic nuclei, and bilateral insula featured prominently across the strongest correlates, reflecting a transition to higher-order compensation and large-scale functional stabilisation. Decreasing correlation magnitudes but increasing spatial dispersion of relevant features suggest that the later stages of recovery rely less on focal mechanisms and more on coordinated adaptation across multimodal networks. This temporal migration from local efficiency to subcortical routing to distributed cortical integration provides a mechanistic scaffold for interpreting long-term recovery trajectories.

A cumulative analysis further demonstrated that some predictors remained consistently influential such as disconnection-derived path-length indices and key efficiency measures while others were time-restricted. This combination of stable and dynamic features suggests that stroke recovery is not governed by a single biomarker but by evolving interactions that unfold over months. The strong performance in predictive model, which excluded admission NIHSS, emphasizes that multimodal imaging alone can provide clinically meaningful prognostic information when early clinical scores are unavailable.

Taken together, the results highlight the value of integrating structural and functional measures into a unified modelling framework. Structural indices captured the downstream propagation of white-matter injury, while graph-theory metrics quantified the dynamic adjustments in network topology that accompany recovery. 

This study demonstrates that combining lesion burden, tract-specific disconnection, and resting-state network topology yields a robust, temporally sensitive model of post-stroke recovery1,3. The framework uncovers a reproducible sequence of predictive transitions from peri-lesional efficiency early on, to subcortical integration at mid-phase, and finally to distributed cortical compensation in later stages. Importantly, the multimodal models maintained strong predictive performance even without clinical scores, underscoring their translational relevance for settings where early assessments are limited. By unifying optimised lesion correction, disconnectome mapping, lag-adjusted fMRI processing, and graph-theory analysis, this work offers a comprehensive platform for probing neurovascular interactions and advancing individualised recovery modelling after stroke1,2,3,12.
