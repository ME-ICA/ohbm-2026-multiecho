Introduction

Visual misperception and face pareidolia are prevalent non-motor symptoms in Parkinson’s disease (PD), often predicting cognitive decline and psychosis. Current theories propose that these abnormal visual illusions arise from degraded sensory input and excessive top-down predictive processing from frontoparietal attention networks to visual cortices. However, the specific neural circuits underlying this dysfunction in visual perception remain elusive. Utilizing submillimeter-resolution fMRI at 7 Tesla, this study investigated layer-specific activity in dynamic face processing areas, in particular the superior temporal sulcus (STS), in early PD patients and healthy controls during ambiguous face perception. Functions of the early visual pathways were assessed with low-contrast achromatic (magnocellular, or M biased) and isoluminant chromatic (parvocellular, or P biased) stimuli. 

Methods

Seventeen patients with early-stage Parkinson’s disease (PD; mean age = 55.1 ± 11 years) and twelve age-matched healthy controls (HC; mean age = 56.8 ± 7 years) were recruited. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no neurological or psychiatric disorders other than PD.


Contrast sensitivity of the M and P pathways was measured using Two-Alternative Forced Choice (2-AFC) contrast detection of sinusoidal gratings (0.5 cycles/degree) presented at two different types of contrast: an achromatic grating biased to the M pathway and an isoluminant chromatic grating biased to the P-pathway (Figure 1A). The contrast threshold for each stimulus was estimated using two randomly interleaved staircases (60 trials each). To assess their perceptual recognition ability, participants completed a binary video discrimination task (Figure 1B). They first watched two 2-min movies in original contrast, and then judged which movie each binary video clips belong to. A total of 100 trials were collected for isoluminant chromatic or low-contrast achromatic binary video clips.


During 7T fMRI scans, participants performed a face detect task while watching 1-min movie clips presented in the original color (Original), red/green isoluminant binary contrast (P binary), achromatic binary contrast (M binary), or phase scrambled binary contrast (P scrambled and M scrambled) (Figure 1C). Before MRI scans, they viewed two 1-minute original movie clips to establish a strong perceptual prior. The fMRI paradigm consisted of two runs, each consists of multiple 60-second video blocks, separated by 18-second baseline period of uniform gray. Participants were instructed to press a button when they perceived faces. A dynamic face localizer was used to localize face-selective regions (Figure 1D).


 Data were acquired on a 7T SIEMENS Magnetom scanner with a 32-channel receive and 1-channel transmit coil. Functional images were obtained using a Dual-Polarity EPI sequence (0.85-mm isotropic voxels, FOV = 180 × 180 mm, TR = 3000 ms, TE = 22 ms, flip angle = 20°, bandwidth = 1082 Hz/Px, phase encoding: anterior→posterior) with window averaging to suppress odd–even echo phase errors, reducing low-frequency artifacts and improving tSNR and functional sensitivity. T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired using an MP2RAGE sequence (0.7-mm isotropic voxels, FOV = 224 × 224 mm, 256 sagittal slices, TE = 3.02 ms, TR = 4000 ms, TI1 = 750 ms, TI2 = 2500 ms, flip angles = 5°/4°, bandwidth = 240 Hz/Px, phase partial Fourier = 7/8, slice partial Fourier = 6/8, GRAPPA = 3).

Data were processed using AFNI/SUMA, FreeSurfer, mripy, and custom MATLAB scripts. Region-of-interest (ROI) analyses focused on face-processing areas, including the superior temporal sulcus (STS), fusiform face area (FFA), and occipital face area (OFA), defined by the dynamic face localizer (Figure 1D). In the laminar analysis, gray-matter volumes were divided into three equi-volume depths. Whole-brain surface-based analyses were conducted to examine response differences between the PD and HC groups under each stimulus condition. 

Results

Behaviorally, compared to healthy controls, PD patients exhibited no significant difference in contrast sensitivity (Figure 2A) or the discrimination performance of binary videos (Figure 2B). However, in the face-detection task, PD patients adopted a more liberal decision criterion, producing higher "face" response rates (Figure 2C). 

In the ROI analysis, PD patients showed significantly stronger responses to phase scrambled stimuli in STS compared to the control group (Figure 2D, MS: p = 0.015; PS: p = 0.046). Laminar analysis revealed that this effect was significant in the superficial and deep cortical depths (Figure 2E, Sup: p = 0.0251; Mid: p = 0.0338; Deep: p = 0.0667), consistent with feedback from higher cortex. In support of this, whole-brain surfaced analysis found enhanced activity in the inferior frontal cortex (IFC) in PD compared to HC (Figure 2F), suggesting feedback signals from the frontoparietal attention network. Figure 2G shows the whole-brain results for all stimulus conditions. In the binary structured video conditions (PB and MB), PD group exhibited reduced activation in the frontoparietal cortex (IFC, superior parietal lobe, SPL) and inferior temporal cortex including the lateral occipital complex (LOC), parahippocampal place area (PPA), and fusiform face area (FFA).

Conclusion

Using 7T fMRI, our findings provide mesoscale, layer-specific functional evidence for the neural mechanisms underlying abnormal visual illusions in PD. In the phase-scrambled condition, enhanced activity in the deep and superficial layers of the superior temporal sulcus (STS), along with increased activation in the inferior frontal cortex (IFC), suggests aberrant hyperactivity of predictive feedback from frontoparietal regions to the visual cortex. In contrast, in the structured binary contrast condition, reduced activity in the IFC and inferior temporal cortex (IT) indicates degraded top-down priors originating from higher-order cortical areas.
