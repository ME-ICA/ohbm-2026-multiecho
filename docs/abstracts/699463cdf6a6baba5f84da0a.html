Introduction

Adolescence is a critical period for brain development, as brain function is significantly altered from pre- to post-adolescence. The trajectory of adolescent brain development has far-reaching implications on final adult brain topology and behaviour. Sex appears to be a particularly important influence on adolescent brain development. Subtle yet significant sex differences in neurodevelopmental trajectories emerge during puberty and persist into adulthood. 

To disentangle the complex effects of age and sex on brain development, a longitudinal model is essential. Here, we used a longitudinal rat model, which has a number of methodological advantages over human neuroimaging studies. Rodent development occurs over experimentally tractable timescales - rodents reach adulthood in less than three months, allowing for quicker, easier, and low cost collection of longitudinal data points. Furthermore, rodent models allow for carefully controlled conditions, thus enabling causal inferences. Finally, as functional brain networks and their features are largely conserved cross-species, there is a strong analogy between the networks identified using resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI; the methodology used in this dataset) approaches in humans and in rats. 

In this exploratory study, we aimed to accurately characterise sex differences in the developmental trajectories of functional network topology, from pre-puberty to early adulthood. Using graph theory metrics to quantify the architecture and organisation of functional networks, we examined age-related changes in network topology in 36 Wistar rats at 5 timepoints across adolescence, with a particular focus on the effects of age and sex. 

Methods

Eighteen male and 18 female Wistar Han rats were weaned on postnatal day 21. Animals were housed in pairs and handled daily. Each cage had nesting material, a red polycarbonate tunnel and a red tall rat loft. Animals were kept in climate‐controlled rooms set to 22°C (±2°C) with relative humidity levels of 50%. Experimenters (two males) handled and carried out all procedures on the rats, while animal care staff were both male and female. 

Animals were handled daily, with puberty onset tracked, and the oestrous cycle tracked in females once puberty was reached via daily vaginal swab. Puberty onset was recorded as the day of vaginal opening in females and balanopreputial separation in males. Oestrous smears were stained using Wrights-modified stain and imaged at 10x and 20x using a light microscope.

A Bruker 7T BioSpec 70/30 scanner equipped with a 1H receive-only 8 x 1 rat surface array coil, 72mm transmit coil, BGA9S gradients (675mT/m) and Paravision 7 software was used for all imaging sessions. Animals underwent five MRI scans at approximately postnatal day 28, 35, 49, 70 and 91. Scans were acquired over four waves of longitudinal data collection, with 4 to 12 animals per wave. 

The StandardRat protocol^1 was followed for both anaesthesia/sedation and MRI scanning parameters (see Figure 1). Anaesthesia induction was carried out using 4% isoflurane in 1000ml/min oxygen and 400ml/min nitrogen. This was followed by a bolus of medetomidine (0.05mg/kg, s.c.). Animals were maintained at 0.4% isoflurane in 200ml/min oxygen and 400ml/min nitrogen, with a continuous infusion of medetomidine (0.1 mg/kg/h, s.c.) starting 15 minutes after the bolus. The EPI scan began 40 minutes after the bolus of medetomidine had been delivered. Isoflurane concentration was set reduced to 0.4% at least 10 minutes before starting the EPI sequence. Once scanning was complete, the continuous infusion was stopped and atipamezole (0.5mg/kg, s.c.) was delivered to reverse the effects of medetomidine. Respiration and temperature were monitored throughout the scan using a respiratory pillow and rectal probe, with body temperature maintained at 37°C using a water heating system connected to the MRI cradle. For the anatomical scan, a T2w turboRARE sequence was used with repetition time (TR) = 2500ms, echo time (TE) = 30ms, RARE factor = 8, 18 interleaved axial slices of 1 mm with 0.1mm gap, FOV = 25.6mm x 25.6mm, and matrix size of 128 x 128. For the functional scan, a gradient echo-echo planar sequence was used with the following parameters: TR = 1000ms, TE = 17ms, 18 interleaved axial slices of 1 mm with 0.1mm gap, FOV = 25.6mm x 25.6mm, and matrix size of 64 x 64.

Preprocessing was carried out using the open-source RABIES software^2. EPI scans were corrected for inhomogeneities then registered to the population average template using a non-linear registration. Confound correction was then carried out on the native space EPI timeseries and included voxelwise detrending, bandpass filtering (0.01-0.1Hz) and removal of 30 seconds from either end of the timeseries. Next, six rigid motion parameters and the global signal were regressed from the data. Finally, a spatial Gaussian smoothing filter was applied at 0.5mm full-width at half maximum. The output from this was then registered to the common space population average.

Graph metrics were obtained using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (https://sites.google.com/site/bctnet). Only positive and weighted connections between regions were included. All matrices were thresholded to include only the top 75% of connections. Graph theory metrics assessed were: clustering coefficient, betweenness centrality, density, modularity, and global efficiency. 

Linear mixed effects models were used to examine how each graph theory metric changed as a function of both age and sex. 

Results

A total of 179 functional scans were collected: 1 was excluded due to excess motion, 7 were excluded due to data loss/preprocessing issues. 

For clustering coefficient, there was no significant effect of age or sex. However, there was a significant age X sex interaction (p < .001). 

For betweenness centrality, there was no significant effect of sex. However, there was an effect of age (p < .001). 

For density, there was no significant effect of sex. However, there was an effect of age (p < .001), and a signifigant age X interaction (p < .027). 

For global efficiency, there was no significant effect of age or sex. However, there was a significant age X sex interaction (p < .014). 

For modularity, there was no significant effect of sex. However, there was effect of age (p < .001).

Conclusion

We examined changes in network topology in 36 Wistar rats at 5 timepoints across adolescence, with a particular focus on the effects of age and sex. 

A number of interesting trends were uncovered. For the effect of age, increases in modularity and betweenness centrality were observed across time, along with decreases in density. These changes are consistent with a move towards an adult-like topology. Clustering coefficient and efficiency did not change across time, suggesting that these topological features may be largely consolidated before adolescence. 

Interestingly, age X sex interactions appear to emerge around postnatal day 49, which is the scanning timepoint at which both males and females have reached puberty. This supports the suggestion that puberty reorganises functional brain networks. 

However, there were no main effects of sex for any metric, suggesting that these sex differences are subtle and emerge only in interaction with age, rather than representing fixed differences across the lifespan. 

Further analyses shall be performed with more sophisticated non-linear models to better characterise metric trajectories. Data from a future stressed cohort of rats will undergo same analyses.
