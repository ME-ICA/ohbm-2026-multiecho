Introduction

Language lateralization constitutes a fundamental and defining organizational principle of the human brain. However, establishing a stable, individual-specific, and trait-like measure of hemispheric dominance, and comprehensively delineating its neural architecture, remains a significant and unresolved challenge in cognitive neuroscience. Traditional laterality research has typically relied on assessing hemispheric asymmetry using a single, isolated task or focusing exclusively on a narrowly defined region of interest (ROI)1. This methodology inherently yields only a fragmented and modality-specific view, failing to capture the dynamic and integrated complexity of the extended language system as a whole.

Language processing is intrinsically multi-scale and multimodal. Its operations cover a broad spectrum of neural organization, ranging from the specific processing of peripheral sensory-motor pathways that transduce inputs and outputs2, 3, through specialized semantic regions responsible for abstract conceptual representations across sensory modes4, up to large-scale, distributed cortical networks consistently engaged in complex combinatorial and syntactic processing5. Given the profound functional and computational differences inherent across these various spatial scales, it is highly plausible that lateralization patterns, despite sharing a foundational origin, exhibit partially independent variation. This variability suggests that laterality measures derived from a single context or region are simply inadequate for providing a reliable and stable characterization of an individual’s overall hemispheric dominance for language. This necessitates a critical investigation: Can a specific neural scale or network be identified that yields a robust representation of language lateralization that maintains high consistency across diverse modalities, including listening, speaking, and reading?

Methods

One hundred and twenty left-handed adults (mean age = 22.47 years, SD = 3.26; 85 females) participated, with handedness assessed by the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory. Participants completed three distinct language tasks during 3T fMRI scanning: covert speaking, visual reading, and passive listening, with activity modeled using a general linear model. Language lateralization was analyzed at three spatial scales: (1) Task-Specific ROIs (Broca's area, VWFA, AWFA)1, 2, 3; (2) a Semantic Network (SN) comprising bilateral ATL, pMTG, and AG4 and (3) a Large-Scale Language Network (LN) defined using the SN220 language-selective mask5 (Fig. 1). Laterality indices (LIs) were computed from surface-based activation maps using an area-weighted vertex-based measure. To evaluate stability, we performed pairwise correlations of LIs across spatial levels and across tasks (within levels). Cross-task reliability was quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC[3,1]). Participants were classified into subgroups (left-lateralized, right-lateralized, mixed) based on the consistency of their LN-derived LIs across the three tasks. Finally, participants completed three independent behavioral tasks outside the scanner (face recognition, line bisection, and dichotic listening) to examine behavioral correlates of LN-based lateralization groups.

Results

Within individual language tasks, LIs from task-specific ROIs, the semantic network (SN), and the large-scale language network (LN) showed significant positive correlations (Fig. 2A). For listening and reading, correlations between SN and LN were strongest (r = 0.73 - 0.78), indicating tight coupling at the network level. In contrast, for speaking, the strongest association was observed between Broca-based and LN-based LIs (r = 0.94), consistent with the central role of frontal language regions in speech production. Overall, ROI-based LIs showed moderate coupling with network-level measures, with the specific pattern depending on task demands.  Critically, cross-task generalization varied substantially across spatial levels. At the task-ROI level, cross-task correlations were heterogeneous and often weak (e.g., speaking-reading: r = 0.53; reading-listening: r = 0.21), indicating strong modality dependence. In stark contrast, LN-based lateralization consistently exhibited the strongest and most uniform cross-task consistency across all paired modalities (r = 0.74-0.86), strongly supporting the hypothesis of a shared, stable lateralization axis captured at the large-scale network level (Fig. 2B).

This finding was confirmed by reliability analyses: cross-task reliability was lowest at the task-ROI level (ICC = 0.38), increased substantially at the SN level (ICC = 0.64), and reached its highest value at the LN level (ICC = 0.79), indicating that LN-based LIs provide the most stable measure of individual differences in language organization (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the LN-based lateralization subgroups (consistently left-lateralized, right-lateralized, and mixed) differed significantly on independent behavioral measures sensitive to hemispheric bias (Fig. 2D). Specifically, significant group effects were observed for reaction-time asymmetry in the face recognition task and ear-advantage indices in the dichotic listening task, highlighting the behavioral relevance of the stable LN-level lateralization measure.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate a clear pattern of increased stability across spatial scales in language lateralization. While modality-specific regions exhibit variability dependent on the task, lateralization quantified at the level of the large-scale language network is highly robust across listening, speaking, and reading, showing superior individual-level reliability and supporting a shared underlying neural organization. These results identify language-network–level lateralization as a stable, trait-like neural marker of individual differences in hemispheric organization, highlighting the critical importance of adopting a multi-scale, network-based approach for deriving reliable measures of hemispheric dominance in clinical and research settings.
