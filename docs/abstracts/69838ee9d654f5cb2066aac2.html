Introduction

In fMRI studies, hemodynamics is typically modeled by the combination of neural activities as primary signal and other physiological changes as noise. However, non-neurogenic dynamical characteristics of the cerebral vasculature can reveal potential biomarkers for diseases with vascular, metabolic, or glial etiologies, such as Alzheimer’s disease. To that end, ultra-high-field fMRI is a powerful tool for non-invasively investigating such hemodynamic processes in the human brain at the laminar level. Efforts to understand them, such as slow vasomotion at 0.1 Hz, have mostly been limited to animal research. Recent attempts to identify slow dynamics in humans at high resolutions have been successful^1; however, their exact spatial and spectral distributions, which are important to precise localization and profiling of vascular aberrations, remain understudied. To better understand these dynamics at the layer level in human subjects, we extracted spatiotemporal patterns of laminar fMRI that are stable across different experimental conditions and uncoupled from task variables. Using primary visual cortex, V1, as a model area and informed by insights from animal research, we hypothesized that these slow signals are spatially smooth, distributed across spatial scales, temporally stable, and differentially manifested across cortical layers.^1^2 To investigate spatial scale characteristics, we employed geometric analysis using eigenmodes derived from the Laplace-Beltrami Operator (LBO) on the V1 surface.^3 To extract stable oscillations and infer each layer’s contribution to these dynamics, we used Hankel’s Alternative View of Koopman (HAVOK) analysis.^4 Finally, to infer both macro- and micro-vascular origins of the hemodynamics, we compared three fMRI contrasts: gradient-echo (GE) BOLD (Blood Oxygenation Level Dependent), spin-echo (SE) BOLD, and vascular space occupancy (VASO).

Methods

7T fMRI data were acquired from five healthy participants. Three single-shot sequences were used, with 0.8 mm isotropic resolution and an oblique-coronal slab covering the early visual cortex: 1) GE-BOLD (TR = 3000 ms, TE = 24 ms); 2) SE-BOLD (TR = 3000 ms, TE = 38 ms); and 3) SS-SI VASO (effective TR = 5000 ms, TE = 25 ms). The paradigm included a 10-minute resting state GE-BOLD acquisition and 20 GE-BOLD, SE-BOLD, and VASO runs of the ocular dominance column block-design task. Each of 8 stimulus blocks, stimulating each eye separately, lasted 30 seconds with pseudorandomized ordering. Following standard preprocessing, geometric distortions were resolved using the Gradient-Based Boundary method to align surfaces with functional borders. Time series were sampled onto each of 11 equidistant surfaces across the cortical depth. Respiration and heartbeat traces were detrended, convolved with their respective physiological response functions, and downsampled to match the fMRI repetition times. Anonymized data and a more detailed description of the data, acquisition techniques, experimental setup, and data preprocessing are publicly accessible.^5

For spatial scale analysis, we solved the discretized Laplace-Beltrami Operator (LBO) on the V1 mid-cortical mesh, restricted to vertices sampled within the acquisition volume across all functional contrasts and depths. The resulting eigenmodes were filtered to represent distinct spatial scales ranging from the V1 extent down to 2 mm (Figure 1a). Laminar time series, stacked across 11 layers to form a multivariate state vector, were projected onto each LBO mode separately and z-scored. We constructed time-delay coordinates with a ~60s sliding window, covering 2 stimulus blocks. The delayed embeddings were factorized via Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), truncated by hard singular value thresholds optimized for the asymptotic Mean Squared Error (MSE), and analyzed with HAVOK (Figure 1b). Each HAVOK model, composed of the linear attractor matrix A and intermittent force B, was fitted independently for each subject, run, hemisphere, and spatial mode according to Figure 1c. The matrix A was eigendecomposed, yielding eigenvectors representing the oscillatory components and the associated eigenvalues indicating their frequencies. Time series of respiration volume per time (RVT) and heart beats per minute (BPM) were similarly analyzed by HAVOK.

Results

Low-frequency physiological coupling across spatial scales

We quantified the relationship between the low-dimensional embeddings of RVT and BPM and the laminar fMRI ones using Canonical Correlation Analysis. We found significant coupling (Pillai’s trace, p<0.05, permutation testing, FDR-corrected) across all contrasts, experimental conditions, and hemispheres. This suggests that even after optimizing for asymptotic MSE via truncated SVD, fMRI signals across spatial scales remain heavily influenced by slow physiological dynamics (Figure 2a).

Depth-dependent frequency gradients

We observed a distinct laminar dissociation within the 0-0.16 Hz range: superficial layers exhibited stronger slow oscillations, while fast oscillations were stronger at deeper layers. To quantify this, we generated 2D power spectra by binning the Koopman mode powers of the 11 layers according to frequencies derived from their HAVOK eigenvalues. Testing the relationship between cortical depth and frequency stability (2D Pearson correlation) revealed a significant negative correlation (depth vs. frequency) across numerous LBO modes (p<0.05, permutation testing, FDR-corrected). This effect showed no bias toward coarse or fine spatial scales. Among acquisition contrasts, GE-BOLD exhibited the highest number of significant LBO modes and the strongest mean effect size, while SE-BOLD and VASO demonstrated sparser significant modes and weaker effects (Figure 2b, 2c).

Control analyses

Pearson’s correlations between dominance changes in the visual input and the intermittent forcing term of the HAVOK models yielded no significant results, suggesting the identified dynamics are uncoupled from the task events. The observed depth-frequency correlation did not covary with the power of the ODC block frequency (0.033 Hz) at each LBO mode, surface curvature, B0 field alignment, or cortical thickness variability. This confirms that the laminar frequency gradients reflect intrinsic spectral signatures rather than task-evoked, geometric, or susceptibility-induced confounds.

Conclusion

We identified a depth-dependent spectral profile of cerebral hemodynamics that manifests across spatial scales and persists across experimental conditions. Based on the statistical evidence found across different fMRI contrasts with differential sensitivities along the vascular hierarchy, this observation cannot be explained solely by noise: it follows a low-dimensional dynamical manifold aligning with slow physiological signals and known hemodynamics of the neurovascular unit in animal research. We established a framework to investigate such characteristics non-invasively in humans, combining ultra-high-field fMRI with LBO eigendecomposition and Koopman operators. This offers a window into human neurovascular health, thereby extending our understanding of healthy hemodynamics beyond animal models and providing a baseline for identifying vascular aberrations in clinical populations.
