Introduction

Around 40% of females with epilepsy experience cyclical seizure exacerbations linked to menstrual phases, a pattern termed catamenial epilepsy, which is frequently treatment-resistant1. Fluctuations in ovarian neurosteroids across the menstrual cycle modulate cortical excitability, with oestradiol broadly enhancing glutamatergic excitation and progesterone, via its neuroactive metabolite allopregnanolone, enhancing GABAergic inhibition2,3. The most common catamenial epilepsy subtype, perimenstrual catamenial epilepsy, is characterised by a two-fold rise in seizure frequency around menstruation3. The leading mechanistic hypothesis proposes that PCE reflects a withdrawal-like state precipitated by the rapid premenstrual drop in allopregnanolone after sustained luteal-phase exposure3. To test changes in excitation and inhibition balance over the menstrual cycle, we combine hormonal assays with electroencephalography (EEG) measures: (1) visually induced gamma oscillations, which provide a sensitive index of GABAA receptor–mediated inhibition, and (2) long-term potentiation (LTP), an NMDA receptor–dependent form of synaptic plasticity that reflects glutamatergic excitatory drive. Together, these measures allow characterisation of menstrual cycle–related changes in both inhibitory and excitatory dynamics.

Methods

This study employed a within-subject, repeated-measures, counterbalanced observational design. Twenty females with epilepsy and twenty-five age-matched females without epilepsy (controls) completed three EEG sessions timed to specific menstrual cycle phases: perimenstrual (days −3 to +2), mid-follicular (days 5–8), and mid-luteal (days −9 to −5), with day 1 defined as the first day of menstrual bleeding. Ovulation status and session timing were confirmed using urinary ovulation tests and blood hormone assays. At each session, serum blood samples were collected to quantify progesterone, oestradiol, and allopregnanolone, and EEG was recorded using a 64-channel system while participants performed two simple visual tasks. Visual LTP was induced using repetitive low-frequency visual stimulation administered before and after a high-frequency stimulation (tetanus) and quantified as changes in visual evoked potential amplitude from baseline to early and late post-tetanus time blocks. Data was pre-processed using Fieldtrip and analysed using SPM12 in MATLAB.

Visually induced gamma oscillations were elicited using a visual stimulation task comprising four blocks of 84 trials, in which high-contrast (90%) black-and-white annular gratings subtending 16° of visual angle were presented centrally on a computer screen.  Data were pre-processed using Fieldtrip in MATLAB. Source-level gamma activity was estimated using a linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer applied to data band-pass filtered between 30 and 90 Hz. Beamformer output was projected into Montreal Neurological Institute space, and the voxel exhibiting peak gamma power was identified for each participant and session. This coordinate was used to construct a virtual sensor, from which peak gamma frequency and power were extracted for subsequent analyses using SPSS. 

Results

There were no significant differences in any of the measured hormones between cohorts (all ps > 0.5). However, there was a significant effect of menstrual cycle phase on visual LTP in both cohorts, specifically on the potentiation of the P2 component of the visually evoked potential in the late post-tetanus block. In controls, P2 potentiation was significantly greater in the mid-follicular phase compared with the perimenstrual phase (t(144) = 4.72, p = 0.001, FWE-corrected; Fig. 1A). In contrast, females with epilepsy showed significantly greater P2 potentiation in the perimenstrual phase than in both the mid-follicular (t(108) = 4.58, p = 0.002, FWE-corrected) and mid-luteal phases (t(108) = 4.45, p = 0.003, FWE-corrected; Fig. 1A).

For visually induced gamma oscillations, controls exhibited a significantly lower mean peak gamma frequency in the perimenstrual phase compared with the mid-follicular phase (Δ = 3.59 Hz, SE = 1.63, p = 0.034, uncorrected; Fig. 1B). In contrast, females with epilepsy showed no significant cycle-related variation in peak gamma frequency (all ps > 0.5; Fig. 1B).

Conclusion

Despite comparable hormone concentrations between cohorts, females with and without epilepsy exhibited distinct neural responses to the visual EEG tasks, emphasising the critical role of receptor-level dynamics in menstrual cycle–related neural modulation. Nearly all epilepsy participants (14/17) exhibited LTP patterns that diverged sharply from controls, underscoring the robustness of our findings. Given that LTP is an NMDA-mediated process4, these results suggest that females with epilepsy exhibit altered glutamatergic dynamics across the menstrual cycle, with a breakthrough of excitation, presumable NMDA-mediated, during the perimenstrual phase. Remarkably, this perimenstrual surge in excitation appeared across all females with epilepsy, regardless of catamenial epilepsy subtype, indicating a broader epilepsy-related physiological mechanism rather than one specific to catamenial epilepsy. 

Visual gamma peak frequency can be related to inhibitory GABAergic, and excitatory glutamatergic changes5. In controls, the reduction in peak gamma frequency during the perimenstrual phase aligns with the expected reduction in GABAergic inhibition during this phase following allopregnanolone withdrawal3. In contrast, cycle-related changes in peak gamma frequency were not detected in females with epilepsy, potentially due to a masking effect of GABAergic antiseizure medications, altered GABAA receptor function or other dysregulation of excitation-inhibition balance. Overall, these findings reveal aberrant menstrual cycle modulation of glutamatergic excitation and GABAergic inhibition in females with epilepsy, suggesting relative glutamatergic excitation dominance during the mid-luteal and perimenstrual phases. While GABAergic mechanisms are often emphasised in catamenial epilepsy research and management, our results highlight the potential contribution of NMDA-mediated excitatory processes.
