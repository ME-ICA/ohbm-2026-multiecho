Introduction

Brain stimulation like direct electrical stimulation triggers brain activity which can propagate through large-scale white-matter pathways shaped by the structural connectomes. To date, existing predictive network-level models such as the neural mass model and network communication models (graph theory models) face challenges in either lacking spatial resolution and requiring heavy parameterisation ,or not capturing temporal dynamic patterns for the propagation process.^1, 2 Here, we introduce a Resistor–Capacitor (RC) circuit model of the human connectome that predicts the direct electrical stimulation signal propagation measured by stereo electroencephalography (SEEG).

Methods

Each brain region was modelled as one capacitor weighted by the node size for that region, while white-matter tracts between regions were modelled as resistors weighted by structural connectivity strength and the fibre tract length. Our Structural Connectome (SC) and Euclidean Distance (ED) matrices are based on the group-average connectome from the data using the Human Connectome Project (HCP) and contain 360 cortical regions and 14 subcortical structures. ^3 For each stimulation site, we extracted the RC model predicted maximal nodal net current and compared it against empirical SEEG amplitude and response-probability matrices from the F-TRACT project using Spearman correlations across valid region pairs and indirect pathways. ^4 To compare the model performance, we also evaluated a suite of established network communication models in our correlation process – including diffusion efficiency, search information, communicability, navigation efficiency and shortest-path efficiency. ^5

Results

The RC model reproduced key propagation patterns observed in SEEG recordings. Predicted maximum current maps showed significant correspondence in the key region pairs (sufficient stimulation-response events being recorded) with empirical probability (r=0.59, p<0.001) and amplitude (r=0.44, p<0.001).  Also RC model can capture the propagation patterns along indirect pathways, yielding robust correlations for the indirectly connected nodes (both in probability (r = 0.37, p < 0.001) and amplitude (r = 0.44, p < 0.001)). Across all comparisons, our RC model outperformed several benchmark graph-theoretic communication models. And our model also captures the indirectly connected node-pair time response parameters (the time for the stimulation propagation) and has a strong correlation (r = 0.68, p < 0.001) with the empirical time delay-to-peak records by F-TRACT.

Conclusion

A simple biophysically grounded RC network can capture large-scale network of direct electrical stimulation propagation. Our RC model can handle both a high spatial and temporal resolution for the propagation process in the structural connectome network. This framework provides a mechanistic foundation for predicting electrical stimulation signal propagation and the SEEG responses across the human brain, which can support future applications in personalised stimulation target optimisation.
