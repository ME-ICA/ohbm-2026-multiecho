Introduction

Thermoregulation has been traditionally understood through the lens of the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the ANS, which operate through segregated peripheral pathways. This anatomical separation has long supported the assumption that central thermoregulatory control is likewise localized within distinct brain regions. Classic rodent work has reinforced this view by highlighting two areas - the brainstem, a key autonomic integrator involved in temperature regulation, and the insula, an interoceptive hub representing bodily states - as putative “sympathetic” and “parasympathetic” centers. However, emerging human neuroimaging evidence challenges this modular framework. Recent network-based analyses suggest that autonomic regulation is not confined to isolated nodes, but instead arises from dynamic interactions across distributed cortical and subcortical systems. This perspective reframes human thermoregulation as an emergent property of flexible, state-dependent connectivity patterns rather than static activation of specialized regions. Motivated by this framework, we examined how functional connectivity between canonical thermo-regulatory structures (i.e. brainstem and insula) and higher-order cortical networks is reconfigured when the body is exposed to opposing thermal conditions (cooling vs. warming). Our findings indicate that whole-brain networks adaptively reorganize in response to thermal challenges, demonstrating that human thermoregulation is implemented through broad, context-sensitive connectivity changes rather than isolated region-level processing.

Methods

fMRI data was collected from 61 healthy young adults (28M/33F; mean age = 25.1 ± 3.4 years, range 20–31) using a 3 T Siemens Verio system (gradient-echo EPI; TR = 2.6 s, TE = 29 ms, voxel size = 2×2×3 mm³). Participants underwent an oscillating whole-body thermal challenge delivered via a temperature-controlled garment, alternating five 5-min epochs of cold (2–4 °C) and neutral (31–34 °C) stimulation (total = 25 min). This produced skin-temperature oscillations of ~4 °C. Cooling and warming epochs were defined as 3-min windows centered on the points of maximal temperature change (±1.5 min). The brain was parcellated into 246 cortical and subcortical regions using the Human Brainnetome Atlas, and 16 brainstem nuclei were localized using the Harvard Ascending Arousal Network Atlas (total = 262 regions). For each region, time series corresponding to the cooling (3min) and warming (3min) epochs were extracted. Pairwise functional connectivity (FC) was computed as zero-lag Pearson correlations for all unique region pairs under each condition. Significant FC (i.e. significantly increased correlations or anti-correlations) across the connectome was obtained using the False Discovery Rate (pFDR<.05). Finally, brainstem and insula connectivity profiles were constructed that are unique to cooling (significant during cooling but not during warming) and warming (significant during warming but not cooling) conditions.

Results

Brainstem connectivity profile (Figure 1) – during the parasympathetic (warming) condition, positive and negative connectivity was well balanced, characteristic of a system that is approaching homeostasis (Figure 1b). Positive correlations were observed between the brainstem and temporal cortex, while significant anti-correlations involved frontal, parietal, occipital, and limbic cortices. Functionally, positive coupling with temporal cortex may indicate interoceptive integration, while significant negative connectivity with frontal, parietal and visual cortex may reflect transient inhibition of higher-order executive evaluation and suppression of visual attention consistent with reduced sensitivity to exteroceptive inputs. Conversely, during the sympathetic (cooling) condition, positive connectivity of the brainstem with distributed whole-brain regions was markedly increased (p<0.001) (Figure 1a), suggesting that the brainstem plays a central coordinating role when re-establishing homeostasis. Increases in connectivity were distributed across all target regions (all p < 0.01), indicating that deviation from thermal equilibrium recruits a highly synchronized, excitatory network architecture aimed at mobilizing physiological and behavioral countermeasures to regain thermal neutrality.

Insula connectivity profile (Figure 2) – during the sympathetic (cooling) condition, the total number of positively correlated connections was markedly greater than in the parasympathetic (warming) condition (p<0.001), indicating enhanced insular engagement with cortical networks to drive recovery towards thermal equilibrium. Connections were broadly distributed across frontal, temporal, subcortical, brainstem and parietal regions (Figure 2a). The connectivity pattern changed significantly during the parasympathetic (warming) condition: connectivity of the insula with the parietal region increased, with concurrent loss of temporal, limbic, occipital, subcortical, and brainstem connections (Figure 2b). This change in network connectivity is consistent with a shift from broad integrative processing to a state favoring associative networks. The anti-correlation connectivity pattern showed a complementary reorganization – negative connectivity was greater during warming as compared to cooling (p<0.01). When approaching thermal equilibrium (warming), the insula showed strong negative connectivity with the frontal, temporal and visual regions, suggesting a release from executive and associative engagement as the system transitions to a restorative state. In contrast, during deviation from thermal equilibrium (cooling), the insula coupled negatively primarily with the visual region, indicating a suppression of visuo-spatial processing to prioritize interoceptive and autonomic demands.

Conclusion

Our findings provide additional evidence that at the level of CNS organization, functional specificity emerges not from isolated regional activations, but from dynamic, state-dependent interactions among distributed brainstem, limbic, subcortical, and cortical systems. As such, functional specificity represents an emergent property of the system’s dynamic architecture: an ongoing recalibration of connectivity that enables the brain to flexibly regulate physiological and behavioral states in response to changing environmental and interoceptive demands.
