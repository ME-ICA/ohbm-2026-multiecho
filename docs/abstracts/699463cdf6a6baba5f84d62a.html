Introduction

Hemispheric differences in brain structure are thought to underlie functional lateralisation of language, and there is some evidence that hemispheric asymmetry of the white matter tracts and properties of the corpus callosum in particular contribute to functional lateralisation of language in healthy adults1. However, it is unclear whether these structural properties are set early in life or whether they remain plastic in later life, for instance to accommodate learning of new languages. Language learning in adulthood has been shown to effect structural changes in grey matter, such as increases in hippocampal volume and cortical thickness of regions in the left perisylvian network2, as well as in white matter, with rapid changes in lateralisation of the arcuate fasciculus, which showed a non-linear correlation with proficiency3. However, these studies did not directly test whether and how these structural changes contributed to functional changes in language processing or lateralisation. In our previous studies with cross-sectional and longitudinal experiments with language learners (ages 17-65 years), we characterised functional differences and changes associated with real-world language learning, showing that greater/increasing proficiency in a new language is associated with changes in language and modality-specific hemispheric dominance4. In the current study, we combine these previous functional results with new tractography analyses to examine the structural correlates underlying the functional plasticity of language networks and their lateralisation.

Methods

Cross-sectional experiment I was conducted with 34 adult Spanish natives (20-65 years, 17 female) studying Basque at the same language school at either the basic (A2 level, n = 17) or advanced level (C1 level, n = 17). The two groups were matched on age, gender, IQ and native language proficiency. Longitudinal experiment II was conducted with 24 adolescent Spanish-Basque natives (17 years, 18 female) taking part in an immersion-style after-school programme for B1 level English students, scanned at two timepoints 3 months apart. Inside the MRI scanner, whole-brain diffusion-weighted images (DWI) were acquired and participants also performed in-scanner reading, speech comprehension and production tasks in their native and new languages. Tract reconstruction of association and commissural fibres was conducted with Reproducible Tract Profiles–2 pipeline5 using MRtrix, ANTs and FSL. Fractional anisotropy (FA) was calculated for 100 points along each tract. For the 12-association fibres, we modelled FA ~ Group * Tract * Hemisphere * Point (Session instead of Group in Experiment II), with Bonferroni correction for the number of tracts. For the commissural fibres, the corpus callosum was segmented into 8 sections, and modelled as FA ~ Group * Segment * Point (Session instead of Group in Experiment II). We then tested the association between FA of tracts showing significant effects of Group or Session with individual proficiency scores in the language tasks, and whether they predicted functional lateralisation indices.

Results

1) Behavioural performance on the language tasks showed significant Group x Language (Experiment I) and Session x Language interactions (Experiment II), showing significant differences/changes in proficiency measures. 

2) Functional lateralisation also showed Group/Session x Language x Modality effects. However, individual proficiency scores were not significantly associated with the functional lateralisation indices.

3) Analysis of the association fibres revealed significant Group x Hemisphere effects in the posterior segment of the Arcuate Fasciculus and ventral hippocampal segment of the Cingulum Bundle, with significantly higher FA in the right hemisphere tracts in the advanced group (Experiment I). These results were supported in the longitudinal data (Experiment II), though the effect of proficiency was only significant in the Arcuate Fasciculus. Moreover, FA of the two right hemisphere tracts were found to be significantly correlated with proficiency on the language tasks in both experiments.

4) The corpus callosum showed significant Group x Segment effects, with significantly higher FA in the advanced group in the Forceps Major and the Motor segments (Experiment I). Longitudinal results (Experiment II) supported this pattern of results, though they were not significant. In both experiments, FA of the Forceps Major was significantly associated with both proficiency scores and functional lateralisation of the language network, while the Motor segments contributed specifically to lateralisation of verbal production alone.

Conclusion

Overall, we found that language learning was associated with proficiency-related plasticity of right hemisphere white matter tracts, specifically, the posterior Arcuate Fasciculus, the hippocampal segment of the Cingulum Bundle, as well as the Forceps Major. Fractional anisotropy (FA) of these tracts were positively associated with language proficiency scores, while FA of Corpus Callosum segments were positively associated with functional lateralisation of the language network. These findings highlight the role of language, memory and cross-hemispheric communication in language learning and the white matter correlates underlying functional plasticity of the language network.
