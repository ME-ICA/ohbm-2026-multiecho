Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative condition traditionally characterized by motor impairment; however, non-motor symptoms are now widely recognized as major determinants of disability, disease progression, and reduced quality of life.¹ Among these, visual hallucinations (VHs) represent a clinically relevant manifestation whose neurobiological basis remains only partially understood. VHs are commonly classified into minor visual hallucinations (mVHs)—including transient illusions and peripheral visual misperceptions—and complex visual hallucinations (cVHs), characterized by vivid, well-formed images. Importantly, mVHs often precede the onset of cVHs and may reflect an early vulnerability to hallucinatory phenomena. Beyond cortical and higher-order subcortical regions, growing evidence supports the involvement of brainstem nuclei in the pathophysiology of VHs, given their central roles in arousal regulation, sensory filtering, and neuromodulatory balance.² Emerging studies suggest a neurochemical dissociation between hallucinatory subtypes: whereas cVHs are mainly associated with dopaminergic and serotonergic dysfunctions, mVHs appear to arise from early disruptions of cholinergic and noradrenergic systems. However, despite robust neurochemical support for brainstem involvement, in vivo investigation of these nuclei has long been limited by the lack of reliable segmentation methods. Recent advances in histology-informed probabilistic atlases now enable reliable volumetric assessment of these nuclei, including the AAN atlas³ and other next-generation histology-based resources.⁴ In this study, we tested the hypothesis that selective atrophy of key cholinergic and noradrenergic brainstem nuclei—namely the pedunculopontine tegmental nucleus (PPTg), the laterodorsal tegmental nucleus (LDTg), and the locus coeruleus (LC) —is associated with increased vulnerability to mVHs, beyond the influence of demographic, clinical, cognitive, and other structural factors.

Methods

Structural MRI, clinical, and neuropsychological data were analyzed from 641 patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) enrolled in the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI), a large multicenter observational cohort designed to characterize early disease stages.5 Only baseline T1-weighted MRI scans acquired prior to the initiation of dopaminergic therapy were included, thereby minimizing potential confounding effects of medication on both brain structure and hallucinatory symptoms. 3T MRI acquisition parameters were as follows: slice thickness = 1.0 mm; in-plane resolution = 1.0 × 1.0 mm²; 192 contiguous slices per volume; field of view = 256 mm; matrix size = 256 × 256; no zero-filling or interpolation; anterior–posterior phase encoding.
Participants were classified according to the presence of minor visual hallucinations (mVHs) during the first year of follow-up. Patients reporting brief, non-distressing visual phenomena with preserved insight were classified as PD-mVHs⁺ (n = 64), whereas those consistently reporting no hallucinations were classified as PD-mVHs⁻ (n = 577). Demographic characteristics, disease duration, motor severity, affective measures, and a comprehensive neuropsychological battery were available for all included participants. Group differences in demographic, clinical, and neuropsychological variables between PD-mVHs⁺ and PD-mVHs⁻ patients were assessed using independent-samples tests. Anatomical MRI data were processed using an advanced, operator-independent morphometric pipeline. T1-weighted images were preprocessed and segmented using FreeSurfer (v8.0.0), integrated with two complementary probabilistic atlases specifically designed to improve the anatomical definition of deep neuromodulatory structures: the Ascending Arousal Network (AAN) atlas³ and a histology-informed atlas derived from post-mortem cytoarchitectonic data.4 This multi-atlas framework enabled reliable volumetric reconstruction of seven small brainstem nuclei of interest that are typically inaccessible to conventional MRI-based segmentation, including the left and right LC (Figure 1a), the left and right PPTg (Figure 1b), the left and right LDTg (Figure 1c), and the serotonergic dorsal raphe (Figure 1d). In addition, the histology-based atlas provided volumetric measures of a broad set of subcortical, cerebellar, and cortical regions, supporting exploratory whole-brain analyses. To directly test the a priori hypothesis that degeneration of cholinergic and noradrenergic brainstem nuclei is associated with mVHs, volumetric differences in brainstem nuclei were examined using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), adjusting for age, sex, education, intracranial volume, and global cognitive status. Correction for multiple comparisons was applied to the primary set of brainstem nuclei (p = 0.05/7 = 0.007). Subsequently, exploratory ANCOVAs were conducted across additional brain regions to identify other candidate morphometric correlates of mVHs. Finally, hierarchical logistic regression models with bootstrap resampling were implemented to quantify the independent contribution of noradrenergic and cholinergic brainstem volumes relative to demographic, clinical, cognitive, and broader morphometric variables, allowing direct comparison between neuromodulatory brainstem nuclei and other potential structural predictors of hallucinatory vulnerability.

Results

Comparisons between PD-mVHs⁺ and PD-mVHs⁻ patients revealed several differences in clinical and neuropsychological characteristics. Patients experiencing mVHs showed longer disease duration (p = 0.01) and a higher burden of affective symptoms (geriatric depression scale, p < 0.001), alongside verbal episodic memory performance (p = 0.04). In contrast, global motor severity and overall cognitive status were largely comparable between groups. Hypothesis-driven volumetric analyses demonstrated reduced baseline volumes of key brainstem neuromodulatory nuclei in PD-mVHs⁺ patients. In particular, after adjusting for demographic factors, intracranial volume, and global cognition, reduced volume of the left LDTg (p = 0.005) was observed in the PD-mVHs⁺ group, together with a concomitant reduction in the left LC (p = 0.004). These effects survived correction for multiple comparisons and were not accompanied by volumetric differences in other brainstem nuclei not central to the a priori hypothesis, such as the pedunculopontine or dorsal raphe nuclei. The selective involvement of LDTg and LC supports a specific association between mVHs and early alterations of cholinergic and noradrenergic brainstem systems. Exploratory whole-brain analyses (uncorrected p < 0.05) identified additional volumetric differences across a distributed set of regions in PD-mVHs⁺ patients, including other brainstem nuclei, cerebellar structures, thalamic subdivisions, hippocampal subfields, and selected cortical areas. These findings suggest that, beyond focal brainstem alterations, mVHs may be embedded within a broader network-level vulnerability involving structures implicated in arousal regulation, sensory integration, and perceptual stability. However, these exploratory results were interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating. In hierarchical logistic regression analyses, demographic, clinical, and affective variables initially accounted for a significant proportion of variance in hallucinatory status. When LC and LDTg volumes were added to the model, predictive performance improved, indicating that neuromodulatory brainstem integrity provides information beyond clinical and cognitive measures alone. In particular, reduced LDTg volume showed a robust and independent association with mVHs after bootstrap validation, whereas LC volume showed a consistent but weaker contribution. When a comprehensive set of morphometric predictors was included, only a limited subset of regions retained explanatory value, underscoring the relative specificity of brainstem neuromodulatory nuclei in relation to mVHs.

Conclusion

Using a histology-informed, multi-atlas MRI framework, this study provides an in vivo structural characterization of mVHs in PD. Our findings demonstrate that mVHs are associated with selective atrophy of cholinergic and noradrenergic brainstem nuclei, particularly the laterodorsal tegmental nucleus and, to a lesser extent, the locus coeruleus. These associations were observed independently of global cognitive impairment, motor severity, and major demographic factors, highlighting a specific link between neuromodulatory brainstem integrity and hallucinatory vulnerability. The involvement of the LDTg is especially relevant given its central role in arousal regulation, REM sleep generation, and sensory gating—processes that are increasingly recognized as critical contributors to hallucinatory phenomena in PD. Reduced structural integrity of this nucleus may reflect early cholinergic dysfunction, potentially impairing the filtering and integration of sensory information and thereby promoting transient perceptual instability. Parallel, albeit weaker, involvement of the LC suggests that noradrenergic degeneration may further compromise attentional modulation and perceptual precision, acting in concert with cholinergic deficits. Importantly, while clinical and affective factors remained strong contributors to hallucinatory status, the addition of brainstem volumetric measures significantly improved explanatory models, indicating that structural alterations of neuromodulatory nuclei capture variance not explained by behavioral measures alone. At the same time, the limited number of additional morphometric predictors surviving multivariable modeling suggests that mVHs are not driven by diffuse brain atrophy, but rather by targeted vulnerability within specific subcortical systems. Overall, these findings support a model in which minor visual hallucinations in PD arise from early dysfunction of brainstem neuromodulatory circuits involved in arousal and sensory regulation. Identification of these nuclei as structural correlates of mVHs may contribute to improved stratification of non-motor symptoms and enhance understanding of early hallucinatory processes in PD.
