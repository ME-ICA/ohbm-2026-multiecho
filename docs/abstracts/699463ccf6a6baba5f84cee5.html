Introduction

For over half a century, the quest to understand sex differences in human cognition has been a focal and controversial theme in neuroscience. While women often outperform men in communication ability and men show advantages in visuospatial performance, the extent to which these patterns reflect biological constraints versus sociocultural influences remains unresolved1. In response, major scientific bodies have called for a paradigm shift: initiatives such as NIH’s SABV policy, the SAGER guidelines, and the EU’s Horizon 2020 program emphasize the need to integrate sex and gender systematically in research. At the same time, large-scale open neuroimaging repositories, now encompassing thousands of participants with multimodal MRI and behavioral data, offer an unprecedented opportunity to revisit these questions at the population level. This scale has transformed human neuroimaging into a branch of computational neuroscience, allowing replicable brain-phenotype associations to be tested across diverse contexts. Among neuroimaging metrics, resting-state functional connectivity (FC) provides a particularly powerful window into brain organization: it captures distributed network-level architecture, links directly to cognition, and demonstrates robust reproducibility across cohorts. However, most large-scale analyses still rely on a binary sex category, typically self-reported as female or male and treated as purely biological. In practice, this measure conflates biological and sociocultural dimensions, embedding gender-role constructs within the sex variable, and its designation as gender/sex (G/S) is more appropriate. Ignoring this confound risks reinforcing essentialist interpretations and underestimating the influence of sociocultural factors on brain organization2. This study moves beyond a binary simplification by explicitly modeling how educational context moderates the relationship between G/S, brain connectivity, and cognitive performance in young adults.

Methods

Participants. Resting-state fMRI (rfMRI) data were analyzed from the MRiShare cohort (mean age 22 ± 2.25 years; 72% women; n = 1,771). Sex was self-reported as male or female and is referred to hereafter as G/S. Participants completed a 15-minute rfMRI scan with eyes closed, followed by the Resting-State Questionnaire (ReSQ 2.0, 24-item self-report) to assess resting-state thought profiles (RSTPs). 

Educational context. Academic study field was used as a proxy for educational context and classified into (i) content-oriented disciplines (e.g., STEM, literature, economics) and (ii) person-oriented disciplines (e.g., psychology, education, medicine). This categorization follows the distinction between non-social and social domains, previously documented at both cognitive and neurofunctional levels. Importantly, both categories had similar G/S composition (~75% women) and preserved G/S cognitive differences, ensuring that context effects were not driven by sample demographics or baseline performance.

Cognitive performance. Cognitive abilities were assessed in domains typically showing G/S differences: emotion recognition (Genova Emotion Recognition Test, score range 0-42; n = 1,396, Fig. 1A) and verbal list learning (Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test, score range 0-30; n = 1,331, Fig. 1B) in women, and mental rotation (Mental Rotation Test, score range 0-5; n = 1,251, Fig. 1C) and visuospatial reasoning (Raven’s Progressive Matrices Test, score range 0-5; n = 1,164, Fig. 1D) in men.

Functional connectivity (FC). Individual functional connectomes were computed using a standardized preprocessing pipeline and the GINNA atlas3. The non-overlapping version of GINNA includes 439 brain regions, 32 cortical functional networks, yielding 496 Fisher-z transformed edges per participant (32x31/2), and seven core functional networks (e.g., social and communication, self-awareness, visual)4. To reduce G/S-related physiological confounds, edge-wise FC values were normalized by the mean correlation (Fisher z-transformed) across all region pairs of participants within each G/S group. Edge pre-selection identified FC features potentially associated with G/S. FC values across all edges were modeled simultaneously using a linear mixed model including G/S, covariates (age, estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV), RSTPs), a random intercept per participant, and random slopes of G/S for each edge. Nested cross-validation (10 repetitions × 5 outer folds) ranked edges by absolute slope, and stability selection (frequency ≥0.8) with Bonferroni-corrected p<0.05, yielding 72 edges. These were entered into a LASSO logistic regression predicting G/S, adjusting for covariates; 19 edges had non-zero coefficients (Fig.1E). From this subset, multiconnected seed FC indices (MSFCs) were computed by summing edge weights sharing the same seed (i.e., nodes connected to at least two distinct networks). This procedure yielded two MSFCs in participants classified as female and seven in those classified as male. 

Statistical analyses. For each G/S-related cognitive task, a distinct moderated mediation model was estimated for each G/S-specific MSFC, testing MSFC-specific mediation between G/S and cognitive performance (indirect effect, path ab) and its modulation by educational context (moderator). In this framework, the indirect effect of G/S cognitive outcomes via MSFC can vary depending on the level of the moderator (content- vs. person-oriented discipline), allowing us to assess context-dependent neural mediation (Fig. 1F). All models included age, eTIV, and RSTPs as covariates. Indirect effects were estimated using a bootstrap approach (PROCESS Model 595), and only those with FDR-corrected p < 0.05 were retained. This approach operationalizes contextual contingency by examining not only whether G/S-related FC differences exist, but also under which conditions they relate to cognition.

Results

Our analyses revealed that G/S-specific MSFCs exerted context-contingent indirect effects on G/S differences in cognitive performance. 

In content-oriented fields (Fig. 2A), two male MSFCs (5 and 15), functionally anchored in performance monitoring, memory retrieval, and syntactic processing, and spanning fronto-temporo-parietal, insulo-cingular, and medio-parieto-temporal regions, showed significant indirect paths mediating approximately 10–11% of the total G/S effect on mental rotation and visuospatial reasoning. Indirect effects (ab) were: MSFC 5: b = 0.033, CI95 [0.003, 0.079] and b = 0.032, CI95 [0.002, 0.074]; MSFC 15: b = 0.029, CI95 [0.001, 0.075] and b = 0.032, CI95 [0.003, 0.076]. In all cases, the direct effects (c') remained significant, indicating partial mediation. 

In person-oriented fields (Fig. 2B), the female MSFC 28, engaging self-monitoring, theory of mind, and sentence-comprehension networks within fronto-temporo-parietal and medial frontal regions, showed robust indirect effects, mediating about 24% of the G/S difference in verbal list learning and emotion recognition (b = 0.169, CI95 [0.207, 0.358]; b = 0.286, CI95 [0.105, 0.519]). For both outcomes, the direct paths were non-significant, indicating full mediation of the G/S effect in this educational context. 

Only the female MSFC 3, centered on occipital visual networks, mediated emotion-recognition performance independently of academic field (content-oriented: b = 0.069, CI95 [0.001, 0.180]; person-oriented: b = 0.156, CI95 [0.030, 0.302]). This dissociation between MSFC 3 and MSFC 28 in women reinforces the context-dependent sensitivity of FC-cognition associations. 

Overall, these findings show that canonical FC-cognition relationships are preserved, but that their effect sizes, network topology, and mediation structure depend on the academic field moderator. The results support a model in which educational context shapes the expression of G/S-related cognitive differences by selectively strengthening context-sensitive MSFC-cognition pathways.

Conclusion

These results challenge the widespread assumption that sex differences in brain and cognition are solely biologically determined. FC not only reflects intrinsic neural organization but also encodes cognitive patterns shaped by learning environments. Embedding educational context within large-scale FC analyses provides a framework for moving beyond binary sex categories toward multidimensional models of gendered brain function. Our study invites a reappraisal of essentialist narratives: apparent “sex differences” in cognition may emerge from dynamic interactions between biological predispositions and environment-specific functional reconfigurations, revealing the mechanisms through which experience modulates variability in the human brain.
