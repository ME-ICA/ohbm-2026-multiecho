Introduction

Experimental evidence has shown that meso- to macroscale neural activity propagates as spatiotemporal travelling waves in the cortex. These waves often traverse pathways following the hierarchical organisation of the cortex—from unimodal sensory areas to transmodal association areas. However, the mechanism by which these waves emerge and shape the functional organisation of the human brain, especially at the individual level, remains unclear. Neural field theory (NFT),1 an established biophysical model that approximates the mean-field properties of neural populations, can help reveal these mechanisms. Recent work has shown that resting-state and task-evoked functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) can be modelled using NFT, revealing that cortical waves supporting fMRI signals are constrained by the physical geometry of the human brain.2 However, most existing NFT applications rely on group-averaged cortical geometries, potentially obscuring the influence of individual anatomical variability on wave propagation and functional organisation.

Inter-individual variability in geometrical features, including cortical folding, surface area, and thickness, has been linked to behavioural differences. This motivates biophysical modelling at the individual level to assess whether geometric variation leads to differences in wave timing, hierarchical pathways, and spatial scale, providing a mechanistic account of inter-individual functional differences.

Here, we apply a fully individualised NFT-based model of cortical wave dynamics to participants from the Human Connectome Project (HCP). For each individual, we develop an NFT model suited to their cortical geometry and fit it to resting-state fMRI data to estimate the optimal propagation length scale. We then examine how visual evoked waves emerge in space and time and how they track the visual hierarchy. This framework reveals both shared and unique principles that shape cortical wave dynamics across individuals.

Methods

We analysed data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) S1200 release, focusing on participants with complete structural and resting-state fMRI data (n=1018).3 All analyses were performed at the individual-subject level. We used subject-specific midthickness cortical surfaces, each represented as a triangular mesh with 32,492 vertices per hemisphere. We also used minimally preprocessed resting-state fMRI data, as provided by HCP, which applied MSMAll multimodal surface alignment, ICA-FIX automated denoising, and temporal high-pass filtering.3

For each subject, we implemented an NFT-based model of cortical wave dynamics using their cortical surface as the only input.1 Cortical geometric eigenmodes were derived by performing an eigendecomposition of the Laplace-Beltrami operator of each subject’s midthickness cortical surface.2 The NFT model only has one free parameter, rs, which represents the characteristic length scale of wave propagation.

To estimate the optimal rs for each subject, we simulated resting-state neural activity using spatially white noise input projected onto the individual cortical surface. For these simulations, cortical dynamics were expressed using the first 500 eigenmodes, and rs was systematically varied across a predefined range. Simulated neural activity was passed through a hemodynamic model to generate synthetic BOLD-fMRI time series.

Simulated and empirical fMRI time series were parcellated using the 360-region Glasser atlas4, and functional connectivity dynamics (FCD) were computed. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) distance between simulated and empirical FCD distributions was used as the objective function for parameter optimisation, and the value of rs that minimized the KS distance was selected as the best-fit parameter for each subject. To evaluate the effectiveness of the resulting subject-specific rs values, additional static and dynamic functional connectivity metrics were computed, including edge FC correlation, node FC strength correlation, and the FCD KS statistic.

Using the optimised NFT model for each individual, we next examined the organisation of wave propagation along the visual hierarchy. Following Pang et al.,2 we applied a brief impulse stimulus to the primary visual cortex (V1) on each subject’s cortical surface (Figure 1). Visual-evoked simulations were expressed using a higher-resolution basis comprising the first 5,000 cortical geometric eigenmodes, allowing finer spatial detail in the propagating activity.

After stimulation, activity was allowed to propagate freely in space and time for 200 ms (see example propagation for each subject in Figure 1). For each subject, we extracted the peak activation time for regions known to form part of the visual hierarchy, i.e., V1, V4, 7m, 7Am, TE1p, 7AL, 24dd, 2, 24dv, 8BM, 10r, 10v, 8BL, 10pp, 10d, 9-46v, and 9-46d.5 To assess whether the model-predicted temporal ordering of regional activation recapitulates the ordering of regions along the empirical visual hierarchy, we compared the peak activation time with empirical T1w/T2w ratio of each subject. The T1w/T2w ratio has been shown to be a good in vivo marker of intracortical myelin content and cortical hierarchy, with early sensory areas exhibiting higher values than higher-order association areas. For each subject, we computed the Spearman rank correlation between regional peak activation time and regional T1w/T2w values, with correlations approaching -1 indicating close agreement between model-predicted temporal ordering and the expected anatomical hierarchy.

All simulations and analyses were performed independently for each participant, enabling systematic quantification of subject-specific variability in wave parameters, FC correspondence, and hierarchical propagation patterns.

Results

For each individual, we identified the optimal spatial propagation parameter rs that best matches the spatiotemporal statistics of model-simulated activity to those of empirical resting-state fMRI data. Figure 2A shows that, across all HCP individuals, the optimal rs showed substantial variation (mean 35.8 mm, SD 8.5 mm), indicating individual differences in the effective spatial spread of cortical activity. This variability demonstrates that the model captures meaningful subject-specific differences. Figure 2B shows the performance of the individualised models in capturing both static and dynamic characteristics of fMRI data. Static FC was measured with edge and node FC correlations (edge FC: mean 0.30, SD 0.028; node FC: mean 0.14, SD 0.12) and dynamic FC was measured with the FCD KS metric (mean 0.05, SD 0.02). Note, however, that our simple model seems to struggle in capturing empirical data in a subset of subjects (e.g., see the negative node FC correlations in Figure 2B), which could be due to various reasons such as poor data quality, poor cortical surface reconstruction, or the model requires further complexities. Nonetheless, these results establish the utility of our individualised modelling approach, demonstrating that we can capture meaningful inter-subject variability in model parameters and performance metrics.

We next evaluated the ability of our model to capture visual hierarchical organisation. As discussed in the Methods section, we used the optimal rs for each individual and simulated neural activity in response to a brief 1 ms stimulus to the primary visual cortex (V1). We then examined the resulting propagation of activity across the cortical surface. After V1 stimulation, early activity remained restricted to V1/V2 before bifurcating into two distinct pathways: a dorsally directed trajectory towards parietal visual areas and a ventrally directed trajectory towards inferotemporal regions (Figure 1). This pattern was consistently observed across subjects. This suggests that cortical geometry may provide a minimally sufficient constraint to produce waves that track both the dorsal and ventral stream, which is important for cognition.

Quantitatively, we extracted peak activation times for regions along the visual pathway and compared their temporal ordering to the known hierarchical organisation of the brain, as quantified by T1w/T2w ratio (Figure 2C). Note that a rank correlation of -1 in Figure 2C indicates that the model-predicted peak activation times perfectly match the empirically estimated hierarchy in the brain. Across all subjects, the rank ordering of peak times closely mirrored the rank ordering of regional intracortical myelination, yielding strong correlations between temporal propagation hierarchy and anatomical hierarchy (Figure 2D; correlation statistics: mean -0.70, median -0.72, SD 0.13). Interestingly, some subjects had waves that did not track well the T1w/T2w hierarchy (see outliers in Figure 2D), which could either be due to poor model fitting in Figure 2B or related to meaningful cognitive differences.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that individual cortical geometry shapes both resting-state dynamics and stimulus-driven propagation in the visual system. Using a cortical wave model based on neural field theory, we identified subject-specific optimal spatial propagation parameters, capturing meaningful variability in functional connectivity patterns. Simulations of visual-evoked activity revealed a robust separation of dorsal and ventral pathways emerging from V1 across subjects. The temporal ordering of peak activation across visual areas closely corresponded to regional myelination gradients, supporting a link between cortical structure and functional hierarchy. These results indicate that geometry-constrained wave dynamics offer a principled, mechanistic framework for studying individual differences in cortical organisation. Future work will extend this approach to explore the behavioural associations of inter-subject variability in wave statistics and broader applications to clinical populations, e.g., in patients with schizophrenia that are known to have vision-related impairments, thereby helping to better understand how individual structural features constrain functional dynamics and hierarchical processing.
