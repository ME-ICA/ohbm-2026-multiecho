Introduction

Protecting working memory (WM) from interference is crucial for goal-directed behaviour, yet the neural mechanisms supporting this protection remain debated. Prior decoding work shows that distractors can disrupt sensory representations while sparing parietal areas such as the intraparietal sulcus (IPS)1,2,3. In parallel, the Multiple-Demand (MD) network is implicated in flexible cognitive control and may stabilise WM when attention is diverted 4,5. However, it remains unclear whether MD regions maintain or enhance WM representations when an interrupting task requires cognitive effort. We tested whether the MD system, and IPS in particular, protects WM representations during interruption.

Methods

Twenty-one participants (N=21; after exclusions) memorised a morphed image of a tool or animal and maintained it for a delay. During this delay, they performed a digit-sorting task that served as an interrupter. Task demands were manipulated by comparing easy vs. hard digit sorting, or a passive condition requiring no sorting. fMRI data were collected across eight runs. Multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) decoded item category (tool vs. animal) using beta estimates from a GLM. Decoding was performed in the early visual cortex and 13 MD subregions, as well as a combined MD ROI. Effects of interrupter difficulty on decoding were tested using ANOVA and follow-up comparisons.

Results

Digit-task performance confirmed the intended demand manipulation, with slower and less accurate responses in the hard condition. WM recall was impaired when participants performed active digit tasks relative to the passive condition.
Despite this behavioural interference, decoding revealed enhanced WM representations in the MD network when attentional demands were higher. In the combined MD ROI, decoding accuracy was significantly higher in the hard vs. passive condition. At the subregion level, left IPS showed the strongest modulation, with decoding accuracy increasing systematically with digit-task difficulty. Early visual cortex reliably encoded memoranda across all conditions but showed no effects of interrupter difficulty. Decoding changes were not related to BOLD magnitude, indicating representational sharpening rather than global activation changes.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that the MD network, especially IPS, adaptively protects WM from interruption. Rather than simply maintaining stable representations, MD regions enhance task-relevant codes as cognitive demands increase, suggesting a flexible control mechanism that boosts WM fidelity when attention is diverted. This work extends prior accounts of parietal involvement in WM protection1,2,3 and supports the view that the MD system implements representational control that helps maintain WM robustness under interference. These results provide insight into how cognitive control supports WM resilience and may inform interventions for clinical populations with impaired WM.
