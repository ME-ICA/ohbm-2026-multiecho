Introduction

Latin America (LATAM) is a region marked by cultural, linguistic, and demographic diversity, shaped by economic disparities, colonial history, and complex social dynamics. These historical, political, and socioeconomic conditions, together with current heterogeneity, may pose barriers to interaction and collaboration within the region, including in scientific research.1 Recent regional initiatives in neuroscience and neuroimaging research are contributing to a more equitable research ecosystem; however, the extent of intra-regional collaborations in LATAM is unknown and based on anecdotal evidence. To address this gap, we use the number of publications and their weighted citation impact as metrics of scientific collaboration between LATAM countries and compare these patterns with collaborations involving the global north.

This effort is timely, as data from LATAM is increasingly essential for broadening population diversity in research and for validating diagnostic methods, biomarkers, and emerging therapies across populations.2 Documenting existing regional collaborations and alliances, and analyzing their relational dynamics, can inform strategies to strengthen and sustain neuroscientific networks within the region.

Methods

Data from LATAM countries was compiled from the publication’s metadata extraction and preprocessing tool, SciVal from Elsevier, for the period of 2020 to 2024. SciVal is based on Scopus data and allows the extraction of benchmarks such as the number of publications, number of co-authored publications within countries, and Field-Weighted Citation Impact (FWCI), an index of citation impact that allows a comparison to the world average (FWCI = 1) for each subject field, publication type, and publication year. Therefore, the number of co-authored publications and their FWCI for all LATAM countries were extracted for the neuroscientific research field (as defined by ASJC, All Science Journal Classification), and all fields of research.

Latin America was defined by the World Bank definition, with a total of 42 countries.3 Countries without an income group definition in the last World Bank report were excluded from analysis. FWCI outliers, identified as the ones outside of median ± 3*median absolute deviation, were removed to reduce the effect of inflated values. We also excluded from the analysis countries with 0 citations or missing information regarding publications and citations. The final number of LATAM countries included in this study is 33.

Data analysis was made with R v4.5.1 and RStudio. The publication’s metadata was transformed into a log scale due to its skewness. Means were compared with Wilcoxon’s test, and pairwise analyses in scenarios with more than two levels were corrected for multiple comparisons with Holm’s method. Map plots present the mean number of co-authored publications and mean FWCI for global regions as defined by the World Bank.

Results

Analysis indicated that there is higher scientific production (increased co-authored publications) when Latin American researchers collaborate with countries outside of their region (p < 0.0001), and these publications have higher impact (p < 0.0001). When detailing the analysis to explore the internal collaborations and their differences between the Global North and Global South, there is a higher Citation impact when collaborating outside LATAM, despite North or South (p ≤ 0.0001 corrected for multiple comparisons) and a higher number of publications co-authored with Global North researchers (Global North > LATAM > Global South, all p ≤ 0.001 corrected for multiple comparisons).

We investigated whether these findings could be unique to the neuroscience research field by doing the same comparisons with data from all fields of scientific research. The same pattern is present in all research fields. Co-authored publications with Global North authors are in higher numbers (Global North > LATAM > Global South, all p ≤ 0.0001 corrected for multiple comparisons) and with higher impact than those with only LATAM authors (p ≤ 0.0001 corrected for multiple comparisons).

Figure 1 illustrates the mean number of co-authored publications across global subregions. Visual analysis confirms that the strongest international research collaboration for LATAM-based researchers is with North America, evidenced by the highest values (yellow) in that region. Conversely, collaboration is notably lower with African and many intra-regional (South American) subregions (purple/darker colors).

Conclusion

The results indicate that there is an imbalance in intra-regional collaborations established in LATAM and collaborations with the Global North. These findings can be interpreted as a proxy for assessing the data sharing environment within LATAM, and considering the possibility of existing barriers for geographically and culturally close countries.4 We propose further investigation to identify which barriers are imposing this scientific distance and to better understand how limited intra-regional collaboration relates to sociodemographic factors across LATAM that may underlie these constraints.

The confirmation of the main hypothesis proposed in this work is a call to action to foster new bridges and strengthen existing neuroscientific collaborations in LATAM. The burden of mental and brain health is continuously increasing while we face particular and shared scenarios that favor the occurrence of modifiable risk factors.5 Increased collaboration among LATAM countries will enable improved data sharing across countries and foster greater innovation in scientific research.
