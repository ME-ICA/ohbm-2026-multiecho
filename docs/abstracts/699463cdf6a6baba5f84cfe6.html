Introduction

Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition concerning about 1 in 100 people. Beyond its core social-communication differences and restricted, repetitive behaviors, many findings point to hippocampal involvement^1: difficulties encoding and retrieving episodic memories, altered hippocampal connectivity on fMRI, and reduced exploration and spatial navigation. Autistic individuals can struggle with some social-cognitive processes such as interpreting emotion and context, which also recruit hippocampal circuits. Finally, hippocampal function is a plausible mechanistic contributor to atypical memory in autism. Structural studies in autism report morphological differences in the hippocampus, although directions vary across cohorts.

Incomplete hippocampal inversion (IHI) is a developmental variant in which fetal inversion of the hippocampus is incomplete, yielding a rounder, more vertical hippocampus and often a deep collateral sulcus. IHI lies on a continuum and is relatively common (pronounced forms up to ~20% of people in the left hemisphere, ~9% right). Because binary definitions have been inconsistent, a multi-criteria system frames IHI as a continuous score with better reproducibility.^2 First described in epilepsy, IHI has also been described with other neurodevelopmental anomalies, suggesting broader relevance to brain development.

Despite converging evidence linking autism to hippocampal function and morphology, the specific role of IHI in autistic traits has, to our knowledge, not been tested. Automated scoring of IHI from T1-weighted MRI now makes such work feasible across large datasets.^3 Here we motivate an investigation of IHI in autism, studying autism as a spectrum with continuous behavioral and cognitive measures (Social Responsiveness Score, SRS, and Intelligence Quotient, IQ) alongside diagnosis, to clarify whether atypical hippocampal development contributes to autistic phenotypes.

Methods

We assembled four autism cohorts with T1-weighted MRI: the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange 1 (ABIDE1) (n=814; 430 neurotypical (NT), 384 autistic participants; 20 sites; mean age 18.3±8.4; 14.3% female), ABIDE2 (n=811; 452 NT, 359 autistic participants; 16 sites; mean age 15.1±9.6; 23% female),^4 and the Longitudinal European Autism Project (LEAP) (n=581; 235 NT, 346 autistic participants; 6 sites; mean age 17.0±5.7; 31% female).^5 Each participant contributed one T1 MRI, yielding 2,206 scans (978 autistic, 1,228 NT). Preprocessing followed the T1-volume pipeline: T1 registration to MNI, tissue segmentation, then images were cropped in a region of interest around the hippocampus. Images failing manual quality control were excluded. IHI was quantified using an automated rater^3 that predicts four anatomical criteria: hippocampal roundness, collateral sulcus verticality, hippocampal mediality, and occipito-temporal/collateral sulcus depth, and sums them into a 0-8 composite score per hemisphere (higher indicates more pronounced IHI, figure 1a). The convolutional neural network (5 convolutional layers, 3 fully connected) was trained on manually annotated adolescent/young-adult cohorts and validated on independent data. 

We first compared IHI scores between participants with autism and NT people. To move beyond a binary case-control contrast, we then characterized autism dimensionally using Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS) scores and total IQ. This approach follows prior work showing that hierarchical clustering on SRS T-scores and IQ, following a PCA on a large sample of autism related metrics to identify main axes of variance, yields a stable three-cluster solution with distinct clinical, genetic, and neuroimaging profiles, enabling dimensional stratification that is not constrained by diagnostic labels. We evaluated both hierarchical (Ward) and k-means clustering with 3 and 4 clusters and selected a three-cluster k-means solution based on improved silhouette scores. Critically, clustering was computed on the broadest available phenotype sample across cohorts, regardless of MRI availability, to maximize statistical power and generalizability. This full-sample set included participants with SRS and IQ from ABIDE, LEAP, and the external InoVAND cohort yielding N=2,210 (figure 1b). We filter out NT participants and participants with intellectual deficiency (ID). Cluster 1 is the closest to the NT behavior, while cluster 3 shows behaviors more similar to the ID group (figure 1.c.). InovAND contributed to clustering only and was excluded from imaging analyses because its scans were 1.5T while our IHI model was trained on 3T data. After clustering, we retained cluster labels and then restricted the study to participants with a valid T1 MRI and a successful automated IHI rating from ABIDE1, ABIDE2, and LEAP (n=1747). 

For statistical analyses, SRS raw scores were converted to age, sex, and rater-standardized T-scores and capped at 90. We adjusted the IHI composite score and each individual criterion for age, age squared, sex, and site using ordinary least squares and used residuals in downstream tests. Autism risk per unit increase in IHI was estimated with logistic regression, reporting odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals. We computed ORs between autistic individuals of each cluster and the NT population using the same methodology. False Discovery Rate (FDR) was used to make up for multiple testing. Associations between adjusted IHI and dimensional traits (IQ, SRS) were tested using Spearman correlations given non-normal IHI distributions. Finally, we modeled both continuous outcomes as a function of the set of scaled individual IHI criteria, adjusting for age, age squared, sex, and site, and controlled multiple testing (FDR). Both correlations and regressions were performed on the entire sample for IQ and only on the autistic population for the SRS t-score to counter group effects.

Results

In figure 2.a, odds ratios (ORs) for autism diagnosis are shown for each criterion (C1–C5 in left and right hemispheres) and for the composite score, both by cohort and pooled across cohorts. Across datasets, point estimates clustered tightly around 1.0 and most 95% confidence intervals crossed the one point mark. A small set of contrasts achieved nominal p < 0.05 (asterisks): criterion C3 in ABIDE1 and in the pooled “ALL” analysis, the composite score in the right hemisphere for ABIDE1, and the composite score bilaterally in the pooled analysis. These departures from unity were modest, did not survive FDR correction, and showed no coherent lateralization. Overall, the evidence does not support a robust association between IHI features and case status; however, the nominal signals suggest potential heterogeneity and motivate follow-up analyses stratified by autistic subtypes to evaluate whether specific phenotypic groups drive these effects.

Given autism’s heterogeneity, we next tested whether IHI relates to phenotypic subtypes by comparing the autistic subgroup within each SRS-IQ cluster to neurotypical participants (NT) using logistic regression; in each contrast, the autistic cluster is the event group (OR > 1 indicates higher IHI odds) and significance is FDR-corrected. Cluster 1 vs NT showed ORs close to 1.0 across all criteria, with no FDR-significant effects, including the composite scores. Cluster 2 vs NT yielded point estimates mostly around 1.0 and likewise no FDR-significant differences. Cluster 3 vs NT exhibited elevations for several criteria, but no individual criterion remained significant after FDR; the largest (moderate) increases were for C1_L and C3_R. Both composite scores were moderately elevated. We then examined an intellectual-disability (ID) subgroup without autism against NT and observed moderate elevations for C1 and C3 bilaterally and for C2_R. While no single criterion survived FDR correction in ID either, these converging increases resulted in a significant elevation of the composite score in the right hemisphere for ID relative to NT after FDR correction. Overall, when referenced to NTs, higher IHI burden is concentrated in low-IQ phenotypes, with criterion-level trends strongest for C1/C3 and a right-hemisphere composite effect in ID.

Instead of using classifications, we investigated autism associated continuous metrics. We correlated residualized composite IHI scores (adjusted for age, age squared, sex, and site) with total IQ and SRS T-scores in the pooled sample using Spearman’s rho. For IQ, both hemispheres showed weak negative correlations: left r = -0.04 (p = 0.050) and right r = -0.05 (p = 0.023). After FDR correction, neither remained significant. Effect sizes were small (|r| = 0.05), explaining minimal variance. For SRS, neither hemisphere showed evidence of association with the composite IHI score (left r = 0.04, p = 0.274; right r = 0.01, p = 0.791), and these findings remained non-significant after multiple-comparison control. 

Finally, we assessed which criteria contributed uniquely to the continuous outcomes in multivariable models (figure 1.d.). In the IQ model, only C1_L, relating to the roundness of the hippocampus, showed a statistically significant association after FDR correction for eight tests (standardized β = -0.09; p ≤ 0.006). All other criteria had small effects that did not survive multiplicity correction; the largest non-significant trends were positive for C3_L (β = 0.06) and negative for C2_L (β = -0.05). In the SRS model, no criterion reached FDR-corrected significance, and all effects were small (|β| ≤ 0.09); the largest uncorrected associations were positive for C1_L and negative for C5_L.

Conclusion

This study examined whether incomplete hippocampal inversion, quantified with five anatomical criteria and a composite score, relates to autism when modeled categorically and by related metrics. Four conclusions emerged.First, across analyses, we found no robust link between IHI and autism: in case-control models, pooled odds ratios for every criterion and the composite were near 1, only few being moderately significant and none survived multiple testing. Second, when autistic subgroups were compared with neurotypicals by SRS-IQ subtype, differences with NT were only identified in low-IQ phenotypes: in the lower-IQ cluster no individual criterion survived FDR correction, but C1_L and C3_R were moderately elevated; in an intellectual-disability (ID) subgroup, C1 and C3 were moderately elevated bilaterally and C2_R was also increased, which together yielded an FDR-significant rise of the right-hemisphere composite score relative to NT. High or average IQ clusters did not differ from NT. Third, consistent with this IQ-centric pattern, correlation analyses showed a weak negative association between the composite IHI score and IQ that did not remain significant after FDR; associations with SRS were null, indicating minimal explained variance. Finally, criterion-level multiple regression reinforced the same through-line: after covariate adjustment, only C1_L, capturing hippocampal roundness, retained an association with IQ after FDR correction (standardized β = −0.09), whereas all other coefficients were small and non-significant and none related to SRS. Taken together, the data indicate that IHI is not a diagnostic marker of autism; rather, IQ stratification reveals a subtle signal, most consistently indexed by C1_L at the individual level and, at the group level, by a right-hemisphere composite increase limited to the ID subgroup, suggesting that IHI relates modestly to cognitive ability rather than to social-symptom severity.A plausible interpretation is that IHI indexes a mild deviation in hippocampal development with small downstream cognitive effects detectable only in large samples. Prior work suggests IHI is not tied to total hippocampal volume, yet greater IHI burden relates to selectively smaller CA1 subfield volumes; hippocampal volume also correlates positively with verbal IQ in typical development in male children. Together, these observations are compatible with the negative IHI-IQ tendencies observed here and predict subfield‑specific rather than global morphological correlates.

It is notable that C1, the only criterion with a corrected association to IQ in the multiple regression, captures the roundness/flatness of the hippocampal body. Similar alterations in left hippocampal shape have been reported by Arnaud Cachia, Claire Cury et al. in other conditions; for example, patients with schizophrenia who experience audiovisual hallucinations show flatter left hippocampi than both patients with only auditory hallucinations (Chi2 = 7.864, p = 0.005) and healthy controls (Chi2 = 4.028, p = 0.044).^5 Historically, this shape feature was among the first descriptors of IHI, lending face validity to its relevance as an anatomical marker.

Alternative explanations include residual confounding from cohort composition, measurement error, and site effects, as well as reduced power when analyses required both SRS and IQ. The ID group only counted 27 individuals, limiting the statistical power associated to this study. An additional limitation is the detection of right-hemisphere IHI, which is less prevalent and subtler, challenging automated pipelines. Improved or hybrid autism scoring methods may enhance sensitivity.

Overall, IHI is not a categorical biomarker for autism. Instead, it shows a small, FDR significant, SRS-independent relationship with cognitive ability, localized primarily to a left-sided shape criterion. In the future, volumetric and morphometric studies could help elucidate the underpinnings of this association.
