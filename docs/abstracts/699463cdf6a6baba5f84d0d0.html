Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major cause of long-term disability and is associated with persistent symptoms due to incomplete recovery1. Acute TBI is known to begin with a period of high excitotoxicity2, however, the mechanisms of injury and recovery long-term remain unknown. Examining neurobiological mechanisms is crucial for improving prognostic assessment and developing targeted therapies. The excitation to inhibition (E:I ratio) is thought to play an important role in pathophysiological mechanisms of many diseases and disorders in addition to TBI, including autism, schizophrenia, and other intellectual disabilities. Impairments in both glutamatergic and GABAerbic activity are known to occur for some period after TBI2. 

However, studying E:I dynamics in vivo in humans remains challenging. Biophysical modeling offers a promising tool to infer E:I dynamics from functional, non-invasive neuroimaging data. Parametric Feedback Inhibition Control (pFIC) models, which assume regional heterogeneity in synaptic properties, have captured developmental and cognitive variability linked to changes in GABAergic function3,4. This framework has not yet been applied to recovery trajectories after TBI recovery, but doing so may provide insight into how E:I dynamics evolve from subacute to chronic stages (6 months - 1 year after injury). Here, we applied pFIC modeling to longitudinal MRI and PET data to estimate regional E:I ratio both in individuals with TBI and in healthy controls (HCs). We evaluated TBI-related differences in E:I, as well as changes in E:I over time from 4-6 months to 1 year post-injury, and, finally, examined whether modeled E:I changes relate to changes in [11C]-Flumazenil (FMZ) binding to GABAA receptors. 

Methods

Longitudinal FMZ-PET, dMRI, and resting-state fMRI were acquired from individuals with mild to severe TBI and non-injured healthy controls (HCs). TBI subjects were scanned at 4-6 months and 12 months post injury; HCs were scanned twice ~6-8 months apart. DMRI and fMRI data were collected from 16 TBI patients (4 female, age 19-73, mean 47) and 10 HCs (4 female, age 33-77, mean 54). FMZ-PET data was collected from 7/16 TBI patients (3 male, aged 33–58, avg 48) and all 10 healthy controls. Regional binding potential (BPnd) to GABAA receptors was extracted via PMOD software5, and is thought to reflect neuronal integrity

Using the pFIC modeling framework3 to estimate E:I ratio, excitatory-excitatory (wEE) and excitatory-inhibitory (wEI) coupling strengths, regional noise amplitude (σ), and the global structural-connectivity scaling parameter (G) were optimized using observed data. wEE, wEI, and σ were expressed as linear combinations of the principal resting-state FC gradient and T1w/T2w myelin maps, yielding nine linear coefficients plus the global SC scaling parameter, G.These 10 free pFIC model parameters were optimized using covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES), minimizing the mismatch between various measures of functional connectivity (FC) derived from the empirical and simulated BOLD signals. Specifically, the cost function contained 3 terms: the Pearson correlation (r) between the z-transformed, vectorized entries of the upper triangular part of the empirical and simulated FC matrices, the absolute difference (d) between the element-wise mean of the empirical and simulated FCs, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) distance between the distributions of entries in the empirical and simulated dynamic (sliding-window) functional connectivity (FCD). The total cost was (1 – r) + d + KS such that a lower cost indicates a better fit to the empirical data. 

DMRI and fMRI resting state data was processed using standard pipelines and parcellated into 68 cortical regions according to the Desikan Killiany atlas. Group-level FCs (Pearson correlation of regional time series), SCs and FCDs were constructed for TBI and controls. FCD was computed with a ~60s sliding window for each subject, following previous work3, and averaged for each group. Structural connectivity via probabilistic tractography using streamline counts, and individual SC matrices were averaged for each group. Connections present in <50% of our HCs were set to 0. After fitting, the optimized pFIC model was used to simulate excitatory (SE) and inhibitory (SI) synaptic gating variables. After optimization, the model generated excitatory (SE) and inhibitory (SI) synaptic gating variables, and the E:I ratio was defined as the ratio of their temporal means.

Results

Figure 1A shows regional E:I ratios for TBI and HC groups with baseline metrics shown at the top and second sessions at the bottom and the relative % change in the middle. As shown in Fig. 1B, HCs exhibited a modest downward shift in E:I from session 1 to session 2, whereas TBI participants showed a broad distribution with generally lower values than HC at the subacute stage which subsequently narrowed at the chronic stage and increased to approach the HC distribution. Regional changes across sessions are quantified in Fig. 1C. A Mann-Whitney U test indicated significantly greater E:I change in the TBI group compared to HCs (z = 9.64, p < 0.001, r = 0.83). A separate pFIC model was trained on averaged SC, FC, and FCD from the 7 TBI participants with longitudinal PET and MRI. Longitudinal changes in modeled E:I were significantly correlated with changes in FMZ BPnd (Pearson r = 0.47, p < 0.001; 10,000 permutations; Fig. 1D), indicating regions with larger increases in neuronal integrity over time also had larger E:I increases.

Conclusion

Using the pFIC modeling framework, we identified a generally lower cortical excitation-inhibition (E:I) ratio in subacute TBI compared to non-injured controls, followed by significant increases in this metric  toward control ranges in the chronic stages after TBI. These results suggest a potential injury to recovery trajectory wherein the E:I balance favors lower excitation/increased inhibition at subacute followed by a re-establishment of E:I balance in the chronic stages. Regionally, TBI subjects’ sensory areas (i.e. visual, somatomotor) exhibited decreases in E:I ratio over time, whereas association regions (i.e. frontal lobe) showed increases, highlighting heterogeneous trajectories across functional systems and potentially network-specific neuronal activity recovery trajectories.

Importantly, longitudinal changes in modeled E:I ratio were significantly positively correlated with changes in FMZ-PET BPnd, indicating that increases in E:I ratio aligned with increases in available GABAA receptors. This relationship supports the sensitivity of the pFIC model to underlying GABAergic dynamics in TBI, and both measures could be revealing neuronal integrity increases in TBI recovery.

Together, these results demonstrate that biophysical modeling can capture circuit-level changes during TBI recovery and may provide a mechanistic marker for tracking chronic pathology, recovery, or compensatory processes after TBI.
