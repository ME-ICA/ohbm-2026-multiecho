Introduction

Neonatal critical illness can have a systemic impact disrupting early brain development and increasing the likelihood of neurodevelopmental difficulties. Previous studies have demonstrated persistent alterations in brain morphology of children born very preterm and with congenital heart disease.1–3 However, little is known about how other neonatal conditions, such as biliary atresia (an early-onset liver disease), affect brain development, despite emerging evidence of long-term neurodevelopmental sequelae across various early-onset medical illnesses.4 Investigating transdiagnostic similarities and disease-specific differences across conditions may improve our mechanistic understanding of early brain disruption and its consequences for neurodevelopment. Such insights could help identify windows of opportunity for neuroprotection and guide targeted interventions that may be applicable across patient groups. Therefore, this study investigates brain morphology and cognition in three adolescent cohorts with neonatal critical illness and compares them with healthy controls.

Methods

This is a secondary analysis of pooled data from multinational observational cohort studies (King’s College London, University Children’s Hospital Zurich, EU-AIMS) using comparable structural MRI protocols and neurocognitive assessments. Adolescents aged 10–24 years at assessment were eligible, including those with early critical illness requiring neonatal intensive care and/or infant surgery (very preterm birth <32 weeks’ gestation, congenital heart disease requiring infant open-heart surgery, or biliary atresia requiring Kasai surgery) and typically-developing, healthy controls. Using 3-Tesla MRI, three-dimensional T1-weighted images were acquired at 1x1x1mm voxel resolution. Cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentation were conducted with FreeSurfer and quality was checked by two reviewers. This analysis examined total and regional brain volumes. Cognitive performance (total IQ) was assessed with standardized neuropsychological assessments. Linear mixed models were used to test group differences of brain volumes and associations with IQ, adjusted for age and sex. Regional volumes were adjusted for total brain volume to delineate regional vulnerability. Site was included as a random effect to account for scanner-specific variability. FDR correction for multiple comparison was applied. 

Results

This analysis included a total of 515 participants (age: M±SD=15.0±4.1 years, biological sex: 57% male, 43% female). Of these, 58 participants were born very preterm (gestational age at birth: M±SD=28.4±1.6 weeks), 57 participants had congenital heart disease (age at open-heart surgery: M±SD=2.2±2.8 months, 68% cyanotic defect), 36 participants had biliary atresia (age at Kasai surgery: M±SD=2.1±0.7 months, 72% required liver transplant), and 364 were healthy, typically-developing controls.

Total brain volumes were smaller in adolescents with early critical illness compared to healthy controls, with the greatest reduction observed in congenital heart disease (β=–0.32, pFDR<0.001), followed by very preterm birth (β=–0.22, pFDR<0.001), and the smallest in biliary atresia (β=–0.10, pFDR=0.084), which did not remain significant after multiple-comparison correction. Regional alterations were disease-specific after adjusting for total brain volume: Adolescents with congenital heart disease had reduced corpus callosum volumes (β=-0.15, pFDR=0.002) and enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces (β=0.16, pFDR=0.007). Adolescents with biliary atresia had enlarged cerebral white matter volumes compared to healthy controls (β=0.06, pFDR=0.002). Higher IQ was significantly associated with larger total brain (β=0.16), cortical (β=0.26) and subcortical (β=0.19) grey matter, cerebral white matter (β=0.18), and hippocampus (β=0.29) volumes irrespective of group (all pFDR<0.05). 

Conclusion

This study pooled data internationally to enable comparisons of adolescents with different types of early critical illness. We replicated previous studies demonstrating altered brain morphology in those born very preterm or with congenital heart disease and provide first evidence for brain alterations in youths with biliary atresia – although these appear less pronounced than those observed in very preterm birth or congenital heart disease. Differences in prenatal vs. neonatal disease onset and condition-specific pathological mechanisms (e.g., hypoxia, liver toxins, infection) may contribute to these distinct patterns of brain development, but further research is needed to disentangle these effects. This can help identify sensitive windows for neuroprotection and guide targeted interventions both across and within disease groups. Importantly, the variability in brain morphology substantially overlaps across conditions, and simple group comparisons may mask substantial effects in specific subgroups. As a next step, we will apply normative Bayesian modelling to map individual differences against healthy control reference models, enabling single-subject predictions and improving the identification of brain abnormalities.
