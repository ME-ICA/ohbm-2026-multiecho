Introduction

Cognitive impairment (CI) is a core feature of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and related dementias. As CI progresses, individuals not only experience worsening memory and executive dysfunction but also develop a wide range of neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS), including apathy, depression, anxiety, irritability, agitation, psychosis, and sleep or appetite changes (Lyketsos et al., 2002). Although CI and NPS are often viewed as distinct symptom domains, recent evidence suggests meaningful interrelations between them (Eikelboom et al., 2021), though the direction of effect of these associations remains unclear. These disorders are posited to be neurobiologically linked, particularly in the context of cortical thinning (Zhang et al., 2024). Our current study investigates the longitudinal relationship between neuropsychiatric symptoms and cognitive impairment as well as whether cortical thinning serves as a mediator of this temporal dynamic.

Methods

Our study utilized 211 (age 73.0±9.1, female n = 123) participants from the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC) who completed at least two study visits with complete cognitive, neuropsychiatric, and structural MRI data. Participants ranged from cognitively typical (CT, n = 127) to mild cognitive impairment (MCI, n = 67) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD, n = 17). Cognitive impairment was indexed using the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDR). Neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) were assessed with the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI), which provides total symptom severity scores covering four domains (mood, apathy, hyperactivity, and psychosis). NPI scores in our participants range from 0-17 out of a possible 36. Structural MRI data were processed using FreeSurfer to generate cortical thickness measures from the Destrioux segmentation. Cortical thickness values were subsequently entered into pre-trained Bayesian lifespan normative models from the Predictive Clinical Neuroscience Toolkit (PCN-Toolkit) to generate longitudinal z-difference (z-diff) scores (Bučková et al., 2025) that represent the longitudinal change in the brain measure referenced to the normative aging-related longitudinal change. Thus, these scores quantify within-individual deviations from the normal age-related longitudinal trajectory of cortical thickness, adjusted for sex and site effects.

Feature selection was performed using an independent sample from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI-3) to identify brain regions for inclusion in subsequent NACC analyses. Within ADNI-3, we identified individuals with cognitive impairment but no neuropsychiatric symptoms (CDR > 0, NPS = 0; n = 46, 26 female) and individuals with neuropsychiatric symptoms in the absence of cognitive impairment (CDR = 0, NPS > 0; n = 39, 28 female). This contrast was designed to isolate brain regions preferentially associated between neuropsychiatric symptoms and cognitive impairment, independent of their co-occurrence. In ADNI, cross-sectional cortical thickness z-scores were generated using the same pre-trained normative models that were applied to NACC. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify brain regions that significantly differentiated the CDR+/NPS− and CDR−/NPS+ groups. Multiple comparisons were controlled using false discovery rate (FDR) correction, and regions surviving correction were the areas of focus for subsequent longitudinal analyses in the NACC dataset.

In NACC, to examine longitudinal associations between CI, NPI, and cortical thickness, we estimated two-wave cross-lagged panel models (CLPMs) using the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012) for each cortical region that survived feature selection. Each model included autoregressive paths for CI (CDR-SB) and NPS (NPI-Q) to account for temporal stability across waves, as well as bidirectional cross-lagged paths to assess longitudinal directional associations between CI and NPS. Cortical thickness z-diff scores were included as time-invariant mediators, positioned between baseline (T1) and follow-up (T2) clinical measures. Specifically, baseline CI and NPS predicted cortical thickness, which in turn was modeled as a predictor of follow-up CI and NPS. The model adjusted for age, sex, years of education, study site, and the interval between visits. Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square test of model fit and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA).

Results

Three brain region z-diffs survived feature selection in ADNI-3: right anterior insula, right sub-parietal sulcus, and left cuneus cortical thickness. CLPM models were generated for each brain region, the insula model is visualized in Figure 1. Across all models, CDR and NPI demonstrated strong longitudinal stability from baseline to follow-up, as reflected by significant autoregressive paths (CDR: β = 0.875–0.886, p < .001; NPI: β = 0.326–0.336, p < .001). Baseline cognitive impairment consistently predicted greater neuropsychiatric symptom severity at follow-up across all regions examined (β = 0.326–0.336, p < .001), whereas baseline neuropsychiatric symptoms did not predict subsequent cognitive impairment (β = 0.041–0.043, p = 0.355-0.372).

Regional cortical thickness z-difference scores for the right anterior insula, right sub-parietal sulcus, and left cuneus were not significantly associated with baseline cognitive impairment (insula β = 0.151, p = .082; subparietal β = 0.051, p = .509; cuneus β = 0.017, p = .848) or baseline neuropsychiatric symptoms (insula β = 0.014, p = .851; subparietal β = -0.026, p = .509; cuneus β = -0.106, p = .099) and did not predict follow-up cognitive (insula β = 0.068, p = .081; subparietal β = -0.011, p = .619; cuneus β = -0.011, p = .619)  or neuropsychiatric outcomes (insula β = -0.010, p = .880); subparietal β = -0.080, p = .090; cuneus β = -0.080, p = .090). These findings indicate a robust unidirectional longitudinal association in which worsening cognitive impairment precedes increases in neuropsychiatric symptoms, independent of regional cortical thickness differences.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate a robust unidirectional relationship in which baseline cognitive impairment predicts subsequent increases in neuropsychiatric symptoms, whereas baseline neuropsychiatric symptoms do not drive future cognitive decline. Contrary to our hypothesis, longitudinal changes in cortical thickness (assessed in the right anterior insula, right sub-parietal sulcus, and left cuneus) did not mediate these effects. However, we were not able to test sub-cortical areas such as hippocampus as it was possible to obtain z-diff scores only for cortical thickness measures. These results suggest that cognitive decline may be a primary driver of emerging neuropsychiatric symptoms in older adults, independent of regional cortical thinning, highlighting the importance of early cognitive assessment and intervention to potentially mitigate subsequent neuropsychiatric burden.
