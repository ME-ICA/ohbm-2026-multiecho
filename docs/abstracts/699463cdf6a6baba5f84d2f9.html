Introduction

Psychotic-like experiences (PLEs)—unusual perceptions, thoughts, or beliefs—are common in childhood and represent early indicators of subclinical psychotic symptoms. Although most PLEs do not progress to psychotic disorders, those that persist and cause distress in early adolescence are linked to later mental health problems.1 Emerging evidence suggests that mechanisms underlying PLEs differ across subtypes, particularly paranoid versus hallucinatory forms, highlighting the need for dimension-specific risk models.2

Structural brain alterations, especially in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), are critical risk factors for psychosis.3 Youth at clinical high risk who convert to psychosis show pronounced gray matter reductions in mPFC, a region undergoing significant remodeling during adolescence. Cortical thinning in this area has emerged as a key biomarker of future conversion.

Genetic liability may partly drive these changes.4 Polygenic risk scores for schizophrenia (PRS-SCZ) predict PLEs in youth and appear more strongly associated with paranoia than hallucinatory subtypes. Frontal cortical thickness is highly heritable and linked to PRS-SCZ, suggesting a potential mediating role between genetic risk and psychosis vulnerability. Yet, relationships among PRS-SCZ, mPFC cortical thickness, and distressing PLE subtypes remain poorly understood.

PLEs typically decline from preadolescence to early adolescence, a period of rapid mPFC maturation. Persistent distressing PLEs may signal elevated risk for psychosis.5 Understanding how genetic risk influences mPFC cortical thinning—and its contribution to paranoid and hallucinatory PLE trajectories—could inform early identification and targeted interventions.

This study aimed to (1) characterize developmental trajectories of paranoid and hallucinatory PLEs and (2) examine associations of PRS-SCZ and mPFC cortical thickness with these trajectories.

Methods

Design
This longitudinal cohort study used data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (Release 5.1), which enrolled children aged 9–10 years from 21 U.S. sites to examine brain development. Participants missing baseline genetics, neuroimaging, or PLE data were excluded. Written parental consent and child assent were obtained; procedures were approved by the University of California, San Diego IRB and local site IRBs.

Psychotic-like Experiences (PLEs)
PLEs were assessed using the 21-item Prodromal Questionnaire–Brief Child Version (PQ-BC). Two items captured paranoid experiences and ten captured hallucinatory experiences. For endorsed items, distress was rated on a 5-point scale (1=minimal, 5=extreme). Distressing PLEs were defined as distress ≥3. Domain-specific scores reflected the maximum distress rating per subtype. Data from baseline and three annual follow-ups were used to model trajectories.

Imaging Acquisition and Processing
Structural MRI was performed on 3T scanners using harmonized protocols. High-resolution T1-weighted images (1-mm isotropic voxels) were acquired while participants viewed child-appropriate movies. Cortical thickness was extracted using FreeSurfer (v5.3) based on the Desikan atlas. mPFC regions of interest included frontal pole, superior frontal, rostral middle frontal, and medial orbitofrontal areas.

Polygenic Risk Score for Schizophrenia (PRS-SCZ)
PRS-SCZ was computed using PRS-CSx-meta, which integrates multi-ancestry GWAS summary statistics (EUR, EAS, AFR, ADMIX) via Bayesian modeling and inverse-variance-weighted meta-analysis. Individual scores incorporated genomic annotations and ancestry-specific LD panels. The first four principal components were included as covariates to account for population stratification.

Statistical Analysis
PLE Trajectories: Latent class mixed models (LCMMs) were fit separately for paranoid and hallucinatory PLEs using the lcmm R package, adjusting for age, sex, and race/ethnicity. Only participants reporting distressing symptoms (≥3) at any wave were included. Models with 1–4 classes were compared using BIC and interpretability; four-class solutions were selected (Figure 1). For subsequent analyses, trajectory membership was dichotomized: increasing-distress vs. all others.

Structural Equation Modeling (SEM): SEM examined associations among PRS-SCZ, mPFC cortical thickness, and increasing-distress trajectory membership. Thickness values were residualized for intracranial volume, age, sex, and clustered by family ID. Model fit was evaluated using χ², CFI/TLI (>0.90), and RMSEA (<0.05). Sensitivity analyses used raw thickness measures. Analyses were conducted in SPSS (v29), R (v4.5.1), and Mplus (v8). Statistical significance was set at p<.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Descriptive Statistics

This study included 11,666 participants aged 9–10 years (mean [SD] age=9.92 [0.625] years; 6076 males [52.1%]; 249 non-Hispanic Asian [2.1%], 1751 non-Hispanic Black [15.0%], and 6081 non-Hispanic White [52.1%]; 2360 Hispanic [20.2%]; 1223 multiracial/other [10.5]; Table 1). The mean (SD) income-to-needs ratio was 4.16 (3.32), and 59.4% of parents held a college degree or higher. The mean (SD) financial adversity score was 0.47 (1.10).

Group-based Paranoid and Hallucinatory PLE Trajectories 

For distressing paranoid PLEs (n=2,411), the best-fitting model identified four trajectory groups: Increasing distress (n=325), high baseline distress, steadily decreasing (n=179), transient increase, then decreasing (n=734), high baseline distress, rapidly decreasing (n=1,173).

For distressing hallucinatory PLEs (n=3,934), four analogous trajectories emerged: Increasing distress (n=502), high baseline distress, steadily decreasing (n=751), transient increase, then decreasing (n=975), high baseline distress, rapidly decreasing (n=1,706).

After dichotomizing trajectory membership, 88 individuals had increasingly distressing PLEs for both paranoid and hallucinatory PLEs, representing 27% and 18% of those with increasing paranoid and hallucinatory PLEs, respectively. 

Associations Among PRS-SCZ, mPFC Thickness, and PLE Trajectories

Higher PRS-SCZ was significantly associated with reduced cortical thickness in the right frontal pole (β=−0.028, 95% CI=−0.045, −0.011) and marginally with reduced thickness in the left frontal pole (β=−0.020, 95% CI=−0.037, −0.003). Reduced right frontal pole thickness was associated with greater odds of increasingly distressing paranoid PLEs (aOR=0.95, 95% CI=0.91, 0.99). No other mPFC regions were significantly associated with increased paranoid or hallucinatory PLEs. Higher PRS-SCZ was directly associated with greater odds of increasingly distressing hallucinatory PLEs (aOR=1.05, 95% CI=1.01, 1.09). Sensitivity analyses using raw thickness values yielded consistent results.

Conclusion

This study identified distinct genetic and neural pathways underlying distressing paranoid and hallucinatory PLEs in early adolescence. We observed heterogeneous developmental trajectories for both subtypes, with a subgroup showing increasing distress from ages 9 to 13. Only 88 individuals exhibited increasing distress for both, underscoring substantial heterogeneity and suggesting partially independent processes.

Structural equation modeling revealed that PRS-SCZ was linked to both subtypes through different mechanisms: a direct association with hallucinatory PLEs and an indirect association with paranoid PLEs via right frontal pole thinning. These findings highlight symptom-specific neural pathways. Prior work suggests frontal pole abnormalities, influenced by genetic liability, contribute to paranoid ideation. The mPFC—especially the frontal pole—supports planning, decision-making, and self-referential processing; disruptions reflected by cortical thinning may impair emotional regulation and amplify threat detection, fostering paranoia. Functional studies show increased coupling between limbic regions and orbitofrontal cortex in paranoia, further enhancing salience attribution.

Hallucinatory PLEs may involve distinct circuits, particularly temporal lobe and hippocampal abnormalities that disrupt sensory processing and memory integration, leading to misattribution of internal experiences. We also observed hemispheric differences: right frontal pole thinning was associated with paranoia, consistent with evidence of right-lateralized prefrontal thinning in early psychosis. The right mPFC’s role in emotional salience may explain this specificity.

Our findings align with longitudinal evidence that frontal pole thinning precedes psychosis onset and accelerates in adolescence. Detecting these deviations in preadolescents suggests risk mechanisms emerge earlier than previously recognized. Although PRS-SCZ has modest predictive power, integrating genetic and neuroimaging markers could improve early identification.


Several limitations are noted. PLEs were self-reported; clinical interviews are needed. Trajectories covered ages 9–13, limiting inference about persistence into later adolescence. PRS-CSx improves cross-ancestry prediction but remains less accurate in non-European groups. Analyses focused on mPFC; whole-brain and network-level studies may reveal additional mechanisms. Environmental exposures and gene-environment interactions were not modeled.


Genetic liability for schizophrenia was associated with distinct pathways: via right frontal pole thinning for paranoid PLEs and directly for hallucinatory PLEs. Neural mechanisms differ by symptom subtype, and early adolescence is a critical window for detecting structural changes before psychopathology onset. Future research integrating genomic, neuroimaging, and clinical data will be essential for targeted early interventions.
