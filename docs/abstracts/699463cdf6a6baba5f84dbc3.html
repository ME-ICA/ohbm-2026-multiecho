Introduction

While plasticity of the immature brain is considered critical for optimal neurodevelopment, early brain focal damage can result in lifelong neurocognitive deficits, suggesting far more widespread damage than just the injured territory. The long-term inter-individual variability remains insufficiently understood.

Neonatal arterial ischemic stroke (NAIS) provides a valuable model for studying post-lesional plasticity in early development. In approximately two-thirds of cases, lesions affect the left hemisphere, most often within the middle cerebral artery territory. This lesion pattern typically damages one or several regions of the language network, particularly areas in the inferior frontal, parietal (dorsal stream), and superior temporal gyri (ventral stream). Remarkably, many affected children nonetheless achieve near-typical language abilities, supported by homotopic language reorganization in the right hemisphere. 

Studies have attempted to demonstrate the microstructural changes associated to right hemisphere language in left lesion patients, but most were restricted to one metric (most often FA), and a few tracts known to be involved in language processing (mostly the arcuate fasciculus ¹) 

In this study, we attempted to better understand the microstructural correlates sustaining atypical language lateralization (left lesions), as compared to typical language specialization (right lesions), in patients with NAIS compared with age related controls, while accounting for sex, and lesion severity. We used a multiparametric approach from 2 different diffusion models (FA, ADC and FD), in 14 tracts involved in language processing 

Here, we first present the microstructural characterization of language networks depending on the lesion side. Second, based on the functional characterization of language hemispheric lateralization, we investigated the correlations between language performance and both volumetry and microstructure depending on language lateralization.

Methods

The AVCnnADO project relies on a prospective cohort of subjects with neonatal stroke (n= 100 newborns at recruitment) followed until adolescence (n = 30, 14 F, 16M), who performed extensive language and neuropsychological testing and multimodal MRI at 16 years of age. After exclusions based on lesion territory, 31 patients remained. Age-matched controls (N=30, 14 F, 16 M) underwent identical MRI but simplified clinical assessments.

MRI data were acquired on a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma Fit scanner with a 64-channel head coil. Anatomical imaging included T1-weighted MPRAGE (TR=2300 ms, TE=3.05 ms, TI=248 ms, 0.9 mm isotropic) and T2-FLAIR (TR=5000 ms, TE=396 ms, 0.9 mm isotropic). Diffusion-weighted spin-echo EPI consisted of two shells (b=1000 s/mm², 30 directions; b=1500 s/mm², 60 directions), each preceded by reverse phase–encoded b0 images (TR=4.12 s, TE=55 ms, 1.8 mm isotropic).

A Sentence Generation Task (SGT) block-design paradigm was used to determine the individual language hemispheric specialization and stratify the patients.

Language Lateralization Index (LI) were computed from individual SGT Z-maps using the SPM12 LI toolbox2, Patients were stratified into Typical (left) (> 0.2) and Atypical language representation (< 0.2) groups for subsequent analyses. 

Diffusion MRI preprocessing was performed using MRtrix, including denoising, motion and distortion correction (TOPUP/EDDY), slice-to-volume registration, and bias correction. Given the alternating acquisition of fMRI and dMRI, AP/PA b0 pairs were assigned per shell to minimize inter-shell motion artifacts. 

Fractional anisotropy (FA) maps were computed from b=1000 images. Tissue response functions were estimated using five-tissue-type images derived from CAT12 tissue probability maps and lesion masks. Control-derived response functions were averaged to compute fiber orientation distributions (FODs) by spherical deconvolution, followed by amplitude normalization before computing FD. 

White-matter segmentation and tractography (60,000 streamlines per tract) were performed using TractSEG. Analyses focused on language-related dorsal (AF, SLF I–III), ventral (IFO, UF, MLF), thalamic (T_PREC, T_POSTC, T_PAR), and striatal (ST_PREC, ST_POSTC, ST_PAR) pathways, with CST as a control. Tract masks were visually inspected, and ipsilesional masks were corrected to exclude streamlines intersecting the lesion. Tracts with <5% of the group maximal streamline count were deemed misreconstructed because of the lesion and excluded.



For each tract, weighted mean FA, ADC, FD and tract volume were extracted using streamline density–weighted maps derived from tractography. Microstructural metrics were analyzed using general linear models (GLMs), including both control hemispheres and, for patients, the language-dominant and non-dominant hemispheres. The applied model was specified as:

Metric ~ Sex × Status + Status × Side + Lesion Severity

Where Status is a qualitative variable (Patient/Control), and Lesion severity is a semi quantitative variable, referencing the number of lesional territories as a severity index, built orthogonal to Status effect. 

Models including fewer than four subjects per subgroup were excluded.

From the fitted models, the Status × Sex and Status × Side interactions were considered of primary interest. The former was motivated by previously published sex-related differences 3, while the latter aimed to investigate potential effects of functional homotopy related to language dominance. Interaction effects were explored using post hoc marginal mean differences, residualized relative to corresponding control values [packages]. In the present abstract, only Status × Side interaction results are reported. Residual differences between patient hemispheres according to language dominance were compared using built-in t-tests and corrected for multiple comparisons across 14 tracts using false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Results

Severity, Status, and Sex effects (not shown)

In the non-dominant hemisphere, overall model results (not shown) revealed higher ADC (+5%, +5% per lesion severity level) and lower tract volume (−15%, −5% per severity level) in patients compared with controls, with effects increasing with lesion severity. FA and FD effects were more heterogeneous; however, consistent reductions in FA and FD were observed in the MLF and UF (approximately −4% per severity level). Local Status × Sex interactions were identified mainly in thalamic radiations, with lower FA and FD values in females (≈−4%).

In the dominant hemisphere, Status and lesion severity effects were of moderate magnitude and more spatially restricted (not shown). A Status effect was observed primarily for tract volume, with slightly reduced volumes (5–10%) in patients. More focal effects were detected for ADC and FA, showing mildly increased ADC (+2%) and reduced FA (−2%). Lesion severity was significantly associated with widespread reductions in FD. No or minimal Status × Sex effects were observed in the dominant hemisphere.

Side interaction effects: Figure 1

Figure 1 presents post hoc contrasts derived from GLMs fitted to ADC, FA, FD, and tract volume measures, focusing on the Status × Side interaction. Bars represent estimated marginal mean differences (± standard error) corresponding to [Patient − Control] contrasts for each hemisphere; the zero value on the x-axis corresponds to the control mean for the respective hemispheric contrast.

The associated t-tests assess whether
 [(Patient right − Control right) − (Patient left − Control left)],
 i.e., the residual hemispheric difference in patients, differs significantly from zero. Results are FDR-corrected across 14 tracts, with p-values < 0.05 indicated by a red star.

No significant differences were observed between left- and right-dominant hemispheres, despite marginal trends. In contrast, significant side-dependent differences were detected in the non-dominant hemisphere. ADC was significantly higher in the left hemisphere for the MLF, ST-PAR, ST-POSTC, T-PAR, and T-POSTC, corresponding to patients with left lesions and atypical language dominance, compared with the right hemisphere (right lesions and left lesions with typical language lateralization). Tract volumes were also significantly lower in the left hemisphere for the MLF, T-PAR, and ST-POSTC.

Although not shown, an additional ipsilesional/contralesional stratification analysis yielded comparable trends, though statistical power was reduced due to an imbalance in lesion laterality, reflecting the predominance of left-hemisphere lesions in neonatal arterial ischemic stroke.

Conclusion

These results suggest minimal differences in the dominant hemisphere, independent of language lateralization, once residualized for control hemispheric asymmetry.

In contrast, in the non-dominant hemisphere, findings indicate higher free-water fraction (as reflected by increased ADC) and reduced tract volumes, consistent with more pronounced atrophy in the left hemisphere following stroke when language shifts to the right. This pattern may reflect enhanced pruning or diaschisis of underutilized language-related structural networks in the left hemisphere.

Previous studies in perinatal stroke have primarily reported ipsilesional and callosal microstructural impairments or lesion-side–dependent alterations affecting other structural networks, notably motor pathways. To our knowledge, this is the first study in the perinatal stroke literature to investigate white-matter microstructure as a function of language specialization. Our results suggest that language lateralization plays a meaningful role in shaping post-stroke structural reorganization and contributes to the complex interplay between developmental, lesion-related, and functional factors underlying clinical outcomes after neonatal stroke.

Taken together, these findings, in line with our previous work, support a nuanced and multifactorial framework for understanding long-term outcomes and early brain plasticity following neonatal stroke.
