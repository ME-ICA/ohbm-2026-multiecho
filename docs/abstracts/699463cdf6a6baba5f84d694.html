Introduction

Large-scale neuroimaging studies have enabled the investigation of subtle associations between brain structure and complex traits, yet scalable multivariate methods remain limited. Brain-wide association studies (BWAS) are widely used due to their computational efficiency, but their mass-univariate nature fails to capture global brain–trait relationships and shared neuroanatomical patterns across traits. Linear mixed models overcome these limitations by modeling all brain features jointly, yielding biologically meaningful quantities such as morphometricity—the proportion of phenotypic variance explained by brain structure—and grey-matter correlation (rGM), which quantifies shared neuroanatomical signatures between traits or disorders. However, these models require individual-level data, substantial computational resources, and—critically for rGM—the availability of multiple traits measured in the same cohort, severely limiting cross-cohort and cross-disorder analyses.

Here, we introduce SumR2 Regression, a novel framework inspired by linkage disequilibrium score regression in genetics, that estimates morphometricity and grey-matter correlation using only BWAS summary statistics. This approach removes the need for individual-level neuroimaging data and enables fast, scalable, and previously infeasible cross-cohort analyses of brain–phenotype associations.

Methods

SumR2 Regression leverages the observation that brain vertices highly correlated with other regions tend, on average, to exhibit larger association statistics in BWAS (Figure 1). We quantify this property using SumR2, defined as the sum of squared correlations between each vertex and all other brain measurements. By regressing vertex-wise χ² statistics on SumR2 using weighted least squares, we obtain an estimate proportional to morphometricity. This framework naturally extends to a bivariate model, allowing estimation of grey-matter correlation between two traits using BWAS summary statistics derived from entirely independent samples.

Results

We validated SumR2 Regression using both simulated and real data from up to 39,655 UK Biobank participants with vertex-wise grey-matter measurements derived from FreeSurfer, as described in a previous paper1. Across 1,200 simulated traits with varying architectures, SumR2 Regression accurately recovered true morphometricity values and showed strong agreement with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimates, with modest downward bias only for traits associated with very few vertices. In real data, morphometricity estimates for 16 UK Biobank phenotypes were highly concordant with REML (r = 0.97), while standard errors were comparable or smaller for most traits. Importantly, computational costs were reduced by more than an order of magnitude, with run time and memory usage independent of sample size.

We further applied SumR2 Regression to estimate grey-matter correlations. Within the UK Biobank, rGM estimates showed strong agreement with REML for robust trait pairs (r = 0.93 for significant correlations). Leveraging summary statistics enabled novel cross-cohort analyses between UK Biobank phenotypes and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) brain association maps derived from a meta-analysis of 11 clinical cohorts (Figure 2). We observed strong grey-matter correlations between AD and known risk factors such as smoking, hypertension, and alcohol use, as well as novel associations with maternal smoking around birth and chronic pain. Negative correlations with number of children and age at first sexual intercourse suggest potential links between social or early-life factors and neuroanatomical vulnerability to AD.

Conclusion

Overall, SumR2 Regression provides a fast, accurate, and scalable alternative to individual-level mixed models, enabling cross-cohort and cross-trait analyses that were previously impractical. By facilitating the reuse of BWAS summary statistics, this method opens new avenues for large-scale collaborative neuroimaging research and the discovery of shared neuroanatomical mechanisms across traits and disorders.
