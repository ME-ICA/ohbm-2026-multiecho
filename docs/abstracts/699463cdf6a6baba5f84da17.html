Introduction

Even in the absence of overt pathology, aging is accompanied by widespread changes in both the structural and functional organization of the brain. Diffusion magnetic resonance (MR) imaging studies consistently demonstrate age-related decline in white-matter microstructural integrity, with more pronounced degradation in anterior compared to posterior white-matter tracts.1 Functional MR imaging studies indicate that large-scale brain networks in older adults exhibit lower within-network connectivity and higher between-network connectivity, reflecting reduced functional network segregation. These age-related reductions in segregation are also more pronounced in anterior brain regions that largely underlie heteromodal associative networks.2

Structural-functional coupling (SFC), defined as the extent to which functional connectivity is constrained by underlying white matter pathways, remains poorly characterized in aging. Prior SFC studies in healthy adults report mixed findings, including some showing negligible age effects and others showing age-related decreases when SFC is averaged across the whole brain (see review in 3). Network-specific SFC patterns in aging remain largely understudied. Research on biological sex differences in SFC is even more limited, despite evidence for sex differences in both structural connectivity (e.g., higher microstructural integrity of frontotemporal association tracts in females) and functional connectivity (e.g., greater segregation of association networks in females).2,4 To address these gaps, this study examines sex-stratified, age-related patterns of SFC across six large-scale cortical brain networks in healthy adults. 

Methods

Participants and data: Resting-state functional, diffusion, and T1-weighted data were acquired using a 3 T scanner (GE Discovery MR750, Waukesha, Wisconsin, USA) from 118 males (mean age: 46.5 years, range: 18.2–79.9 years) and 177 females (mean age: 48.9 years, range: 18.4–85.3 years) enrolled in the Calgary Normative Study.5 Cognitive healthy status for both sexes was defined based on a Montreal Cognitive Assessment score ≥ 24.

Defining anatomical regions of interest: T1-weighted images were processed using default parameters (FreeSurfer v6.0.0) to obtain cortical parcellations based on the 200-region, 7-network Schaefer-Yeo atlas.

Structural connectome generation: Diffusion preprocessing (MRtrix3) included denoising, Gibbs-ringing removal, intensity normalization, skull-stripping, bias-field correction, and motion and eddy-current correction. Single-shell, three-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution estimated fiber orientation distributions. Anatomically constrained probabilistic tractography with 107 SIFT2-generated, biologically weighted streamlines per subject. Cortical parcellations were rigidly registered to diffusion space, and the number of streamlines connecting each pair of regions was extracted and stored in subject-specific structural connectomes. The diagonal of each connectome was zeroed to exclude self-connections.

Functional connectome generation: Resting-state functional preprocessing (FSL) included intensity normalization, skull-stripping, interleaved slice-time correction, spatial smoothing (6 mm FWHM), and spatial independent component analysis. Components of neuronal and non-neuronal origin were automatically classified using FIX. Preprocessed functional scans were registered to cortical parcellations using boundary-based registration. Mean time series from cortical nodes were extracted and further denoised (Python NiBabel libraries), including regression of six motion parameters and their corresponding derivatives, squared terms, and squared derivatives, as well as mean time series from white matter and cerebrospinal fluid. High-motion volumes (relative framewise displacement ≥ 0.9 mm) were censored (set to zero) from time series, along with one preceding and two subsequent volumes (four volumes per high-motion event). Temporal bandpass filtering between 0.008 and 0.15 Hz was applied to residual time series. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between all pairs of processed time series and stored in subject-specific functional connectomes. The diagonal of each connectome was zeroed, and Fisher's r-to-z transformation was applied. Negative values were also zeroed from subject-specific functional connectomes.

Exclusion of the limbic network: Cortical nodes corresponding to the limbic network (n = 14) were excluded from subsequent analyses due to low MR signal in orbito-frontal brain regions.

Structural-functional coupling: For each participant, SFC was quantified using Spearman rank correlation per cortical region (nodewise) between corresponding structural and functional connectomes. Correlation values were averaged across network-specific nodes to estimate SFC values for the frontoparietal (FPN), default mode (DMN), dorsal attention (DAN), ventral attention (VAN), sensorimotor (SMN), and visual (VIS) networks.

Statistical analysis: Sex-specific generalized additive models (GAMs) were fit for male and female subgroups for each network. Age was included as a smooth term (spline), enabling estimation of potentially nonlinear age effects based on effective degrees of freedom (edf). GAMs results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Holm-Bonferroni method across the six networks for each sex. Model fit was evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient. 

Results

Figure 1 summarises SFC over age by network and sex. In both males and females, statistically significant age-related decreases in SFC were observed in the VIS (males: edf >1, F = 8.40, pcor = 0.015, R ≈ 0.30; females: edf ≈ 1, F = 16.77, pcor < 0.001, R ≈ 0.29) and FPN (males: edf ≈ 1, F = 7.92, pcor = 0.003, R ≈ 0.34; females: edf ≈ 2, F = 5.37, pcor = 0.01, R ≈ 0.27). In contrast, statistically significant age-related decreases in SFC for the DMN (edf ≈ 2, F = 4.07, pcor = 0.03, R ≈ 0.24) and VAN (edf >1, F = 11.58, pcor < 0.001, R ≈ 0.34) were observed only in females (males: DMN edf ≈ 1, F = 1.09, pcor > 0.05, R ≈ 0.04; VAN edf ≈ 1, F = 4.97, pcor = 0.11, R ≈ 0.18). No significant age-related effects were detected in either sex for the DAN or SMN (pcor > 0.05, R ≈ 0.04–0.10). 

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate network- and sex-specific age-related patterns in SFC. In males, linear age-related decreases in SFC were observed in the visual and frontoparietal networks, indicating a progressive reduction in correspondence between functional connectivity patterns and underlying white-matter pathways within these networks. In females, age-related decreases in SFC were also observed in the visual network, following a similar linear trajectory. In contrast, nonlinear age-related decreases in SFC were observed in females for several heteromodal association networks, including the frontoparietal, default mode, and ventral attention networks. These non-linear decreases suggests that structure-function alignment within these networks may be more vulnerable to age effects at later stages of adulthood. While the present cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, these nonlinear trajectories are consistent with the possibility of increased vulnerability or reorganization during later-life transitions that disproportionately affect post-menopausal females. Notably, SFC within the dorsal attention and sensorimotor networks remained stable across age in both sexes, indicating preserved alignment between structural and functional connectivity. Overall, these results highlight that age-related differences in SFC vary by both network and sex, underscoring the importance of network-resolved and sex-stratified analyses in understanding brain aging. Longitudinal studies will be necessary to verify these trajectories and to determine how changes in structure–function correspondence relate to cognitive, behavioural, and other biological markers across the adult lifespan.
