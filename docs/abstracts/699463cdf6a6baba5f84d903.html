Introduction

Infant temperament emerges rapidly in the first months of life, with early differences in temperament being linked to later personality and social development in childhood. Previous research, for example, has demonstrated that higher levels of negative affect and reactivity early in life are associated with increased risk of behavioral problems and increased risk of developing anxiety symptoms later in life1. One critical factor that is thought to impact infant temperament is a parent’s mental health symptoms experienced during pregnancy. For example, maternal depression has been linked with parents perceiving their infant as more temperamental and reporting increases in infant irritability2. Prenatal anxiety has also been linked with infants who are significantly more irritable and have increased negative affect3. In addition to prenatal mental health, research has begun to examine the neural correlates of infant temperament. Smaller subcortical volumes, such as the hippocampus, have been associated with higher internalizing scores4 while the cerebellum has been linked to social-emotional development in neonates5. Together, prenatal mental health symptoms and brain volume appear to shape early temperament profiles, yet few studies have examined these associations in a large, diverse, and longitudinal infant sample. Using data from the Healthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) study, we examined links between prenatal mental health and infant temperament, and whether neonatal brain structural volumes are associated with later infant temperament. We hypothesize that prenatal mental health symptoms will impact infant temperament, specifically that prenatal parents with higher scores on anxiety, somatic, and sleep measures will report significantly higher instances of negative affect and irritability in their infants. Finally, we hypothesize significant associations between subcortical brain volumes and temperament, including the cerebellum, putamen, and hippocampus.

Methods

Infants (0 – 16 weeks old) with available structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; bilateral cortical and subcortical brain volumes) data (N = 394) from the HBCD (data release 1.1) were included in the sample. 3D T2-weighted (T2w) volumes were collected with 0.8 mm isotropic resolution, full brain coverage, using vendor-harmonized pulse sequences discussed above. Compressed SENSE reconstructions were used to achieve high acceleration factors, resulting in shorter acquisition times, with total acquisition time under 9 minutes for T1w and T2w acquisitions. During the second study visit, T2w images are prioritized, as they provide greater contrast than T1w images at earlier ages due to high water and low myelin content. The structural data were processed using BIBSNet, an open-source, community-driven deep learning model that outputs native-space brain segmentations and masks. Preprocessing includes cropping the neck and shoulders from the averaged image using the SynthStrip-derived brain mask, which identifies the optimal axial cropping plane. Eta-squared is used to choose the optimal workflow (either directly or following ACPC-alignment), and the resulting pair is fed into the next segmentation stage. The aligned images are then segmented using infant MRI data (0-8 months) trained with neural networks (nnU-Net and SynthSeg). The resulting segmentations are then transformed back to native space, which generates ROI volume estimates and masks.

Infant temperament from three to nine months of age was analyzed using the Multidimensional Assessment Profiles Temper Loss Scale (MAPS-TL) and Infant-Behavior Questionnaire-Revised (IBQ-R) (N = 121). The MAPS-TL measures irritability and produces a single summary score. The IBQ-R, however, outputs domains of behavioral inhibition, surgency (positive affect), effortful control, and negative affect. Prenatal mental health symptoms, evaluated at the prenatal visit (first visit), were measured using APA Level-2 subscales of depression, anxiety, somatic, and sleep. Linear models were used to examine all research questions, and all models were adjusted for infant sex and data collection site.

Results

First, linear models were used to examine associations between prenatal mental health symptoms and aspects of infant temperament. The results indicated that higher prenatal mental health symptoms were associated with higher infant temperament scores in irritability and negative affect domains. Specifically, higher prenatal somatic symptoms were associated with higher infant irritability scores (β = 1.02, p = 0.018). Similarly, higher infant negative affect was associated with higher prenatal anxiety (β = 0.05, p = 0.035) and sleep scores (β = 0.08, p = 0.004). No other prenatal mental health symptoms were significantly associated with infant temperament scores (p > 0.05). Next, linear models were used to examine the associations between bilateral subcortical and cortical brain volumes and infant temperament scores (Figure 1). Higher infant surgency scores were associated with smaller brain volumes, including bilateral cerebellar cortex regions (right: β = -.0002, p = 0.03; left: β = -.0002, p = 0.01) and the right putamen (β = -.002, p = 0.04). This same trend-level association was also observed in the left caudate (β = -.002, p = 0.055). Smaller right hippocampal volumes were significantly associated with higher infant effortful control scores (β = -.002, p = 0.046), while smaller left ventral diencephalon (DC) volumes were associated with higher infant irritability scores (β = -.06, p = 0.003). No other brain volumes were significantly associated with infant temperament measures (p > 0.05). Together, these results suggest that early parental mental health symptomology and variations in infant brain volume are associated with aspects of infant temperament. 

Conclusion

In summary, these results suggest that prenatal mental health symptomology and variations in neonatal brain volume are associated with aspects of infant temperament. First, we provide evidence that increased prenatal sleep problems are associated with increased infant negative affect, while increased prenatal somatic symptoms are associated with increased infant irritability, beginning around three months of age. We also found evidence that supports previous work by showing significant associations of prenatal anxiety and negative infant affect3. These results provide valuable insight into prenatal mental health as a factor in infant temperament and identify critical early predictors that can potentially ameliorate the development of a child’s future psychological disorders1. Additionally, this is the first study to examine these prenatal factors on a large, longitudinal scale while also collecting infant MRI data. While recent work has linked the cerebellum with social-emotional development5, this study provides novel evidence that infant temperament is associated with early variations in bilateral cortical and subcortical brain volumes, particularly in the putamen, hippocampus, ventral DC, and cerebellar regions. Smaller bilateral cerebellar cortices and right putamen are associated with higher surgency scores, indicating that these regions are essential for early regulatory and approach-related behaviors. Higher surgency scores could reflect increased infant approach to novelty and reward, or increased activity. Therefore, smaller putamen and cerebellar structures could contribute to reduced regulatory modulation, leading to increased behaviors associated with infant positive affect. Additionally, the right hippocampus is associated with effortful control, supporting its role in arousal regulation. This finding suggests that larger hippocampal volumes could contribute to increased reactivity in early infancy, which could be reflected by reduced effortful control scores. Irritability appears to be associated with the left ventral DC, a region linked to stress and emotional reactivity, with smaller volumes being associated with higher infant irritability. These neural results indicate that the cerebellum and subcortical structures are potential early neural correlates of infant temperament. Taken together, these findings imply that both prenatal environmental experiences and neurobiological variation impact early infant temperament. Future research will be needed to replicate these results, particularly with subsequent HBCD data releases, which will contain even more data and will include more time points to examine trajectories of these infant temperaments. In summary, this work highlights the importance of examining early prenatal and neurobiological predictors of later emotional and behavioral difficulties.
