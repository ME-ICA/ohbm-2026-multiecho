Introduction

Understanding brain aging requires integrating heterogeneous information spanning brain structure and function, molecular biomarkers, cognition, clinical characteristics, and lifestyle factors. These features jointly contribute to brain health trajectories, yet differences in scale, as well as incomplete and uneven feature availability, have made large-scale multimodal integration challenging. As a result, aging is often studied through isolated modalities rather than through large-scale analyses.

Recent advances in representation learning, including large language models (LLMs), offer a new opportunity to construct unified embeddings from heterogeneous, subject-level data. By mapping diverse variables into a shared latent space, such models enable large-scale integration of heterogeneous data without requiring modality-specific encoders or rigid, model-dependent preprocessing pipelines.

Using multimodal data from 48,490 individuals across 16 cohorts consolidated by the iSTAGING (imaging‐based coordinate SysTem for AGIng and NeurodeGenerative diseases) consortium (Skampardoni et al. 2024), we construct a unified latent representation of brain health in aging using pretrained LLM embeddings. Rather than optimizing for prediction or diagnosis, our goal is to explore the structure of this embedding space and assess whether variation within it reflects biologically meaningful aging processes. As an initial interpretive step, we use targeted counterfactual manipulations of selected variables as a probing strategy to characterize latent dimensions related to neurodegeneration, metabolic health, and cognitive reserve.

Methods

We analyzed multimodal data from 48,490 participants drawn from 16 cohorts consolidated by the iSTAGING consortium, including the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), Australian Imaging, Biomarkers and Lifestyle Study of Ageing (AIBL), Biomarkers of Cognitive Decline Among Normal Individuals (BIOCARD), Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (BLSA), Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA), Harvard Aging Brain Study (HABS), Healthy Aging in Neighborhoods of Diversity Across the Life Span (HANDLS), Human Connectome Project (HCP), Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), Open Access Series of Imaging Studies (OASIS), University of Pennsylvania Memory Center cohort (Penn-PMC), Pre-symptomatic Evaluation of Experimental or Novel Treatments for Alzheimer Disease (PreventAD), Study of Health in Pomerania (SHIP), UK Biobank, Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study (WHIMS), and the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer’s Prevention (WRAP). The sample was predominantly cognitively normal (93.3%) and included a slight majority of women (53.6%). Available data spanned structural magnetic resonance imaging, functional connectivity measures, proteomics, cardiovascular risk factors, cognitive scores, lifestyle factors, and demographics. Feature availability varied across cohorts and modalities, resulting in incomplete and heterogeneous data profiles at the subject level. To flexibly integrate this data, we converted subject-level measurements into structured text summaries and embedded them using a pretrained LLM, Qwen-3 Embedding 8B (Zhang et al. 2025).

This textualization strategy enables integration of heterogeneous modalities without extensive preprocessing, modality-specific encoders, or complete feature availability, making it particularly well suited to large, multi-cohort settings. In addition, LLM models pretrained on broad and diverse textual corpora can implicitly model interactions among diverse features. The resulting unified embedding for each individual provides a compact and expressive representation for downstream exploratory analyses of aging processes.

To probe and interpret this space, we performed targeted counterfactual perturbations of selected variables. We modified one variable of interest at a time while keeping all other subject information unchanged. We highlight three representative examples among the myriad model explorations one can perform. In particular, we examined how hippocampal volume (as a marker of neurodegenerative change) and obesity and education (as modifiable characteristics) influenced trajectories in the comprehensive brain space. For each variable, we computed the average embedding difference between paired counterfactual conditions (e.g., lower vs higher education, non-obese vs obese), yielding a direction vector in the embedding space that reflects how variation in that feature manifests within the full multimodal brain-aging context. Individual subject embeddings were then projected onto each axis to obtain continuous scores reflecting alignment with that dimension.

We assessed axis validity by examining distributions of these projection scores across diagnostic groups, genetic risk (APOE4 genotype), and amyloid status categories for the hippocampus axis, cardiometabolic conditions for the obesity axis, and education levels for the education axis (Figure 1). To biologically characterize each axis, we then computed Spearman correlations across subjects between the projection scores and individual features, including regional brain volumes, functional connectivity measures, and proteomic markers, with multiple-comparison correction applied separately within each feature category (Figure 2).

Results

Across all three counterfactual variables, projection scores exhibited coherent stratification across clinically and biologically relevant factors, supporting interpretability within the multimodal embedding space (Figure 1).

The hippocampal projection axis showed graded shifts across cognitively normal, mild cognitive impairment, and Alzheimer’s disease groups, as well as across amyloid status and APOE4 genotype categories (Figure 1A), indicating sensitivity to neurodegeneration-related variation embedded within the latent brain representation. This axis was strongly associated with medial temporal, thalamic, and limbic brain volumes. Higher hippocampal-axis values were also associated with reduced large-scale functional connectivity across default-mode, attention, and frontoparietal networks. At the molecular level, this axis was associated with circulating proteins involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, oxidative stress regulation, and inflammatory signaling, including matrilysin, metalloproteinase inhibitor 4, extracellular superoxide dismutase, and chemokine-related proteins (Figure 2A). These patterns are consistent with biological processes implicated in neurodegeneration and tissue remodeling (Navakkode and Kennedy 2024).

The education projection axis showed systematic variation across educational attainment levels (Figure 1B), indicating that the counterfactual manipulation captured a meaningful education-related dimension of the embedding space. This axis was associated with larger hippocampal and subcortical volumes, and selective functional connectivity changes. Proteomic associations included markers related to growth and hormonal signaling, cytoskeletal organization, proteolysis, and vascular regulation (e.g., growth hormone receptor, alpha-actinin-2, myosin light chain 3, renin, and coagulation factor XI) (Figure 2B). These are processes commonly implicated in neural maintenance and resilience rather than neurodegeneration (Wilson et al. 2019).

For the obesity axis, a progressive elevation was observed with worsening cardiometabolic risk status (Figure 1C). The obesity axis was dominated by systemic cardiometabolic and inflammatory associations rather than focal brain structural effects (Ly et al. 2023), with strong links to proteins involved in lipid metabolism, endothelial function, proteolysis, and hormonal regulation, including fatty acid–binding protein (adipocyte), endothelial cell-specific molecule 1, insulin-like growth factor–binding protein 2, cathepsin B, and sex hormone–binding globulin. Structural brain correlations were weak and functional connectivity associations were modest and distributed (Figure 2C).

Conclusion

By embedding large-scale, heterogeneous multimodal data into a unified latent space, this study demonstrates that LLM-derived representations can capture interpretable, biologically meaningful dimensions of brain aging across imaging, molecular, and clinical domains. Projection axes derived from hippocampal volume, obesity, and education capture complementary patterns related to neurodegenerative vulnerability, systemic metabolic health, and brain resilience.

These results provide an initial step toward a unified, interpretable model of brain health in aging. Counterfactual probing serves as a practical entry point for interpreting high-dimensional, multimodal embeddings, motivating future embedding-driven analyses aimed at identifying dominant aging dimensions that arise directly from the latent space itself.
