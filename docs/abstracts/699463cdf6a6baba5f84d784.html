Introduction

LIDs, or Levodopa-Induced Dyskinesias, are involuntary choreic/dystonic movements that develop in over 40-50% of Parkinson's disease patients following chronic dopaminergic therapy1. Once developed, dyskinetic movements are extremely debilitating, impacting the quality of life considerably. The pathophysiology of LID and the differential neuromodulation of levodopa in PD patients with (dyskinetics) and without LID (non-dyskinetics) remains unclear. Our present study aims to (i) identify robust morphological and functional features in multimodal neuroimaging data that can distinguish dyskinetics and non-dyskinetics, facilitating early optimisation of clinical treatment, (ii) investigate the potential differential effect of levodopa on the cortico-subcortical motor and executive function networks in dyskinetics and non-dyskinetics, and explore its correlation with brain morphology, clinical characteristics and behavioural parameters, and (iii) look into the differential fMRI connectivity of caudate nucleus subdivisions (Head/Body/Tail) in both motor and cognitive loops. 

Methods

Multimodal MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma scanner from 32 PD patients (17 dyskinetic, 15 non-dyskinetic) and 21 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Structural (T1-weighted), resting-state, and task-based fMRI data were collected during a simple sequential finger movement task and a probabilistic reversal learning task2, probing motor and executive function, respectively. PD patients were scanned in both OFF and ON medication states. Analyses included voxel-based morphometry, vertex-wise subcortical shape analysis, ROI-based task activation, and ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity, implemented using FreeSurfer, FSL, SPM, and the CONN3 toolbox, respectively. Region-of-interest masks were generated for bilateral caudate (Head/Body/Tail) using WFU PickAtlas software4. Statistically, outliers (mean ± 2.5SD) were removed on a per-region, per-group basis. After running the distribution check and filtering the data, appropriate tests were conducted.

Results

Our results revealed dyskinetics have distinct region-specific volume alterations, including sensorimotor enlargement (p=0.0002) and inferior frontal gyrus thinning (p=0.025), alongside shape deformation (outward) (pTFCE < 0.05) in the right caudate compared to non-dyskinetics. In the resting state, dyskinetics showed disrupted corticostriatal-thalamic-cortical connectivity (p-FDR < 0.05). Task-based analyses revealed distinct motor and cognitive network alterations, confirming prominent executive function deficits and associated neural correlates. During the simple sequential finger movement (motor) task, dyskinetics showed significantly higher activation (both OFF & ON medication) in the bilateral caudate, right precentral, right middle frontal, right inferior frontal and bilateral paracentral gyrus than non-dyskinetics. Behaviourally, dyskinetics performed significantly worse (mean RT, p=0.012) in the OFF medication state than in the ON state. In the probabilistic reversal learning (cognitive) task, we found a significant effect of medication in the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex and lateral orbitofrontal cortex during the final reversal trials. ROI-to-ROI connectivity (p-FDR< 0.05) showed condition-specific disruptions in fronto-striatal and fronto-limbic connectivity in dyskinetic patients, particularly during cognitive processes related to error monitoring and reversal learning. Behaviourally, dyskinetics exhibited less cognitive flexibility, with significantly fewer reversals (p=0.012), fewer correct trials (p =0.034), and a higher number of trials required for reversals (p=0.003), compared to non-dyskinetics. Within caudate subdivisions, dyskinetics showed decreased connectivity of the caudate head and body with the precentral gyrus and the caudate tail with the middle frontal gyrus in the motor and cognitive tasks, respectively. 

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that LID is associated with convergent structural, functional, and network-level alterations within motor and executive control systems, with the caudate acting as a central hub exhibiting task-specific subdivision involvement. Together, these multimodal findings support the concept that dyskinesia reflects an imbalance between compensatory cortical plasticity and maladaptive network reorganisation, with concurrent deficits in both motor and executive control circuits underpinning the heterogeneity of dyskinetic symptoms in PD. The specific topographic disconnection of the caudate nucleus: affecting the head/body in motor tasks and the tail in cognitive tasks, suggests that LID is not solely a dopaminergic overstimulation but a systems-level failure of segregated cortico-striatal loops. These findings highlight potential biomarkers for early risk stratification and optimization of therapeutic strategies.
