Introduction

Human primary auditory cortex (PAC) is characterized by an orderly representation of acoustic frequency, or tonotopy. Namely, there is a well-established mirror-symmetric high-low-high frequency gradient centered on this region’s major anatomical landmark Heschl’s Gyrus1 (HG). Beyond PAC, it is unknown whether cortical folds, particularly small gyri known as plis de passage2 (PdPs), serve as anatomical landmarks for tonotopic map boundaries. It also remains unclear whether the functional organization of auditory cortex, as observed in vision, undergoes protracted development across childhood. To address these gaps, we propose the use of population receptive field (pRF) mapping in auditory cortex3 to quantify each voxel’s tuning properties across frequency space as well as the sharpness of this tuning. To innovate beyond prior limitations preventing auditory cortex mapping in pediatric populations, we designed two gamified auditory experiments which quantify both pRF properties during fMRI and frequency perception outside of the scanner. By examining how the topology of pRF parameters varies across the cortical sheet and also changes from childhood to adulthood, we hope to expand our understanding of tonotopic organization in human auditory cortex, its relationship to cortical folding, and the developmental origins of frequency representation in the human brain.  Through behavioral data in the same participants, we can provide some of the first neural correlates underlying the protracted development of frequency perception in humans. 

Methods

N=18 children (ages 5 to 10, 9 females) and N=25 adults (ages 21 to 34, 14 females) underwent structural and functional MRI as well as behavioral testing outside of the scanner. During their first MRI visit, children with normal hearing completed mock scanner training with biometric feedback to minimize head motion and to acclimate them to the MRI. T1- and T2- weighted structural MRI (3T, Siemens Prisma) was performed in children who successfully completed the training to reconstruct their cortical surfaces. In a subsequent session, they underwent fMRI while participating in a gamified tonotopic mapping experiment in which 12 pure-frequency tones were presented in random semi-sweeps. Participants completed 3 identical 6.5-minute runs, which were averaged to increase SNR. To evaluate developmental differences, adults with normal hearing also underwent structural MRI and completed the same gamified tonotopic mapping task. Data were fit according to the pRF model such that a 1-dimensional Gaussian with a preferred frequency (μ) and size (σ) was assigned to each voxel. Model fit was assessed by variance explained, with data thresholded at 10%. To relate the functional organization of auditory cortex to underlying structure, we identified PdPs in this region and quantified their proximity to and overlap with tonotopic map boundaries. In our analysis, we focus on a small PdP located posterior to Heschl’s Gyrus. Lastly, to determine the behavioral relevance of cortical frequency representation, all participants performed a gamified three-alternative forced choice (3-AFC) tone-in-noise detection task4 to measure their thresholds for detecting pure-frequency tones (150 Hz, 2000 Hz and 4000 Hz) embedded in white noise.

Results

We identified six novel PdP’s in human auditory cortex that could be identified consistently in all participants (Fig 1A) with anatomical locations so consistent that they survive cortical averaging and can thus be observed on the FreeSurfer average cortical surface (Fig 1A). When comparing the location of the PdP just posterior to Heschl’s gyrus to tonotopic organization, we observed that in 23 adult and 16 pediatric participants this PdP overlaps with the low frequency portion of an additional set of tonotopic maps in auditory cortex (Fig 1B). Qualitatively, both children and adults show high-quality data with strong spatial similarities of their tonotopic representation in superior temporal cortex (Fig 1C) with low and high frequency representations progressing in largely similar locations in the two groups. To quantify development of frequency representations, we compared the parameters of pRFs (μ and σ) between children and adults within individually defined tonotopic maps. Adults showed significantly lower μ values than children (p<0.05) in left PAC (Fig 1D), with no developmental differences observed in σ in either hemisphere. We also examined auditory cortex development by producing cochleotopic coverage plots in which we simulated each pRF’s 1-D guassian in frequency space and averaged over all pRFs to produce a “coverage” histogram in each participant, which were then averaged across participants within each age group (Fig 1E). In these cochleotopic coverage plots, we find that low frequency pRF coverage increases with age. Given that the greatest developmental difference in pRF coverage occurred within the frequency range of the human voice (age × frequency interaction: F(1,64) = 13.22, p < 0.001), we hypothesize that pRFs reshape with development to expand coverage of ecologically relevant tonotopic space. This developmental difference in frequency representation is consistent with results from our 3-AFC tone-in-noise detection task which demonstrated that children have higher thresholds for detecting low frequencies (150 Hz) embedded in white noise compared to adults (age × frequency interaction: F(1,26) = 7.71, p = 0.01; post hoc p < 0.05), but exhibit adult-like performance at high frequencies (4000 Hz). 

Conclusion

Our gamified tonotopic mapping paradigm allows for an unprecedented quantification of auditory receptive field development in children where such mapping was not previously possible and provides high-quality data in all participants beyond primary auditory cortex. In mapping auditory responses outside of PAC, we observe additional tonotopic maps that appear to be located consistently across participants relative to small cortical folds (PdPs) which have not yet been described in human auditory cortex. We find that this structure-functional coupling is also observable within children for a secondary tonotopic map located posterior to PAC. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the development of auditory receptive fields and tonotopic organization in children. By including children, we made the novel observation that auditory cortex pRFs undergo protracted development from childhood to adulthood and that this development is behaviorally relevant. In a gamified behavioral task requiring participants to identify pure tones embedded in noise, we find that children show adult-like performance for detecting high frequencies but demonstrate significantly higher thresholds for detecting low frequencies. From this work, we will be able to produce the first functionally informed neuroanatomical atlas of auditory cortex that also describes its development. This model will also provide the necessary framework for understanding auditory deficits implicated in several neurodevelopmental disorders.
