Introduction

The human brain contains large-scale networks whose regions display coordinated activity despite being distributed across the brain. It has long been proposed that some networks are engaged in antagonistic relationships, where the activation of one network leads to a deactivation of another. This has been observed both during a task-free resting-state and task performance.1 This antagonism (or anticorrelation) is thought to be important for cognitive performance and its alterations may be a marker of cognitive impairment and/or neurological and psychiatric conditions, including depression and schizophrenia.2 Prior group-wise neuroimaging studies have indicated one network in particular, the default network (DN), as being prominently anticorrelated with other networks, including the dorsal attention network, salience network, and/or frontoparietal control networks.1-3 Several unanswered questions remain regarding the nature of these antagonistic dynamics and the mechanisms that establish them, including which networks are anticorrelated with which, how consistent these relationships are across subjects and time, and why anticorrelations are typically diminished in magnitude compared to positive correlations. Further, recent within-individual precision fMRI studies have revealed that the canonical default network (DN) comprises at least two distinct networks, DN-A and DN-B,4 and it remains to be established whether these networks show antagonistic relationships with different systems. In this study, we leveraged high-resolution 7T fMRI data to address these questions with individual-level precision.

Methods

This study analyzed high-resolution (1.8-mm isotropic) 7T fMRI resting-state data from the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) collected at the Center of Magnetic Resonance Research at the University of Minnesota. Six participants (ages 23–27) contributed 100–180 minutes of resting-state data each (6–35 runs of 5 minutes), after excluding two participants for excessive motion. This data was preprocessed using slice-timing correction and spatial resampling to correct for head motion, EPI distortions, and gradient imperfections. We additionally applied bandpass filtering and regressed out head movement parameters, deep cerebral white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signal, and the global signal. We created individual-specific vertex-wise cross-run averaged blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) correlation matrices (Pearson’s product moment correlations; r), input to a data-driven multisession hierarchical Bayesian model (MSHBM)5 to define individualized distributed cortical networks on the surface. To investigate anticorrelations, a mean timeseries was calculated for each MS-HBM-defined network in each run, which was then correlated to all vertices on the surface to create maps and to the mean timeseries for each of the other networks. Within-run correlations were quantified, and r values were also averaged across runs within each subject. 

Results

We initially focused on SAL, which notably did not show reliable anticorrelation in any subject (except one) with DN-A nor DN-B. Instead, we observed that SAL was consistently positively correlated with frontoparietal control network B (FPN-B) and cingulo-opercular network A (CON-A) and was negatively correlated with dorsal attention network B (dATN-B) and a somatomotor (SM) network. These effects were confirmed to be of lower magnitude: positive correlations between SAL and other networks were around 2-3x greater in magnitude than cross-network negative correlations. We also focused analyses on networks DN-A and DN-B. We found evidence that DN-B shows broader anticorrelation with a cluster of networks that included CON-A and CON-B, SM and dATN regions. These negative correlations were again lower in magnitude than positive correlations. In contrast, DN-A was reliably anticorrelated only with CON-B.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest, that contrary to some accounts, the predominant pattern of antagonism of SAL in the resting-state is not with default network regions, but rather with SM and dATN-B networks. We also observed that dATN-B and SM were negatively correlated with DN-B, even though DN-B was not consistently strongly correlated with SAL. The results suggest that dATN-B and SM may transiently and independently enter into anticorrelated patterns with SAL and DN-B, a possibility that is the subject of ongoing analyses. Such a pattern could result in the lower magnitude of negative correlation typically observed, due to such transient effects being averaged over time. Additionally, this study confirms an antagonistic relationship between default and dorsal attention systems,1 but this dynamic may be localized predominantly within DN-B rather than DN-A. Furthermore, we observed that DN-B was anticorrelated with a set of multiple networks (e.g., “megaclusters”), which suggests this network may be particularly prominent in its antagonistic properties. Ongoing research is investigating what circuit properties may establish these antagonistic dynamics and how changing cognitive context may drive different antagonistic patterns.
