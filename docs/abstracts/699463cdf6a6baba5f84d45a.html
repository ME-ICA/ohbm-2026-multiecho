Introduction

Understanding the functional configuration of brain networks and how it relates to behavioral performance provides a more holistic view of the functional brain connectivity profile. The functional configuration of brain networks is often inferred from resting-state functional connectivity (rsfMRI) using multivariate approaches, such as principal gradient analysis1 or covSTATIS2,3. A fundamental step before characterizing the functional configuration is brain parcellation, which defines brain regions. Traditionally, group or atlas-based parcellation derived from healthy samples was used. In recent decades, researchers have delineated individualized parcellations to account for individual variation and improve the precision of brain mapping 4,5. Existing multivariate approaches do not allow different numbers of parcels or networks, which could vary across individuals. Here, we proposed IndivSTATIS—a novel multivariate method that extracts latent dimensions underlying multiple connectivity patterns and allows the number of parcels and networks to vary—and demonstrated its use in analyzing brain-behavioral relationships.

Methods

IndivSTATIS (Fig.1A) jointly analyzes each participant’s K × In correlation matrix (denoted Xn) that correlates two sets of time series: one derived from K parcels of a group reference (e.g., Gordon 333) and one extracted from In parcels of individualized parcellation of the n-th participant. Xn matrices are first normalized and concatenated, then decomposed by singular value decomposition (SVD) to form a common component space, called the compromise. The column factor scores represent individuals’ parcels in the compromise. With the compromise, IndivSTATIS places individuals in the same space, enabling direct quantification and comparison. To demonstrate this method, we used rsfMRI data from the Midnight Scan Club (MSC) with a group reference (Gordon 333 parcel) and individualized parcellations (OpenNeuro: ds000224)4. Other group references (e.g., Shaefer 400, vertex, shared network) can also serve as common references to achieve similar outcomes. We examined the relationships between such configurations and the Picture Vocabulary Task (NIH toolbox) and age (24-34y) from the MSC dataset to showcase its potential applications.

Results

Fig.1B shows the mean column factor scores of each participant’s networks, highlighting MSC01. Participants’ network configurations are aligned while allowing individual-specific parcels (e.g., UN29 of MSC01) and networks (e.g., mVIS of MSC01). Notably, UN29 of MSC01, a parcel located in the occipital lobe (a typical visual network region), showed strong synchronization with the default mode network (DMN), leading the community detection algorithm to classify it as a unique community. This node was correctly projected onto the compromise space close to MSC01 and others’ DMN. Fig.2 is an example of analysis using outputs from IndivSTATIS. Fig.2A correlated column factor scores with performance. It showed that individuals with greater segregation of the DMN from the cingulo-opercular and auditory networks along Component 1 (i.e., the attention component) exhibit better performance on the Picture Vocabulary task. Fig.2B quantified network homogeneity in the compromise and showed that 1) the four networks with the strongest correlation to task performance also featured medium-level age effects (Fig.2C) and 2) visual processing networks become more homogeneous from 20 to 30 years old (Fig.2D).

Conclusion

We introduced a novel multivariate approach for examining brain configuration with individualized parcels and networks, advancing precision human brain mapping. This level of precision is beneficial for studies of populations with known diversity in brain structure and function, such as adolescents, aging, lesions, and mental disorders.
