Introduction

Grey matter (GM) atrophy is a clinically relevant hallmark of multiple sclerosis (MS), linked to disability, cognitive decline, and disease progression.1 Regional GM loss appears early, advances over time, and follows non-random spatial patterns, with regions such as the thalamus shrinking faster than others.2 Substantial inter-patient heterogeneity exists, with MS-related atrophy patterns clustering into distinct subtypes that do not necessarily match conventional clinical phenotypes. 

Although GM atrophy in MS is clearly non-random, the mechanisms shaping its spatial and temporal organization remain unknown, especially at the individual level. Some mechanisms have been studied in MS, but in isolation using heterogeneous cohorts and methods, leaving their distinct contributions unclear.1 A network-based perspective allows investigation of broader brain organizational principles, integrating structural and functional connectivity with transcriptomic profiles from normative datasets.3-5

Four mechanisms have been proposed to explain GM atrophy patterns in MS: nodal stress, whereby highly connected regions (hubs) are more susceptible due to metabolic demand; transneuronal degeneration, in which atrophy spreads along brain connections; lesional disconnection, where MS-lesions disrupt white matter tracts leading to secondary degeneration; and transcriptomic vulnerability, entails that regions with similar gene expression profiles tend to be similarly affected by disease processes. 

Here, we systematically assessed these mechanisms of MS-related GM atrophy. We first examined them at the group level, then extended analyses to patient subgroups and individuals to capture inter-subject variability and identify mechanism-specific susceptibility, as well as potential epicenters of disease. Finally, we evaluated whether this network-based framework could predict future atrophy progression. 

Methods

We collected structural MRI and clinico-demographic data from people with multiple sclerosis or clinically isolated syndrome (pwMS), and healthy controls (HC), from three European cohorts (Amsterdam, Graz and Prague). All underwent 3T MRI, including 3D-T1w and FLAIR. Lesions were automatically segmented using LST-AI and used to fill lesions in 3D-T1w scans through NiftySeg’s lesion filling. T1w lesion-filled scans were processed with FreeSurfer’s cross-sectional and longitudinal pipelines to obtain thicknesses of 100 cortical regions from the Schaefer atlas and volumes of 14 subcortical structures from FreeSurfer’s Aseg atlas. Subject-specific structural disconnection matrices were derived by combining individual lesion masks in MNI space with a normative tractography atlas using the Lesion Quantification Toolkit. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using Schaefer-400 parcellation.

Quantifying atrophy

In HC, linear models estimated normative effects of age, sex, site, and intracranial volume (for subcortical regions) on cortical thickness and subcortical volumes. Each participant’s observed region-wise thickness or volume was then expressed as a deviation from expected values (W-scores), quantifying  baseline regional GM atrophy. W-scores were sign-flipped so that higher values indicated more severe atrophy. Group-level atrophy was summarized with Cohen’s d of the difference between W-score maps of pwMS and HC and tested using False Discovery Rate (FDR)-corrected t-tests. 

Network-based mechanisms

Connectivity strength was obtained using normative structural (SC) and functional (FC) connectomes from the Human Connectome Project dataset.3 Region-by-region correlated gene expression (CGE) matrices were derived from the Allen Human Brain Atlas.5 Nodal stress, transneuronal degeneration, axonal transection and transcriptomic vulnerability were operationalised as the spatial similarity between regional atrophy and maps of SC/FC centrality, neighbourhood atrophy (mean atrophy of a region’s first-degree neighbours) weighted by SC/FC, region’s disconnection, and CGE-weighted neighbourhood atrophy, respectively. Statistical significance of spatial correlations between each mechanism and empirical atrophy maps was assessed against null distributions obtained by 10000 random spatial permutations using the ENIGMA toolbox.3

Individualized  analyses

Mechanisms were also assessed at the individual level. For each pwMS, their W-score map was correlated with each network-based map, generating r-to-z transformed values of each mechanism. We compared these values across MRI-driven atrophy subtypes derived with the HYDRA algorithm and across clinically defined phenotypes (early relapsing (ER), late relapsing (LR), secondary progressive (SPMS), primary progressive (PPMS)), using FDR-corrected linear models.

To identify potential epicenters of neurodegeneration, we compared each region’s normative SC and FC profile with individual atrophy maps. Regions whose connectivity patterns most strongly matched atrophy were defined as candidate epicenters. Individual Fisher-z-transformed epicenter scores were tested at the group level using one-sample, one-tailed t-tests with FDR correction.

Longitudinal prediction

Longitudinal atrophy progression was assessed in pwMS with at least two MRI visits. Regional trajectories were estimated using linear mixed-effects models, resulting in group-level and subject-specific annualized atrophy rates. Using baseline data, we modelled subsequent atrophy progression with generalized additive models incorporating non-linear terms for network-based predictors, baseline atrophy, demographics, follow-up duration, spatial coordinates, and random effects for patients and regions. To assess the added value of network-derived metrics in predicting subsequent atrophy, we also fitted a baseline model lacking network measures, and we evaluated the predictive value using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and likelihood-ratio testing.

Results

We included 1963 pwMS (70% female, 45.3±9.8 years) and 224 HC (62.5% female, 41.1±12.4 years) from three European centers. For 565 pwMS with longitudinal MRI data (mean follow-up 3.2 years), the first MRI scan was used for cross-sectional analyses, while all timepoints were used for longitudinal modelling. Relative to HC, pwMS showed widespread deep GM atrophy, most pronounced in the thalami (Cohen’s d=1.07, p<0.001), and cortical thinning in areas like temporo-polar, sensorimotor, posterior cingulate and precuneal cortices (Figure 2). 

At the group-level, cortical regions with high cortico-cortical functional strength exhibited greater atrophy in pwMS (r=0.38, p=0.01), unlike subcortical regions or structural hubs (nodal stress). Regional cortical atrophy was associated with that of functionally (r=0.41, p=0.005) and structurally (r=0.51, p=0.004) connected neighbours, whereas this effect was not present for subcortical regions (transneuronal degeneration). Trend-level relationships were observed between atrophy and lesion-estimated disconnection for cortico-cortical and subcortico-cortical regions (lesional disconnection), as well as between cortico-cortical  or subcortico-cortical atrophy with transcriptomically dissimilar cortical areas (transcriptomic vulnerability). 

Individual-subject analyses revealed significant expression of all mechanisms (pFDR< 0.05), with the largest effect sizes again observed for structural and functional neighbourhood atrophy (d = 1.76 and 0.61, respectively). A data-driven HYDRA analysis identified two atrophy subtypes (i.e. widespread cortical/subcortical and selective subcortical) with stronger network-mechanism expression in the widespread subtype. When analyzing individual results across different clinical phenotypes, we found that mechanisms were also more pronounced in progressive and late-relapsing MS compared with early-relapsing disease.

We identified disease epicenters by correlating connectivity profiles of each region with individual atrophy maps. Both functional and structural epicenters were found in the visual, sensorimotor, and temporal cortices, as well as in deep GM regions like the thalamus, hippocampus, and amygdala. These analyses therefore indicate that these regions may serve as main areas where neurodegeneration could start and spread (Figure 2). All findings replicated in a 400-parcel cortical atlas.

We built a generalized additive model (GAM) to predict future atrophy progression in individual patients using baseline information. The network-based model explained 30.3% of the deviance in longitudinal atrophy progression (adjusted R² = 0.30). Key predictors included structural and functional network strength, lesional disconnection, and neighbourhood atrophy, significantly improving prediction over a baseline model (AIC: 518,785.0 vs 519,108.9, p < 0.001).

Conclusion

We investigated network-based mechanisms driving GM atrophy in MS using MRI from a large multi-center cohort. Our findings suggest that functional cortico-cortical hubs are more susceptible to MS-related atrophy, supporting the nodal stress hypothesis. We also found that transneuronal degeneration plays a key role in atrophy, propagating along anatomically and functionally connected regions. These mechanisms appear to be specific to MS-related atrophy, operating differently across disease subgroups or stages. Lesional disconnection and transcriptomic vulnerability had less impact on atrophy. While MS pathology is traditionally linked to lesions and inflammation, our findings suggest that disease progression can occur independently of acute inflammatory activity. Nodal stress and transneuronal degeneration may be crucial for driving atrophy even in the absence of new lesions, offering potential targets for treatments aimed at slowing disease progression.

Functional hubs showed significant associations with cortical atrophy, while structural hubs did not. This may suggest that anatomically connected regions are protected due to a structural redundancy as a compensatory mechanism, while functional hubness may increase vulnerability, possibly due to metabolic demand. Beyond hub status, cortical areas with stronger structural and functional neighbor atrophy exhibited greater atrophy, supporting the transneuronal degeneration hypothesis, were atrophy spreads along brain connections. 

Lesional disconnection did not show associations with GM atrophy, likely due to a plateau effect of lesion-induced damage. The impact of lesion-induced disconnection may peak early after a lesion occurs, making it harder to detect in cross-sectional studies. Moreover, binary lesion masks do not fully capture lesion heterogeneity, and diffusion MRI could offer a more quantitative view of white matter damage. No relationship was found between regional GM atrophy and transcriptomic vulnerability, indicating that atrophy in MS might be specifically induced by the disease rather than premorbid genetic factors.

Data-driven analyses identified two distinct patterns of GM atrophy: one with widespread cortical and subcortical neurodegeneration, and the other with selective subcortical atrophy and relatively preserved cortex. These patterns align with previous MRI-driven subtypes and suggest distinct modes of atrophy rather than consecutive disease stages. Network-based mechanisms were more strongly associated with widespread atrophy and advanced disease stages, reinforcing the hypothesis that these mechanisms are specific to MS atrophy, and supporting the notion of a network collapse underlying clinical progression.

We also identified disease epicenters in the visual, sensorimotor, and temporal cortices, and in deep GM areas like hippocampus and thalamus. These regions may serve as starting points for the propagation of neurodegeneration. Notably, thalamic atrophy and secondary visual cortex atrophy are common early manifestations of MS. This underlines the hypothesis that atrophy in MS is not random, not only regarding its underlying mechanisms, but also with regard to longitudinal progression. This could have implications for patient management, identifying which regions to monitor for early treatment escalation.

Finally, longitudinal modeling showed that network-derived metrics enhance the prediction of future atrophy progression. Patient-specific network information was more strongly associated with future progression than normative connectomes, emphasizing the need for individualized network analyses in predicting disease courses. Although challenging, implementing this information in patient care could therefore provide more accurate disease prediction algorithms.
