Introduction

Epileptic seizures are increasingly recognized as disturbances of large-scale brain networks rather than isolated focal events. It is essential to understand how functional brain connectivity differs across seizure types for characterizing seizure dynamics and propagation. Scalp electroencephalography (EEG) is a non-invasive tool to study the electrical discharges of the brain, and it has been widely used to interpret the network connections [1]. Studies have explored various coherence measures, such as phase locking value, and magnitude-squared coherence to analyze the patterns of functional connectivity. However, these measures are affected by volume conduction and common reference effects. Imaginary coherence addresses this limitation by capturing non–zero-lag interactions and provides a more physiologically meaningful estimate of functional connectivity [2]. In this study, we examine how the imaginary coherence based connectivity patterns differ in the ictal state of varied seizure types, including focal non-motor seizures (FNSZ), generalized non-motor seizures (GNSZ), and tonic–clonic seizures (TCSZ). 

Methods

The scalp EEG of ten epilepsy patients were considered for this study [3]. Standard 10–20 EEG channels were selected and mapped to a common scalp montage. Expert annotations were used to extract ictal segments corresponding to FNSZ, GNSZ, and TCSZ. The signals were preprocessed using a band-pass filter between 0.5 and 80 Hz to select the useful frequency range and a notch filter to remove power-line interference. Functional connectivity was estimated using imaginary coherence for each ictal segment, resulting in weighted connectivity matrices [1]. These matrices were used to build functional brain networks. Graph-theoretical metrics, including network strength and clustering coefficient were extracted to quantify global integration and local segregation, respectively [4]. Results

The results reveal the distinct functional network patterns across all the three seizure types. It is observed from Figure 1(a) that FNSZ network exhibits relatively sparse and spatially restricted connectivity, with stronger coherence between T3-P3 channels. On the other hand, GNSZ and TCSZ show dense and widespread interactions between channels spanning frontal, temporal and central regions, thus indicating increased synchronization during generalized seizure activity. To be precise, FNSZ connectivity matrices demonstrates localized or focal coupling patterns, while GNSZ and TCSZ illustrate globally distributed connectivity with higher overall connection strength.

Further, the connectivity matrices are quantified using clustering coefficient and network strength and their distribution across seizure types are illustrated in Figure 2. The results show clear distinction between the three seizure types. The extracted features show statistically significant difference across the seizure types (p < 0.05). The median of clustering coefficient is found to be higher in GNSZ, followed by TCSZ which indicates that these two seizures show stronger node connectivity compared to FNSZ. Similarly, network strength follows the same trend, confirming the globally synchronized patterns in TCSZ and FNSZ. Overall, the findings suggest that the proposed brain network connectivity analysis framework has the potential to discriminate the seizure types and may be useful in clinical decision making.

Conclusion

Focal non-motor seizures are characterized by locally clustered networks, whereas generalized and tonic–clonic seizures exhibit increased global synchronization. The findings of the present study support this network-level interpretation of seizure dynamics and demonstrate a potential for robust EEG based connectivity analysis in epilepsy. Thus, the proposed functional connectivity based approach may serve as a valid and useful tool for the classification of clinical seizure types and assist clinicians in making appropriate treatment decisions.
