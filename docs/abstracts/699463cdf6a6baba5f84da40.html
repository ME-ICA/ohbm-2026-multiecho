Introduction

Cerebral perfusion has been known to change with age ^1 that has led to many large consortia acquiring ASL data to non-invasively map perfusion. However, these studies have acquired data at low-spatial resolutions (e.g., ≥3.5mm in-plane and 5-7mm slice thickness). Whilst this may be sufficient to map cortical perfusion and age- and disease-related perfusion changes at the scale of whole brain areas, it is insufficient to resolve ^2 smaller, deeper brain areas such as hippocampus, thalamic nuclei that are known to be impacted in disorders such as Alzheimer’s ^3 or Parkinson’s ^4. For this ongoing cross-sectional study, we optimised a high-resolution ASL protocol (2.5 mm isotropic voxels) to acquire CBF maps in clinically-feasible times (< 6 min) at 3 T, and demonstrate its utility to capture perfusion-changes in thalamic nuclei in a cohort of cognitively normal older adults.

Methods

2.5 mm isotropic 3D-GRASE pCASL (TR/TE=3.93s/20ms, GRAPPA 3, PF=6/8, Label=1.8s,PLD=1.8s,TF=12,Seg=4,EPI Factor=27,BW=2480Hz/Px, TA=5:42 min) was acquired in 35 healthy older adults (24F, 11M) aged 56.0 to 84.5 years (mean ± SD = 71.9 ± 8.1 years) on a Siemens Prisma 3T (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen). An M0-weighted image was acquired with opposite phase encoding for distortion correction. Anatomical data were acquired using a 1 mm isotropic 3D-MPRAGE (256 slices, GRAPPA 2, TE 3.11 ms, TR 2100 ms, TI=915 ms,TA=4:20 min). ASL data were processed using oxasl, and THOMAS ^5 was used to obtain delineations of thalamus and its nuclei. Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs, antsMultivariateTemplateConstruction2.sh) was used for all registrations and cohort template building.

Results

Group average THOMAS parcellation (Fig 1a) and corresponding volumes (Fig 1b) are consistent with Su et al. 2019 ^5. Perfusion-weighted image and CBF maps (Fig 1c) show observable differences in the thalamus (white outline) and the mean CBF (Fig 1d) differed markedly across thalamic subregions. The habenula exhibited the lowest perfusion (~35 ml/100 g/min), whereas the anteroventral and mediodorsal nuclei showed the highest (~73 ml/100 g/min). Most nuclei fall within one standard deviation of the global thalamic mean (~56 ± 23 ml/100 g/min) and show negligible lateralization. There was a positive trend between nuclei volume and mean CBF (r(10) = 0.50, p = 0.098, R² = 0.25), indicative of higher perfusion in larger nuclei. A non-significant inverse relationship between age and perfusion (slope ≈ –0.15 ml/100 g/min per year) can be observed (Fig 1e). Region‐specific trends are similar between sexes, with females having consistently higher CBF by ~5–15 ml/100 g/min (Fig 1f).

We demonstrate the feasibility of a 2.5 mm isotropic 3D-GRASE pCASL protocol at 3 T to resolve perfusion differences across individual thalamic nuclei in a clinically acceptable scan time (< 6 min) in 35 cognitively normal older adults. We show an approximately two‐fold range in baseline CBF across nuclei (Hb ~ 35 ml/100 g/min to AV and MD ~ 73 ml/100 g/min). The observed perfusion differences indicate differing cytoarchitecture and metabolic demands of thalamic subregions. As this is an ongoing study, our results establish a normative baseline to compare subregional thalamic dysfunction in early diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression in Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease.

Conclusion

Despite a relatively small sample size (N=35), we show high-resolution ASL at 3 T overcomes the limitations of conventional ASL to non-invasively map perfusion and resolve CBF changes in small, deep grey-matter structures.
