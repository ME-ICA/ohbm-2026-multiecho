Introduction

Insomnia disorder (ID) is a highly prevalent condition characterized by difficulties initiating and maintaining sleep and by substantial daytime impairment, yet its objective neurophysiological markers remain poorly defined1. Cognitive and hyperarousal models propose that excessive vigilance and sleep-related worry interfere with the normal downregulation of arousal at sleep onset, but existing EEG and static fMRI indices primarily capture global state differences rather than the neural–physiological dynamics of falling asleep.

The discovery of the glymphatic system has provided a compelling framework linking sleep to metabolite clearance, whereby slow vasomotor and hemodynamic oscillations drive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow through perivascular pathways and promote removal of neurotoxic waste2. Building on this work, recent rs-fMRI studies have identified a characteristic lagged coupling between the global BOLD (gBOLD) signal and ventricular CSF inflow as a noninvasive marker of glymphatic function and arousal-related brain dynamics3.However, this gBOLD–CSF coupling has been studied almost exclusively in healthy participants during conventional resting-state (CRS) conditions, leaving its role in insomnia and, critically, during the vulnerable stage of sleep initiation largely unknown.

To more directly probe this transition, we developed a “try-to-sleep” (TTS) paradigm, which instructs participants to close their eyes and allow themselves to drift toward sleep, thereby transforming spontaneous drowsiness fluctuations from a confound into an experimental feature4. Leveraging this approach, the present study investigates whether gBOLD–CSF coupling is disrupted in ID during sleep initiation, how such disruption differs between TTS and CRS conditions, whether coupling measures relate to insomnia severity and subjective sleep quality, and the extent to which its temporal variability and spatial distribution are altered in ID.

Methods

Participants and clinical assessment

This case–control study recruited patients with ID from the Sleep Psychology Center of Daping Hospital and Southwest University, and age-, sex-, and education-matched healthy controls (HCs) from the community. All participants completed semi-structured clinical interviews and standardized questionnaires assessing sleep quality (PSQI), insomnia severity (ISI), and depressive symptoms (BDI-II). Inclusion criteria for ID were chronic insomnia according to ICSD-3, age 18–65 years, and absence of other major psychiatric, neurological, or sleep disorders. HCs had no history of sleep complaints or mental disorders and scored below clinical cut-offs on PSQI and ISI. After excluding participants who fell asleep in the scanner, showed excessive head motion, or could not complete the MRI protocol, the final sample comprised 34 ID patients and 21 HCs. All procedures were approved by the local ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained.

Experimental design and MRI acquisition

Figure 1 summarizes the overall workflow from screening and clinical assessment to imaging and gBOLD–CSF coupling analysis. Each participant underwent one high-resolution T1 scan and two fMRI runs in a single session: a CRS run and a TTS run. In CRS, participants were instructed to rest quietly with eyes closed; in TTS, they were asked to keep their eyes closed, remain still, and allow themselves to drift toward sleep. Scan order was randomized, and all sessions were scheduled between 14:00 and 17:00 to reduce circadian variability. Imaging was performed on a 3T Siemens Prisma system with a 64-channel head coil. Functional images were acquired using a T2*-weighted EPI sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, 62 slices, voxel size = 2×2×2.3 mm³, 200 volumes per run). Structural images were obtained using a 1-mm isotropic T1-weighted sequence.

Preprocessing and signal extraction

Functional data were preprocessed in DPABI, including slice-timing correction, realignment, spatial smoothing (6-mm FWHM), linear detrending, and band-pass filtering (0.01–0.10 Hz). The gBOLD signals were extracted from individualized gray-matter masks derived from the Harvard–Oxford atlas in native space. Within each mask, voxelwise time series were z-standardized and averaged to yield the gBOLD time course. CSF signals were extracted from manually delineated masks on the bottom slice of each fMRI run near the cerebellar base, exploiting inflow-related sensitivity to CSF dynamics. Masks were drawn and cross-checked by two trained raters.

The gBOLD–CSF coupling, dynamics, and spatial analyses

For each run, gBOLD and CSF signals were z-scored and entered into cross-correlation analyses over temporal lags of ±20 s. Following prior work, the negative peak at +4 s (CSF following gBOLD downswings) was taken as the primary coupling index. To capture temporal dynamics, we applied a sliding-window approach (window length 80 s, step 8 s) and computed lag-4-s coupling within each window. For spatial analyses, BOLD signals were also averaged within seven canonical resting-state networks (Yeo 7-network atlas), and network-specific BOLD–CSF coupling indices were derived in the same way.

Statistical analysis

Condition (CRS vs. TTS) and group (ID vs. HC) effects on coupling indices were tested using mixed ANOVAs, with post-hoc t-tests where appropriate. Sliding-window trajectories were modeled with linear mixed-effects models including time, condition, group, and their interactions as fixed effects and subject as a random factor. Associations between coupling measures and clinical scores were examined with partial correlations controlling for age, sex, education, and depressive symptoms.

Results

Sample characteristics

The final sample included 34 patients with ID and 21 HCs. The two groups did not differ in age, gender, or years of education (all p > 0.05), and the ID group showed markedly higher PSQI, ISI, and BDI-II scores than HCs (all p < 0.001), confirming substantial sleep and mood disturbances in the clinical group.

Global gBOLD–CSF coupling and condition effects

Cross-correlation analyses revealed the canonical gBOLD-CSF profile in both groups: a positive peak at lag −4 s and a negative peak at lag +4 s, with all lag +4 s coefficients significantly below permutation-based null distributions (all p < 0.001). Coupling indices at lag +4 s were ID-CRS: -0.26, ID-TTS: -0.23, HC-CRS: -0.18, and HC-TTS: -0.33 (Figure 2A and B). 2 (Group: ID vs. HC) × 2 (Condition: CRS vs. TTS) ANOVA on the lag +4 s index showed no main effect of group (F₁, ₅₃ = 0.01, p = 0.914), a trend-level main effect of condition (F₁, ₅₃ = 3.33, p = 0.074), and a significant Group × Condition interaction (F₁, ₅₃ = 6.82, p = 0.012, η²p = 0.114). Post-hoc tests indicated that HCs exhibited stronger coupling during TTS than CRS (t₂₀ = 2.35, p = 0.029), whereas ID patients showed no condition-related modulation (t₃₄ = −0.80, p = 0.430, Figure 2C). These findings suggest that flexible, state-dependent enhancement of gBOLD–CSF coupling during sleep initiation is preserved in HCs but blunted in ID.

Clinical associations

Within the ID group, stronger (more negative) gBOLD–CSF coupling was significantly associated with greater insomnia severity and poorer subjective sleep quality. Specifically, coupling correlated positively with ISI (CRS: r = 0.42, FDR-p = 0.036; TTS: r = 0.54, FDR-p = 0.009) and PSQI (CRS: r = 0.40, FDR-p = 0.041; TTS: r = 0.46, FDR-p = 0.024). These associations remained significant for TTS after controlling for age, gender, education, and BDI-II (ISI: r = 0.52, FDR-p = 0.024; PSQI: r = 0.48, FDR-p = 0.024). In contrast, no significant correlations were observed in HCs (Figure 2D). Together, these results indicate that altered gBOLD–CSF coupling is tightly linked to subjective sleep disturbance specifically in insomnia.

Temporal dynamics of coupling

Sliding-window analyses (80-s window, 8-s step) revealed distinct temporal trajectories of gBOLD–CSF coupling across groups and conditions. Linear mixed-effects models showed a significant Group × Time interaction (β = −0.072, SE = 0.022, p = 0.001), indicating steeper increases in coupling over time in HCs compared with ID. Critically, a robust Condition × Group × Time interaction was observed (β = 0.063, SE = 0.020, p = 0.002), demonstrating that temporal trajectories differed between groups as a function of condition. As visualized in Figure 2E and F, HCs showed pronounced, condition-dependent changes (particularly stronger increases during TTS), whereas ID patients exhibited relatively flat, weakly modulated trajectories in both CRS and TTS.

Network-level BOLD–CSF coupling

At the network level, repeated-measures ANOVAs across seven resting-state networks identified a significant Group × Condition interaction only in the dorsal attention network (DAN; F₁, ₅₃ = 8.00, FDR-p = 0.047, η²p = 0.135, Figure 2G). In HCs, DAN BOLD–CSF coupling was higher during TTS than CRS (t₅₃ = 3.35, p = 0.004), whereas ID patients showed the opposite pattern, with stronger coupling during CRS than TTS (t₅₃ = −2.16, p = 0.039, Figure 2H). No significant interactions emerged in other networks. Figure 2I illustrates that insomnia-related abnormalities in gBOLD–CSF coupling are not uniformly distributed but are particularly pronounced in attention-related circuitry.

Conclusion

Together, these findings indicate that insomnia disorder is characterized by a state-dependent failure to appropriately upregulate gBOLD–CSF coupling during the vulnerable phase of sleep initiation. Whereas healthy sleepers showed stronger, dynamically increasing coupling during the TTS condition relative to conventional rest, patients with insomnia displayed blunted condition effects, reduced temporal variability, and a loss of the normal trajectory toward stronger coupling over time. The observation that weaker coupling related to worse insomnia severity and poorer subjective sleep quality supports the view that aberrant glymphatic-related dynamics are not merely an epiphenomenon, but track clinically relevant aspects of sleep disturbance.

The network-level results further suggest that these abnormalities are not globally uniform, but are particularly pronounced within the DAN. In healthy controls, increased DAN–CSF coupling during TTS may reflect coordinated downregulation of externally oriented attention and tighter coupling between attentional control systems and glymphatic inflow as the brain transitions toward sleep. In contrast, the reversed pattern in insomnia—higher DAN coupling at rest than during TTS—points to a maladaptive configuration in which attentional networks remain relatively decoupled from glymphatic dynamics when individuals attempt to fall asleep. This network-specific disruption aligns with hyperarousal models of insomnia, in which persistent engagement of attention and monitoring processes interferes with the normal initiation of sleep.

Our results converge with recent diffusion tensor image–analysis along perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) work showing reduced glymphatic activity in insomnia5, but extend prior findings in two important ways: by capturing the temporal evolution of glymphatic-related coupling during an experimentally defined sleep-initiation context, and by mapping its spatial expression across large-scale cortical networks. Taken together, these data support the notion that insomnia involves a systems-level decoupling between arousal-regulating neural networks and glymphatic CSF dynamics. gBOLD–CSF coupling during TTS may therefore serve as a promising noninvasive biomarker for monitoring sleep–wake transitions, stratifying patients according to glymphatic vulnerability, and evaluating targeted interventions, such as cognitive–behavioral therapy for insomnia, neuromodulation, or pharmacological agents, designed to restore both neural and fluid-dynamic aspects of sleep physiology.
