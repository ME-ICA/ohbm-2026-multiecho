Introduction

Drug-resistant epilepsy is a neurological disorder affecting millions of people worldwide. Despite advancements in treatment, only 45-66% of focal epilepsy patients achieve seizure freedom after surgical intervention, with long-term rates of seizure freedom even lower1. Recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly large language models (LLMs), open new avenues for developing methods to predict patient-specific outcomes and provide critical information regarding the potential risks and benefits of surgery 2-5. In this study, we report on the ability of nine different open-source LLMs to predict post-surgical seizure control and five distinct neurocognitive outcomes.

Methods

Nine open-source LLMs were evaluated, ranging in size from one billion to seventy billion parameters. These included Gemma3 (1B, 4B, 12B, 27B), Llama3.1 (8B, 70B), Phi3 (14B), DeepSeek-R1 (32B), and GPT-OSS (20B). Each model was tasked with predicting post-surgical seizure control operationalized through the Engel scale (class) and cognitive outcomes quantified through Reliable Change Indices (RCI). The sample included epilepsy patients who underwent an invasive brain surgery procedure between 2008 and 2025 at the Thomas Jefferson University Comprehensive Epilepsy Center (TJUCEC). Type of epilepsy and surgical procedure were not restricted. The input data consisted of all the information contained in the TJUCEC surgical conference summary, with this multidisciplinary conference providing the forum to reach consensus decision about surgical candidacy. This document collates and summarizes all available pre-surgical medical and clinical records including, but not limited to, the following key data elements: demographic/social/educational/vocational history, seizure and medical history, medication history, MRI/PET imaging, surface EEG, intracranial EEG, neurology treatment notes, surgical history, neuropsychological testing, and TJUCEC epilepsy monitoring unit treatment course. The dataset included 157 verified epilepsy surgery patients. The cognitive measures included the three language (Boston Naming Test total score [BNT], phonemic fluency and semantic fluency total scores), and two memory measures (California Verbal Learning Test, total learning and long delay free recall; CVLT, versions II or III). The RCIs applied to these neurocognitive measures provided a binary classification of pre- to post-surgery change (improvement/stability versus decline). With regard to both the Engel surgical outcomes codes and the separate neurocognitive RCI classifications, each LLM generated two outputs: a binary classification of good versus poor outcome and a quantitative score ranging from -100 to +100, indicating, respectively, the certainty of a poor versus good outcome. The quantitative output scores were utilized to run correlations with the actual patient Engel outcome scores and the actual change in the within-subject pre-to-post surgery neurocognitive raw scores.

Results

The best-performing models for predicting post-surgical seizure control were Llama3.1:70B, GPT-OSS:20B, and DeepSeek-R1:32B. These models achieved seizure control prediction accuracies of 72%, 70%, and 69%, respectively, with strong F1-scores (a combined measure of recall and precision) of 0.82, 0.81, and 0.81, respectively. Interestingly, the model outcome scores for these three LLMs were negatively correlated with actual Engel classifications, indicating that outcomes reflective of more extreme values on the Engel scale (i.e., seizure freedom or very poor seizure control) were associated with high certainty in the LLM models. Conversely, seizure outcomes reflective of mixed or middle ground Engel scores (i.e., clinical improvement but some seizure activity) were associated with LLM models of lower certainty (n.b., weaker predictive value; see Table 1). For cognitive outcome prediction, on average GPT-OSS:20B demonstrated the strongest performance across all five outcome measures, with a mean classification accuracy of 64%. This model was particularly effective at predicting BNT and semantic fluency outcomes, both with accuracy rates of 68%. GPT-OSS:20B prediction accuracy for the CVLT memory and phonemic fluency outcomes were lower (see Figure 1). Also, GPT-OSS:20B produced a BNT quantitative outcome certainty score that showed a positive association with actual pre-to-post surgical change that indicated higher certainty was associated with a better neurocognitive outcome  (i.e., the same or better BNT score post-surgery).

Conclusion

Large language models showed considerable promise in predicting both seizure control and neurocognitive language and memory outcomes following epilepsy surgery. Most LLM models had good success at predicting the extremes of seizure outcomes (i.e., seizure freedom or very poor seizure control), with LLM model parameter size making little difference. In contrast, the neurocognitive outcomes were not predicted with equal success by the LLM models. Notably, across the neurocognitive outcomes the GPT-OSS:20B model demonstrated the most success. Overall, the LLM models had better success at predicting post-surgical seizure control compared to neurocognitive outcomes. This finding may be related to the higher content for seizure compared to neurocognitive outcomes embedded in the open-source data used by the LLMs. Among the neurocognitive tests, prediction of the BNT and semantic fluency by the LLMs was superior to the prediction of verbal memory and phonemic fluency. This may be related to the prominence of temporal lobe surgeries in the corpus used for input, noting that the functional neuroanatomy of the BNT and semantic fluency relies more solely on temporal lobe structures, with the other measures having a more distributed brain representation. Further examination of the role played by greater tuning of model parameters (e.g., the quality, quantity, and selection/weighting of the input features) will be needed to improve predictive accuracy at the individual subject level. Determining on an individual basis the features most important for the accurate prediction of “good” and “poor” outcomes will be key to improving prognostic efforts in epilepsy treatment.
