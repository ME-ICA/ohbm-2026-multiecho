Introduction

Theta power found in frontal midline electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings is hypothesised to have its origins in intrinsic brain oscillations and it is commonly referred to as frontal midline theta (fmϴ). These oscillations have been associated with different cognitive functions, including memory, conflict resolution and response inhibition. However, it remains unclear whether fmϴ is an underlying mechanism for or is merely an epiphenomenon of the mentioned cognitive functions. Transcranial electrical stimulation (tES) has been repeatedly used to demonstrate that fmϴ plays a key role in cognitive control; But the results have been inconsistent. Furthermore, the majority of the studies have not measured EEG during or after the task to compare the theta power with the baseline. Therefore, it is not clear whether the findings can be attributed to the modulation of fmϴ oscillations.

Among tES methods, transcranial alternative current stimulation (tACS) is used generally for the modulation of fmϴ. However, its capacity to stimulate deep brain areas is limited. The electric field created by the electrodes placed on the scalp is stronger on the cortical surface of the brain and weakens as it reaches deeper brain structures. Furthermore, the direction of electric field with respect to the cortical pyramidal neurons plays a pivotal role in the effectiveness of tACS. 

Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) has been proposed as the generator of fmϴ oscillations.^1 ACC lies in the medial wall of each cerebral hemisphere, right above the corpus callosum. Therefore, it is particularly difficult to stimulate ACC, as two currents with opposite direction must be injected to the medial prefrontal cortex for the most effective stimulation. This limitation of tACS and the inconsistent experimental results raise the question whether tACS is able to effectively modulate fmϴ oscillations.

Methods

To investigate this, we simulated the electric fields from tACS in a brain model created based on T1 and T2 MRI images of a healthy subject using two electrode montages from previous studies.^2^3 As some studies have suggested that the use of highly conductive small electrodes and individualised electrode placement is superior, we also used an optimisation algorithm to find an individualised electrode montage for the specific brain model. The SimNIBS Python package was employed for all simulations and optimisations. Finally, we masked the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) to measure the total electric field strength from each montage in this area, as well as the effective component of the electric field normal to the cortical surface.

Finally, we repeated the optimisation using a more recent tES method: transcranial temporal interference stimulation (tTIS). In this method, two high-frequency currents with a slight frequency difference are applied to the brain via two electrode pairs. Superimposing the two electric fields leads to a pattern of amplitude modulation at the difference frequency, e.g. theta, only in the region where the two fields overlap. Thus, the stimulation effect can be externally directed towards a deep brain target. Meanwhile, the effects of a purely high-frequency electric field on the more superficial layers are believed to be minimal. ^4

Results

Our results demonstrate that the mean electric field in ACC, with neither of the electrode montages proposed in literature nor with the individualized montage (mean in ROI ≈ 0.1 V/m), exceeds the minimum stimulation threshold suggested by animal studies (~ 0.2 V/m).^5 Furthermore, the electric field strength is even lower (mean in ROI ≈ 0.01 V/m) if we consider the portion of the electric field normal to the cortical surface. tTIS simulations show that this method leads to higher electric fields in the ACC with 2 mA peak to peak (mean in ROI ≈ 0.02 V/m).

Conclusion

We used the SimNIBS Python package to simulate electric fields induced by tACS and tTIS in a computational model of a healthy brain. We used electrode montages suggested in previous studies, as well as a montage optimised using a finite element modelling algorithm. Our results show that tACS has limited capacity to stimulate the ACC, which could explain inconsistent behavioural observations in conflict-related theta tACS studies. However, tTIS electric fields in the subcortical region of interest (ROI) are stronger. Therefore, tTIS could be a more effective method of modulating fmϴ oscillations.
