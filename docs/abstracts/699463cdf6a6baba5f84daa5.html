Introduction

Heterogeneity in neurodegeneration along the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) continuum is increasingly understood to arise from concomitant pathologies, particularly limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE), a clinical syndrome that commonly reflects underlying LATE neuropathologic change (LATE-NC) and has been reported to co-occur in up to ~50% of individuals with moderate-to-severe AD.1 Individuals with mixed AD+LATE exhibit disproportionate hippocampal atrophy, distinct medial temporal lobe (MTL) vulnerability, and faster cognitive decline, yet macrostructural MRI measures alone cannot reliably identify this phenotype in vivo. Recent work has shown that amyloid-positive individuals in the lowest quartile of hippocampal volume (HV) are enriched for suspected LATE-NC, with autopsy validation confirming early-stage LATE-NC.2 HV-based stratification therefore provides a practical approach for enriching for AD+LATE-like phenotypes antemortem but does not fully capture underlying tissue injury.

LATE-NC is characterized by dendritic simplification, cytoskeletal disruption, synaptic degeneration, microglial activation, extracellular expansion, and subsequent neuronal loss—processes that may precede or complement measurable cortical thinning. Modeling of tissue microstructure using diffusion-weighted MRI, particularly neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI), provides biologically interpretable indices sensitive to these mechanisms: free water fraction (FWF) reflects tissue loss; neurite density index (NDI) reflects cellular packing; and orientation dispersion index (ODI) reflects neurite complexity that hinders diffusion. However, NODDI signatures in LATE remain poorly characterized in vivo.

Here, we characterize cortical microstructural patterns across the AD continuum using HV-based stratification and test whether these differences persist after accounting for cortical thinning, assessing variance beyond macrostructural neurodegeneration.

Methods

Participants from the University of Pennsylvania Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center were retrospectively selected. We included cognitively unimpaired amyloid-negative individuals (CU A–; n=114) and cognitively impaired amyloid-positive individuals stratified by hippocampal volume (HV) following the enrichment framework described in Mundada et al., 2025.2 Amyloid-positive participants were classified as suspected (s) “AD+sLATE–” if HV was above the 50th percentile (n=22) and as “AD+sLATE+” if HV was below the 25th percentile (n=20), reflecting an AD+LATE-like phenotype enriched for underlying LATE neuropathologic change. Hippocampal volumes were transformed to age- and intracranial volume-adjusted measures from a large reference sample, and percentile thresholds were chosen to match those previously shown to enrich for LATE copathology on the AD continuum2.

Image acquisition and processing

Structural and diffusion MRI were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner using a T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (resolution = 0.8 x 0.8 x 0.8 mm) and a multi-shell diffusion protocol (resolution = 1.5 x 1.5 x 1.5 mm). T1-weighted images were bias-corrected and skull-stripped using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) prior to cerebellar, cortical, and subcortical parcellation via multi-atlas segmentation with joint label fusion.3 MTL segmentation was performed using the automatic segmentation of hippocampal subfields-T1 (ASHS-T1, https://sites.google.com/view/ashs-dox/) pipeline,4 which delineates anterior and posterior hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, Brodmann areas 35 and 36, and parahippocampal cortex. Cortical thickness maps were generated using the cortical reconstruction for ASHS (CRASHS) surface-based pipeline, as previously described.5 

Diffusion MRI preprocessing included denoising, Gibbs-ringing removal, bias-field correction, motion correction, and eddy-current and susceptibility distortion correction. NODDI was performed using gray-matter–optimized parameters to estimate FWF, NDI, and ODI. Diffusion maps were registered to T1 space and projected onto the cortical surface to ensure spatial correspondence with thickness measures.

Statistical analysis

Vertex-wise general linear models (GLMs) were fit for each metric (thickness, FWF, NDI, and ODI) using the CM-Rep package (github.com/pyushkevich/cmrep). Group contrasts included CU A– vs AD+sLATE–, CU A– vs AD+sLATE+, and AD+sLATE– vs AD+sLATE+. Models referenced to CU A– included age as a covariate and models comparing the two AD groups included age and pTau217, a measure of tau burden, as covariates. To assess whether microstructural abnormalities reflected processes beyond macrostructural atrophy, secondary GLMs additionally included local thickness as a covariate for each NODDI metric. Permutation testing using the threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) approach with parameters E = 0.5, H = 2, and Δh = 0.01, and 1000 permutations were used to compute family-wise error rate (FWER)-corrected p values at each vertex.

Results

Group differences in macrostructure and NODDI microstructure metrics (Figure 1)

Cortical thickness analyses revealed no major differences between CU A– and AD+sLATE–, likely reflecting limited statistical power to detect subtle macrostructural effects at this disease stage. In contrast, AD+sLATE+ individuals exhibited focal thinning predominantly in anterior medial temporal regions, including ERC, BA35, and the anterior hippocampus. Direct comparison between AD+sLATE– and AD+sLATE+ demonstrated thickness differences confined primarily to ERC/BA35, consistent with preferential anterior MTL vulnerability in the AD+LATE-like phenotype rather than widespread cortical atrophy.

Across NODDI metrics, microstructural abnormalities showed metric- and region-specific patterns. FWF demonstrated the most spatially extensive differences. Relative to CU A–, both amyloid-positive groups showed elevated FWF within MTL cortex and the anterior hippocampus, with stronger and more consistent effects in AD+sLATE+. Direct comparisons between AD+sLATE– and AD+sLATE+ suggested modest differences in ERC, though these effects were limited in spatial extent, likely reflecting reduced power for between–A+ contrasts. Overall, FWF abnormalities were most prominent in cortical MTL regions, consistent with loss of tissue and associated neurites. NDI effects were more spatially restricted and localized primarily to the hippocampus. AD+sLATE+ individuals exhibited lower NDI in the posterior hippocampus, with minimal differences in MTL cortex, indicating preferential involvement of hippocampal neurite integrity in the AD+sLATE-like phenotype. ODI showed subtle and spatially limited effects. AD+sLATE+ individuals demonstrated modest ODI differences primarily within the posterior hippocampus, with additional involvement of portions of the anterior hippocampus. ODI effects were less spatially extensive than those observed for FWF or NDI and were largely confined to hippocampal regions.

Group differences in NODDI microstructure metrics controlling for thickness (Figure 2)

Relative to CU A–, and after controlling for local cortical thickness, FWF remained the most robust microstructural marker. AD+sLATE+ individuals showed persistently elevated FWF within MTL cortex and the anterior hippocampus, indicating microstructural alterations not explained by concurrent macrostructural thinning. NDI differences also persisted after adjustment in comparisons with CU A–, remaining localized to the posterior hippocampus and consistent with focal hippocampal neurite degeneration beyond atrophy. In contrast, ODI showed only focal anterior and posterior hippocampal differences relative to CU A– after accounting for thickness.

Direct comparisons between AD+sLATE– and AD+sLATE+ revealed qualitatively similar but more spatially limited patterns. Specifically, AD+sLATE+ showed trends toward higher FWF and lower NDI, with more focal ODI differences in the anterior hippocampus, even after controlling for tau burden and local thickness; however, these effects were weaker and did not reach the spatial extent observed in CU-referenced contrasts, likely reflecting limited statistical power.

Taken together, these results demonstrate a spatial dissociation across imaging markers: FWF abnormalities were most prominent in MTL cortex, NDI and ODI effects were primarily hippocampal, and thickness effects involved both cortex and hippocampus with a clear anterior bias in AD+sLATE+. This pattern suggests that macrostructural and microstructural measures capture complementary aspects of tissue injury and may reflect partially distinct underlying pathological mechanisms.

Conclusion

Using HV-based stratification to enrich for AD+sLATE+, we demonstrate that diffusion MRI-derived microstructure metrics provide biologically complementary information to a macrostructural metric of tissue injury and reveal spatially distinct patterns of change. While cortical thickness differences were modest and largely confined to anterior MTL regions, microstructural metrics identified additional abnormalities not fully explained by atrophy. FWF emerged as the most sensitive marker, capturing microstructural alterations in MTL cortex and anterior hippocampus that persisted after accounting for thickness. In contrast, NDI identified microstructural degeneration in the posterior hippocampus, whereas ODI showed more focal effects in anterior and posterior hippocampus.

Together, these findings highlight a dissociation between macrostructural and microstructural degeneration across MTL regions and suggest that different imaging markers capture distinct aspects of underlying tissue injury. Specifically, both AD+sLATE– and AD+sLATE+ were associated with tissue loss reflected by elevated FWF, which was more pronounced in AD+sLATE+ and localized to cortical MTL subregions, whereas AD+sLATE+ showed disproportionate neurite degeneration in the posterior hippocampus reflected by reduced NDI, along with more focal alterations in neurite organization captured by ODI. By capturing microstructural alterations linked to tissue loss and neurite degeneration—processes associated with worse memory performance, steeper clinical decline, and accelerated medial temporal atrophy in AD+sLATE+, these measures provide biological context beyond macrostructural MRI alone. Ongoing work incorporating additional NODDI data from ADNI will increase power and enable more precise characterization of these microstructural effects.
