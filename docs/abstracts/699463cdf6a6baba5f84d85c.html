Introduction

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has provided fundamental insights into large-scale brain organization, including the widely adopted “Triple Network” model, which plays a critical role in cognition and mental health1. This framework comprises the default mode network (DMN), anchored in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC); the salience network (SN), anchored in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC); and the central executive network (CEN), anchored in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and often referred to as the lateral cortical network (LCN) in rodents. Despite its success, fMRI remains an indirect measure of neural activity and lacks the cellular resolution required to elucidate the neuronal mechanisms underlying network dynamics. 

The prefrontal cortex serves as a central integration hub for diverse cognitive functions2. In humans, distinct prefrontal subregions are anatomically segregated and align with individual large-scale networks1. In contrast, the rodent prefrontal cortex lacks clear anatomical boundaries corresponding to these networks (Fig. 1A). Notably, accumulating evidence from rodent fMRI studies3, including our own work4, suggests that homologs of the DMN, SN, and LCN converge within a single medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) region. We therefore hypothesized that the rodent mPFC contains distinct functional subpopulations of neurons that differentially couple to large-scale brain networks. Testing this hypothesis requires a platform capable of simultaneously capturing cellular-scale neuronal activity and brain-wide network dynamics in awake animals. Here, we introduce a concurrent multimodal framework that integrates silent, distortion-free Steady-state On-the-Ramp Detection of INduction-decay signal with Oversampling (SORDINO) fMRI5 with MRI-compatible calcium miniscope imaging, enabling direct investigation of the cellular substrates underlying large-scale functional brain networks.

Methods

This study incorporated two key technical innovations: (1) a recently developed SORDINO fMRI technique, which samples free induction decay signals at near-zero echo time under silent, distortion-free, and motion-insensitive conditions, and (2) an in-house–modified MRI–compatible calcium miniscope based on the nVista platform (Inscopix Inc.) (Fig. 1B). All MRI experiments were conducted on a Bruker 9.4T MRI system. A total of twelve mice underwent simultaneous SORDINO fMRI and calcium imaging under awake, head-fixed conditions.

Experimental design. Each mouse underwent three fMRI sessions within the same experimental paradigm, with concurrent Inscopix calcium miniscope recordings in all sessions. The first session consisted of a 20-minute resting-state fMRI scan. The second and third sessions each consisted of 20-minute fMRI scans during auditory oddball stimulation, using a counter-balanced design across sessions to control for stimulus frequency and sweep direction. 

Calcium imaging data acquisition and preprocessing. To enable cellular-resolution calcium imaging, AAV-CaMKIIα-jGCaMP8m was injected into the mPFC, followed by implantation of a 0.6 mm diameter MRI-compatible gradient refractive index (GRIN) lens targeting the mPFC (Fig. 1B,C). Calcium imaging data were acquired at 10 Hz. Initial preprocessing was performed using Inscopix Data Processing Software (IDPS) following standard pipelines. The relative change in fluorescence (ΔF/F) was calculated as (F(t) − F₀(t)) / F₀(t), where F₀(t) was estimated using a moving baseline. To infer neuronal spiking activity and mitigate the slow decay kinetics of the calcium indicator, a constrained deconvolution algorithm based on a first-order autoregressive model was applied to the ΔF/F traces.

Functional network classification during resting state. To characterize the functional identity of individual mPFC neurons, their calcium activity was related to three large-scale brain networks defined by resting-state fMRI acquired during the resting session. Regions of interest (ROIs) included the M1, representing the LCN; the RSC, a core node of the DMN; and the vAI, a key node of the SN. For each subject, a general linear model (GLM) was constructed using the resting-state fMRI time series extracted from these ROIs as regressors. To align temporal dynamics across modalities, calcium traces were convolved with a hemodynamic response function prior to regression. Neurons were classified based on the polarity of statistically significant regression coefficients (p < 0.05): neurons with positive coefficients (β > 0) were labeled synchronous (SYN), whereas neurons with negative coefficients (β < 0) were labeled anti-synchronous (ANTI). 

Event-related stimulation analysis. Event-related responses were assessed using data from the second and third fMRI sessions, both of which involved auditory oddball stimulation. Auditory stimuli consisted of frequency-modulated sweeps spanning 6–14 kHz. In the second session, upward sweeps (6–14 kHz) served as deviant events (“ON,” 1 s duration, randomized inter-stimulus intervals), while downward sweeps (14–6 kHz) formed the background (“OFF”). This assignment was reversed in the third session. Population-level time series were computed by averaging Z-scored traces across neurons within each functional subtype. Event-related response amplitudes were quantified using a GLM in which the binary stimulation paradigm was convolved with a canonical calcium response function (CRF) modeled as a double-exponential function (τrise = 0.1 s, τdecay = 1.2 s).

Statistical assessment. To account for intrinsic temporal autocorrelation in calcium signals, statistical significance was assessed using circular permutation testing. For each subject and neuronal subtype, population calcium traces were circularly shifted by random temporal offsets exceeding 5 s and refitted using the same general linear model procedure (10,000 permutations). 

Results

Seed-based resting-state functional connectivity analysis using SORDINO fMRI reliably identified the DMN, SN, and LCN, consistent with prior rodent fMRI studies (Fig. 1D). Regression analysis linking cellular calcium activity to fMRI-derived network signals revealed distinct subsets of mPFC neurons significantly associated with each large-scale network (Fig. 1E). Neurons were classified as synchronous or antagonistic based on the sign of their regression coefficients, indicating positive or negative coupling with network-level activity, respectively. Importantly, neurons associated with the DMN, SN, and LCN were spatially intermingled within the same mPFC field of view rather than anatomically segregated, demonstrating that multiple large-scale networks converge at the cellular level in a cortical region. Quantitatively, approximately 10% of recorded neurons were correlated with the SN, with roughly equal proportions showing synchronous and antagonistic coupling (∼5% each). Similarly, about 10% of neurons were associated with the LCN, again split evenly between synchronous and antagonistic populations. In contrast, RSC–related DMN signals accounted for a larger fraction of mPFC neurons, with approximately 20% of cells showing significant correlations (∼10% synchronous and ∼10% antagonistic) (Fig. 2A). For each network, the presence of both synchronous and antagonistic neuronal populations indicates bidirectional cellular coupling with large-scale network dynamics rather than uniform recruitment.

We next examined how these functionally defined neuronal populations responded during auditory oddball stimulation (Fig. 2A). Network specific populations exhibited markedly divergent event related response profiles (Fig. 2B). Neurons classified as synchronous with the vAI-defined SN showed significant positive activation in response to deviant auditory stimuli, consistent with the network’s role in salience detection and event encoding. In contrast, neurons antagonistically coupled to the M1-defined LCN displayed significant suppression during deviant events, suggesting a disengagement of lateral cortical control processes during salient sensory processing. Neuronal populations associated with the RSC-defined DMN exhibited comparatively modest responses. To visualize the temporal consistency and specificity of these responses, we generated population activity heatmaps of all recorded neurons (Fig. 2C). The top panel reveals a structured, time-locked response to the oddball stimulus across the population. Crucially, when mapping the activity of these same neurons during the non-oddball auditory period using the identical sorting order derived from the stimulation response, we observed no structured activity (Fig. 2C, bottom). This confirms that the observed high-amplitude responses are event-specific and not driven by intrinsic baseline hyperactivity. Importantly, the observed response patterns were not attributable to stimulus frequency content or sweep direction, as the effects were preserved across the counter-balanced oddball design (upward vs. downward sweeps). Together, these results demonstrate that distinct, intermingled neuronal subpopulations within the rodent medial prefrontal cortex selectively encode and modulate large-scale network dynamics during both resting-state and event-driven conditions.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that large scale brain networks are supported by a functionally multiplexed cellular architecture within the rodent medial prefrontal cortex. By combining silent and distortion free SORDINO-fMRI with MRI-compatible calcium miniscope recordings, we provide direct cellular level evidence that neurons within the same cortical region selectively synchronize or antagonize with DMN, SN, and LCN dynamics. These findings establish a direct link between neuronal population activity and brain wide network organization in awake animals.

Our results challenge the conventional view that large scale brain networks are spatially segregated entities at the cortical level. Instead, we reveal that neurons associated with distinct and even opposing networks coexist within a shared anatomical territory in the rodent mPFC. The presence of both synchronous and antagonistic neuronal populations for each network suggests that network level coordination emerges from balanced and bidirectional cellular interactions rather than uniform recruitment. This organizational principle provides a mechanistic explanation for how the rodent mPFC supports diverse cognitive processes despite its lack of anatomical subdivision compared to the human prefrontal cortex.

These findings have important implications for cross species translational neuroimaging. In humans, prefrontal network specialization is supported by anatomical segregation across medial, lateral, and cingulate regions. In rodents, however, homologous networks appear to be compressed into a single mPFC region, with functional differentiation emerging at the cellular level. This evolutionary divergence highlights the necessity of interpreting rodent fMRI results through a cellularly informed framework rather than assuming direct anatomical correspondence with human networks.

The multimodal framework introduced here bridges the gap between cellular resolution neuroscience and whole brain network analysis in awake animals. Its longitudinal stability and compatibility with behavioral paradigms make it well suited for investigating how cellular level dysfunction contributes to abnormal network dynamics in models of neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders. More broadly, this approach is readily extensible to other brain regions and circuit systems and provides a powerful platform for dissecting the cellular foundations of large-scale brain organization.
