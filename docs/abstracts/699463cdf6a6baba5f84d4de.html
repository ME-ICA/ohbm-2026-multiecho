Introduction

Adoption of state-of-the-art deep learning in clinical and research neuroimaging remains limited by two critical barriers: deployment complexity and data privacy. High-performing models often require specialized hardware, complex software ecosystems, and command-line expertise, placing them out of reach for non-technical users. At the same time, strict privacy regulations prohibit the transfer of protected health information to third-party servers, rendering many cloud-based deployment solutions unsuitable for real-world clinical settings. 

These challenges are particularly pronounced for foundational preprocessing tasks such as skull stripping– the removal of non-brain tissue from neuroimaging scans. Existing classical methods like BET1 and ROBEX2 are fast but often fail under contrast and acquisition variations. More recent deep learning approaches, such as the state-of-the-art, SynthStrip,3 achieve strong robustness but rely on large, parameter-heavy architectures with substantial computational and memory demands, limiting their usability in resource-constrained environments such as laptops and web-browsers. 

To address this tradeoff between performance and accessibility, we introduce MindGrab, a lightweight, fully convolutional deep learning model for omnimodal skull stripping. MindGrab achieves high accuracy (dice: 95.9 ⁤± 1.6) competitive with state-of-the-art methods while using 95% fewer parameters, enabling 10-40x faster inference and up to 33x lower memory usage. This architectural efficiency enables novel deployment modalities, including direct in-browser execution, without compromising data privacy. We release MindGrab as both a command-line tool and zero-installation in-browser model through the BrainChop4 platform, making robust brain extraction broadly accessible in practical clinical and research workflows. 

Methods

MindGrab was designed from first principles using a spectral interpretation of dilated convolutions. In the frequency domain, dilation acts as a tuning mechanism: introducing gaps between kernel weights replicates the filter response across k-space, allowing a small convolutional kernel to interact with multiple, widely separated spatial frequency bands without increasing parameter count. MindGrab is constructed from five consecutive decreasing dilation blocks (16→8→4→2→1), resulting in a 26-layer network (25 dilated convolutional layers followed by a 1x1x1 projection layer) with parameter-free Channel Normalization (GroupNorm with groups=channels). This high-to-low dilation sequence compels the network to encode boundary information into increasingly coarse, robust representations without downsampling or skip connections.

MindGrab was trained exclusively on synthetic data generated with SynthSeg5 using 171 anatomical label maps. Synthetic images were quantile-normalized (2nd-98th percentile), and brain labels were merged into a binary mask with smoothed boundaries. The model was trained on approximately 250,000 synthetic samples using  soft Dice loss, Adam optimizer, OneCycle learning rate scheduling, and batch size of one. MindGrab was evaluated on the publicly available SynthStrip benchmark of 606 adult brain scans spanning 16 datasets and multiple modalities (T1w, T2w, FLAIR, DWI, EPI, PD, qT1, MRA, CT, PET).

Results

MindGrab demonstrates robust and competitive skull stripping performance across imaging modalities while substantially improving computational efficiency. Quantitative Dice score comparison on the 16 evaluation datasets show that MindGrab consistently outperforms classical methods and achieves scores highly comparable to SynthStrip (Fig. 1, Panel (1)). Across datasets, MindGrab demonstrates higher average precision than SynthStrip (97.9 ± 1.6 vs. 96.6 ± 1.6) with slightly lower average recall (94.2 ± 3.7 vs. 96.6 ± 2.3), reflecting a more conservative boundary delineation approach. 

Qualitative comparisons reaffirm these scores: BET and ROBEX exhibit frequent over- and under-segmentation, whereas MindGrab and SynthStrip produce closely aligned brain boundaries across modalities. Minor discrepancies between MindGrab and the ground-truth are typically confined to regions with abrupt intensity transitions or truncated anatomy, and otherwise consist of  small boundary deviations (Fig. 1, Panel (4)). 

Crucially, MindGrab achieves these results with dramatically reduced computational cost. On an Apple M2 Max 64GB shared RAM, MindGrab requires 10-40x less runtime and up to 33x lower memory usage than SynthStrip. 

Conclusion

MindGrab resolves the tradeoff between accuracy and accessibility in skull stripping by delivering state-of-the-art performance with substantially reduced computational cost. It is freely and publicly available as both a low-memory command-line tool (pip install brainchop; <20MB) and zero-footprint in-browser model (brainchop.org).
