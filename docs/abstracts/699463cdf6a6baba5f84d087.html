Introduction

The default mode network (DMN), classically associated with internally oriented cognition such as autobiographical memory, mind-wandering, and semantic processing, has recently been shown to respond systematically to visual stimulation. Several studies have shown positive and negative responses in DMN regions that  follow a topographic organization, analogue to topographic patterns observed in early visual cortex. Additional evidence suggests a contralateral visual field bias in those DMN regions that are located closer to visual areas.1   the role of these visually driven responses remains unknown.

Converging evidence from naturalistic stimulus paradigms and computational models suggest that DMN regions contribute to predictive processing mechanisms, providing top-down signals that modulate sensory cortices during perception.2,3 Feedback signals are thought to be critical in the generation of illusory contours and surfaces, with higher-order areas enhancing internally generated shape representations in visual cortex. In early visual areas, illusory shapes are known to evoke topographically organized responses similar to those induced by real shapes. We therefore hypothesize that the DMN may also exhibit topographic activation patterns for illusory shapes, reflecting either top-down predictive influences or prediction-error-related computations in higher-order cortices.

To test this, we assessed DMN responses to real and illusory shapes presented in the left and right visual fields using 3 Tesla fMRI.

Methods

28 healthy volunteers (aged 20–34 years, M=26.22, SD=3.67, 14 females) participated in the study. Each participant completed two sessions with seven runs per session: three localizer and four main experiment runs. During all runs, participants performed a central fixation task to maintain attention.

Localizer runs consisted of flickering checkerboard patterns presented at spatial locations corresponding to either the real and illusory shape positions in the main experiment or the background surrounding the shape. This resulted in four conditions: left, right and background stimulation as well as baseline (grey screen). Main experiment included runs with either illusory Kanizsa or real shapes presented in the left or right visual field, along with their respective baseline conditions (see Figure 1). Each block lasted 13 s. Shape stimuli flickered every 500 ms against their respective backgrounds.

MRI data were collected using a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Vida scanner. In both sessions, a structural scan was collected first, followed by the seven functional runs. Functional images were acquired using a T2*-weighted EPI sequence with BOLD contrast (50 axial slices; TR = 2000 ms; TE = 34 ms; flip angle = 77°; FOV = 220 mm; voxel size = 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm; Multiband factor 2).

Preprocessing was done using fMRIPrep 24.1.0 with default pipelines for motion correction, distortion correction, co-registration, and normalization.

First-level GLM analyses were conducted in individual subjects’ surface space using Nilearn 0.12.1. In both localizer and main-experiment runs, each condition was modeled as a separate regressor convolved with a canonical HRF. For the localizer runs, contrasts were defined for left, right, and background stimulation relative to baseline. For the main experiment, contrasts quantified the responses to illusory and real shapes presented in the left or right visual field, each compared to its corresponding baseline condition. 

For each participant, contrast maps from the localizer were used to identify visually responsive vertices separately for left- and right-field stimulation. Vertices showing increased activation (Loc+; z > 1.96) and decreased activation (Loc–; z < –1.96) were extracted independently. Consequently, Loc+ vertices from one condition that did not overlap with Loc+ vertices from any other condition were selected. The same procedure was applied for Loc– vertices. These vertices were then used to extract effect-size values from the main experiment contrasts. This resulted in two matrices per contrast: one containing Loc+ vertices, and one containing Loc– vertices from the localizer task.

Three DMN ROIs were defined using the multimodal parcellation4 following Sandhu and colleagues (2021): anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and lateral parietal cortex (LPC). In addition, early visual areas (V1, V2, V3, V4) were defined applying the same atlas for comparison. For each participant, mean effect sizes were extracted from Loc+ and Loc– vertices within each ROI for each contrast of the main experiment.

Additionally, session-to-session reliability in DMN responses was assessed using Pearson correlations between localizer contrasts from session 1 and session 2; correlation values were then averaged after a Fisher z-transform.

Visual cortex results were first tested for asymmetry in hemispheric differences using paired-sample t-tests. As no hemispheric effects were detected, contralateral and ipsilateral responses of both hemispheres were averaged. DMN areas in each hemisphere were analyzed separately. One-sample t-tests (FDR-corrected) were applied to assess non-zero activation in both visual and DMN ROIs. Finally, to directly evaluate spatial specificity, activations to contralateral versus ipsilateral shape presentations were compared using paired-sample t-tests.

Results

In the visual cortices, illusory shapes lead to a significant increase of activation in V1, V2, V3 and V4 in the contralateral hemisphere (all pFDR< .001). In contrast, real shapes produced significant contralateral activation only in V4 (pFDR< .001).

Among the DMN regions, the LPC showed shape-related responses. Loc+ vertices showed significant activation for illusory shapes (left hemisphere, shape presented on the right: pFDR=.005; right hemisphere, shape presented on the left: pFDR=.007). In the right hemisphere, these vertices additionally responded to real shapes (pFDR=.03) (see Figure 2). Loc– vertices within the left LPC showed increased activation for both real (pFDR=.02) and illusory shapes (pFDR=.02) presented on the left. Finally, the LPC showed a clear hemispheric asymmetry: contralateral responses to illusory shapes were significantly stronger than ipsilateral responses (pFDR<.001). No significant modulation was observed for either positive or negative vertices in any of the remaining DMN nodes we examined.

Reliability analyses revealed a significant session-to-session consistency in the LPC (left hemisphere: Fisher-z-average: 1.11, p<.001; right hemisphere: 1.38, p<.001). The PCC also showed significant reliability (left: .72, p<.001; right: .87, p<.001), whereas the ACC did not exhibit consistent activation across sessions (left: .21, p=.07 right: .31, p=.05).

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that illusory shapes evoke topographically organized responses in one of the regions typically associated with the DMN. The LPC showed consistent contralateral activation to illusory shapes in vertices positively driven by visual stimulation. Contralateral-ipsilateral differences were significant, yielding additional evidence that the LPC exhibits spatially specific illusory shape activity. Enhanced responses to both real and illusory shapes were additionally observed in the left LPC for Loc– vertices. This may indicate that in this area, regions that are suppressed by the bottom-up input represent the subjective presence of a shape. Together, our results suggest that regions typically associated with the DMN may be combining bottom-up sensory information with internal subjective content to represent the subjectively perceived shapes in a topographic manner.
