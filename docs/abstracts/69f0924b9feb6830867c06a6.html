Introduction

Atypical neural connectivity has long been proposed as a convergent neurobiological explanation behind the many ‘autisms’. This is evident in differences in macroscale structural and functional brain connectivity measured through neuroimaging. For instance, static functional connectivity analysis of resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) shows that autism is characterized by alterations in whole-brain connectivity gradients, idiosyncratic connectivity profiles, mosaic patterns of hypo- and hyper-connectivity, and distance-dependent effects. In contrast to static approaches, other studies have shown that rsfMRI exhibits intrinsic non-stationarities, or ‘dynamics’, requiring analysis of how dynamic connectivity patterns emerge and change over time.

Understanding these dynamics requires moving beyond description toward mechanistic accounts. Recent work has emphasized the role of metastability, a dynamical systems concept describing the brain’s flexible switching between functional configurations. However, because metastability captures whole-brain configurations, frameworks from other disciplines can extend it to characterize spatial differences more effectively. One such framework is turbulence, which draws on principles from fluid dynamics. Turbulence can be viewed as the spatiotemporal extension of metastability. A key property of turbulent flow is its ability to rapidly transfer energy across spatial scales, enabling efficient information propagation. Deco and Kringelbach1 showed that treating the brain as a system of coupled oscillators allows turbulent markers to be identified in rsfMRI, offering a detailed view of spatiotemporal synchronization dynamics.

This study investigates the dynamics of functional synchronization across spatial scales in rsfMRI in autism. We tested the hypothesis that these dynamics differ between individuals with autism and those with typical development (TD) by applying the turbulence-dynamics framework to rsfMRI scans of autistic individuals.

Methods

We analyzed data from the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE I and II). After preprocessing, participants with poor image quality or mean framewise displacement (FD) > 0.5 mm were excluded from subsequent analyses. To account for higher FD in the autism group, we matched autistic and typically developing (TD) participants. Because ABIDE includes data from multiple scanners, we used ComBat to correct for site-related variability in BOLD signals and connectivity. After all filtering steps, the final sample included 505 autistic individuals (mean age = 16.29, SD = 9.00) and 504 TD individuals (mean age = 16.68, SD = 8.94).

To formalize the turbulence dynamics framework, we adopt the approach of viewing the brain as a system of coupled oscillators. In this model, the Hilbert-transformed time series of the 1,054 brain parcels serve as fundamental elements (Figure 1A–B). We quantified the magnitude of synchronization using the Kuramoto order parameter, R. R was computed either uniformly across the whole brain (Kuramoto Global Order Parameter, KGOP) or across a range of spatial scales (Kuramoto Local Order Parameter, KLOP), denoted by λ (Figure 1C–E). KGOP values indicate the extent to which the entire brain is in phase synchrony at a given time. In contrast, KLOP values indicate the degree to which a given parcel is in phase synchrony with a selected portion of its neighbouring regions at a given time. The 'selection' of these neighbouring regions is determined by the spatial scale λ, which ranges from 0.01 to 0.28 in ten steps of 0.03 and indicates the spatial extent over which neighbouring regions contribute to synchronization. Higher λ values correspond to narrower weighting kernels, while lower values correspond to wider kernels.

        To summarize, the KLOP formalizes the turbulence dynamics framework by quantifying how much (the scalar value) and over which distance (the spatial scale) synchronization is exerted in the brain. The three-dimensional KLOP matrices (parcels × timepoints × λ) were used in the subsequent analysis to compute higher-order statistics. First, we computed four statistics using the KLOP for each λ, thus making them susceptible to spatial scales:

(1) Amplitude turbulence – variability of synchronization at that spatial scale.
(2) Scale-specific synchronization – central tendency of synchronization at that scale.
(3) Information cascade flow – propagation of synchronization from a lower to its adjacent higher spatial scale.
(4) Information transfer – spatial extent over which synchronization is maintained at a given scale.

Second, we computed two whole-brain summary statistics using KGOP:

(5) Metastability – temporal variation in whole-brain synchronization (sd(KGOP)).
(6) Global synchronization – central tendency of whole-brain synchronization (mean(KGOP)).

Third, we computed parcel-level turbulence for each λ as the standard deviation of each parcel’s KLOP vector over time, capturing how its synchronization varies across scales.

                To assess group differences in whole-brain measures, we performed ANOVAs on linear models including sex, mean FD, diagnosis, age, and diagnosis×age as independent variables. For measures across spatial scales, we used ANOVAs on linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects of diagnosis, λ, age, sex, and mean FD, plus diagnosis×λ and diagnosis×age. Random effects modeled repeated measures of λ with random intercepts and slopes. To assess parcel-level turbulence differences across λ, we calculated Cohen’s d for each of the 1,054 parcels. We examined their similarity to hierarchical cortical gradients, including the sensorimotor-association (S-A) axis and the primary functional connectivity (FC) gradient.2,3 Statistical comparisons used Spearman ρ with significance assessed via spatial ‘spin tests’ (10,000 repetitions) that preserve spatial autocorrelation. The resulting p-values were FDR-corrected.

Results

Autism is characterized by higher variability in synchronization at local/small spatial scales 

This study applied the turbulence-dynamics framework to rsfMRI data from the ABIDE dataset (autism n = 505; TD n = 504). First, we examined differences in synchronization variability by looking at our whole-brain measure of synchronization (KGOP) using a metric called metastability (i.e., sd(KGOP)). Metastability differed significantly between groups (Figure 2a, F(1,1003) = 3.92, p = 0.048, d = 0.13), with lower levels in autism, indicating reduced whole-brain variability over time. We further examined how synchronization variability (i.e., sd(KLOP) or ‘amplitude turbulence’) changes with spatial scale (λ). Although there was no main effect of diagnosis (F(1,1041) = 0.09, p = 0.763), we found a significant λ×diagnosis interaction (F(1,1198) = 8.58, p = 0.003; Figure 2b). Follow-up comparisons showed significantly higher synchronization variability in autism at small/local spatial scales, but this difference disappeared at larger scales (i.e., lower λ). This strong contrast is important for validating the idea that functional synchronization is more turbulent at small, localized spatial scales and less turbulent at the whole-brain level in autism.

Variability of parcel-level functional synchronization maps onto sensorimotor-association and functional gradients in autism

Next, we utilized parcel-level turbulence metrics to better understand how variability in functional synchronization manifests along well-known cortical hierarchical gradients. We focused on the sensorimotor-association (S-A) axis (Figure 2c), a neurodevelopmentally relevant hierarchy similar to primary functional connectivity (FC) gradients (Figure 2d).2,3 We calculated parcel-level turbulence group difference maps and then used Spearman ρ and spatial permutation spin tests to test for statistically significant similarity with the S-A or FC maps. Here we found significant correlations with S-A and FC maps at almost all spatial scales except the very large ones (e.g., λ = 0.07-0.01) (Figures 2e,f). These results show that differences in synchronization variability align with S-A and FC gradients, with autism characterized by higher synchronization variability in sensorimotor regions and lower synchronization variability in association areas.

Weaker, less spatially correlated, and rapidly decaying functional synchronization over space in autism

Beyond variability, our framework allowed assessment of synchronization strength. Autism showed reduced global synchronization (F(1,1003) = 22.62, p = 0.000002, d = 0.300; Figure 2g) and reduced scale-specific synchronization across λ (F(1,996) = 20.90, p = 0.000005; Figure 2h), indicating weaker synchronization across all spatial scales.

We also were interested in testing how synchronization cascades over different spatial scales (i.e. progressively larger distances). To test this we used a measure called information cascade flow. We found a main effect of diagnosis (F(1,1020) = 9.04, p = 0.003), with autism showing lower cascade flow at nearly all scales (Figure 2i). This result suggests that functional synchronization propagation across larger spatial scales is reduced in autism.

Finally, we assessed the decay of synchronization across space using a measure we call information transfer (linear modeling of KLOP correlations as a function of distance). We found a main effect of diagnosis (F(1,995) = 9.07, p = 0.003), with autism showing lower information transfer for all λ except the largest scales (λ = 0.01, 0.04; Figure 2j). This indicates more rapid spatial decay of synchronization at short and medium distances. Taken together, we observed the following in autism: (1) decreased functional synchronization strength across all distances; (2) altered propagation of synchronization across medium and large spatial scales; and (3) faster spatial decay of synchronization at short and medium distances.

Conclusion

In this study, we applied the turbulence-dynamics framework to examine functional synchronization across spatial scales in resting-state fMRI scans of individuals with autism. Our findings reveal robust alterations in synchronization both within and between scales. Compared to typically developing individuals, we observe increased variability (amplitude turbulence), faster spatial decay (lower information transfer), and reduced synchronization strength at short distances in autism. This increased variability is primarily driven by sensorimotor and unimodal regions. At long distances, synchronization strength also remains reduced. Additionally, the influence of lower spatial scales on adjacent higher scales (information cascade flow) was diminished. Decreased synchronization variability at long distances was mainly found in association and transmodal areas.

How do these findings align with long-standing views of altered functional connectivity as a convergent feature of autism? A common theme proposes local overconnectivity alongside long-range underconnectivity. We find partial support: underconnectivity appears at both short and long distances. Variability shows distance dependence—short distances exhibit increased variability, whereas at longer distances the group differences shrink and may shift direction.

From local chaos to global decoupling: Multiscale connectivity alterations in autism

The combination of heightened local variability, faster spatial dissipation, and reduced synchronization strength suggests a state of “enhanced local chaos,” potentially reflecting underlying synaptic mechanisms. Prior research shows that normative mTOR-dependent processes facilitate synaptic pruning early in development. Increased synaptic spine density and related microstructural features in autism may contribute to this enhanced local instability, reflected in increased variability, more rapid decay over space, and weakened local synchronization. Beyond local chaos, our finding of reduced information cascade flow suggests a form of “global decoupling.” This may involve disruptions in long-range anatomical pathways. Diffusion imaging studies report reduced integrity of long-range white matter tracts in autism at ages similar to our sample. Such alterations may therefore limit the propagation of synchronization across larger spatial scales.

Together, these results suggest that local overconnectivity may produce overly chaotic dynamics at small scales, which fail to sustain coherence over longer distances, leading to reduced synchronization strength and variability at longer distances. At larger scales, long-range underconnectivity and white matter abnormalities may further contribute to a decoupled, rigid organization. This scale-dependent pattern extends previous reports of scale-independent hypervariability.

Altered synchronization patterns relate to hierarchical brain organization

Beyond distance-dependent findings, substantial research suggests that the brain is organized along a hierarchical gradient spanning sensorimotor to association regions (the S–A axis).3 This axis has been reported as atypical in autism, including evidence of a contracted functional hierarchy.4 Using the turbulence framework, we find further support for S–A involvement: increased short-range variability in sensorimotor areas and decreased long-range variability in association areas. These results align with broader evidence of hierarchical processing differences in autism and may index developmental alterations in how large-scale organization emerges along the S–A axis.5

Limitations and future directions

Limitations include small effect sizes and reliance on older autistic individuals with average to high intellectual ability. Future work should examine younger participants, a wider range of intellectual ability and autism subtypes, and track turbulence dynamics longitudinally to assess developmental trajectories.
