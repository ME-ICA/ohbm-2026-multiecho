Introduction

Childhood represents a critical stage of neurodevelopment, marked by ongoing cognitive development and refinement of neural circuits. Central to this process is the continued development of the cerebral cortex, particularly its distinct laminar architecture composed of layers with specialized cytoarchitecture and connectivity. However, the developmental dynamics of layer-specific cortical microstructure and its associated brain functions remain largely unexplored. Noninvasive MR imaging, such as diffusion MRI (dMRI), enables in vivo characterization of cerebral cortical cytoarchitecture in the developing human brain. Previous studies have been constrained by technical limitations in high-resolution acquisition and precise image analysis. Consequently characterization often uses a single value across all cortical layers at a specific reconstructed cortical surface vertex, obscuring layer-specific developmental patterns within functionally distinct laminae. Developing novel laminal cerebral cortical cytoarchitecture measures via noninvasive dMRI will potentially enable virtual neuropathology for longitudinally monitoring disease progression and intervention effects in developmental neuropsychiatric disorders. In this study, we develop a novel pipeline to measure cortical laminar microstructure and apply it to our in-house cutting-edge submillimeter multi-shell dMRI (0.86 mm isotropic) of typically developing children aged 5 to 12 years. By quantifying the mean diffusivity (MD) in both the inner and outer cortical layers, we aim to characterize spatiotemporal patterns of layer-specific cortical maturation. This establishes a normative benchmark for developmental cortical laminar microstructure during childhood and provide a foundation for future investigations of altered cortical development in clinical populations. 

Methods

Participants and data acquisition: 63 typically developing children aged 5 to 12 years were recruited, and 54 of them (8.53 ± 2.13 years) complete both T1-weighted (T1w) and dMRI scans on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner. Earplug and headphones were used to minimize noise exposure. T1w images were acquired using a 3D MPRAGE sequence (TR/TE = 2400/2.4 ms, 0.8 mm isotropic). Cutting-edge multi-shell (b = 0, 1000, and 2500 s/mm2, 30 gradient directions per non-zero shell) dMRI with the highest resolution to date (0.86 mm isotropic) was acquired using gSlider-SMS^1 sequence. 

Data processing: Diffusion-weighted images were processed through a robust brain image analysis pipeline for high-fidelity tensor fitting offline: susceptibility-induced distortions were first corrected, following correction for eddy current distortions and subject motion. 

Cortical laminar processing pipeline: As shown in Figure 1, T1w images were segmented into white matter (WM), gray matter (GM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Cortical layer masks for inner and outer layers were generated using the LayNii toolbox with an equi-volume laminar approach^2-3, which divides the cortex into equal-volume layers rather than equal-thickness layers to account for cortical curvature. To minimize partial-volume effects from WM and CSF, layer masks were smoothed with a 3D Gaussian kernel (σ = 0.8 mm) to generate probabilistic maps, which were subsequently skeletonized to capture the core of each layer. The resulting skeleton masks were linearly registered to diffusion space for MD extraction, which were then transformed back to structural space for surface-based visualization and analysis. To create continuous volumetric maps of laminar MD, values from skeleton voxels were propagated to all cortical voxels using a nearest-neighbor algorithm, yielding spatially complete and anatomically coherent laminar microstructural measurements. 

Statistical analysis: Developmental trajectories of laminar MD were assessed using linear regression models with age as the predictor variable. Regional analyses were performed using cortical  parcellations from the Desikan-Killiany atlas. Correlation coefficients (R) and significance values (p) were computed to characterize the strength and statistical significance of age-related changes. Surface-based maps were generated to visualize spatial patterns of laminar MD and age-related changes across the cortex.

Results

Figure 2b presents the surface maps of MD in the inner and outer cortical layers, as well as the laminar contrast (outer minus inner MD), for representative subjects at ages 6, 9, and 12 years. Clear laminar microstructural difference is observed across the cerebral cortex at all ages, with the outer layer consistently exhibiting higher MD than the inner layer. This pattern aligns with established cytoarchitectural differences between superficial and deep layers, such as lower cellular density and greater extracellular space in superficial layers.^4 Both cortical layers demonstrate significant age-related decreases in MD across the cortex, though regression analyses revealed that the rate of decline was slightly attenuated in the outer layer compared to the inner layer in many regions. Additionally, both laminar MD maps display a characteristic spatial gradient, with higher values in frontal regions relative to posterior areas, suggesting regional heterogeneity in cortical microstructural organization. Fig. 2d-e illustrate developmental trajectories of laminar MD across the whole cortex, four major lobes, and three representative regions (sensorimotor, auditory, and superior frontal cortex). All cortical regions examined exhibited age-related MD decreases in both layers throughout childhood, with the outer layer maintaining consistently higher MD values than the inner layer across all ages. The rate of MD decline was relatively consistent across the cortex, with only subtle regional variations. Both cortical layers showed similar developmental trajectories within each region, though the outer layer demonstrated a slightly more gradual decline in some areas. Consistent with this observation, the laminar contrast maps (Figure 2c) showed subtle positive regression slopes, suggesting a subtle increase in laminar differentiation with age. These findings indicate widespread and coordinated microstructural maturation across the cortex during middle childhood.

Conclusion

This study provides the first in vivo characterization of laminar cortical microstructural development in typically developing children aged 5 to 12 years. The ultra-high-resolution multi-shell dMRI acquisition (0.86 mm isotropic) makes it possible to separately quantify microstructural properties of the outer and inner cortical layers, which are primarily involved in cortico-cortical and cortico-subcortical circuits, respectively. Using the novel cortical laminar analysis pipeline, we observed clear MD differences between the inner and outer cortical layers at all ages examined, reflecting fundamental cytoarchitectural differences across cortical depth, including variations in cellular density, dendritic complexity, and extracellular space composition.^4 For example, the consistently lower MD in the inner layer across all ages likely reflects inherent structural differences in deeper cortical layers. Both inner and outer cortical layers demonstrated significant age-related decreases in MD across the cortex, consistent with ongoing maturational processes such as dendritic arborization, synaptogenesis, and increased cellular density.^4 The slightly attenuated rate of decline in the outer layer, along with the subtle increase in laminar differentiation observed with age, suggests potential layer-specific differences in developmental timing or mechanisms. These findings align with histological studies indicating that cortical layers mature along distinct but coordinated timelines. The normative developmental trajectories established here provide critical reference for future investigations of atypical cortical development in neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders, where aberrant laminar organization is often implicated.^5 The cortical laminar analysis pipeline developed in this study offers a framework for high-precision measurement of layer-specific cortical microstructure. This approach can be potentially applied to clinical populations and extended to explore links between laminar cytoarchitecture and cognitive or behavioral outcomes. Future studies with larger samples and longitudinal designs will enable more detailed characterization of regional and layer-specific maturational trajectories and their functional significance during this critical period of brain development.^5
