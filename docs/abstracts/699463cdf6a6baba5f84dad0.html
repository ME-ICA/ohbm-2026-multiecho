Introduction

There has been a surge of interest in studying brain rhythms in the last few years with respect to cognition. Brain rhythms like alpha (8-13 Hz), beta (13-30 Hz) and gamma (30+ Hz) have shown to be involved in different cognitive factors like attention1, working memory2 and higher order cognition whereas theta (4-7 Hz) and delta (1-4 Hz) are shown to be associated with mental imagery, meditation and memory processes. Recently aperiodic activity in the brain has found widespread interest as something impacted by both state and trait properties of an individual. But how both aperiodic and periodic properties of the brain are associated with state and trait features is still an area of active investigation. In this study, we analyze the Human Brain Network (HBN)-EEG dataset3 across a large number of individuals (n=3155) to quantify exactly how the brain oscillations, particularly alpha waves via the individuals’ peak alpha frequency (PAF) and the aperiodic component might relate to the psychopathological parameters, namely p-factor, internalization, externalization and attention as well as the relationship of these factors with age. We also investigated state level features using various metrics for cognitive performance, viz, mean response time and hit rate for a contrast change based visual discrimination task, as well as during resting-state and movie-watching tasks and how they are related to PAF and aperiodic exponents. Our study investigates the use of various brain periodic and aperiodic biomarkers to analyze state and trait features in individuals.

Methods

For our study we used the (HBN)-EEG dataset, wherein the Child Mind Institute (CMI) Healthy Brain Network (HBN) project has collected phenotypical data and EEG recordings from 3240 children and young adults between the ages of 5 and 21. It is hosted on Nemar as well as AWS buckets, both of which are accessible through HBN’s data portal. The data was recorded with Geodesic’s GSN 200 128 channel EEG at a sampling rate of 500hz. We used 3155 subjects with the complete data across the various modalities, with the mean age being 10.25 and the age distribution being heavily right skewed. For the purpose of our analysis, we loaded the data with the EEGChallengeDataset object in the eegdash library, which has the recordings downsampled to 100hz and then applied a bandpass filter on it to filter it from 1hz to 40hz. Frequency over one over F (FOOOF)4 tool was used to isolate the 1/f aperiodic component and the periodic oscillations, and then PAF was calculated from the periodic component keeping the range of alpha dynamic, depending on the age of the subject, in accordance with how we currently understand the alpha band5. The correlations were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation when the data was normally distributed and Spearman’s otherwise.

Psychopathological parameters like internalizing, externalizing, attention and p-factor were collected using Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL). The participants underwent multiple EEG tasks as described here: https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/cmi_healthy_brain_network/MRI_EEG.html. EEG task related parameters like hit-rate, mean response time were computed for a contrast change based visual discrimination task. PAF and aperiodic exponent were calculated for eyes open and eyes closed resting state data along with movie-watching (a clip from Despicable Me movie).

Results

We found that the PAF and aperiodic exponent have a statistical significant correlation (r(2118) = -0.329, p<0.00001), however keeping age as control the significance goes away suggesting the strong role age has on both PAF and aperiodic exponents. Age is significantly correlated to both PAF (r(2281)  = 0.755, p< 0.00001) and aperiodic exponent (r(2281)=-403,p<0.00001). Additionally, we find notable correlations between internalizing and PAF (r(2244)=0.261, p<0.00001), externalizing and PAF(r(2244)=-0.198, p<0.00001), aperiodic exponent and internalizing(r(2643)=-0.134, p<0.00001) and aperiodic exponent and hit rate on the contrast detection task (r(1671)=-0.198, p<0.00001). P-factor and Attention did not have a correlation of more than 0.1 with any feature except for P-factor & Internalizing and P-factor and Externalizing. Since mean response time for the contrast change detection task was not normally distributed, we look at Spearman’s correlation. We find that for mean response time and PAF (ρ(1411) = -0.399, p<0.00001), and for mean response time and aperiodic exponent (ρ(1670) = 0.229). The PAF across various paradigms like eyes closed and eyes open resting state (r(2281)=0.825, p<0.00001) and movie-watching  (r(2281)=0.375, p<0.00001) were also significantly correlated. Other correlations are pointed out in Figure 1. We also find very clear ranges for PAF across age groups. The PAF for subjects in the range of 5 to 8 years varies from 7hz to 9hz, for ages 8 to 10 its 8hz to 10hz and for ages 10 onwards its 9hz to 11hz. Only 12 subjects out of 3155 did not follow this trend, accounting for 0.38% of the subjects.

Conclusion

In the study we analyzed the associations between age and psychopathological factors like internalizing, externalizing and p-factor with PAF and aperiodic exponent. We found that age is positively correlated with both PAF, higher the age, the higher the PAF but it plateaus around 18-20 years of age as previous studies have also shown. Age is negatively associated with aperiodic exponent suggesting that as we age the spectrum becomes flatter. We found that PAF and aperiodic exponent can show strong correlations but are more governed by age suggesting that it is not a feature of the spectral analysis or outcome of the flattening of the frequency spectrogram but an impact of development on both periodic and aperiodic oscillations in the brain and both can be potentially used as independent biomarkers for capturing different neural activity. We also find more evidence of the practicality of using them as biomarkers with the correlations with the Psychopathological parameters as well as the performance metric like hit rate which shows visual discriminatory ability. The range of PAF as age increases has a very clear change, going from 7 to 9 Hz at ages 5 to 8 years old, to 8 to 10 Hz from ages 8 to 10 and 9 to 11 Hz for ages 10 onwards, with very few outliers. This might imply that PAF increases discretely as we reach 8 and 10 years of age, and not continuously. More research is needed as to why we see such developmental changes.
