Introduction

Siblings of individuals with disabilities or chronic illnesses (SID-sibs) experience a wide range of psychological effects. They often demonstrate positive traits like higher empathy and prosocial behavior. However, they are also vulnerable to negative aspects such as anxiety, depressive tendencies (internalizing problems), and heightened stress sensitivity. These ambivalent effects suggest that the psychological impact is highly complex.1,2 The impact is thought to be influenced by the siblings' own cognitions toward their brother or sister, where positive cognitive styles serve as protective factors, while negative styles link to increased psychological symptoms. However, research directly examining the implicit cognitions of SID-sibs remains scarce. Existing cognitive neuroscience studies primarily focus on the disability itself, rather than the siblings' unique perceptions. Furthermore, most studies use subjective questionnaires, which are susceptible to social desirability bias and fail to capture implicit information. The present study aimed to elucidate the implicit cognitions that SID-sibs hold toward their siblings from a cognitive neuroscience perspective. We employed the Implicit Relational Assessment Procedure (IRAP), which assesses relational directionality through reaction time differences,3 combined with Event-Related Potentials (ERPs). We focused on the N400 component, which indexes semantic incongruity,4 and the P3 component, which reflects attention and stimulus evaluation.5 We hypothesized that if SID-sibs hold different implicit cognitions than siblings of typically developing individuals (TDI-sibs), these differences would manifest both in behavioral IRAP performance and in neural indices such as N400 and P3 components.

Methods

A total of 39 healthy adults participated, including 13 with siblings who had disabilities or illnesses (SID-sibs group; 5 males; mean age = 22.5 ± 2.5 years) and 26 with typically developing siblings (TDI-sibs group; 8 males; mean age = 21.9 ± 1.6 years). Seven participants (six TDI-sibs, one SID-sibs) were excluded due to excessive EEG noise. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Keio University, Japan (#240020000). An IRAP was used to evaluate participants’ implicit cognition. Each trial presented a label, a target word (six positive, six negative), and two response options (“the participant’s sibling’s name” / “not the sibling’s name”), with positions counterbalanced. Participants completed two conditions: a sibling-affirming condition (selecting the sibling’s name for positive words and the alternative for negative words) and a sibling-denying condition with reversed mappings. The eight blocks (four per condition) were presented alternately, and each block contained 168 trials. Trials began with a fixation (500 ms) and a blank screen (500 ms). Participants responded using the “d” (left) or “k” (right) key, and incorrect responses required correction before continuing. Practice blocks (24 trials per condition) were repeated if accuracy was insufficient. E-Prime 3 controlled stimulus presentation (Figure 1). EEG and electrooculogram (EOG) signals were recorded with the Biosemi Active2 system (2048 Hz) from 32 electrodes according to the 10–20 system, with CMS/DRL placed at AF1/AF2 and earlobes serving as references. Behavioral analyses included accuracy and IRAP scores. Trials with reaction times below 100 ms were excluded. For each pair of affirming–denying blocks, we calculated mean reaction times and their standard deviation. The IRAP score for each pair was computed as (denying RT − affirming RT) / SD, and the final score was the average of the four values. Higher scores indicated relatively positive implicit cognition toward the sibling.3 EEG data were band-pass filtered (0.05–30 Hz), notch-filtered (50 Hz), re-referenced to the earlobes, and segmented from −100 to 1000 ms relative to stimulus onset. Baseline correction used the −100 to 0 ms interval, and ICA was applied to remove ocular artifacts. Trials exceeding ±50 μV were rejected. ERPs were averaged, and P3 (300–400 ms) and N400 (340–500 ms) amplitudes and latencies were extracted at Pz using Brain Analyzer 3. Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.1. IRAP scores were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA (group × target valence). P3 and N400 amplitudes and latencies were analyzed using two-way ANOVAs (group × condition). The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Accuracy on the IRAP task was high (mean accuracy > 90%). IRAP scores were positive across all groups, reflecting an overall positive implicit cognition. A two-way ANOVA on IRAP scores revealed a significant main effect of stimulus combination (F(1,37) = 10.41, p < .01, η2 = .03). Crucially, the main effect of group and the interaction were not significant. Thus, behavioral performance did not significantly differ between SID-sibs and TDI-sibs. P3 latency analysis showed a significant main effect of target stimulus (F(1,30) = 11.27, p < .01, η2 = .02) and a significant group × condition interaction (F(1,30) = 10.27, p < .01, η2 = .02). Post hoc analysis revealed that in the SID-sibs group, P3 latency was significantly longer in the sibling-affirming condition (356.5 ± 4.2 ms) than in the sibling-denying condition (347.8 ± 4.1 ms). Furthermore, in the sibling-affirming condition, the SID-sibs group exhibited significantly longer latency than the TDI-sibs group (343.0 ± 3.4 ms). For P3 amplitude, the main effect of target stimulus was significant (F(1,30) = 27.67, p < .001, η2 = .06). N400 amplitude analysis showed a significant main effect of target stimulus (F(1,30) = 17.96, p < .001, η2 = .04) and a significant group × condition interaction (F(1,30) = 4.21, p < .05, η2 = .01). Post hoc analysis revealed that in the SID-sibs group, N400 amplitude was significantly less negative (more positive) in the sibling-affirming condition (1.14 ± 0.74 μV) than in the sibling-denying condition (-0.78 ± 1.05 μV, p < .01). In the sibling-denying condition, the SID-sibs group tended to show more negative amplitudes than the TDI-sibs group (p = .10) (Figure 2). Neither P3 nor N400 latency or amplitude showed significant main effects or interactions in response to negative words.

Conclusion

This study investigated the implicit cognitions of SID-sibs toward their siblings using the IRAP and ERPs, focusing on the divergence between behavioral and neural data. Behavioral IRAP results showed that both SID-sibs and TDI-sibs groups exhibited positive implicit cognition toward their siblings. The absence of group differences in behavioral IRAP scores, which measure overt response speed, suggests that SID-sibs do not explicitly differ from TDI-sibs in their general positive evaluation. Despite the behavioral consistency, significant group differences emerged at the neurophysiological level, indicating underlying differences in cognitive evaluation processes. P3 latency analysis suggests that SID-sibs may experience greater cognitive effort or delay in establishing or accessing positive associations with their sibling compared to the TDI-sibs group. Conversely, the N400 amplitude difference in the sibling-denying condition suggests that for SID-sibs, explicitly associating negative attributes with their sibling may violate semantic expectations or be processed as semantically less natural, indicating a strong internal cognitive framework that favors the positive perception of the sibling.4 The apparent dissociation between the behavioral (no difference) and neurophysiological (significant difference) findings is the most critical contribution of this study. It underscores the limitations of relying solely on behavioral or self-report measures in studying SID-sibs. Neural data indicate that SID-sibs experience latent cognitive conflict or increased cognitive load when evaluating their sibling. Yet this is not apparent behaviorally, as they successfully maintain a positive attitude. This dual pattern—internal rejection of the negative and delayed processing of the positive—provides a nuanced neurophysiological signature of the complex, often ambivalent, psychological environment experienced by SID-sibs. These results have significant clinical and theoretical implications. Understanding these neural markers of cognitive conflict can inform intervention strategies, suggesting that support programs for SID-sibs should focus not just on external coping behaviors but also on developing cognitive strategies to manage the underlying emotional and cognitive burden associated with complex sibling evaluations, potentially reducing the observed increased processing time (P3 latency) and cognitive dissonance. Future research should address several limitations. First, the task design may still allow for strategic responding in the IRAP. Second, due to the nature of the IRAP, direct comparison between processing positive and negative words remains challenging. Furthermore, the limited sample size prevented the control of crucial confounding factors, such as the type of disability, birth order, and gender. Prior research indicates that these factors significantly influence psychological impacts. Future large-scale comparative studies must account for these variables to provide a more comprehensive picture of the effects on SID-sibs and to validate these neurophysiological markers across diverse populations.
