Introduction

ADHD and ASD show substantial cognitive, attentional, and socio-emotional variability, reflected in structural differences across multiple regions rather than a single disorder-specific pattern. Increasing evidence suggests that symptom dimensions, including inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, and emotional symptoms, better capture this variability, as these traits span diagnostic categories and share underlying neural mechanisms. Structural MRI studies describe deviations in frontal, temporal, parietal, and striatal regions, as well as in cognitive control, salience, self-referential, and affective networks. Large-scale analyses show that these alterations cluster at the network level, indicating coordinated differences across systems involved in executive function and emotion regulation. Emotional and internalizing symptoms, common across neurodevelopmental conditions, are linked to alterations in fronto-limbic and cognitive control systems, underscoring their relevance to developmental trajectories. Adolescence is a period of major neural reorganization within socio-emotional and regulatory networks. Developmental neuroimaging studies demonstrate that brain-behavior associations during this period may follow nonlinear rather than uniform age-related trends, and flexible modeling approaches have been used to characterize these complex trajectories.

Building on this framework, the present study examines how inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, and emotional symptoms relate to regional brain volume across cortical and subcortical regions. Linear, quadratic, and spline models were used to test linear and nonlinear associations with regional volume, and with hemispheric asymmetry included as a secondary measure to contextualize lateralized effects. We hypothesized symptom dimensions would be associated with distributed variation across networks, and that emotional symptoms would contribute uniquely to these patterns given their involvement in fronto-limbic development.

Methods

Participants

Data were drawn from 380 children and adolescents (ages 3–27 years; M = 12.80, SD = 3.41) in the Province of Ontario Neurodevelopmental Network (POND). A T1-weighted MPRAGE structural MRI sequence was used to assess regional brain volumes across participants. 

Participants were classified into ADHD-Inattentive (ADHD-I), ADHD-Combined (ADHD-C), and Non-ADHD groups using SWAN symptom thresholds. ADHD-I was defined as SWAN-I ≥ 4 and SWAN-HI < 4 (n = 109), and ADHD-C as SWAN-I ≥ 3 and SWAN-HI ≥ 4 (n = 138). Individuals with subthreshold scores on both dimensions (SWAN-I < 3 and SWAN-HI < 4) were classified as non-ADHD (n = 112). A small number of participants exhibited elevated hyperactive–impulsive symptoms without elevated inattentive symptoms (n = 33). These individuals did not meet criteria for either ADHD-I or ADHD-C and were therefore excluded prior to analysis. HI-only presentations were not analyzed as a distinct subtype because hyperactive–impulsive symptoms show marked developmental attenuation and reduced diagnostic stability in adolescence1. In contrast, inattentive symptoms remain more persistent and clinically informative across development1. This allowed analyses to focus on the two ADHD subtypes with stable and interpretable symptom profiles.

Diagnostic group (ADHD or ASD) was included as a covariate in all models. All data were de-identified and obtained from a pre-characterized observational dataset.

Measures

Internalizing symptoms were indexed using the RCADS Total Anxiety and Depression T-score (ANXDEP T-score), treated as a continuous predictor. ROIs were identified from a harmonized set of 79 cortical and subcortical regions present in both hemispheres.

MRI Processing

Structural MRI data and precomputed ROI volumes were obtained from POND. Volumes exceeding ±4 SD from the hemisphere-specific ROI mean were replaced with missing values and excluded from asymmetry calculations. 

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.1). Because the association between internalizing symptoms and brain structure may be nonlinear, we first determined the optimal functional form of the ANXDEP predictor separately for each ROI. For each model, we computed corrected AIC (AICc), adjusted R², and 5-fold cross-validated RMSE. The best-fitting ANXDEP form for each ROI was selected first by lowest AICc and, among ties, lowest CV-RMSE. A sensitivity analysis repeating this comparison without subtype assessed whether model selection was driven by subtype × ANXDEP interactions.

Each ROI was then refit using its selected ANXDEP model. Regression coefficients, standard errors, t statistics, and p values were extracted. To provide a standardized estimate of effect magnitude for each model term, t statistics were converted to Cohen’s f² for 1-df tests using the identity F = t², and approximate achieved power was computed using these effect-size estimates. 

To characterize the magnitude of symptom-related variation within each subtype, model-based predicted ROI values were generated across the 5th–95th percentile range of ANXDEP T-scores while holding covariates constant. For each subtype and ROI, a curvature metric, the peak-to-trough difference in predicted values, was computed, capturing the amplitude of ANXDEP-related structural variation.

Results

Across fronto-striatal and frontoparietal networks, anxiety and depression showed significant nonlinear associations with regional volume, with consistent subtype differences in curve shape, turning-point location, and curvature magnitude. 

In the striatum, nonlinear effects were present in all regions that survived correction. In the right nucleus accumbens, early symptom-related spline terms were significant (adjusted P = .009, power = .79), and subtype-symptom interactions were also present, including a strong interaction between ADHD-C and the first spline component (adjusted P = .004, power = .87). These effects indicated distinct symptom-volume patterns across ADHD-I, ADHD-C, and non-ADHD groups. In the right post-commissural putamen, the second spline term was also significant (adjusted P = .009, power = .82), supporting nonlinear effects across dorsal as well as ventral striatal regions. Turning-point analyses showed subtype differences in modeled peak locations: ADHD-C consistently showed later turning points at higher anxiety/depression values, ADHD-I showed earlier turning points, and non-ADHD values typically fell between the two or showed additional late inflection points. For example, in the right post-commissural putamen, the main inverted-U peak occurred at anxiety T-scores of approximately 51 in ADHD-I, 48 in non-ADHD, and 65 in ADHD-C. Curvature estimates demonstrated a consistent group ordering across all striatal structures; for example, in the right post-commissural putamen, curvature was highest in non-ADHD (510), intermediate in ADHD-I (296), and lowest in ADHD-C (129).

In the frontoparietal control network, quadratic models identified significant nonlinear associations in the left supramarginal gyrus, right angular gyrus (adjusted P = .027, power = .71), and left postcentral gyrus. These regions showed a common combination of negative linear and positive quadratic symptom components (supramarginal gyrus quadratic term, adjusted P = .028, power = .74). Subtype-symptom interactions were strongest in the supramarginal gyrus, where both ADHD-I (adjusted P = .014, power = .86) and ADHD-C (adjusted P = .006, power = .92) showed significant quadratic interactions, and in the postcentral gyrus, where ADHD-I showed the largest subtype-related effects (adjusted P =.045, power = .62). Turning-point estimates again followed the sequence observed in the striatum: ADHD-C showed later vertices, ADHD-I earlier vertices, and non-ADHD either early peaks or U-shaped patterns. Parietal curvature values were large, with non-ADHD showing the steepest modeled changes and both ADHD subtypes showing lower curvature. For example, in the supramarginal gyrus, curvature reached approximately 1336 in non-ADHD, compared with 767 in ADHD-I and 700 in ADHD-C, exceeding frontal and striatal curvature magnitudes.

Within the default mode network, effects were more restricted. The right angular gyrus and left posterior cingulate gyrus showed significant nonlinear associations and subtype interactions in at least one model. In the posterior cingulate, the interaction between ADHD-I and the quadratic term was significant (adjusted P = .045, power = .59). Turning-point estimates again showed subtype differences, although curvature magnitudes were smaller than those in fronto-striatal or frontoparietal regions. Other DMN-related medial temporal regions, including the subiculum, alveus, and temporal poles, showed no effects that survived correction.

Conclusion

This study shows that internalizing symptoms relate to brain structure through nonlinear, subtype-dependent patterns distributed across large-scale networks. Anxiety and depression were linked to coordinated changes in curve shape, turning-point timing, and curvature magnitude, indicating that symptom dimensions interact with neurodevelopment differently in ADHD-I, ADHD-C, and non-ADHD youth. Across networks, a consistent hierarchy emerged, where ADHD-C displayed later and shallower responses, ADHD-I showed earlier and intermediate responses, and non-ADHD showed the steepest, most dynamic curves. This pattern aligns with evidence that ADHD comprises of heterogeneous neurodevelopmental subgroups rather than a single mechanistic profile,2 and extends this view by demonstrating subtype-specific modulation of shared nonlinear patterns.

The striatum showed the strongest responses. Multi-phase trajectories across ventral and dorsal nuclei indicate that symptom effects unfold across several severity ranges rather than following a single inverted-U. Subtype differences in turning-point timing, earlier in ADHD-I, later in ADHD-C, mirror accounts of distinct maturational pacing across ADHD presentations, with inattentive profiles diverging earlier and combined profiles diverging more gradually from normative development.2 The consistent curvature hierarchy across striatal nodes suggests that internalizing symptoms modulate subtype-linked developmental profiles in a predictable direction. These findings align with work showing that internalizing symptoms shift the balance between reward-related and self-referential systems during adolescence, altering the sensitivity of striatal circuits. Frontal regions showed weaker nonlinear associations. This pattern is consistent with known functional distinctions, frontal circuits in ADHD often exhibit reduced task engagement and increased signal variability, which may limit the extent to which structural metrics track symptom severity.² 

Parietal regions showed the largest curvature magnitudes. These nodes form the core of frontoparietal control and dorsal attention systems and are highly sensitive to regulatory demands. Youth with attentional and affective dysregulation demonstrate reduced stability in these circuits during cognitive control,3 The curvature hierarchy, steepest in non-ADHD, reduced in both ADHD subtypes, may reflect differences in regulatory flexibility, with internalizing symptoms further shaping frontoparietal–default mode interactions during adolescence. This pattern also explains the consistent subtype ordering of turning points observed across regions. Default mode areas showed more modest effects, but subtype-specific curve shapes in the posterior cingulate and angular gyrus indicate that internalizing symptoms also influence networks supporting self-referential and socio-emotional processing. These regions undergo substantial reorganization during adolescence and are sensitive to internalizing symptoms,4 providing a plausible basis for the observed effects. The absence of corrected findings in medial temporal and limbic structures likely reflects subtler effects or processes not captured by static volumetric measures.

Together, these results support a dimensional and developmental account of neuroanatomical heterogeneity. ADHD subtypes do not express distinct structural phenotypes; instead, they deform shared nonlinear symptom–brain relationships along predictable axes of timing and magnitude. This view is consistent with models proposing that emotional symptoms contribute to network-level reorganization and that neurodevelopment is shaped differently across clinical subgroups.5 Modeling symptoms dimensionally captures variability that categorical contrasts obscure, providing a clearer picture of how internalizing symptoms influence brain structure during adolescence.
