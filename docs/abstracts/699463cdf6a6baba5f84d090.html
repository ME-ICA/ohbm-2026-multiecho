Introduction

Functional MRI is a valuable tool for pre-surgical planning for pediatric epilepsy patients, yet nearly 50% of scans are compromised by motion. Standard practice evaluates motion at the volume level, where volumes are collected every 2-3 seconds. However, motion can occur anytime, and volume-level measurement is less sensitive to rapid movements.  Emerging slice-level monitoring techniques offer more precise detection of motion at the individual slice acquisition, which occurs every 50-100 milliseconds, capturing intravolume motion. This project aims to determine if applying slice-level motion metrics to fMRI processing improves resting-state network quality for pediatric epilepsy patients.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI sequences were collected from 16 epilepsy surgery candidates recruited from Boston Children’s Hospital. Imaging was performed at a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner using simultaneous multi-slice (SMS) acquisition with a multi-band factor of 4. Acquisition parameters were: TR = 1.0 s, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 52°, voxel size = 2.4 × 2.4 × 2.4 mm³. The number of volumes varied per subject (360, 480, or 960), based on participant cooperability. Data was preprocessed with fMRIprep, and slice-level motion metrics were extracted retrospectively using a Slice Localization Integrated Motion Monitoring (SLIMM) tool, developed at our institution.^1 Volume level motion was determined using framewise displacement from fMRIprep, where motion was defined as displacement above 0.3mm based on prior literature in pediatric samples.^2 Slice-level motion was determined using a comparative displacement measure at the same threshold, where if any slice within a volume reached this threshold, the volume was removed. Motion-corrupted volumes were regressed out using AFNI, producing both volume- and slice-level scrubbed images. All data was processed through an in-house MATLAB-based Independent Component Analysis (ICA) script previously decribed^3, using the MELODIC tool in FSL to extract language and motor networks. Non-regressed ICA results were used as a baseline for comparison. Network quality was assessed by the percent of subject functional voxel overlap with corresponding template masks, laterality index (LI), and Amplitude of Low-Frequency Fluctuation (ALFF). The laterality index was derived from hemispheric ALFF results, where negative values indicated right dominance, and positive values indicated left dominance.

Results

Of the 16 subjects (mean age: 2.65 ± 5.66 years), 14 exhibited significant motion based on volume-level displacement, and 8 exhibited significant motion based on intravolume slice-level displacement. Two subjects in each group were excluded due to failure of the ICA network extraction. Of the final group, 5 subjects were successfully processed using both volume- and slice-level approaches (mean age 10.87 ± 5.70 years). For these 5, the Friedman test suggested a trend toward an effect of motion correction method for percent network overlap with the language template (χ²(2) = 5.20, p = 0.074), with higher overlap mean rank for slice-level scrubbing. Post-hoc testing indicated greater language overlap for slice-level versus baseline (p=0.043), but no difference between slice-level and volume-level, or volume-level vs baseline. For the motor networks, motion correction method significantly affected percent overlap, (χ²(2) = 6.40, p = 0.041), with volume-level scrubbing outperforming standard preprocessing (p = 0.043), and a trend towards greater motor network overlap for slice-level vs baseline (p=0.08). At the individual level, slice-level scrubbing improved language network overlap, and language laterality was consistent with the laterality from the baseline results for all subjects (6/6). In contrast, the volume method was more mixed, with 4/12 demonstrating reduced network overlap and 5/12 having discordant ALFF language laterality from baseline. Furthermore, among the 5 subjects processed with both methods, 1 demonstrated reduced overlap with volume level scrubbing. However, for this subject, volume-level regression had a greater overall volume loss compared to slice-level regression (38%, 15% respectively). This trend was similarly observed for motor networks.

Conclusion

Overall, our results suggest that motion scrubbing enhances resting-state language and motor network identification relative to non-regressed processing. Slice-level scrubbing was associated with more consistent identification of the language networks, including improved percent overlap with functional templates and preserved functional lateralization patterns. In contrast, volume-level scrubbing produced more variable effects, improving motor network overlap at the group level, but reducing language/motor network overlap and more discordant hemisphere lateralization for some subjects at the individual level. These findings suggest that intravolume, slice-level motion correction may provide a more reliable framework for identifying clinically relevant resting-state networks. Studies with larger samples are needed to extend these findings and examine the impact of slice-level motion correction on functional network integrity. In addition, future studies should explore incorporating prospective, slice-level motion monitoring during acquisition, which may further improve data quality, reducing the need for post-scan data removal.
