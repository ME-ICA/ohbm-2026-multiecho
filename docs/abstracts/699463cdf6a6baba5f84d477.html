Introduction

Psychiatric disorders are among the leading causes of global disability and are increasingly conceptualized as overlapping conditions rather than discrete entities, given their shared genetic liabilities, symptom dimensions, and high comorbidity1. This convergence implies common neurobiological mechanisms; however, the transdiagnostic architecture of brain alterations remains incompletely understood. Prior neuroimaging studies have reported cross-disorder abnormalities in both structural and functional connectivity, including vulnerability of hub regions to white-matter dysconnectivity2 and network-specific alterations in functional variability3. Notably, most transdiagnostic work has focused on single imaging modalities, despite strong evidence that brain structure and function are tightly coupled across spatial and temporal scales4. How this structural–functional coupling is altered across psychiatric disorders, and whether such alterations follow principled cortical hierarchies, remains unclear. Here, we investigated transdiagnostic patterns of morphometric–functional coupling across five major psychiatric disorders and examined their hierarchical, cellular, and molecular underpinnings.

Methods

We analyzed multisite neuroimaging data from 958 patients diagnosed with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), bipolar disorder (BD), major depressive disorder (MDD), or schizophrenia spectrum disorder (SSD), and 1,064 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Cortical morphology was characterized using morphometric inverse divergence (MIND) networks derived from T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), while functional connectivity (FC) networks were constructed from resting-state functional MRI. The cortical surface was parcellated into 308 regions, generated by subdividing the Desikan–Killiany atlas into spatially contiguous parcels.

For each vertex, five morphometric features (cortical thickness, gray matter volume, surface area, sulcal depth, and mean curvature) were extracted and standardized. Each region was represented as a multivariate distribution of these features, and pairwise regional similarity was computed using symmetric Kullback–Leibler divergence, yielding individual-level MIND networks. FC matrices were derived using Pearson correlations between regional time series and Fisher z-transformed. Structural and functional networks were integrated at the individual level using similarity network fusion. Regional fusion strength was quantified as the sum of weighted connections for each node. Case–control differences were estimated using Cohen’s d for each disorder (Fig. 1a). To identify shared transdiagnostic effects, we applied principal component analysis (PCA) to the concatenated disorder-specific effect maps (Fig. 1b). Spatial patterns were contextualized using the Yeo-7 functional networks (Fig. 1c) and von Economo cytoarchitectonic classes (Fig. 1d), with statistical significance assessed using spin tests to account for spatial autocorrelation.

To link transdiagnostic effect to molecular architecture, we performed partial least squares (PLS) regression between the cross-disorder effect map and whole-brain gene expression data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (Fig. 2a). Cell-type-specific gene sets were obtained from Seidlitz et al.5. These gene sets cover seven canonical cell types: microglia, oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, astrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursors (OPCs), and excitatory and inhibitory neurons. Enrichment significance was evaluated using spatial permutation tests and gene-level bootstrapping (Fig. 2b), followed by pathway enrichment analysis using Metascape (Fig. 2c).

Results

Disorder-specific analyses revealed distinct yet partially overlapping alterations in morphometric–functional coupling across diagnostic groups (Fig. 1a). ADHD showed no significant case–control differences. ASD and SSD exhibited increased coupling in visual networks and reduced coupling in default-mode and frontoparietal networks, whereas BD and MDD showed unique alterations in somatomotor and dorsal attention networks, respectively (all pSPIN < 0.05). The first component of PCA identified a dominant transdiagnostic dimension explaining 34.1% of the variance across disorders. This component followed a sensory–association axis, differentiating visual cortices from frontoparietal/default cortices (Fig. 1b). This gradient aligned with both large-scale functional organization (Yeo-7) and cytoarchitectonic hierarchy, differentiating primary sensory from association cortices (Fig. 1c & 1d).

PLS analysis revealed a gene expression pattern (PLS1) explaining 39.5% of variance in the cross-disorder effect (pSPIN < 0.001), with strong spatial correspondence (Fig. 2a; r = 0.63, pSPIN < 0.001). Cell-type enrichment analyses showed that negatively weighted PLS1 genes were significantly overrepresented in astrocyte-associated gene sets (Fig. 2b; pPERM = 0.022). These genes were enriched for metabolic and glial-related pathways, including small-molecule metabolism, glial cell differentiation, and calcium signaling, whereas no significant cell-type enrichment was observed for positively weighted genes (Fig. 2c).

Conclusion

Our findings reveal a shared transdiagnostic alteration in morphometric–functional coupling that is organized along the cortical sensory–association hierarchy and is selectively linked to astrocyte-related molecular pathways. This work provides a biologically grounded, dimensional framework for understanding convergent brain network dysfunction across psychiatric disorders.
