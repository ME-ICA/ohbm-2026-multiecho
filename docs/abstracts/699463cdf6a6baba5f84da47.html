Introduction

Understanding how affective states reorganize large-scale brain dynamics is a central goal of human brain mapping. EEG microstates—brief periods of stable scalp topography—are thought to reflect the coordinated activity of distributed neural generators and to provide a temporally precise window into network-level processing. Recent work has shown that self-generated emotional states induce systematic shifts in microstate dynamics, particularly involving microstates linked to salience, attention, and default-mode processing.^1 However, far less is known about how externally induced, ecologically valid stressors reshape these dynamics and how individual traits modulate such effects.

Immersive virtual reality (VR) enables controlled induction of acute stress while preserving naturalistic sensory and behavioral context. From a mechanistic perspective, microstate changes are increasingly interpreted in terms of underlying network generators and functional meaning, rather than in terms of descriptive topographies alone.^2 This framework predicts that stress-related microstate reorganization should reflect shifts in salience detection, threat monitoring, and regulatory control.

Here, we investigated how an immersive VR-simulated earthquake modulates EEG microstate temporal dynamics, emotional and anxiety reactivity, and salivary cortisol. Building on prior work on internally generated affect, we further tested whether resilience and childhood trauma shape the coupling between microstate dynamics and stress responses. By combining mobile EEG, endocrine markers, and immersive VR, this study probes how large-scale brain dynamics adapt to acute, externally driven affective challenge.

Methods

Sixty-five healthy adults (36 women; mean age = 22.8 ± 5.9 years) were randomly assigned to a VR earthquake stress condition (VR-ES, n = 30) or a non-stressful VR control condition (VR-CTRL, n = 35). Participants experienced a 10-minute immersive scenario in a CAVE-based VR system. The VR-ES condition simulated an unexpected earthquake using multimodal visual, auditory, and tactile cues, whereas the VR-CTRL condition involved calm exploration of the same environment.

High-density EEG (64 channels, 1 kHz) was recorded before (PRE), during, and after (POST) VR exposure. Salivary cortisol was collected at five time points (T0–T4). Emotional and anxiety reactivity were assessed repeatedly using visual analog scales. Resilience (CD-RISC) and childhood trauma (CTQ) were assessed prior to EEG acquisition using validated self-report scales.

EEG preprocessing included filtering (1–40 Hz), artifact rejection, AMICA decomposition, ICLabel-based component classification, channel interpolation, average re-referencing, and downsampling to 125 Hz. Microstate analysis was performed using a two-level k-means clustering approach (individual and group levels), yielding six canonical microstates (A–F). Temporal parameters (mean duration, occurrence) were extracted for PRE, POST, and VR periods.

Non-parametric statistics were used for microstate comparisons (Wilcoxon signed-rank, Mann–Whitney tests; FDR correction). Behavioral and cortisol data were analyzed using mixed ANOVAs. Moderation analyses tested whether resilience or trauma modulated associations between microstate dynamics and affective measures.

Results

Across conditions, six EEG microstates accounted for 90.41% of variance in PRE–POST data and 84.57–85.14% during VR exposure, with highly similar spatial topographies between VR-ES and VR-CTRL (all r > 0.90) (see Figure 1).

Behavioral results

Emotional reactivity showed a significant Time × Group interaction (F(4,310)=5.86, p<0.001, η²ₚ=0.070). In the VR-ES group, emotional reactivity increased immediately after VR (T0: 8.50±10.81; T1: 24.43±25.77; p<0.001), whereas no sustained increases were observed at later time points. As seen in Figure 2, anxiety reactivity followed a similar pattern (interaction: F(4,305)=9.20, p<0.001, η²ₚ=0.108), with a significant VR-ES increase at T1 (T0: 8.41±11.01; T1: 24.33±25.77; p=0.047).

Cortisol results:

Cortisol levels showed significant main effects of Time (F(4,316)=5.18, p<0.001, η²ₚ=0.062) and Group (F(1,316)=4.00, p=0.046, η²ₚ=0.013). In VR-CTRL, cortisol decreased from baseline (T0: −1.84±0.48) to T4 (−2.32±0.43; p=0.001), while VR-ES levels remained relatively stable. In VR-ES, cortisol change scores correlated positively with resilience (ΔT1–T0: r=0.53, p=0.009).

EEG microstates during VR exposure:

Compared to VR-CTRL, the VR-ES group exhibited shorter mean duration of microstate C (83.78±10.41 vs 94.09±12.21 ms; p<0.001, rb=0.55) and longer duration of microstate D (87.51±12.03 vs 76.89±6.34 ms; p<0.001, rb=0.52). Microstate D also occurred more frequently in VR-ES (2.42±0.62 Hz) than VR-CTRL (1.71±0.54 Hz; p<0.001, rb=0.63).

Moderation results:

Microstate dynamics were moderated by resilience and trauma. For example, the mean duration of microstate C during VR was positively associated with emotional reactivity in high-resilience individuals (β = 0.52, p = 0.012). Microstate D occurrence was negatively associated with emotional reactivity in the same subgroup (β = -0.01, p = 0.018). Thus, the decrease in C and increase in D microstates observed during VR-ES predicted lower anxiety after VR in highly resilient participants. In addition, trauma scores also moderated associations between microstate dynamics and anxiety reactivity in opposite effects with resilience (all p<0.05).

Resting EEG microstates before (PRE) and after (POST) VR exposure:

After VR-ES, microstate E showed a significant reduction in mean duration (PRE: 75.44±6.06 ms; POST: 73.51±6.31 ms; p=0.042, rb=0.58). In opposition to the EEG microstate pattern during the reactive VR-ES phase, in the recovery after VR-ES, microstate C increased in occurrence (PRE: 2.25±0.31 Hz; POST: 2.35±0.33 Hz; p=0.044, rb=0.55) and microstate D decreased (PRE: 1.66±0.59 Hz; POST: 1.85±0.52 Hz; p=0.042, rb=0.59). No comparable effects were observed in VR-CTRL.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that natural disaster stress induced by immersive VR produces selective and statistically robust reorganization of EEG microstate dynamics, accompanied by transient changes in emotional, anxiety, and endocrine responses. Compared to self-generated affective states, which primarily modulate microstates associated with internally oriented processing, externally induced stress led to pronounced changes in microstates linked to salience detection and attentional control, particularly microstates C and D.^3

In current microstate generators, microstate C has been linked to task-negtive frontal–parietal regions that serve as hubs of the default mode network. In contrast, microstate D is consistently linked to task-positive attention and executive functioning generators involved in cognitive control and threat monitoring.^2 The increased duration and occurrence of microstate D during the VR earthquake, alongside reduced representation of microstate C, is consistent with a shift toward externally oriented vigilance and adaptive control under acute stress.

Importantly, microstate D dynamics were systematically related to emotional and anxiety reactivity and moderated by resilience and trauma. This finding aligns with recent meta-analytic evidence showing that alterations in microstate D are among the most reliable EEG markers of mood disorders, including major depression, where reduced flexibility or aberrant engagement of executive functioning-related control networks has been reported Chivu et al., (2024).^3 In this context, the present results suggest that microstate D may index a dimension of stress sensitivity, bridging adaptive responses to acute threat and vulnerability to affective dysregulation.

The observed moderation effects further indicate that resilience and trauma shape how large-scale brain dynamics translate into subjective and physiological stress responses. Individuals with higher resilience showed distinct microstate–emotion coupling, supporting the notion that trait-level factors influence the functional deployment of salience and control networks under stress.

Together, these findings extend microstate research beyond internally generated affect to ecologically valid, externally driven stress, and position EEG microstates as sensitive markers of adaptive and maladaptive network dynamics. Integrating immersive VR with mobile EEG and stress biomarkers offers a powerful framework for mapping affective brain dynamics in real-world contexts.
