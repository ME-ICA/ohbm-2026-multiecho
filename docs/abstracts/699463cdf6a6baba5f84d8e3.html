Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by impairments across multiple functional domains, including motor, sensory, behavioral, and cognitive functioning. Although the biological mechanisms underlying ASD remain poorly understood, increasing evidence suggests that deficits in information processing may represent a core feature of the disorder, affecting multiple large-scale neural systems. One brain structure central to information processing and neural integration is the thalamus, which plays a critical role in brain development and in the transmission of information throughout the brain. While it has traditionally been viewed as a sensory relay to the cortex, growing evidence indicates that the thalamus is involved in a broad range of neural processes that modulate, integrate, and coordinate information flow to the cortex and between cortical regions. 

Consistent with this view, previous studies have implicated the thalamus in ASD, reporting both structural and functional connectivity abnormalities^1, including abnormal thalamic volume and both hyper- and hypoconnectivity of the thalamus with the cerebral cortex. However, the thalamic microstructural properties underlying these deficits remain poorly understood, and studies examining thalamic microstructure remain limited^2.  Therefore, in the present study, we investigate thalamic microstructure in ASD versus typically developing participants using diffusional kurtosis imaging (DKI). Associations of the thalamic microstructure with symptoms and age are also examined. Core to ASD, autistic symptoms include impairments in social communication and interaction and restricted and repetitive behaviors. 

Methods

Diffusion imaging and anatomical data were acquired in 16 males with high functioning ASD (IQ>80) and 17 comparison typically developing (TD) participants18-25 years old. The two groups were frequency matched for age and IQ.  The ASD diagnosis was confirmed using Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) administered for all ASD participants and Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R) administered for 11 available caregivers. Whereas the ADOS describes current behaviors, the ADI-R captures a more historical perspective of the autistic traits.  ADOS and ADI-R measures describing communication (COM), reciprocal social interaction (RSI), and restricted interest/stereotyped behaviors (RI/SB) were employed to quantify the severity of autistic traits in the ASD group.

Diffusion data was acquired for two b values (1000 and 2000 s/mm2) and for b0=0 s/mm2. Twelve uniformly distributed encoding directions were sampled for b=1000s/mm2 and 42 for b=2000s/mm2. Two averages were collected for the diffusion weighted images and 10 for the b0 image. Images were corrected for distortions due to B0 field inhomogeneities using the acquired field map. Image registration was used to correct for motion. A kurtosis approximation of the diffusion signal was used to calculate mean kurtosis (MK) for each subject at each voxel. Mean kurtosis is assumed to be a measure of tissue complexity, and has been used previously to describe gray matter development and integrity.^3,4 Thalamic segmentation was performed for each subject using the anatomical image and the FreeSurfer software package. The segmented maps were then registered to the diffusion data and subject-specific masks were obtained for the left and right thalamus. Mean MK values were then obtained for left and right thalamus in each participant. Two-tailed independent samples t-tests and Spearman’s rank correlations were used to examine group differences and relationships between imaging and behavioral variables. The general linear model was employed to test for groupxage interactions. Tests were considered significant at p≤0.05.

Results

MK was decreased in ASD versus TD participants in both right and left thalamus, with results reaching significance for the left thalamus (right thalamus: p=0.09, Cohen’d d=0.6; left thalamus: p=0.05, Cohen’s d=0.7). MK associations with age (Figure 1) were significant in ASD but not TD participants for the right thalamus (rho=.54, p=.03), suggesting continuing microstructural changes in the ASD but not the TD group during the examined age period.  A significant groupxage interaction was noted for MK of the right thalamus (p<0.05). Notably, group differences in MK appear to be larger in the younger participants, with older ASD participants showing similar MK values to the TD participants of similar age.

Significant associations were noted between MK and the ADOS and ADI-R metrics. The MK of the left thalamus was associated with ADI-R scores on reciprocal social interaction (rho=-0.73, p=0.01), and restricted behavior (rho=-0.70, p=0.02). The MK of the left thalamus was associated with ADOS score on restricted social interaction (rho=-0.52, p=0.04). 

Conclusion

Thalamus microstructural integrity appears to be impaired in young adults with high-functioning ASD. Examination of the age-dependence of the thalamic microstructure suggests different developmental trajectories in ASD compared with TD participants.  More specifically, our data indicates early deficits that may be potentially compensated by a protracted development. Future studies should examine more extended age periods to confirm our findings. 

Notably, we observed associations between thalamic microstructure with reciprocal social interactions and restrictive behaviors assessed from both current (ADOS) and/or developmentally perspectives (ADI-R). These results are consistent with animal model work^5 suggesting decreased number of neurons and non-neuronal cells that associate with ASD-like behavior. Therefore, MK may capture alterations in the cellular thalamic make-up relevant to ASD-like behavior and may serve as a biomarker of thalamic development and integrity in ASD.

Future work will aim to discriminate between the microstructural properties of the different thalamic nuclei and confirm these results in larger data samples.
