Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) affects over 322 million people worldwide and is a leading cause of disability. About 30% of patients develop treatment-resistant depression (TRD), unresponsive to conventional antidepressants. Non-invasive brain stimulation, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) targeting the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (L-DLPFC), offers a promising alternative. Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT) is a high-dose, accelerated intermittent theta burst stimulation protocol guided by functional connectivity, achieving rapid and robust clinical improvement—62% mean reduction in depression severity in five days, with large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.71).

Despite these clinical gains, the neurophysiological mechanisms of SNT remain unclear. Resting-state fMRI studies suggest modulation of the default mode network, salience network, amygdala, and striatum, yet the low temporal resolution of fMRI limits insight into fast cortical dynamics. Transcranial magnetic stimulation combined with EEG (TMS-EEG) enables millisecond-level tracking of cortical excitability. TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs; N45, N100, P200) reflect excitation/inhibition balance and are abnormal in MDD, normalizing with treatment. However, progressive neurophysiological changes during rapid, high-dose SNT have not been characterized.

Here, we used single-pulse TMS-EEG to track day-by-day cortical responses during a 5-day SNT protocol in TRD patients (n=24; 12 active, 12 sham), assessing global TEPs, Global Mean Field Amplitude, and source-localized subgenual anterior cingulate (sgACC) activity. We hypothesized that high-dose SNT induces cumulative reductions in cortical reactivity, correlating with clinical improvement and persisting at follow-up, consistent with evidence of long-term potentiation-like plasticity and potential stimulation site suppression underlying antidepressant efficacy.

Methods

Twenty-four participants with treatment-resistant depression (TRD; 12 active, 12 sham) were included from a double-blind, randomized trial of Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03068715). All met Maudsley criteria for TRD, were TMS-naïve, and had moderate-to-severe depression (MADRS ≥20). Exclusion criteria included seizures, substance use disorder, psychosis, unstable medical illness, active suicidal intent, and contraindications to TMS or MRI. Written informed consent was obtained, and the study was IRB-approved.

SNT consisted of 5 consecutive days of high-dose intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) targeting individualized L-DLPFC sites showing maximal anticorrelation with the sgACC. Ten daily sessions (1800 pulses each; 50-min intersession interval) totaled 90,000 pulses. Stimulation was applied at 90% resting motor threshold using a MagPro X100 with Cool-B70 coil. Sham followed the same protocol with the coil angled at 90° to mimic auditory/somatosensory input.

Single-pulse TMS-EEG recordings were acquired at baseline, before and after the 9th session each day, and at 1-month follow-up (Figure 1). Pulses were delivered at 120% resting motor threshold to individualized L-DLPFC targets. EEG was recorded with a 64-channel system (BrainAmp DC, 5000 Hz, referenced to FCz, impedances <5 kΩ). Data were preprocessed using the AARATEP pipeline, including artifact removal, ICA-based rejection, and re-referencing.

TEP morphological consistency was assessed via cosine similarity and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) to distinguish systematic treatment-induced amplitude changes from random variability. Global cortical responses were quantified using Global Mean Field Amplitude (GMFA) in canonical post-stimulation windows: N45 (20–80 ms), N100 (80–150 ms), and P200 (150–250 ms). GMFA area under the curve (AUC) was normalized to baseline, and group × time effects across 14 sessions were analyzed with linear mixed-effects models (LMEs) and Bonferroni correction.

Early local TEPs (EL-TEPs) were computed from electrode clusters near stimulation (L-DLPFC) and control (Cz) sites to assess spatial specificity. Topographic changes were evaluated with two-stage permutation testing (within-subject, 1000 iterations; between-group, 1000 iterations) to identify significant spatiotemporal clusters. Singular value decomposition (SVD) extracted dominant topographic components, with cosine similarity quantifying alignment between individual and group-level patterns. Euclidean distance from baseline quantified neurophysiological change across days. Participants were classified as early or late adaptors based on ≥25% N45 amplitude reduction by day 3, and associations with clinical response were examined using Pearson correlations.

Source-level sgACC activity was extracted from an ROI at MNI [4, 21, –8], normalized to baseline, and analyzed with LMEs (group × time). Trials with dipole strength <10% of maximum were excluded. Early sgACC changes (days 1–3) were correlated with MADRS reduction at post-treatment and 1-month follow-up.

Results

Spatio-temporal consistency of TEPs
TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs) displayed stable, individual-specific morphological signatures across the 5-day SNT protocol. Canonical components (P15, N25, N45, N100, P200) maintained consistent topographies, confirmed quantitatively by cosine similarity analyses. Within-subject similarity (0.733 ± 0.104) significantly exceeded between-subject similarity (0.403 ± 0.077; t(20) = 20.81, p < 0.001), indicating highly reproducible and individual-specific neurophysiological patterns (Figure 2, panels described below). Session-by-session comparisons across 14 sessions revealed excellent consistency (mean cosine similarity = 0.76 ± 0.05), particularly between adjacent days. These findings suggest that TEPs provide a reliable marker for tracking systematic neuroplastic changes during intensive treatment.

Progressive modulation of cortical excitability
Active SNT induced progressive reductions in cortical reactivity at the stimulation site. Linear mixed-effects models on normalized N45 GMFA (20–80 ms) revealed a significant GROUP × TIME interaction (F(13,286) = 4.23, p < 0.001), with steady declines from baseline (100%) to 60.2 ± 0.4% by day 5 post-iTBS, partially rebounding to 72.1 ± 0.4% at 1-month follow-up. Sham participants showed no systematic change (98–101%). Control site stimulation (Cz) elicited no significant changes for any TEP component, confirming spatial specificity (Figure 2A–B). Later components (N100, P200) showed no significant group differences after correction. Early local TEPs corroborated these findings, with suppression confined to the treatment site in the active group.

Spatiotemporal neurophysiological trajectories
SVD analysis revealed that subject-specific topographic amplitudes closely tracked group-level plasticity patterns. In the active group, SVD-derived amplitudes declined to ~60% of baseline by day 5, with partial recovery at 1-month follow-up, while sham participants showed no systematic change (Figure 2C). Euclidean distance from baseline topographies increased progressively over treatment days for active participants (GROUP × TIME: F(13,286) = 3.21, p < 0.001), confirming cumulative, treatment-specific plasticity. Early adaptors (≥25% reduction by day 3) showed greater clinical improvement than late adaptors, with moderate correlations to MADRS reduction both post-treatment (r = 0.54) and at follow-up (r = 0.50).

sgACC suppression and network-level effects
Source-localized sgACC activity demonstrated immediate and sustained reductions in the active SNT group, beginning from the first post-iTBS session and persisting at 1-month follow-up, whereas sham participants remained stable (GROUP × TIME: F(13,222) = 4.93, p < 0.001). Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc comparisons confirmed significantly lower sgACC activation at day 1 post-iTBS (Δ = -1.02, p < 0.01), day 3 (Δ = -1.65, p < 0.001), post-treatment (Δ = -1.88, p < 0.001), and 1-month follow-up (Δ = -1.77, p < 0.001) (Figure 2D). Matched control regions showed no significant changes, indicating circuit-specific effects. Network-level analysis revealed selective modulation of the Default Mode, Frontoparietal, Dorsal Attention, and Limbic networks, with Visual, Sensorimotor, and Salience networks unchanged. Baseline sgACC activity predicted clinical improvement in the active group (immediate: r = -0.67, p = 0.023; 1-month: r = -0.67, p = 0.025), but not in sham participants, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for treatment response. 

Conclusion

Daily TMS-EEG measurements during Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT) in treatment-resistant depression revealed robust neurophysiological signatures of therapeutic response. First, TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs) exhibited high morphological consistency and individual specificity across the 5-day intensive protocol, supporting their use as stable biomarkers. Second, SNT induced progressive reductions in cortical excitability, with early local TEP (N45) amplitude decreasing by ~28% by day 3 and reaching ~40% by day 5, while sham participants showed minimal change (<5%). Third, spatiotemporal analysis identified three windows of treatment-related change (49ms, 137ms, 242ms), progressing from localized frontal regions near the stimulation site to distributed frontoparietal networks, consistent with transsynaptic propagation through connected cortical circuits. SVD-derived topographies demonstrated that individual participants’ responses closely tracked these group-level patterns, with Euclidean distance metrics confirming cumulative, treatment-specific cortical reorganization absent in sham participants.

Early neurophysiological adaptation (≥25% N45 reduction by day 3) was associated with stronger clinical improvement, suggesting that early engagement of cortical circuits predicts therapeutic response. Source-localized analysis revealed selective and sustained suppression of sgACC activity in the active group, beginning with the first post-iTBS session and persisting through 1-month follow-up, while control regions remained stable. Baseline sgACC activity strongly predicted clinical improvement (r = -0.67), highlighting its potential as a precision biomarker for patient selection. These findings align with circuit-based models of depression, indicating that L-DLPFC–sgACC modulation is central to SNT efficacy.

Importantly, SNT’s therapeutic mechanism appears distinct from classical iTBS effects in motor cortex. While iTBS typically enhances excitability, clinical benefit in depression was associated with progressive cortical suppression at the prefrontal stimulation site, likely reflecting cumulative LTD-like plasticity or preferential engagement of inhibitory interneurons. The temporal evolution of cortical suppression parallels the emergence of clinical improvement, supporting a mechanistic link between neurophysiological changes and symptom reduction. P200 modulation further suggests network-level propagation beyond the stimulation site. Persistent changes at 1-month indicate long-lasting neuroplasticity that may underlie SNT’s rapid and durable antidepressant effects.

Overall, these results identify key neurophysiological signatures—morphological stability, progressive cortical suppression, circuit-specific sgACC modulation, and early adaptation—that can guide precision medicine approaches in TMS. Baseline sgACC activity could inform patient selection, while day 3 monitoring of cortical excitability may allow early identification of non-responders and adjustment of stimulation parameters. Together, these findings provide a framework for biomarker-driven optimization of high-dose, accelerated TMS protocols, with the potential to improve outcomes in treatment-resistant depression while reducing trial-and-error approaches in clinical practice.
