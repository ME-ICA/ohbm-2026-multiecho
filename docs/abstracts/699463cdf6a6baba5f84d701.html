Introduction

Healthy brain function depends on a balanced excitation–inhibition (E/I) signaling and its disturbance has been implicated in various psychiatric disorders, including depression. Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), a bioactive lipid synthesized by the enzyme autotaxin (ATX) from lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) originating from the liver, modulates glutamatergic transmission through LPA₂ receptors, enhancing glutamate release and thereby increasing neuronal excitability ^1. Dysregulation of the LPC–ATX–LPA axis has been linked to both metabolic ^2 and affective ^3 disturbances via alterations in cortical E/I balance in mice. However, direct evidence for this mechanism in humans has remained lacking.

Methods

The present study aimed to translate these findings to humans by examining whether fasting—a metabolic manipulation known to alter lipid metabolism ^2 — modulates the LPC–ATX–LPA axis and, consequently, cortical excitability and affective states. Thirty healthy male participants completed a randomized within-subject study with two conditions: a 72-hour fasting period and a satiated control condition. Assessments included metabolic profiling, depressive symptom ratings, and multimodal neuroimaging. Blood plasma was analyzed for different LPC and LPA moieties, and Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP) was quantified as a marker of astrocytic activity. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II). Neurophysiological measures were obtained using magnetoencephalography (MEG) to assess the power spectrum as an indirect marker of cortical E/I balance. Structural imaging included diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) for white matter microstructure and T1-weighted MRI (T1w-MRI) for gray matter volume (GMV).

Results

Fasting significantly increased circulating LPA 18:1 concentrations (p<.005, R²=.14), without affecting other LPA subtypes. Elevated LPA 18:1 levels were accompanied by higher BDI-II scores in the fasted state (p<.05), with 11% of participants reporting clinically relevant and 37% subclinical depressive symptoms. MEG analyses revealed a significant reduction in the 1/f exponent within the low gamma range (30–50 Hz; p<.05), indicating a flatter spectral slope and a shift toward cortical excitation. These spectral changes correlated positively with LPA 18:1 concentrations (p<.05), linking peripheral lipid metabolism to central excitability.

DWI revealed widespread decreases in fractional anisotropy during fasting (pTFCE-FWE<.001) across major fiber tracts implicated in glutamatergic signaling, including the thalamic radiation, corticospinal tract, fronto-occipital fasciculus, middle and superior longitudinal fasciculi, and the uncinate fasciculus. Elevated GFAP levels indicated enhanced astrocytic activation, potentially contributing to reduced white matter integrity through extracellular matrix alterations. T1w-MRI demonstrated fasting-induced gray matter volume (GMV) reductions in regions enriched in glutamatergic signaling—such as the lower cortical layers in the middle frontal gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, pre- and postcentral gyri, middle temporal gyrus, the hippocampus, and anterior cingulate cortex (pTFCE-FWE<.05). 

Conclusion

Together, these findings provide the first human evidence that fasting induces subclinical symptoms of depression which correlated with an increase in LPA 18:1 able to mediate modulation of cortical E/I balance, concomitant with changes in white matter integrity and cortical volume in glutamatergic areas. The results establish the LPC–ATX–LPA axis as a key interface between metabolic and affective regulation and lay the groundwork for targeted interventions, including emerging pharmacological approaches targeting ATX inhibition ^4.
