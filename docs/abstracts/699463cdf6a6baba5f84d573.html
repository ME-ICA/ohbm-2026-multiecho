Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by α-synuclein–containing Lewy pathology. However, not all neuronal populations are equally vulnerable to Lewy pathology.1，2 Although PD etiology remains incompletely understood, iron-mediated oxidative stress has been proposed as a potential pathogenic mechanism. Beyond well-established subcortical iron accumulation in PD, converging evidence indicates that cortical involvement also occurs. Experimental and animal studies demonstrate that iron promotes α-synuclein aggregation and that both cortical and striatal regions exhibit iron deposition and Lewy pathology. Quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM), which sensitively reflects regional iron content, provides a promising tool to characterize whole-brain pattern of iron deposition in vivo. The first whole-brain study in PD reported a spatial distribution of cortical susceptibility abnormalities that follow the spatial pattern of established α-synuclein pathology,3 supporting the notion that iron mapping may serve as a proxy signature of PD cortical pathology. 

Here, we sought to systematically characterize cortical magnetic susceptibility alterations in PD and explored their structural, functional, and molecular underpinnings. We first applied refined QSM processing with explicit vein removal to minimize vascular confounds, and then compared whole-brain susceptibility between PD patients and controls, benchmarking these findings against morphometric measures. We further examined the spatial correspondence of susceptibility abnormalities with large-scale cortical atrophy maps from the ENIGMA-PD consortium, macroscale cortical organization, and neurotransmitter receptor distributions. Finally, we integrated QSM findings with post-mortem transcriptomic data, to identify biological pathways, cellular components, cortical layers, and cell types associated with PD-related cortical susceptibility alterations. 

Methods

A total of 561 participants (355 PD patients and 206 healthy controls) were included in this study. An overview of the study workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1. Disease duration was defined as years since symptom onset. Among the PD cohort, 148 patients were classified as early-stage PD, defined by a Hoehn and Yahr stage less than 2 and a disease duration of less than 3 years. MRI data acquisition were performed on a 3.0 Tesla MRI. QSM image reconstruction was performed in Susceptibility Tensor Imaging (STI) Suite, including Laplacian-based phase unwrapping, V-SHARP filtering and STAR-QSM inversion. Susceptibility values were referenced to the mean susceptibility of the whole brain.

Cortical and subcortical susceptibility values were extracted using the Desikan–Killiany (DK) atlas. To minimize venous contamination, subject-specific vessel probability maps were generated from QSM images using a multiscale vessel filter implemented in Nighres.4 The resulting venous mask was subtracted from the ribbon-constrained DK labels to obtain pure cortical regions of interest free of venous-related susceptibility contributions for each subject. Regional QSM values were then extracted from the susceptibility maps within each venous-free ROI. Regional cortical thickness, subcortical volume and total intracranial volume were estimated using FreeSurfer and parcellated according to the DK atlas. Group differences between PD patients and controls were examined for cortical and subcortical QSM values, cortical thickness, and subcortical volumes using general linear models. To evaluate the discriminative performance of QSM and T1-derived features in distinguishing patients with PD from HCs, we implemented a machine learning approach consisting of feature selection using Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), where the most informative features were retained based on model-derived feature importance; model training with logistic regression classifiers, and performance evaluation with leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV).

To account for spatial autocorrelation across brain regions, statistical significance was assessed usng spatial permutation tests. Cortical parcellations were projected onto a spherical surface and randomly rotated 1000 times to generate spatial null distributions. For subcortical regions, where spin tests are not applicable, null distributions were generated by randomly shuffling labels. Cortical QSM effect sizes were compared with cortical atrophy pattern from the ENIGMA-PD consortium.5 The spatial distribution of PD-related cortical QSM alterations was further examined across macroscale brain organization using Yeo intrinsic functional networks and von Economo–Koskinas cytoarchitectonic classes, with regional effect sizes aggregated within each category and tested against spatial null models. Spatial correspondence between cortical QSM alterations and neurotransmitter receptor and transporter densities was also evaluated using group-averaged PET-derived maps spanning multiple neurotransmitter systems. Regional microarray gene expression data were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas and processed using the abagen toolbox. Probe reannotation, intensity filtering, differential stability–based probe selection, spatial matching, and normalization were applied, and genes with low differential stability were excluded. Regional expression values were averaged across donors. Partial least squares regression was used to identify gene expression patterns associated with regional cortical QSM alterations. Ranked gene weights were used for gene set enrichment analysis based on Gene Ontology biological process and cellular component terms. To characterize cell types and laminar correlates of cortical susceptibility alterations, regional QSM alterations were compared with spatial maps of cell-type–specific and layer-specific gene expression derived from human transcriptomic datasets. 

Results

QSM revealed widespread, bilateral cortical susceptibility increases in PD, involving temporal, frontal, parietal, occipital cortices. Importantly, similar patterns were already evident in early-stage PD (HY stage ≤ 2 and disease duration ≤ 3 years), indicating that cortical iron-related changes emerge early. For subcortical regions, susceptibility was elevated in putamen, thalamus, and hippocampus, with striatal changes most prominent and also present in early-stage patients. In contrast, T1-derived morphology showed only scattered cortical thinning and subtle subcortical volume reductions, with minimal or nonsignificant alterations in the early-stage subgroup, supporting greater early sensitivity of QSM versus morphometry.(Fig2. A) 

We found a significant spatial correlation between QSM-derived cortical susceptibility map and the ENIGMA PD cortical thickness atrophy pattern (r = -0.303, p_spin = 0.012), whereas cortical thickness map did not (r = -0.042, p_spin = 0.731), indicating that regions with greater susceptibility overlapped with those showing stronger atrophy from multi-site ENIGMA datasets. We utilized machine learning to evaluate the diagnostic performance of models derived from QSM and T1w in distinguishing patients with PD from controls. Across the whole PD cohort, the QSM-based classifier showed better discrimination between patients with PD and controls than the T1w-based model. The QSM-based model achieved an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI [0.73, 0.82]), whereas the T1w-based model yielded an AUC of 0.56 (95%CI [0.51, 0.61]). DeLong’s test confirmed that the QSM-based significantly outperformed the T1w-based model (P < 0.001). In the subgroup of early-stage PD, the QSM classifier achieved an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI [0.77, 0.85]), compared with 0.58 (95% CI [0.52, 0.64]) for the T1w model. DeLong’s test indicated a significant difference between the two classifiers (P < 0.001). (Fig2. B)

Mapping to macroscale organization showed non-uniform distribution of cortical abnormalities: the strongest effects were in the frontoparietal network (p_spin=0.045) and in von Economo association cortex (especially “association cortex 2”; p_spin=0.007), pointing to preferential involvement of higher-order, transmodal systems (Fig2. C) . Neurotransmitter-based mapping analyses related cortical susceptibility to serotonergic distributions, including 5-HT6 (r=0.339, p_spin=0.007), 5-HTT (r=-0.337, p_spin=0.024), and 5-HT2A (r=0.267, p_spin=0.040). Subcortical susceptibility aligned with dopaminergic and cholinergic markers such as D2 (r=0.692, p_spin=0.005), DAT (r=0.583, p_spin=0.028), and VAChT (r=0.539, p_spin=0.050) (Fig2. D). PLS1 regional weights correlated with cortical susceptibility (r=0.568, p=0.009). Positively weighted genes, highly expressed in regions with increased susceptibility, were enriched for mitochondrial organization and regulation of synaptic signaling. Negatively weighted genes were enriched for immune response, MHC complex assembly, and gliogenesis. Cellular component mirrored these processes, highlighting synaptic and mitochondrial-related terms (positive), versus immune/adhesion/extracellular-matrix structures (negative). Laminar and cell-class analyses showed that regions with greater susceptibility increases were transcriptionally enriched for neuronal markers, particularly excitatory neurons, and for layers II–V, whereas regions with lower susceptibility showed higher astrocyte, endothelial, OPC, and microglia signatures and layers I/VI markers. Notably, these analyses converged and aligned with established cortical cytoarchitectonic principles, suggesting that cortical iron accumulation in PD preferentially involves neuron-rich middle layers (II–V).(Fig2. E) 

Conclusion

In this study, We found extensive cortical increases in QSM susceptibility in PD, largely consistent with the first whole-brain QSM investigation in PD reported by Acosta-Cabronero et al. Importantly, this widespread pattern of cortical susceptibility increase was already evident in the early-stage patients, in contrast to conventional morphometric measures derived from T1w imaging, which showed only scarce and largely non-significant changes. The dissociation between QSM and morphometric findings suggests that iron accumulation may precede overt atrophy. Beyond group-level differences, QSM-based models consistently outperformed T1w-based models in distinguishing PD from controls. These results underscore the diagnostic value of QSM, suggesting that it captures disease-related alterations that are less apparent in conventional T1-derived structural measures.

Moreover, the cortical distribution of QSM alterations in our cohort showed a significant spatial correspondence with the large-scale cortical atrophy pattern reported by the ENIGMA consortium. This observation indicates that, even in the absence of robust morphometric changes, QSM captures a pattern of cortical vulnerability that aligns closely with established atrophy maps derived from large multisite cohorts. One possible explanation is that QSM reflects upstream processes such as iron accumulation and oxidative stress, which are mechanistically closer to the disease process, occur earlier, and may predispose cortical regions to subsequent reductions in neuronal density and synaptic integrity. By contrast, morphometric measures are downstream markers, reflecting volume loss that follows cellular degeneration. Such structural changes typically emerge only at more advanced disease stages and are often difficult to detect in small to medium size cohorts, requiring large samples to achieve robust statistical power.

Our network- and cytoarchitectonic-level analyses revealed that the spatial pattern of cortical susceptibility abnormalities in PD is not uniformly distributed across the brain, but preferentially affects higher-order transmodal systems. The most pronounced effects were observed in the frontoparietal network and association cortex, regions that are involved in executive control and attention. Importantly, these findings also align with the neuropathology of α-synuclein progression described by Braak and colleagues. In clinical stages, most PD patients already reach Braak stage 4 of 6 when cortical involvement emerges, particularly in the temporal lobes, prefrontal cortex, insular and anterior cingulate cortices, and high-order sensory association isocortex. However, first-order sensory areas tend to be relatively spared from α-synuclein pathology, with only occasional mild changes observed in the primary sensory cortex and the primary motor cortex. Our enrichment analyses showed that cortical regions with increased susceptibility in PD were enriched for mitochondrial and synaptic pathways, whereas relatively spared regions were enriched for immune and repair-related processes. Susceptibility increases preferentially involved neuron-dense middle cortical layers and excitatory and inhibitory neuronal markers, consistent with postmortem evidence that α-synuclein pathology and iron accumulation selectively target synapse- and mitochondria-rich neuronal populations.

In summary, our findings reveal that cortical magnetic susceptibility alterations in PD follow a hierarchical and molecularly defined spatial organization, linking iron-related processes to the brain’s functional, microstructural, and cellular architecture. This integrative, multi-scale perspective bridges imaging-derived magnetic susceptibility measures with neurobiological organization. We also highlight QSM as a promising and more sensitive biomarker, and note that future longitudinal studies will be essential to determine whether QSM can also track disease progression over time.
