Introduction

Reading and visuospatial ability are core cognitive skills that synergistically support school-aged children’s academic development and daily functioning1. Unlike alphabetic languages (e.g., English), Chinese is a logographic writing system characterized by unique spatial properties: Chinese characters consist of strokes and radicals arranged in a square configuration, requiring precise visuospatial analysis during recognition and processing2,3. This spatial specificity implies that Chinese reading may have a distinct and dynamic association with visuospatial ability compared to alphabetic reading, making it an ideal model to explore cross-domain cognitive interactions.

A few cross-sectional studies have suggested mutual associations between Chinese reading and visuospatial ability, but longitudinal evidence for bidirectionality remains scarce, leaving the developmental trajectory of their relationship unclear. Another critical gap is the neglect of individual differences in initial ability levels. Developmental cognitive theories propose that cognitive interactions vary with developmental maturity1. 

Neurally, Chinese reading relies on a specialized network involving the left middle frontal gyrus (MFG) and left fusiform gyrus (FFG)2,3. The left MFG is critical for spatial working memory and orthographic-phonological integration, while the FFG (visual word form area, VWFA) specializes in orthographic processing4,5. Previous studies have linked the MFG and FFG to both reading and visuospatial ability, but their role as potential neural mediators of the dynamic relationship between the two skills remains unexamined.

Methods

Participants

Neuroimaging and behavioral data were obtained from the Children School Functions and Brain Development Project. Reading achievement and visuospatial ability have been tested yearly. Children were recruited from dozens of primary schools in Beijing. Informed consent was obtained from the parents/guardians (written) and children (oral). The exclusion criteria included a history of neurological/psychiatric disorders, the use of psychoactive drugs, significant head injury, and physical illness not suitable for MRI scanning. All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Normal University in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Reading achievement test

Using the project team of the National Children’s Study of China (NCSC) designed reading achievement test, which was based on the national curriculum standard and focused on the content and capability domain. Children completed the test within 45 min individually under the supervision of a professionally trained master examiner.

Visual spatial ability test

The visual-spatial ability test consists of two sub-tests, namely the visual-spatial representation ability test and the spatial relation perception ability test. Among them, the visual-spatial representation ability subtest was tested by the embedded figures test, and the spatial relation perception ability subtest was tested by the mental rotation task.

The task examines an individual's mental ability to rotate a graphic image in a plane or a stereo.

Image acquisition, quality control, and analysis

All MRI scans were acquired on three 3T Siemens Prisma scanners with 64-channel head coil. Blood oxygen level-dependent fMRI was acquired using a whole-brain, single-shot, multislice, echo-planar imaging sequence of 240 volumes. i) Individually visual examination. ii) Carefully checked follow HCP protocol. We quantified the head motion of resting-state fMRI as framewise displacement (FD). The participants were also excluded if the mean FD exceeded 0.5 mm during resting-state scans. Voxel-based morphometry was conducted using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox and SPM12.Resting fMRI preprocessing was performed using DPABI software. Cross-lagged panel analyses were performed using Mplus7. All MRI statistical analyses were performed in DPABI software.

Results

In the descriptive results, reading achievement in the first year significantly predicted reading achievement in the second year (r = 0.520, p < 0.001), and visuospatial ability in the first year significantly predicted visuospatial ability in the second year ( r = 0.439, p < 0.001). When grouped by the levels of reading achievement and visuospatial ability, the results showed that in the lower-ability groups, the developmental pattern of the two abilities exhibited a trend of bidirectional prediction, reflecting reciprocal growth. In contrast, in the higher-ability groups, only reading achievement was found to significantly predict the development of visuospatial ability, while visuospatial ability no longer exerted an impact on reading achievement. In the exploration of neural mechanisms, it was found that children’s reading achievement and visuospatial ability share the left middle frontal gyrus (MFG) of the brain (73 voxels, MNI coordinated: -43.5, 34.5, 30), and this region can mediate the effect of reading achievement on visuospatial ability. Further functional connectivity analysis revealed that the functional connection between the left MFG and left fusiform gyrus (87 voxels) is a shared brain connection underlying the development of children’s reading achievement and visuospatial ability. Mediation analysis showed that this shared brain connection can also mediate the impact of reading achievement on visuospatial ability.

Conclusion

The developmental pattern between reading achievement and visuospatial ability in childhood is moderated by their ability levels. At lower ability levels, the two follow a reciprocal model and mutually promote each other. When abilities develop to a certain level, visuospatial ability no longer influences the development of reading achievement, while the development of reading achievement can still facilitate the further improvement of visuospatial ability. At the neural mechanism level, it was found that the left middle frontal gyrus (MFG) and its functional connectivity with the left fusiform gyrus (FFG) can mediate the developmental relationship between reading achievement and visuospatial ability. This study reveals the stage-specific pattern of the development of reading achievement and visuospatial ability in school-aged children and explores its potential brain mechanisms, providing an empirical basis for the future development of school-aged children and the intervention for children with developmental difficulties.
