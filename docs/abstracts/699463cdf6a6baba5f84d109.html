Introduction

Arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI is a non-invasive, clinically available method for quantifying cerebral blood flow (CBF). CBF is associated with many cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) and multiple sclerosis (MS), and is affected by cardiovascular risk factors (CVRF). Increased CBF, through CVRF management, can improve cognition1. This makes CBF a promising biomarker for early-stage diagnosis, disease monitoring, treatment stratification, and secondary prevention. However, the causes of CBF alterations across the full neurodegenerative continuum are unclear, and conflicting results arise from studies using single, older, heterogeneous cohorts at late stages of disease, and different ASL acquisitions2, limiting the generalisability of such studies. A reference CBF chart, independent of ASL acquisition, provides a vital framework for disentangling CBF change in neurodegeneration and has clinical applicability, offering individualised deviation scores. Normative models account for ASL sequence and site differences, enabling the creation of a healthy reference CBF chart by pooling large datasets of different ASL implementations across MRI scanners and sequences. We investigated the feasibility of developing normative CBF charts and assessed CBF deviations in AD, MS, and hypertension in a uniquely large combined ASL dataset comprising 4.114 subjects across 12 cohorts and 7 ASL sequences. 

Methods

The training dataset consisted of MRI data from healthy controls (HC, n=2359, no cognitive impairment, systolic blood pressure (SBP) <140 mmHg), acquired with 7 different ASL sequences (Figure 1). Patient MRI data (n=1755) consisted of AD-related cognitive impairment, MS (Relapsing-Remitting [n=2], Primary Progressive [n=83], and Secondary Progressive [n=9]), and hypertension (SBP>140mmHg) patients (Table 1). Grey matter (GM) CBF was quantified using ExploreASL using T1w, FLAIR, and ASL images. The GM CBFnormative reference chart was constructed using a non-Gaussian Bayesian regression model and 80% of the training dataset, correcting for cohort and sex differences, and was tested on a hold-out set (20% of the training dataset, stratified for age, sex, and site). Normative model performance was evaluated in the hold-out set using Pearson’s correlation between true and predicted CBF (ρ), standardized mean squared error (SMSE), and explained variance (EV). A quantile-quantile plot (QQ-plot) was qualitatively assessed to evaluate the model's fit. Differences in hold-out set GM CBF distributions before and after normative modelling were statistically compared using an ANOVA with Benjamin-Hochberg (BH) corrected post-hoc tests. Non-normative GM CBF and GM CBFnormative patient values were tested for statistical differences with the HC hold-out set using ANOVA with BH corrected post-hoc tests. 

Results

The GM CBFnormative reference model (Figure 2A) achieved a ρ of 0.6, SMSE of 0.65, and EV of 0.36. The QQ-plot showed an almost perfect linear fit (Figure 2B). Before normative modelling, 11 cohort pairs in the hold-out set were statistically different (p<0.05). After normative modelling, only Insight46, SABRE, and HELIUS differed from the combined StrokeMRI and TOP hold-out set (p<0.02). 

Patients with hypertension (55.6±15.2 mL/100g/min, p<0.001) and AD (57.6±13.1 mL/100g/min, p=0.001) had statistically lower GM CBF compared to HC (62.2±12.2 mL/100g/min). Subjective cognitive decline (SCD, 60.5±13.3 mL/100g/min), mild cognitive impairment (MCI, 61±14.3 mL/100g/min), and MS patients (65.1±11.0 mL/100g/min) did not differ (p>0.45, Figure 2C). GM CBF did not differ among SCD, MCI, and AD groups (p>0.09). 

For GM CBFnormative, HC (61.2±10.4 mL/100g/min) was significantly higher compared with AD (56.6±12.8mL/100g/min) and hypertension (56.7±10 mL/100g/min) patients (all p<0.03), but, unlike GM CBF, also significantly higher compared to MCI participants (58±13.6 mL/100g/min). There were no differences in GM CBFnormative between HC and SCD (59.7±13.2 mL/100g/min, p=0.17) or MS patients (61.3±9.7 mL/100g/min; p=0.22; Figure 2C). GM CBFnormative also differed between SCD and AD (p=0.02), but not between SCD and MCI (p=0.17) or between MCI and AD (p=0.20).

Conclusion

Normative modelling reduced site- and sequence-level GM CBF differences across cohorts, demonstrating its capacity to effectively combine different ASL sequences, thereby increasing the amount of mean GM CBF differences between patient groups and healthy controls. The model’s low explained variance could be attributed to the sensitivity of ASL-CBF to physiology3, which results in GM CBF fluctuations with changes in physiological state and appears to affect the normative model.

Lower GM CBFnormative in patients with hypertension and AD are consistent with the literature. Lower GM CBFnormative was found in the MCI group, which was obscured without normative modelling. This could suggest CBF is altered in early AD stages already, highlighting the potential of CBF-modyifing treatments in preventative strategies. 

The lack of difference between healthy controls and MS may be explained by the inclusion and small sample sizes of patients at different disease stages with potentially higher CBF4 and lower CBF5. These findings encourage future studies with larger and more diverse ASL datasets to assess associations among clinical measures (cognitive tests, SBP, MS disease duration) to improve normative CBF reference models and disentangle CBF change in neurodegeneration. The lack of site-specific healthy controls in the AD group is a limitation that would otherwise help to clarify the effects of normative model harmonisation and data pooling on biological associations.

To conclude, normative GM CBF reference charts can pool heterogeneous ASL datasets and could improve the ability to disentangle CBF changes across the spectrum of neurodegenerative pathology.
