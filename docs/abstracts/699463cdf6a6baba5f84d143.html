Introduction

Global high-amplitude slow (delta) waves are typically observed during NREM sleep; however, brief and localised ‘sleep-like’ slow waves (local sleep) have in recent years been observed during wakefulness.[1] Evidence from EEG investigations have implied these events are use- and time-dependent, occurring more frequently with time spent awake and manifesting in task-relevant regions with implications for objective task performance (lethargic versus impulsive) and attentional control (on task versus mind-wandering versus mind-blanking).[2]

We aim to explore the manifestation of these waves with greater spatial precision, beyond broad scale ‘frontal’ and ‘parietal’ reports using source reconstruction with MEG. Investigation of local sleep using MEG is very limited, and to date has been predominantly exclusive to EEG.[3] We therefore firstly explore wave manifestation during task-free rest-state, applying relevant adjustments to detection parameters for MEG data.

We additionally conducted a novel investigation of local sleep occurrence in children, including comparisons to an adult sample. Traditional oscillatory literature has evidenced posterior-anterior developmental trajectories and greater slow wave power in children compared to adults, suggesting local sleep events may manifest similarly.[4] Understanding of how these events occur during wakefulness is important for our understanding of cognition and cortical functioning.

Methods

Using a 306 sensor MEGIN Triux MEG system, we assessed 42 adults (18-35 years, M = 24.62 years, SD = 4.71) and 28 children (8-11 years, M = 9.22 years, SD = 1.35) during task-free rest state (4 mins eyes-open, 4 mins eyes-closed).

For preprocessing, we used Maxfilter (SSS) with head position estimates continuously tracked using cHPI information for movement correction. Expansion origin was determined by creating a best-fitting sphere centred to individual participant ISOTRAK Polhemus points (nasion, left and right preauricular, and head shape points). Our analyses focused on 102 magnetometers. FastICA was applied to these sensors to remove cardiac and ocular artefacts.

For wave detection, data was resampled to 100Hz and a 0.5Hz high-pass filter was applied. We then extracted high-amplitude slow waves using a ‘delta/theta wave detection’ algorithm (credit T. Andrillon) in MATLAB, originally written for EEG data. Detected waves were then furthered refined by selection criteria: individualised Z-score threshold (waves selected above 3 std dev) dependent on peak-to-peak amplitude, and occurred exclusively in the delta range (0.8 to 4Hz). We then focused on two single sensors: one frontal (MEG0621) and one central (MEG0731). This specific adaption attempts to avoid overestimation of wave density (number of waves per minute) from duplicate detection from adjacent sensors. Data (using all magnetometers, after Maxfilter and ICA, band-pass filtered to 1-10Hz and original sampling rate) was then epoched (-1500ms to 2000ms) around each wave start time. Standard time-locked and time-frequency analysis was conducted from this epoched data, akin to ‘trial’ data. Figure 1 shows confirmation that local sleep waves found in MEG in both adults and children are very comparable to those found in previous EEG studies.

We used 2x2x2 mixed methods repeated measures ANOVAs to assess wave density (waves per minute) and specific wave frequency (Hz) with between-groups factor of age groups (adult vs child), within-group factor of detection sensor (frontal vs central), and within-group factor of condition (eyes-open vs eyes-closed).

Furthermore, we also acquired individual T1-weighted structural MRI scans for coregistration purposes, using a Siemens 3 Telsa Trio Magnetom scanner with a 32 channel matrix head coil. Images were obtained at 1x1x1mm3 with TR=1960ms, TE=3.41ms and 176 slices.

Using FieldTrip (www.fieldtriptoolbox.org), we performed source localisation methods to determine the underlying neural sources for local sleep events. First, MEG sensor locations were co-registered to the individual participant’s head location via ‘head position coils’ worn during their MEG scan. Their structural MRI was co-registered with their head position in the MEG using the participant’s digitised headshape using the ISOTRAK Polhemus and the scalp surface from the segmented MRI. The forward leadfield model used the ‘overlapping spheres’ method using the individual participant’s MRI. Each participant’s MRI was spatially normalised to the template MNI brain. A template volumetric grid of 5x5x5mm spaced points within the brain was warped to the individual participant’s MRI using the inverse-warp parameters. The forward leadfield was computed at these inverse-warped points in each participant.

The LCMV beamformer was then computed using this leadfield and covariance data in several contrasts. First, as the local sleep waves are in the delta band, data was filtered to 0.8 to 4Hz. A ‘common filter’ was computed using a fourth-order Butterworth filter (0.8Hz to 15Hz). The ‘pre’ and ‘post’ time windows of -700ms to -300ms and 0 to 400ms were used and condition-specific source results were computed using the common filter for each time window. This was computed separately for each set of epoched data based on frontal or central sensors for wave detection, as well as for condition (eyes-open and eyes-closed separately) and for age group (adults and children separately).

Results

Figure 2a and 2b presents sensor level high-amplitude slow waves detected by MEG from frontal (left image) and central (right image) sensors for adult and child populations, respectively. Figure 2c presents an example of the grand average (group level) timeseries of detected slow waves from the frontal sensor from the child population during the eyes-closed condition. Note similarity to Figure 1a (previous findings in EEG).

Figure 2d and 2e presents novel source space evidence of local sleep waves detected by the frontal sensor (left image) and central sensor (right image) in the adult and child populations, respectively. Both figures were plotted using data from eye-closed rest-state. Future plans include cluster-based permutation analyses in source space.

For wave density (number of waves per minute), we found a significant two-way interaction (Figure 2f) between age group (adults/children) X sensor region (frontal/central), (F (1,63) = 18.22, p <.001, 𝜂2𝑝 = .224). Children showed significantly more waves than adults overall, at both the frontal region (t = -3.24, p = .010) and central region (t = -6.230, p < .001), for both rest-state conditions (eyes open/closed). Wave density between regions was higher in the frontal region than in the central region for both adults (t = 11.302, p <.001) and children (t = 3.94, p = .001). Importantly, the interaction indicates that the relative larger number of frontal waves in adults (vs central) was not as strong of a difference in children, in line with the posterior-to-anterior shift through development.

Also for wave density, we found a significant two-way interaction (Figure 2g) between sensor region (frontal/central) X rest-state condition (eyes-open/eyes-closed) (F (1,63) = 8.68, p = .005, 𝜂2𝑝 = .121). Both adult and child samples showed significantly more waves in the frontal region than in the central region during both eyes-open (t = 6.90, p <.001) and eyes-closed (t = 10.19, p <.001) rest state conditions. Post-hoc comparisons showed the significant difference in regional wave occurrence between state conditions was based on central occurrence (t = 3.88, p = .001), while no significant difference between conditions was found for frontally sourced waves.

For wave frequency (Hz) within the delta range, we found a significant two-way interaction (Figure 2h) between sensor region (frontal/central) X rest-state condition (eyes-open/eyes-closed), (F(1,63) = 7.233, p = .009, 𝜂2𝑝 = .103). Importantly, there was no significant difference between age groups. Wave frequency was significantly higher in central regions than in frontal regions (during eyes-closed rest-state only) (t = -4.08, p <.001) and was significantly higher during eyes-closed than during eyes-open (in the central region only) (t = -4.02, p <.001). All post-hoc tests were corrected for multiple comparisons (Tukey HSD).

Conclusion

We successfully adapted EEG detection algorithms for use with MEG data to detect localised high-amplitude slow waves during wakefulness; ‘local sleep’. Consistent with previous research, we found local sleep events manifest prominently in frontal cortical regions. We also provide novel evidence of the underlying neural source of these events using source reconstruction (MEG with MRI co-registration), beyond sensor-level topographies provided in previous literature. 

We also provide novel evidence of local sleep events in children (at both sensor and source level) and assessed average wave density (number of waves per minute) and average wave frequency (Hz) between adults and children to determine if local sleep events manifest differently with age. Previous traditional oscillatory evidence suggest children may present a central prominence of local sleep events given established literature of a posterior-anterior trajectory of peak oscillatory frequencies.  While the local sleep events in children in our study did still occur somewhat more frontally than centrally, this occurrence was more balanced compared to the frontal dominance seen in our adult participants.  We also speculated that children may present a lower average wave frequency (Hz), given previous literature suggests children have a lower oscillatory peak frequency, (e.g. typical peak alpha frequency in children is 6 to 10Hz, while in adults is 8 to 12Hz). We found no evidence that this trajectory also applies to local sleep events, where average wave frequency was similar between adults and children. In conclusion, our detection pipeline is successful for use with MEG data for both adult and child participants and can be used to further demonstrate how local sleep events may different between children and adults.  

Beyond our current findings that establish a robust ‘local sleep’ analysis pipeline in MEG data, our future analyses include cluster-based permutation analyses in source space, will incorporate task-based data (go/no-go task with mind-wandering probes) to assess local sleep events during sustained attentional control, and measurements of ADHD symptom severity to examine if local sleep events are mechanistically linked with ADHD.
