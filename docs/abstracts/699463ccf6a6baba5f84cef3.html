Introduction

Prior research has shown detrimental impacts of long working hours on outcomes in health and wellbeing, including anxiety and depression1. Effects of working patterns on brain function have also been observed. For example, greater occupational stress has been associated with prefrontal dysfunction during performance of a verbal fluency task2. To date, however, the association between long working hours and brain function has not been directly tested. The UK Biobank is a mass-scale epidemiological cohort study, containing functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data for over 80,000 participants, alongside data pertaining to demographics and aspects of health and lifestyle, including employment data. This dataset is therefore ideally placed to provide insight into the relationship between how much time people spend at work (as measured by length of working week), and brain function as measured using both task-based and resting-state fMRI (tfMRI; rfMRI), in a representative sample of participants across the UK. For tfMRI analysis, we hypothesised that a longer average working week would be associated with increased activation in both the amygdala and insula during an emotional face matching task, while activation in the fusiform face area (FFA; a control region), would be unaffected. For rfMRI analysis, we broadly predicted that length of working week would be associated with changes in connectivity for associative networks (salience/ventral attention, limbic, control, default mode) but not for basic sensory networks (visual, somatomotor, dorsal attention).

Methods

Length of working week was defined using UK Biobank data field 767 – ‘Length of working week for main job’, measured in hours. Data from this variable were analysed in three formats: (1) Continuous: ranging between 26 and 84 hours worked per week. (2) Categorical1: split into five categories of increasing weekly working hours (26-30; 31-35; 36-40; 41-45; 46-84). (3) Categorical2: split into two categories of hours (28-32; 37-40) which are broadly representative of hours that would be worked during a standard 5-day week and a reduced hours 4-day week in the UK.

For tfMRI, the UK Biobank provides data for an emotional face matching task. We interrogated the mean z-statistic, extracted using FSL Featquery, in regions of interest for the contrast of ‘faces > shapes’, including in the amygdala, FFA and insula (split into six subregions). Each region of interest (ROI) was further split by hemisphere for analysis, resulting in 16 total tfMRI dependent variables.

For rfMRI, we used raw timeseries data split by a 100 region Schaffer parcellation, including regions spread across seven core functional networks; salience/ventral attention, limbic, control, default mode, visual, somatomotor, and dorsal attention. We extracted three forms of dependent variable: (1) Within-network connectivity in each network. (2) Between-network connectivity for each possible pair of networks. (3) ‘Subnetwork’ connectivity for the four networks with three or more anatomically distinguishable subnetworks (default mode, control, dorsal attention, salience/ventral attention). Correlation coefficients between each relevant pair of parcels was extracted and then Fisher Z-transformed for averaging across participants and statistical analysis.

Covariates were carefully selected by consulting guidelines for analysing large MRI datasets from the UK Biobank3, and by conducting a non-systematic review of prior research into effects of length of working week on a diverse range of health outcomes. Covariates spanned the following ‘families’: (1) neuroimaging, (2) demographics, (3) work life, (4) household/socioeconomic status (SES), and (5) health. Covariates were then subjected to variable selection, using random forest machine learning to identify those that predict and do not predict length of working week in our UK Biobank samples. Only those classified as ‘important’ or ‘tentatively important’ for predicting length of working week were retained for further analysis (see Figure 1a).

When factoring in available data fields for the predictor variable and outcome variables, and use of a complete case approach for covariates, there were ultimately 8,528 and 7,589 participants in the tfMRI and rfMRI samples, respectively.

All statistical analysis was performed using multiple linear regression using the lm function in R. For each combination of predictor and outcome variable, five models were run, factoring in covariate families into models in a sequential manner (see Figure 1b). This allowed us to disambiguate whether a certain association remained significant when adjusting for neuroimaging and demographic covariates, but not once also controlling for work life covariates, for example. The best fitting of these five models was selected on the basis of lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) value, and this model was taken forward to statistical inference. P-values reflecting the association between length of working week and the respective outcome variable were extracted, and false discovery rate correction was applied across all retained models within tfMRI or rfMRI analysis. Any significant effects following correction were confirmed using robust estimation models which should lessen effect of influential outlier values. For each model, we also tested for an interaction between length of working week and sex.

This project was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/dmzrb). This document was updated in line with changes to our analysis plan.

Results

For tfMRI descriptive statistics, mean z-statistics in each ROI were plausible, with consistent and strong positive activation in the left and right FFA for faces > shapes,  and weaker positive activation in the left and right amygdala. Activation in the insula was inconsistent across subregions, with some showing very weak positive or very weak negative activation, and others showing no preference for either faces or shapes. For tfMRI analysis, no association was found between length of working week (whether defined continuously or categorically) and the mean z-statistic in any of our ROIs. No interaction was found between length of working week and sex for any model.

For rfMRI descriptive statistics, average within-network and subnetwork connectivity generally ranged between 0.5 and 0.75, consistently higher than values for between-network connectivity, which were all 0.5 or lower. For rfMRI analysis, continuous length of working week was found to be significantly positively associated with within-network connectivity in the somatomotor network (F = 13.64, corrected p = .038, beta = 0.0019 [0.0010–0.0028], standardised beta (0.0642 [0.0348–0.0937]). This was accompanied by a very small partial eta squared effect size of .0018. Significance of this effect was confirmed by robust estimation modelling. No significant association was found between length of working week (defined continuously or categorically) and any other rfMRI outcome variable. No interaction between sex and length of working week was found for any model. Observing mean F-values for all rfMRI models of the association between continuous length of working week and within-network demonstrated a pattern whereby observed effects tended to drastically drop once demographic covariates had been added to models, often pushing effects out of pre-correction significance (see Figure 2). This suggests a pattern whereby any observed effects of length of working week (which are already very small) are explained by individual participant characteristics.

Conclusion

Contrary to our hypotheses, no association was found between length of working week and tfMRI variables, as measured using activation in ROIs for the contrast of faces > shapes for an emotional face matching task. This was the case despite prior evidence of an association between occupational stress and tfMRI outcome variables2. The lack of an association observed here may be reflect that regions activated during emotional face matching (the only tfMRI data available through the UK Biobank) are not sufficiently sensitive to detrimental effects of long working hours. A task with higher executive demands, for example, may have been more sensitive. Alternatively, length of working week alone may not predict adverse outcomes to the same degree as variables such as occupational stress or job demands, which may provide more direct insight into participants’ subjective experience of their working life.

Again, contrary to preregistered hypotheses, length of working week was not found to predict functional connectivity for associative networks including the default mode network. Instead, continuous length of working week was positively associated with increased connectivity within the somatomotor network. This may reflect a maladaptive response of this network in the context of high job demands. Indeed, prior research has demonstrated somatomotor within-network connectivity to be higher in adolescent participants scanned later in the day4, suggesting a possible sensitivity to cumulative behavioural load and circadian rhythms. Alternatively, increased time spent engaged in work may bolster this network in an adaptive manner, allowing workers to more readily process incoming sensory input. This is consistent with evidence that some somatomotor regions are recruited during action planning and internal simulations of goal-directed behaviour5. As such, increased engagement of this network may enable those working longer hours to better cope with the demands of their role. With the employment variables available through the UK Biobank we are not able to directly test these hypotheses – future research using other datasets with richer employment data may benefit from doing so.

While the current study had access to a large sample of participants by the standard of typical fMRI studies, the analyses run are only correlative. Future research in this area may benefit from observing effects in interventional studies on working time reduction, such as the adoption of a 4-day working week, on brain function. Such an intervention would allow for a better understanding of the role of causality in the association between length of working week and brain function.

Several limitations of the current study should be noted. First, while the UK Biobank allows access to data concerning ‘Length of working week for main job’ (the variable used in the present analysis), we are unable to verify whether participants are working beyond these hours in real terms, either through a second job or through unpaid labour including housework and caring responsibilities. Second, despite the large sample size offered by UK Biobank, the fact that the sample by definition is limited to participants who are willing and able to volunteer their time may select for those with less demanding work schedules. As such, the sample analysed may not be entirely representative of the general UK population.

Overall, this study suggests that, using available data from the UK Biobank, tfMRI activation in relevant regions of interest during an emotional face matching task is not sensitive to length of working week. While associations between length of working week and network connectivity as measured through rfMRI are limited, a positive association was found with within-network connectivity in the somatomotor network. Future work using interventional designs and a wider array of variables reflecting subjective experience of employment may further elucidate this relationship.
