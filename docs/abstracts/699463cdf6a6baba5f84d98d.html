Introduction

Screen time such as television viewing, video gaming and social media use is associated with depressive symptoms among youth. However, physical activity is a buffer such that screen time is not related to depressive symptoms among those who meet physical activity guidelines. Recent work^1 found that the association between screen time and depressive symptoms may be mediated by white matter (WM) organization in the cingulum bundle, an emotion regulation tract implicated in depression. However, this prior work did not take into account physical activity; here, we examined the influence of physical activity on the screen time-cingulum bundle organization-depressive symptom relationship in youth. 

Methods

Prospective data were from baseline (mean age = 9.9 yrs) and 2-year follow-up of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study Public Data Release 6.1, a U.S.-based multi-site cohort study. 

-Screen time (continuous; average hours/day) was self-reported via the ABCD youth screen time questionnaire. 

- Depressive symptoms (continuous; DSM-5 oriented scale sum score) were caregiver-reported using the Child Behavior Checklist. 

-Physical activity (dichotomous; meets guidelines, yes/no) was self-reported via the Youth Risk Behavior Survey.

-WM characteristics in the cingulum bundle were measured using fractional anisotropy (FA) derived from diffusion tensor images. FA values across hemispheres and dorsal/ventral divisions were averaged to create one continuous variable denoting cingulum bundle organization. 

Participants were stratified a priori into two groups based on baseline physical activity levels: met guidelines (60 minutes/day, 7 days/week) or did not meet guidelines (<60 minutes/day, 7 days/week). Statistical analyses were repeated in both physical activity groups. First, the effect of baseline screen time on follow-up depressive symptoms and the effect of baseline screen time on follow-up FA in the cingulum were evaluated by multiple regression to ensure that mediation analyses were appropriate. If warranted based on these initial model results, depressive symptoms were regressed on screen time and FA in the cingulum together; this was done to test whether the relationship between baseline screen time and follow-up depressive symptoms is mediated by follow-up WM integrity in the cingulum. All models were adjusted for participant sex and race/ethnicity, and baseline age, household income, depressive symptoms and study site.

Results

7782 participants were in the analytic sample (46.2% female, 44.5% non-White, 16.6% meeting physical activity guidelines). In the low physical activity group (the group that did not meet guidelines), greater screen time was associated with greater depressive symptoms (β=0.03, p<0.01) and lower FA in the cingulum bundle (β=-0.0003, p=0.01), but there was no association between FA in the cingulum bundle and depressive symptoms (β=-0.4728, p=0.57). In the high physical activity group (the group that did meet physical activity guidelines), there was no association between screen time and depressive symptoms (β=0.03, p=0.054), screen time and FA in the cingulum bundle (β=-0.0002, p=0.42), or FA in the cingulum bundle and depressive symptoms (β=-1.39, p=0.40). Given that not all variables were associated, formal mediation analyses were not conducted in either group of participants.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that there may be a longitudinal neuroprotective effect of physical activity against abnormal WM microstructure within the cingulum bundle arising from excess screen time. However, the present analyses did not replicate the expected associations between cingulum WM microstructural organization and depressive symptoms. This may be explained by the fact that the present analysis used FA as a proxy-measure for WM microstructural integrity, whereas prior work used a neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) approach, which provides greater microstructural information than FA. Nonetheless, we found an expected lack of an association between screen time and depressive symptoms in children who met physical activity guidelines, suggesting that physical activity may buffer the screen time-cingulum bundle organization-depressive symptom mediational relationship in youth. Future work could consider (1) using a NODDI approach to confirm our findings, (2) incorporating functional connectivity as part of their analyses and (3) incorporating more nuanced operationalizations of screen time and physical activity. Findings from such work could illuminate relevant mechanisms and intervention strategies for improving mental health in youth.
