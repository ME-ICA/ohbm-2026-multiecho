Introduction

Face detection depends on the interplay between feedforward sensory drive and top-down feedback signals shaped by internal expectations and prior knowledge. These interactions occur across the visual processing hierarchy, encompassing face-selective regions such as the early visual cortex and the fusiform face area (FFA).1 While prior neuroimaging studies have mapped this large-scale network, distinguishing the specific contribution of feedback remains challenging.2,3 Feedback reflects top-down predictions generated by internal models, yet traditional stimulus-driven paradigms make it difficult to characterize these signals independently of feedforward input. Moreover, feedforward and feedback processes are thought to originate in distinct cortical layers that conventional fMRI cannot resolve. 

To address this challenge, we employed a white noise paradigm that elicits illusory face perception in the absence of true stimulus, enabling isolation of feedback-driven signals. Specifically, we sought to use white noise stimuli to elicit activation of higher-level face processing circuits without presenting actual face stimuli. Using high-resolution 7T fMRI, we examine layer-specific activation patterns from visual cortex to integrative hubs such as the insula, a region linking sensory salience with interoceptive and emotional processing. 

Methods

Experimental Design and Procedures. One participant (35M) underwent 7T MRI scans while completing a face detection task adapted from Smith et al., 2012.4 The participant was informed that half of the trials contained a face embedded in noise, and that the noise levels would make the task challenging. They were also informed any face present would appear centrally and upright within the rectangle. Responses were made by pushing one of two buttons to indicate “YES” or “NO.” 

The subject was never exposed to true face stimuli and remained unaware that no faces were actually presented. To perform the task, the participant was therefore required to use their internal representation of a face and attempt to match it to the incoming white-noise stimulus. Beginning with the subjects’ third session, we provided feedback in response to repeated inquiries about task performance and to reduce performance anxiety. Since face stimuli were not actually presented to the participant, we provided a sham computer-generated percentage (50-65%) as an estimated “percent correct.” The participant was also informed of the number of responses they made and the proportion of “face present” versus “no face” responses; if this differed by more than 4%, it was described as approximately a 50/50 split. 

The participant completed five MRI sessions. Each session contained four to six face-detection runs, with each run consisting of 35-40 stimulus presentations. All stimuli consisted exclusively of static white-noise images with an RMS contrast of 25%. Each noise stimuli was presented in grayscale spanning 3º x 2.23º visual angle.5 Images were 43 x 32 pixels, and were presented for 0.5 seconds each. 

Face Detection–Behavioral Performance. We analyzed the percentage of face detection trials for each of the 23 runs and response times (RT) for each condition. Design matrices for fMRI analyses were derived from participant responses (i.e. “face detected” or “face not detected”) which we used to examine voxelwise responses in functional MRI time series. 

Brain Imaging Acquisition. We acquired data on a Siemens Magnetom 7TPlus MRI Scanner using a 32-channel Nova headcoil. Each run consisted of 180 volumes of 3D-EPI with resolution of (0.85 mm)3. Other parameters are as follows: TR = 66 ms (RF), 2.442 s (volume); TE = 21.1 ms; flip angle = 18º; partial fourier = 3/4; GRAPPA = 3/4 (phase y/phase z). During each session, we collected a T1-weighted MPRAGE with the following parameters: resolution = (0.6 mm)3; TI = 1280 ms; flip angle = 7º. 

Brain Imaging Analysis. Analyses of fMRI data were conducted in AFNI (Cox, 1995) using afni_proc.py. Data were first adjusted for motion during the scan, aligned to the high-resolution anatomical, blurred with 1.5mm kernel, and adjusted to percent signal change before being submitted to a multiple regression for events (faces seen vs. faces not seen). Level-two analyses were performed in 3dttest++. 

Results

Behavioral. The participant completed 902 trials while in the scanner. Data from one run was discarded due to button box issues. We analysed the remaining 862 trials and calculated the percentage of "face detected" trials. Despite the presence of only noise, the subject reported the stimuli as “face” on 49.65% of the trials. We included both “face detected” and “face not detected” trial types in the regression analysis of fMRI time series.

Brain Imaging. Whole brain analyses of the contrast (faces > no face) revealed distributed activation across multiple levels of the visual and integrative processing hierarchy. Significant increases in BOLD signal were observed in the primary visual cortex reflecting enhanced early and mid-level visual processing during face perception in noise. Higher-order activation was present in the motor cortex and anterior insula, indicating recruitment of visuospatial integration as well as internal, salience-related, and memory supported processes. Interestingly, the fusiform face area (FFA) did not demonstrate significant activation differences between faces minus no face conditions. Together, these results demonstrate that illusory face perception engages a broad cortical network spanning sensory, attentional, and salience systems. 

Conclusion

Our study indicates that face perception in noise recruited both early visual regions and higher-order areas, suggesting coordinated feedforward-feedback interactions even in the absence of true facial input. 

Faster responses on face-detected trials and widespread activation in the faces > no face contrast suggest that illusory face perception arises when minimal bottom-up structure initiates a rapid feedforward representation that is reinforced by higher-order feedback signals. During face-detected trials, activation extended beyond visual regions to higher-order regions like the anterior insula, reflecting salience evaluation and internal model-based interpretation under ambiguity.  

In contrast, slower responses on no-face trials likely reflect weaker or ambiguous feedforward evidence that fails to recruit the same level of top-down confirmation, resulting in reduced engagement of this broader cortical system. Together, these findings indicate that illusory face perception emerges when bottom-up structure aligns with internal face templates to trigger rapid recognition and higher-order network recruitment, whereas insufficient alignment slows decisions and limits higher-order engagement.
