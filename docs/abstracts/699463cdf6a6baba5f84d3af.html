Introduction

Everyday interactions require not only knowing what objects are and where they are located but also understanding their internal spatial structure or allocentric (object-centered) space. Disruptions in these computations have profound clinical consequences, yet the neural basis of allocentric perception remains unclear. Classical models distinguish a ventral stream for object identity recognition, a dorsal stream for spatial processing, and a fronto-parietal network for attentional control. Here we test the hypothesis that a recently identified region in the posterior infero-temporal cortex (PIT+)1, integrates object and spatial information to compute allocentric coordinates. 

Methods

Thirty-two participants performed novel allocentric, egocentric, and color tasks using realistic stimuli (faces, houses) and control symbolic arrays (C-shapes)- Fig. 1a-b. We combined this paradigm with task-based and resting-state 3T fMRI. We used univariate GLM to identify regions sensitive to allocentric processing, whole-brain searchlight MVPA to assess the discriminability of allocentric versus egocentric representations, and RSA to quantify the representational geometry of reference-frame coding. Finally, we analyzed resting-state and task-dependent connectivity (gPPI) to characterize the functional embedding of PIT+ and determine how its interactions with dorsal and ventral networks shift during allocentric reasoning.

Results

Allocentric reasoning selectively engaged PIT+ across all stimulus types, including symbolic arrays lacking categorical meaning, indicating a stimulus-independent object-centered code (Fig 1c). PIT+ showed stronger allocentric than egocentric modulation and a functional profile distinct from neighboring category-selective ventral areas. MVPA revealed robust and cross-stimulus decoding of allocentric vs. egocentric frames in PIT+ and posterior parietal cortex (PPC). RSA confirmed that PIT+ preferentially encoded allocentric reference frames, whereas PPC emphasized egocentric ones. Connectivity analyses demonstrated that PIT+ is functionally embedded in a posterior dorso-ventral network at rest and dynamically increases coupling with PPC, inferior frontal junction, and category-selective regions during allocentric processing.

Conclusion

Our findings identify PIT+ as a key ventral node for object-centered spatial perception (Fig. 1d). PIT+ flexibly bridges object-based representations in the ventral stream with spatial and attentional computations in dorsal parietal–frontal networks, revealing a distributed but coordinated architecture for allocentric coding in the human brain.
