Introduction

Essential tremor (ET) is one of the most common movement disorders worldwide and is characterized by postural and action tremors that predominantly affect the upper limbs, often interfering with daily activities. Pharmacological treatments can reduce tremor severity. In medication-refractory essential tremor, deep brain stimulation (DBS) remains an invasive procedure. High-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) has emerged as a non-surgical alternative, inducing a permanent small thalamic lesion,¹,² thereby affecting the cerebello-thalamo-cortical network involved in tremor.³ While this procedure modifies structural properties of brain networks², functional changes associated with HIFU-induced clinical improvements and their long-term stability remain poorly understood. We aimed to characterize the longitudinal reconfiguration of tremor-related functional brain circuits following HIFU. We conducted a task-fMRI study in 15 patients treated with HIFU comparing brain network engagement before treatment and at multiple time points until a year following treatment. At each visit, anatomical and functional MRI were acquired during hand posture, with synchronized accelerometry. We investigated brain networks engaged during tremorous hand posture, isolating the effect of tremor amplitude⁴ at the whole-brain and regional levels in cerebellar and thalamic regions. Analyses distinguished brain networks ipsilateral and contralateral to the lesion to study bilateral cerebral and cerebellar functional reorganization. We hypothesized that (i) immediate and pronounced functional changes would occur in ipsilateral cerebral sensorimotor cortices and contralateral sensorimotor cerebellar lobules following treatment, accounting for short-term clinical improvement; and (ii) more subtle functional changes would emerge at later time points in ipsilateral and contralateral motor and non-motor areas, associated with the long-term stability of clinical improvement observed during follow-up.

Methods

During inclusion before treatment (V1) and follow-up visits post-treatment (V2=1 week, V3=6 months, V4=1 year), tremor severity was assessed by a neurologist using the Fahn-Tolosa-Marin Clinical Rating Scale for Tremor (CRST).  Motor symptoms were scored both at rest, during postural maintenance, and during task-based evaluations (CRST Parts A+B), separately for the treated and untreated hands. Clinical change was expressed as the absolute difference from baseline (ΔCRST). The stability of tremor reduction in follow-up visits was expressed as the difference from V2. 

During fMRI, participants performed a postural task involving alternating blocks of posture (raising hands above the torso for 30 seconds) followed by rest periods (15 s). Accelerometry was acquired with a MRI-compatible tri-axial accelerometer (Brain Products; sampling frequency = 5 kHz) placed on the dorsum of the hands. Accelerometry data were detrended, demeaned, segmented into 5 s windows to compute power spectra with 0.2 Hz resolution, and filtered within the 3.5-8 Hz frequency range.⁴  Power spectra were calculated using a Hanning taper, and tremor power time courses were down-sampled to the fMRI repetition time and z-scored to generate scan-to-scan regressors. 

Anatomical T1-weighted images (3D MP2RAGE sequence: TR=5000 ms, TE=2.03 ms, voxel size 1×1×1 mm3) and functional BOLD images (2D multiband EPI sequence : TR = 1000 ms, TE = 34 ms, flip angle 60∘,voxel size 2×2×2 mm3) were acquired on a Siemens Prisma 3T scanner. Functional images were preprocessed using SPM12, including realignment, slice timing correction, coregistration to T1 images, segmentation and normalization to MNI space using Dartel, spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel (6mm). Functional neuroimaging and accelerometry were synchronized to link tremor amplitude to brain activity,⁴ to isolate tremor-related brain activity from voluntary hand movement–related activity. We specified first-level General Linear Models (GLMs) for each subject in SPM12. To eliminate noise and motion artifact, head motion parameters and their derivatives (n=24), CSF and white matter signals were included as nuisance regressors. Accelerometry recordings were included as parametric modulators (pmod) of the postural task regressor. Separate contrast images were generated for the coefficient associated with the tremor-related BOLD responses (the parametric modulator) for subsequent group-level analysis (paired t-tests, SPM12).  

To facilitate comparison between the treated and untreated circuits, we flipped contrast images from left-handed patients (n=3) on the axial plane. We conducted a whole brain exploratory analysis and an analysis with a-priori Regions of Interest (ROIs) covering the cerebello-thalamic circuits. Cerebellar sensorimotor ROIs were defined using the cerebellum SUIT atlas (lobules III, IV/V, VI. VIII). Precise segmentation of the thalamus focusing on the VLPv (the equivalent of the VIM) was performed using the automatic segmentation tool THOMAS (Thalamus Optimized Multi Atlas Segmentation⁵). After normalizing the SUIT and THOMAS ROIs to MNI space, tremor-related contrast signal  was extracted using MarsBaR toolbox. 

For both whole-brain and ROI analyses, we used paired t-tests to investigate (i) the treatment effect by comparing tremor-related activity between baseline and one week post-treatment (V1 vs V2); (ii) intermediate changes (V2 vs V3), and long-term changes (V2 vs V4). We used a multiple regression analysis to test the correlation between tremor-related activity with immediate clinical improvement between V1 and V2, and with the stability of clinical improvement between V2 and later visits. Significance was assessed using voxel-wise p < 0.001 incorrected at the whole brain level combined with cluster-level FWE correction at p < 0.05, and at p < 0.05 corrected for multiple comparisons at the ROI level.

Results

The 15 ET patients included in our study (mean age of 67.1 years (± 8.6), disease duration of 32.9 years (± 15.3), 4 women) had a reduction of tremor amplitude on their treated hand: CRST mean score was 15.8 (± 4.0) at baseline and 4.5 (± 3.6) at three months post-treatment, corresponding to a ΔCRST of 11.3 (± 3.5) points. The reduction of the CRST mean score remained stable at follow-up visits beyond 3 months (0.3<p<0.8).

We measured tremor amplitude from accelerometric data recorded during the task performed in the MRI. Based on the AUC of the frequency peak, tremor amplitude decreased significantly from V1 to V3 on the treated side (t=3.74, p<0.001; Figure 1A), and this decrease remained unchanged for all the post-treatment visits (0.3<p<0.7). Tremor persisted on the untreated side with no significant effect of Visit (0.1<p<0.9). 

We isolated short-term effect of the treatment on task-related activity by comparing recordings one week post-treatment (V2) to before treatment (V1). We observed drastic changes related to the online variation of tremor amplitude in cerebral cortical areas ipsilateral to the lesion, as well as local changes in the ipsilateral VIM and contralateral cerebellar sensorimotor areas. More precisely, reduced activity was observed (i) in the ipsilateral M1 hand area, the supplementary motor area, the cingulate motor area and the anterior insula (whole brain analysis, Figure 1B, left panel); (ii) in the lesioned VIM (t=2.16, p=0.03) and sensorimotor cerebellar lobules contralateral to the lesion (VI, VIII; 2.21<t<2.92, 0.006<p<0.02; Figure 1C, left panel). There was no significant change in the untreated side. 

Six months after treatment (V3) compared to one week after treatment (V2), we observed subtle changes related to online variation of tremor amplitude.   More precisely, increased activity was observed (i) in the bilateral posterior cingulate cortex associated with the tremor amplitude of the untreated hand (Figure 1B, right panel); (ii) in cerebellum VI (ipsilateral to the lesioned VIM, t=2.01, p=0.04) and in the bilateral cerebellar VIII (2.7<t<3.55 , 0.002<p<0.008; Figure 1C, right panel) associated with what remained of the tremor in the treated hand.

We did not observe any significant change one year following treatment (V4) compared to the other follow-up visits (p>0.001). We did not observe significant correlation between clinical improvement from V1 to V2 and tremor-related activity at V2. We observed that bilateral posterior cingulate cortex activity at V3 was correlated with the stability of tremor reduction (difference of CRST scores between V3 and V2; see plot in Figure 2B, right panel). In other words, patients who had greater activity in the posterior cingulate area had less stable reduction of tremor amplitude on the clinical scale.

Conclusion

Our findings show that cerebral and cerebellar networks are differentially affected by unilateral HIFU thalamic lesions in patients with essential tremor. Cortical sensorimotor regions—including the M1 hand area, supplementary motor area, and cingulate motor area (Brodmann area 24)—are associated with a rapid and marked reduction in tremor-related BOLD activity immediately after treatment. In contrast, the posterior cingulate cortex (Brodmann area 31) may contribute at a later stage to the functional reorganization of feedback related to intentional movement and involuntary tremor from the treated and untreated hands, respectively. Consistent with this interpretation, patients who showed less stable improvement in clinical scores at V3 exhibited greater activity in the posterior cingulate cortex.

Sensorimotor posterior cerebellar lobules displayed a more complex temporal pattern of BOLD activity. Cerebellar lobule VI, contralateral to the lesioned VIM, showed an immediate reduction in tremor-related activity following treatment, whereas lobule VIII exhibited a transient reduction followed by a bilateral increase six months post-treatment. This pattern may reflect a beneficial reconfiguration in which the intact cerebello-thalamic network compensates for postural and balance functions of the lesioned hemisphere; however, it could also be maladaptive if the bilateral increase in activity is associated with tremor recurrence.

Overall, these findings support the presence of functionally heterogeneous cortical and cerebellar regions, with the latter playing a key role in post-HIFU functional reorganization. This reorganization may arise from structural alterations in the lesioned cerebello-thalamic pathway and may influence the long-term stability of tremor improvement. Further studies are needed to better understand these mechanisms.
