Introduction

The predictive processing account of brain function suggests that our brains build models of the world based on our prior experiences, and use these models to make predictions about what should happen next. ^1 These models are updated by confronting them with external inputs that are not explained away by internal predictions, resulting in prediction errors.

An alteration of these predictive mechanisms could be at the origin of sensory symptoms observed in psychosis. ^2 For example, there is evidence for a difference between Schizophrenia patients and controls in processing unexpected auditory deviants (oddballs), as represented by the mismatch negativity (MNN) response. ^3

The reduction in the MMN response of patients  compared to controls is widely used as a biomarker of psychosis. However, most studies do not take into account the role of attention and its modulatory effects on top-down signals and MMN dynamics. ^4 Situations in which attention is directed towards or away from a stimulation (rather than mind-wandering) are omnipresent in everyday life,  therefore, we need to investigate how they affect MMN deficiencies in psychosis.

Furthermore, much of the current research was done using electrophysiological methods, leaving another knowledge gap, namely how MMN is manifested at the spatial scale afforded by high-field (7T) functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). The use of 7T sub-millimeter fMRI for studying psychosis allows to test predictive processing accounts of psychosis on a layer-specific level ^5 advancing our understanding of the neuronal circuit signatures of psychosis. 

Methods

Experimental paradigm 

We recorded BOLD signal responses in two groups of participants (17 control participants and 13 early-stage schizophrenia patients). The total scanning time was around 2 hours.

Trials were composed of a sequence of six 150 ms tones for a total duration of 1.5s. On standard trials, all tones were the same (440 Hz), but in the deviant condition (20% of trials) the last tone differed in pitch (620 Hz). Inter-trial intervals were of 10s on average, jittered between 8 and 12s. To control for attention, we also presented an unrelated visual stimulus on each trial (4 “pacman” shapes which formed a Kanizsa figure on 20% of trials and were misaligned on the remaining trials). We instructed participants to do an auditory (detecting a shorter tone in the auditory stimulation) or visual (detecting a subtle flicker in the visual stimulus) task. To confirm  task engagement, we used a performance threshold of at least 80% hit rate and less than 5% false alarm rate over the whole fMRI session.

Participants performed 4 experimental runs. Each run contained 2 parts, one for the visual task and one for the auditory task, in a counterbalanced order (feedback about task performance was displayed at the end of each part). Within each part there were 25 trials: 5 of each dedicated to the visual (flicker) and auditory (shorter tone) task, 5 had standard auditory stimulation with Kanizsa visual stimulation, 5 had deviant auditory stimulation with standard visual stimulation, and 5 had both auditory and visual standard stimulation. Only data corresponding to the fully standard and the auditory deviant trials (20 of each for each attentional conditions across all runs) were further analysed in the present work.

fMRI data acquisition

We scanned participants at the Queen Elizabeth University Hospital with a Siemens 7-Tesla (7T) Terra Magnetom MRI Scanner, equipped with a NOVA Medical 32-channel head coil. The protocol included four task-related runs (310 volumes EPI, 0.8mm voxel resolution, 2000ms TR) and four corresponding top-up runs (five volumes each), an anatomical scan (T1-weighted MP2rage, 0.6mm voxel resolution), and a combined retinotopy/tonotopy functional run (not used here).

Data analysis

We performed fMRI analyses using BrainVoyager, FSL, MATLAB and R. Anatomical data was pre-processed by applying inhomogeneity correction (IIHC), upscaling to a voxel resolution of 0.4mm isotropic (from 0.6mm), and manually aligned to ACPC space. We performed white and grey matter segmentation as well as manual correction using ITK-SNAP in the ROIs, Heschl’s gyrus (HG) and superior temporal gyrus (STG). In BrainVoyager, we generated six equivolume cortical layers for ROI-based depth sampling. Pre-processing steps for functional data included 3D motion correction, slice-time correction, high-pass temporal filtering (five cycles), and cubic spline interpolation. We aligned functional runs using inter-run spatial cross-correlations of signal intensities.

We performed deconvolution general linear model analysis across all cortical layers, in all groups, to acquire peak BOLD response beta values (AR2 corrected and z-scored). We then fit a model predicting peak responses from stimulation (deviant vs non deviant tone), attentional condition (visual vs auditory), ROI (HS vs STG), group (control vs patient), cortical layer (as a linear parameter), all interaction between these factors as well as a random intercept and slope (over cortical depths) by participant. We also fit a model predicting the difference in peak response for deviant - standard trials from attentional condition, area, group, layer, all interaction between these factors and a random intercept by participant (random slopes would induce a singular fit). From these models, we could derive comparisons between parameter levels using estimated marginal means.

Results

From the peak response model, we found that (1) the response to deviant trials is always (i.e. across all combinations of other factors) higher than to standard (non-deviant) trials (all t(2529) > 2.3, all p < .05), (2) the response in the early auditory area (Heschl’s gyrus) is always higher than in the late auditory area (superior temporal gyrus) (all t(2529) > 4, all p < 10-4), (3) control participants always have a higher response than patients (all t(2531) > 4.4, all p < 10-4), (4)  the response is higher when paying attention to the auditory compared to the visual modality (all t(2529) > 2.27, all p < .05), except in the controls’ early auditory area where there is no significant difference (control trials: t(2529) = 1.488, p > .05, deviant trials: t(2529) = 1.8, p > 0.05).

Furthermore, taking cortical layers into account, there is no laminar trend difference for deviant vs standard or visual vs auditory attention in either control or schizophrenia groups.There is a significant laminar effect, under which the peak response control participants in the early auditory area (HG) is significantly more sloped towards superficial layers compared to the later area (STG) (t(2529) = 3.89, p < 10-4).

From the mismatch response model (deviant – standard peak), we found that patients have a higher mismatch response than controls in the auditory attention condition (HG: t(1279) = -2.87, p < 10-2, STG: t(1279) = -2.06, p < .05), however switching to visual attention increases mismatch response in the controls’ late auditory area (STG) (t(1264) = -2.24, p < .05), while it decreases in the patients’ early area (HG) (t(1264) = 3.46, p < 10-3). There is no significant effect of laminar trend in this model.

Conclusion

We examined responses to a mismatched tone in the auditory cortex of schizophrenia patients, and control participants, using high-field 7T fMRI. We directed participants' attention either toward or away from the auditory stimulation.

We found results differentiating the effect of attentional modulation between patients and control participants. Specifically, it seems that there is less differentiation of early vs late auditory areas in patients. In control participants, switching from auditory to visual attention increases mismatch response in the late auditory area, while in patients mismatch response starts higher in both areas but decreases in the visual attentional condition.

Overall, these results expand the idea of distinctive feedback mechanisms as neural correlates of psychosis to include contrasting effects of attentional modulation.
