Introduction

Motor sequences are fundamental to everyday life. Recent studies have shown that during motor sequence learning (MSL), performance improvements often emerge during the rest periods interleaved with practice (1–2). These micro-offline gains (MOGs) have been coined to reflect reactivation of recent experience and/or anticipatory planning of upcoming actions, and are thought to be driven by hippocampal replay/preplay (2–4). Nevertheless, in the classic MSL paradigm, rest blocks are relatively long, typically 10–30 s, whereas neural replay/preplay occur on the millisecond scale, with events compressed by ~20-fold relative to acquisition (3). Given that humans can execute a 5-item motor sequence in ≤1 s, conventional MSL designs may inadvertently limit learning potential by overlooking physiological timing.

In this study, we modified the MSL design to align it with the biological timescale of neural replay/preplay by inserting brief (1-s) rest periods during practice, after the execution of each sequence. We hypothesized that increasing the opportunities for hippocampal replay/preplay would potentiate motor learning by enhancing hippocampal engagement during practice.

Methods

Forty right-handed adults (18–30 years, 21 females) performed a 5-item MSL task [4-1-3-2-4] using the fingers of their non-dominant (left) hand, inside a 3T Magnetom Prisma MRI scanner, following a Task/Rest block design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups, which differed only in the structure of Task blocks (Fig. 1). In the control group (n = 21), sequences were executed continuously for a total of 10 s within each Task block (continuous Task Blocks, cTB). In the experimental group (n = 19), participants also executed the sequence for a total of 10 s, but a 1-s pause was inserted after each completed sequence (spaced Task blocks sTB). In both groups, Task blocks alternated with 10-s Rest blocks (RB). Learning was quantified using two complementary measures: the average inter-key interval (IKI), indexing execution speed, and the number of correctly executed sequences per block, indexing efficiency. We predicted that, relative to continuous practice, spacing practice would shift both behavioral gains and hippocampal activation from the 10-s Rest blocks to the 1-s pauses interleaved with practice.

Image acquisition and analysis

fMRI images were acquired using a 64-channel head/neck coil through motor training using the following parameters: voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3 mm, FOV = 210 × 210 mm, 64 slices aligned with the AC-PC line, no gap, posterior-anterior (PA) phase encoding direction,TR = 500 ms, TE = 25 ms, multiband acceleration factor = 8, no iPAT, BW =2,646 Hz/Px, echo spacing = 0.5 ms, EPI factor = 70, FA = 47°. A T1-weighted image (MPRAGE, 1x1x1 mm) and gradient echo field map (3x3x3mm), were also acquired for normalization and correction of field inhomogeneities using parameters adapted from our previous work (2). 

Preprocessing of fMRI images was carried out using SPM12 (FIL, University College London) and FSL (FMRIB, Oxford). The BOLD images were realigned to the first volume and unwarped using the pre-substracted phase difference image, normalized  to the standard space (via a coregistered T1w image), and smoothed with a gaussian kernel (FWHM = 8mm). The final voxel size was 2x2x2mm. The T1w image was also segmented, and the resulting masks eroded to extract the mean white matter and CSF signals. We performed a standard whole-brain voxelwise general linear model (GLM) analysis to assess the brain regions active during motor execution (Task > Rest) and during the rest periods (Rest > Task) using SPM12. Active areas during each condition were defined using a p < 0.05 threshold, corrected for family-wise errors (FWE) using random-field theory. 

To examine if changing the temporal structure of training shifted the engagement of the hippocampus, a ROI analysis was conducted using the MarsBaR toolbox for SPM12 using functional masks from the Tian et al., atlas (2020). Specifically, we conducted a group-by-block interaction in each hippocampal subfield (anterior and posterior subdivisions of the left and right hippocampus) for the Rest> Task contrast, and applied a correction for multiple comparisons (n = 4 subfields) to the ROIs that met an initial threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

The spaced-task design improved MSL speed and efficiency, and induced MOGs at the sub-second level

Compared to the control, the experimental group reached asymptotic performance more rapidly (Fig 2A, asymptotic performance: control = block 7, experimental = block 4; p <0.05), stabilized at a lower IKI (Fig 2A, two-sample t-test, p = 0.001), and achieved nearly twice as many correct sequences per Task block (Fig 2B, two-sample t test, p= 0.005). 

To determine when learning took place, we separately computed gains in performance during Rest and Task blocks of early learning, i.e. until asymptotic performance was reached (computed as in (1)). Consistent with prior evidence (1-3), gains in the control group emerged primarily during 10-s Rest blocks (Fig 2C: RB), while in the experimental group occurred mainly during 1-s Rest pauses (Fig 2C: sTB) not during sequence execution (group x block interaction: F(2,38)= 5.78, p=0.021; inset, Fig 2C, p = 0.007). These results point to the occurrence of MOGs at the millisecond timescale. 

Note that computing MOGs based solely on the last two key presses yielded similar results (group x block interaction: p=0.044). On the other hand, no further gains were observed in either group once performance reached the asymptote (blocks 10–15; p>0.3). Together, these results make it unlikely that pre-planning alone explains MOGs (5) and suggest that learning meaningfully contributes to this metric.

The spaced-task design increased hippocampal activity during 1-s pauses 

Preliminary fMRI ROI analyses carried out in the bilateral hippocampi identified a group-by-block interaction in the left and right anterior hippocampi (Fig 2D). We found that the anterior hippocampi showed opposite activity patterns across groups: in the Control group it showed greater activity during Rest than during Task blocks, while in the Experimental group it showed greater activity during Task (sTB) than during Rest blocks (RB), consistent with hippocampal recruitment during 1-s pauses [(RB-cTB)-(RB-sTB): left anterior hippocampus t = 2.76, p = 0.018; right anterior hippocampus t = 2.84, p = 0.014, both corrected by Bonferroni for multiple comparisons]. Consistent with our previous work, activity in the posterior hippocampus was not modulated by learning (left posterior hippocampus t = -0.35, p = 0.98; right posterior hippocampus t = -0.04, p = 0.95) (Fig 2D).

Conclusion

Our work has several implications at the basic and translational research levels. First, spacing practice with brief pauses enhances MSL beyond traditional designs, both accelerating learning and increasing efficiency, probably through hippocampal replay/preplay. Second, MSL is mostly driven by MOGs both at the second and millisecond level and not by recovery from fatigue, dissipation from reactive inhibition or pre-planning alone. Third, the fact that gains in performance arise within 1 s also rules out the possibility that MOGs result from consciously rehearsing the sequence in working memory. Our findings demonstrate that motor sequence learning is sensitive to the temporal structure of practice, and show that slight, biologically informed adjustments to the training schedule can markedly enhance MSL, opening new avenues for optimizing rehabilitation and training protocols.
