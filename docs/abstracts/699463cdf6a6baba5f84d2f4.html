Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, characterized by a decline in episodic memory and impaired encoding of new information. Although hippocampal atrophy is a hallmark of the disease, several implicit memory mechanisms appear to remain preserved, such as the sense of familiarity (SoF).1 Musical stimuli are particularly relevant, as musical abilities and memory for old tunes often remain intact even in advanced stages of AD.2 However, whether patients retain the ability to encode new information in these artistic domains remains unclear. This study aimed to determine whether if AD patients retain familiarity-based recognition abilities and to characterize the relationship between familiarity and regional grey-matter density. 

Methods

This study was approved by an ethic committee (CPP Nord-Ouest III, ID-RCB n°2011-A00351-40) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

A total of 31 AD patients (79.4±5.9 years; MMSE = 14.5±5.4) and 20 healthy controls (HC; 77.1±4.9 years; MMSE: 28.8±0.9) were included. Data-driven stratification split AD patients into two subgroups, based on their cognitive impairment: moderate (mAD; n=18; MMSE = 18±4) and severe (sAD; n=13; MMSE = 9±2). 

Participants first completed repeated exposure sessions, in which 8 musical and 8 pictorial items were presented once; they were encouraged to comment on each item without explicit memorization instructions. For each item, familiarity was assessed using a six-point scale tailored for AD patients, based on verbal and behavioral cues. Learning was considered reached when >75% of items have a familiarity score with 4 or above. A recognition session occurred 1-2 days later, presenting targets (previously seen) and distractors (new and not notoriously known). Participants rated familiarity again using the scale. For each participant, mean familiarity scores were averaged per condition. Exposure and recognition sessions were analyzed using non-parametric ANOVA-Type Statistics (ATS), with "Clinical Status» as a between-subjects factor and “Artistic material” and “Session” (for exposure session) or “Item Type” (for recognition session) as within-subject factors. Wilcoxon (within-group) or Dunn (between-group) post-hoc tests with Holm correction were used; effect sizes estimated via rank-biserial correlations.

To assess the degree of hippocampal atrophy in AD patients, high-resolution T1-weighted images were acquired at the Cyceron imaging platform (Caen, France), using a 3D fast-field echo sequence (Philips Achieva 3T, 1mm isotropic, sagittal; TR=20ms; TE=4ms; flip angle=10°; 180 slices; slice thickness=1 mm). Preprocessing was performed with fMRIPrep (v25.0.0), including normalization to OASIS30ANTs, tissue segmented and smoothing.  Whole-brain atrophy was assessed with one-way ANOVA analyses in SPM12, using a with cluster-level FWE correction (pFWE < .05, k > 60). Hippocampal atrophy was compared using Kruskall-Wallis test with Holm-corrected Dunn post-hoc tests. Brain-behavior correlations were explored using multiple regressions on test session familiarity scores, with significance set a punc < .001 and k > 60. Total intracranial volume, age and sex were included as covariates. 

Results

At the end of exposure sessions, only three AD patients failed to reach the learning criterion. Familiarity scores were significantly higher in the last session compared with the first across all clinical groups (ATS (1000), p < .001, r = .89), with a significant interaction Session × Clinical group (ATS (1.91) = 5.55, p < .01). Post-hoc comparisons showed that this interaction was driven by significantly lower familiarity scores in sAD only compared with HC (z = - 3.13, p < .01, r = .40). In the recognition session, no differences between clinical groups emerged for target familiarity. A significant interaction Item type × Clinical groups was detected (ATS (1.61) = 25.27; p < .001), indicating that the target-distractor contrast differed across clinical groups. Robust mains effects of Item type (Target > Distractor, ATS (1) = 563.81, p < .001, r = .87), and artistic material (Music > Painting, ATS (1) = 10.92, p < .001, r = .32) were also found.

Whole-brain analyses revealed marked medial temporal atrophy, including bilateral hippocampi and left amygdala. Individual hippocampal atrophy in AD patients ranged from 12.8% to 27.9%. Group comparisons showed a significant hippocampal density loss in both mAD (χ² = -5.89, p < .001, r = .85) and sAD (χ² = -4.04, p < .001, r = .83). Multiple regression analyses identified positive correlations between grey matter volume and familiarity scores (punc < .001, k > 60), in right hippocampus, right caudate nucleus and left thalamus (Figure 2). This correlation was identified exclusively for musical items, with no significant voxels detected for pictorial items.  

Conclusion

Key findings of this study are that AD patients reached familiarity scores comparable to HC, indicating that implicit encoding mechanisms remain accessible even when explicit memory is severely impaired. By minimizing analytical processing, our paradigm specifically targeted these preserved pathways and the familiarity scale proved sensitive and ecologically valid for capturing familiarity in amnesic populations. Although the sample size and exploratory nature of the correlations temper generalization, the data-driven stratification improved clinical interpretability 

Despite substantial medial temporal atrophy, the persistence of familiarity-based recognition suggests that the residual hippocampal integrity may still support contextual processing. Additional correlations in the caudate nucleus and the thalamus point to contributions from reward learning, sensory integration and implicit memory mechanisms, particularly engaged by emotionally and culturally salient stimuli such as music. 

The distinct variability profiles observed between musical and pictorial items likely reflect their reliance on partly dissociable systems : temporal-frontal circuits supporting musical memory,3 whereas ventral visual pathway supporting pictorial memory.4 The relative preservation of musical-memory regions in AD, coupled with strong lifelong enculturation, may account for the stronger and more consistent familiarity responses to musical stimuli. 

Overall, this study highlights that encoding of a new long-term memory trace and familiarity-based recognition can persist despite marked hippocampal degeneration and they provide structural evidence for the neural circuitry supporting these preserved abilities in Alzheimer’s disease. The complementary fMRI dataset acquired during the recognition session will allow us to characterize the functional dynamics of these networks, offering a critical next step toward identifying the neural mechanisms that sustain spared memory processes in Alzheimer’s disease.
