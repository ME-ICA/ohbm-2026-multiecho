Introduction

The benefits of proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (¹H‑MRS) at high B0 fields (≥ 3 tesla (T)) enable reliable in-vivo profiling of multiple metabolites. Yet, most studies still focus on single-compound measures, neglecting multivariate patterns of metabolic co-regulation. Here, we introduce a novel analytic framework that examines how distinct metabolites organize into a metabolite network architecture. Specifically, we characterize the patterns of co-variation among brain metabolites and uncover their coordinated relationships within a metabolite network. Complementary, we analyze hierarchical principles across all pairwise metabolite ratios and establish a metabolite ratio architecture blueprint, whose generalizability we evaluate across samples, scanning modalities, brain regions and species. From a neurobiological perspective, this offers an opportunity to understand brain metabolism as a coordinated system, revealing organisational principles that underlie the relationships between signaling brain metabolites, primarily involved in neurotransmission and synaptic activity, and non-signaling brain metabolites, associated with energy metabolism, structural integrity, and glial function. From a translational perspective, this framework offers potential to reveal disease-specific biosynthetic pathways, support biomarker discovery and inform the development of metabolically targeted therapeutic strategies. We apply our framework to evaluate inter-metabolite relationships under biological challenges and test whether specific inter-metabolite relationships show targeted reconfiguration under caloric restriction and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), thereby probing network-informed axes of neurometabolic adaptability.

Methods

We use a 7T reference dataset of 53 healthy adults measuring metabolite levels of nine compounds in the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) including glutamate (Glu), glutamine (Gln), gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), aspartate (Asp), N-acetylaspartate (NAA), myo-inositol (Ins), choline-containing compounds (Cho), glutathione (GSH), and lactate (Lac)1. To create the metabolic network structure, we calculated a non-regularized Gaussian Markov random field network of partial correlations among the nine metabolites with the bootnet package in R and calculated node strength, defined as the sum of absolute edge weights, reflecting overall connectivity, as well as expected influence, defined as the sum of signed edge weights, quantifying net positive or negative impact2. In addition, pairwise metabolite ratios were computed for all measured metabolites within each dataset, and the resulting inter-metabolite architecture was summarized by median ratio matrices. We express the distance between two inter-metabolite architectures of different datasets in units of median absolute deviation (MAD), contextualizing cross-cohort variability in relation to common inter-individual variability in the reference sample.  

Generalizability was examined using four independent MRS datasets: (i) a 7T validation cohort of 12 healthy participants with voxel locations in the PCC, midcingulate cortex (MCC), and insula; (ii) a 3T validation sample of 12 individuals with transient global amnesia, acquired in the hippocampus (iii) a 3T intervention dataset of 21 healthy controls and 33 patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) with a voxel located in the anterior cingulate cortex, where patients received either repetitive TMS (intermittent theta-burst over left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex) or treatment as usual (TAU); and (iv) a 9.4T rat hippocampal dataset from seven male Wistar rats3. All datasets were processed using contemporary MRS guidelines4.

Finally, metabolite ratios were compared between baseline and post-intervention sessions in the 7T validation cohort, which included longitudinal measurements alongside a fasting protocol (n=12; PCC acquisitions pre- and 24 h post-caloric restriction) and in the 3T MDD cohorts (ACC acquisitions pre- and post-TMS or TAU, plus repeated scans in controls) using Analysis of variance (ANOVA) implemented in Python. Given the modest sample sizes, we restricted our analysis to a comparative evaluation of effect sizes reported as proportion of explained variance (η²) rather than null-hypothesis significance testing.

Results

In the reference metabolic network, Glu, NAA, and GABA showed the highest node strength (1.74, 1.72, 1.68), indicating hub-like positions within the neurometabolic architecture, while Gln and Ins showed the most segregated profiles, with node strengths of 0.77 and 1.06. Glu exhibited the largest positive expected influence (1.30) and strong connections with NAA, Asp, and GABA (rp=0.34-0.51), whereas NAA combined high node strength (1.72) with both positive (to Glu, rp=0.51) and negative (to Asp, rp=-0.24) connections. Lac showed a large negative expected influence (-0.25), consistent with more antagonistic or mutually exclusive relationships, including the strongest negative connection between Lac and GABA (rp=−0.39). Bootstrapping confirmed that connections with medium or larger effect sizes (|rp|>0.3) were stable and retained sign and relative ranking across resamples.

Mapping median pairwise ratios in the reference sample revealed a metabolite ratio architecture (see Figure 1), which was remarkably conserved across datasets despite heterogeneity in field strength (3T, 7T, 9.4T), scanner hardware, regions (PCC, MCC, insula, hippocampus, ACC), and species (humans, rats). Within-scanner regional differences in ratio patterns were typically below one MAD, whereas cross-site 7T comparisons yielded mean deviations around twice the inter-individual variability (2.15 MAD), and cross-field (7T PCC vs 3T hippocampus) and human–rat comparisons approached 4 MAD, indicating larger but still structured shifts.

Longitudinal analyses revealed selective, intervention-specific modulation of metabolite ratios rather than global reorganization (see Figure 2). During caloric restriction at 7T, the largest effect sizes centered on Gln-related ratios, including Ins/Gln (η²=0.17), NAA/Gln (η²=0.14), and Glu/Gln (η²=0.13), while most other ratios showed small or negligible changes (η²≈0.01–0.05). In the 3T MDD dataset, TMS induced prominent changes in GSH-centered ratios, such as Gln/GSH (η²=0.18), Glu/GSH (η²=0.13), and GSH/NAA (η²=0.11), whereas TAU and healthy control cohorts displayed minimal effect sizes without coherent shifts with respect to a single metabolite. Notably, fasting-related ratio changes were aligned with strongly weighted edges in the baseline network, particularly around Gln and its first- and second-degree network neighbors (Ins, NAA, Cho, Asp), and TMS-related effects followed GSH-centered network connections, suggesting that intervention-sensitive axes overlap with network-privileged connections.

Conclusion

Our multivariate framework demonstrates that inter-metabolite relationships in the human brain follow a structured, partially conserved hierarchy across scanners, field strengths, brain regions, and species. The metabolic network analysis highlights Glu, GABA, and NAA as central hubs with distinct patterns of co-regulation and antagonism, while metabolites such as Gln, Ins, and Lac occupy more segregated positions, consistent with their roles in bioenergetics, and stress-related adaptation. The observed robustness of ratio hierarchies across acquisition modalities and biological contexts supports their reliability for generalizable systems-level analysis. Moreover, the selective modulation of specific Gln- and GSH-centered ratios during caloric restriction and TMS indicates that adaptive changes occur along network-informed axes, meaning that edges with higher inter-connectedness may serve as preferential routes for metabolic reconfiguration under physiological challenge. The observed alignment between network topology and intervention-sensitive ratios supports the potential of the proposed multivariate analytical framework as a robust foundation for biomarker identification and development of neuromodulation strategies in neurological and psychiatric disorders.
