Introduction

Structural brain abnormalities have been commonly observed in neuropsychiatric conditions. Both macrostructural geometry and microstructural white-matter integrity have been identified as candidate biomarkers for both neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders, but these imaging signatures are often confounded by medication, disease progression and do not necessarily reflect the developmental origin of disorders.1 The recent availability of large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) for psychiatric conditions and population-level combined imaging and genetic databases opened up novel opportunities to analyse the shared genetics between imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) and psychiatric disorders. Hence, the first aim of this study is to identify cortical structural IDPs that genetically overlap with neuropsychiatric disorders.

Recent advances in the mapping between GWAS, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics datasets2 allowed us to investigate the cellular mechanisms and biological pathways underlying cortical structure and psychiatric disorders. Both structural IDPs and neuropsychiatric disorders have been theorised to have developmental origins.3 In particular, abnormal gene regulation in cortical progenitor cells have been shown to lead to later neurodevelopmental disorders.4 Hence, the second aim of the study is to prioritise overlapping cell types and biological mechanisms between cortical structure and neuropsychiatric disorders, with particular focus on a possible shared developmental origin.

To achieve these aims, we leveraged publicly available imaging-genetics, psychiatric GWAS, multiomics and known gene-set annotation datasets to systematically investigate the shared genetic landscape between cortical structure and neuropsychiatric disorders at genome, cell-type, single-cell and biological pathway levels.

Methods

We obtained T1-, T2-FLAIR-weighted structural MRI and diffusion MRI images and genotype data from UK Biobank and ABCD. Structural MRI images were preprocessed with FreeSurfer (v6.0.1) following the standard recon-all pipeline. Diffusion-weighted images were obtained in preprocessed form. Neurite orientation dispersion and density indices (NODDI) parameters were estimated using Accelerated Microstructure Imaging via Convex Optimization (AMICO). All images were co-registered using FSL-FLIRT and parcellated using the bilaterally averaged Human Connectome Project atlas, resulting in 180 cortical regions. For each region and globally, we estimated 12 IDPs: surface area, volume, folding index, intrinsic curvature, mean curvature, Gaussian curvature, cortical thickness, neurite density, isotropic volume, orientation dispersion, mean diffusivity, and fractional anisotropy, totalling 2,172 phenotypes. 

We quality-controlled subjects for European ancestry, genetic sex agreeing with self-report and imaging quality (Euler index and framewise displacement < 5 median absolute divergence). Common variants (minor allele frequency > 0.001) that satisfy Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p > 0.000001) and have imputation r2 > 0.4 were included. Combining subject and variant quality control, 53,710 individuals and 17,336,304 variants from UK Biobank were included in genetic analyses (fig. 1). 4863 individuals from ABCD were included for polygenic score analyses.

GWAS was conducted using GCTA fastGWA-mlm for each IDP, details to be found in another abstract from our group. To analyse the shared genetics between cortical structure and neuropsychiatric conditions, we obtained GWAS summary statistics for depression (MDD), anxiety, ADHD, autism, bipolar disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) and schizophrenia from the Psychiatric Genetics Consortium. Polygenic scores for these disorders were estimated using PRS-cs. Genetic correlation and heritability were estimated using linkage-disequilibrium score regression (LDSC). Conditional correlation analyses and common factor GWAS were obtained using genomic structural equation modelling (GenomicSEM).

For significantly correlated IDPs and disorders, we clumped the GWAS summary statistics for independent loci using PLINK (v1.9) at a linkage disequilibrium (LD) r2=0.1 threshold and using a 1Mb window. Clumped loci were used to estimate the regional genetic covariance using LAVA, to identify colocalisation, i.e. shared causal variants in the locus using HyPrColoc, and to test for putative causal directions using Mendelian Randomisation. Gene-level effect sizes for significantly correlated phenotypes were estimated by aggregating variant-level summary statistics using MAGMA.

To investigate the underlying cell types of IDPs and disorders, we mapped GWAS and gene-level summary statistics to foetal single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the Human Cell Atlas5 using parallel methods: (1) scoring the differential expression of genes across cell types and clusters using Cepo, and correlating the specificity scores with gene-level effect sizes using MAGMA gene-covariate analysis; (2) scoring the transcriptomic profile of each cell for the top 1000 significant genes using scDRS, and aggregating cell-level scores to identify cell-type enrichments. We further tested the layer specificity of the genetic enrichment by mapping the GWAS to foetal spatial transcriptomic data using gsMap. To further identify the overlapping biological processes between IDPs and disorders, we conducted gene-ontology enrichment analysis for top genes correlated with the cell-level scDRS scores, interval-based enrichment (INRICH) for significantly colocalised loci as well as genomic regions with significant local correlation. Finally, we prioritised potential overlapping transcription factors by estimating the stratified genetic correlation in the downstream genes regulated by the transcription factors (eRegulons) using GNOVA. 

Results

Using in-house GWAS for 2,172 IDPs, we analysed their genetic correlation with psychiatric disorders (fig. 2A). We identified significant genome-wide overlap between volume, surface area, folding index, intrinsic curvature and ADHD, MDD and anxiety (rg = -0.09 to -0.21). This was confirmed using polygenic score analysis in both the UK Biobank and ABCD, suggesting that these findings are developmentally stable. Controlling for deprivation and adoption status identified consistent results, suggesting no confounding by gene-environment interaction. Finally, sensitivity analyses using different subtypes of ADHD/MDD gave consistent results.

Because the four IDPs are highly genetically correlated (rg > 0.4) and all related to developmental cortical expansion, we extracted the common genetic factor using genomic SEM where 105 loci were significant (fig. 2B). We confirmed that this cortical expansion factor was negatively correlated with all three disorders (rg = -0.12 to -0.21, fig. 2A). No additional overlap between regional IDPs and disorders was found (min p = 6.68e-4) after adjusting for the corresponding global IDP, so we focused on global IDPs and the cortical expansion factor for subsequent analyses. Mendelian randomisation confirmed that cortical expansion was causal to all three disorders and not vice versa (fig. 2C). Conditional analysis between disorders revealed that ADHD still correlated with cortical expansion adjusting for other disorders, but MDD and anxiety no longer correlated with cortical expansion adjusting for ADHD, suggesting that the correlation is likely driven by ADHD genetics. 

To identify the cell types underlying cortical expansion and ADHD, we mapped GWAS summary statistics with foetal scRNA-seq data in both directions. scDRS cell-level scoring showed that cortical expansion was enriched at earlier stages of foetal development compared to ADHD (fig. 2D). Aggregating single-cell scores to cell types (fig. 2E), we confirmed that cortical expansion is enriched in progenitor cell types (radial glia and intermediate progenitor cells, beta = 2.99 to 6.58, p < 0.005), whilst ADHD is enriched in differentiated excitatory neurons (beta = 1.80 to 3.01, p = 0.003 to 0.033). Parallel analysis using Cepo expression specificity scores showed similar enrichment of cortical expansion 

In progenitor cells (beta = 0.023 to 0.040, p < 2e-4), and ADHD in excitatory neurons (beta = 0.010 to 0.022, p = 0.003 to 0.035). Spatial enrichment analysis using gsMap (fig. 2F) further confirmed that cortical expansion was enriched in progenitor cells (Cauchy combination p = 0.027 to 5e-6), and ADHD in deep-layer excitatory neurons (Cauchy combination p = 4.4e-5 to 0.40). Overall, cell-level analysis suggested differentiation of cortical progenitors to neurons may underlie the negative correlation of cortical expansion with ADHD.

To investigate this hypothesis, we first correlated cell-level scDRS scores with pseudotime (a measure of cellular differentiation) to find completely opposite trajectory for cortical expansion and ADHD (fig. 2G). Gene ontology enrichment showed that cortical expansion was positively associated with the mitotic process (p = 8.97e-26), positive regulation of cell cycle (p = 6.89e-21), and negatively associated with synapto- and axonogenesis (p = 8.16e-32). Conversely, ADHD was positively associated with synapto- and axonogenesis (p=3.34e-32). To further identify the overlapping molecular mechanisms, we investigated the 9 genomic loci with negative local genetic correlation in LAVA, and 5 loci with significant colocalisation in HyPrColoc. INRICH analysis confirmed that these loci are enriched in both neuroblast proliferation (p = 2e-4) and regulation of neurogenesis (p = 0.003). Finally, we prioritised transcription factors underlying the overlap using GNOVA to estimate the genetic covariance in downstream regulons. The highest-covariance transcription factors included MEF2C, NFIA and TCF12 (p < 0.01, r < -0.004).

Conclusion

In this study, we conducted a systematic investigation into the shared genetic landscape between cortical structure and neuropsychiatric disorders at genome-wide, cell-type, single-cell and biological mechanism levels. 

At genome-wide level, genetic correlation and polygenic score regression analyses prioritised cortical expansion as a genetically overlapping predictor of neuropsychiatric disorders, whilst calling for caution in interpreting other imaging correlates of disorders. Conditional analysis between disorders further suggested that the biological overlap is likely driven by ADHD, a neurodevelopmental disorder. Hence, we hypothesised that later-life imaging signatures capture the individual variability in early-life cortical expansion, a process which predisposes individuals to psychiatric conditions later in life. Supporting this hypothesis, we identified a causal relationship from the cortical expansion genetic factor to psychiatric disorders but not in the opposite direction.

At single-cell and cell-type level, we combined parallel methods mapping GWAS summary statistics to scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomic datasets. The combined evidence led to the theory that a temporal switch regulating differentiation from neural progenitor cells to neurons likely underlies the overlap between cortical expansion and psychiatric disorders. Our further analyses at the biological pathway level supported this theory, where molecular processes up-regulating differentiation and down-regulating progenitor proliferation are associated positively with ADHD and negatively with cortical expansion. The top transcription factors we identified, MEF2C, NFIA and TCF12 are all known high-level regulators of cell fate determination in neuron progenitor cells. These genes are prioritised as strong candidates for the hypothesised molecular temporal switch.

Our findings on shared biology between cortical expansion and psychiatric disorders in foetal neural progenitor differentiation provides novel insights into Barker’s developmental origins of health and disease (DOHAD) model.3 Whilst some previous theorists considered prenatal chemical exposure and maternal stress as a pathway of familial risk inheritance separate to shared genes and postnatal parental care, our findings suggest that the prenatal developmental origin of disease is also partially genetic. Further studies on gene-environment interaction and possible “genetic nurture” effects in prenatal development will hopefully more clearly outline the developmental predisposition to psychiatric disorders but also somatic diseases.

Our analyses focused on individuals of predominantly European ancestry because of the limited availability of imaging data in non-European ancestries. We also knowledge the heterogeneity of MRI preprocessing and parcellation schemes, so we chose a commonly used approach with maximum spatial resolution to increase the compatibility of our results. Expanding the spectrum of phenotypes to subcortical structure and functional MRI phenotypes will provide a more precise account of the shared biology between brain structure, function and mental health.

In conclusion, by systematically characterising the genetic overlap between cortical structure and psychiatric disorders, we provide unprecedented insights into the developmental origins of psychiatric disorders, identifying neural progenitor differentiation as the key candidate process.
