Introduction

Early life maturation involves rapid and profound changes in functional brain connectivity organisation, which complicates their systematised summary and interpretation. A potential geometric formalism for summarising global relations in brain connectivity during maturation is to use Laplacian embedding of connectivity matrices, in which individual inter-regional connections are expressed as relative distances in a low-dimensional Euclidean space. However, the scope for the analyses of inter-individual differences is limited here because each individual connectome is embedded in its own space, preventing a direct comparison across individuals. A common solution to this, often utilised within the interpretational “gradient” framework (1), is to align individual embeddings to a common target space. Although this then allows relative positions of individual brain areas to be meaningfully analysed, the global inter-individual shift in the connectivity architecture (i.e., global re-orientation of the embedded connectome) is inherently lost for analyses. 

In contrast, the multi-view embedding approach (2) for consensus-based characterisation of data structures for clustering, enforces individual embeddings to reside within a shared space. Importantly, this enforcement is ambivalent to the global orientation of the individual embeddings, which can now become a legitimate target for analysis.

Here, we utilise single- and multi-view embeddings to investigate maturational transformations in the neonatal functional connectome. We address a critical theoretical question: “Does maturation of the brain functional connectivity represent a steady continuation of pre-natal development, or does the birth trigger maturational processes that extend beyond those occurring beforehand?” For multi-view embedding, we implement a novel framework for modelling global shifts in connectome orientation that is compliant with their intrinsic manifold geometry. 

Methods

To address the main study question, we contrasted models that contain birth age and postnatal age at scan as separate variables with the model that contains their sum (i.e., postmenstrual age at scan). If the former outperforms the latter, this will indicate that the postnatal adaptation trajectory deviates from the other  representing a steady continuation of pre-natal maturation.

The study utilises the open-access functional neonatal data collected and prepared for the connectivity analyses by the developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) (3). The sample was composed of the entire set of term-born neonates in the dHCP cohort (517 babies, postmenstrual age at birth > 37 weeks). The age at scan ranged from 37.4 to 44.9 postmenstrual weeks. Group-level maps of functional networks were acquired using 40-component group-ICA (4) from a partially overlapping sample of 311 neonates with mean postmenstrual age = 37.4 weeks (5). The weighted-average network timecourses were computed in native fMRI space using thresholded (Z > 3) and normalised network maps as seeds. Individual functional connectome matrices were formed using L2-regularised partial correlations, computed for the entire set of 40 maps. These were subsequently pruned to retain entries for 29 networks, with the remaining 11 maps discarded as showing signatures of a non-neuronal origin. The multi-view embeddings of the connectome were obtained as described in Kumar et al. (2). The contrast between single- and multi-view approaches is shown in Figure 1. 

The two-staged model comparison procedure utilised likelihood-ratio and Vuong tests, respectively, for nested models (postmenstrual-age-at-scan vs. birth-age and postnatal-age as separate variables) and for models with an equal number of predictors (e.g., age-at-scan vs. postnatal-age). At the first stage, the effects of sex and mode of delivery (natural birth vs. pre-labour Caesarean) were assessed with respect to the intercept model (Bonferroni-corrected as appropriate). The statistically best performing model was used as a baseline model for the second stage where explanatory power of birth age and postnatal age was assessed against that of their sum (i.e., age at scan). The birth-age or/and postnatal-age model was considered as best fitting if it outperformed both the baseline model (also Bonferroni-corrected) and the age-at-scan model.

The effect of maturational factors on the global shift in the connectome orientation was assessed by analysing the rotation of the individual embeddings to the consensus embedding. For this, rotations were converted to their quaternion representations and modelled using Frechet regression as geodesic points on a 4D hypersphere with radius = 1. Residuals were mean-centred by computing the rotation of the predicted value of the quaternion to the geodesic intercept and applying it to the corresponding observed quaternion. These were then projected to the mean’s tangent hyperplane, where Euclidian-metric likelihoods were computed for the multivariate Gaussian with mean=0 and empirically estimated covariance matrix. 

In addition to this, using the standard single-view approach, we analysed the local age-related changes in connectivity, characterised by relative maturation-related displacements of networks within the embedding space, using multivariate regression of network x, y, z coordinates, following their Procrustes transformation to multi-view consensus embedding.

Results

The consensus embedding of the neonatal functional connectome is shown in Figure 2A. Its 3D representation reveals a meaningful anatomical architecture, with 4 distinctive pivots in its structure: 1) primary sensorimotor (SM), 2) occipital (visual), 3) posterior peri-insular (auditory) and 4) parietal. The axes of embedding represent 3 primary “gradients” of functional segregation: 1) primary SM vs. visual cortices; 2) parietal vs. auditory cortices; 3) primary (primary SM+visual) vs. associative (prefrontal+parietal+temporal) cortices.

The model comparison of the global re-organisation shows that the combination of birth age and postnatal age accounted for the orientation of the connectome embeddings better than their sum alone (vs. intercept model, χ2 (6) = 26.22, Bonferroni-corrected p = .0008; vs. postemenstrual age-at-scan model: χ2 (3) = 7.95, p = .047). According to the qualitative assessment of the plots (Figure 2B), birth age and postnatal age produced distinctive patterns of connectome re-orientation.  Several features of this restructuring are: 1) for the “parietal <-> auditory” axis, the postnatal age enforces convergence of the secondary SM and auditory networks, whereas birth age enforces a convergence of auditory network with high associative networks (including parietal), but a greater divergence with primary SM networks. 2) A greater convergence between primary SM networks and higher associative (except parietal) was enforced by postnatal age along the “primary SM <-> visual” axis.  3) A divergence between auditory and parietal cortices along the “associative <-> visual & primary SM” axis was enforced by birth age, with parietal networks and auditory networks moving, respectively, towards the associative and perceptual ends of the axis; in the meantime, the postnatal age enforced a divergence between occipital and primary SM networks along the same axis.

Finally, the postmenstrual age at scan was the best predictor for the individual node (post-Procrustes) trajectory in 10 out of 14 cases where any model that contained one of the age-related factors outperformed the baseline model. The remaining 4 models contained both birth age and postnatal age as predictors. One feature of the local restructuring that was related to the postmenstrual age at scan and pertains to restructuring global organisation is that auditory networks diverged away towards the corresponding end along the “parietal <-> auditory” axis, in contrast to the opposite trend observed for the effect of birth age on the global re-orientation.

Conclusion

In this study we developed a novel approach to the analyses of functional architecture, that separates global and local patterns of its restructuring. When applied to the neonatal functional connectome, we showed that birth age and postnatal age as separate variables in a model together explain the global pattern better than postmenstrual age at scan alone. This implies that the former two contain additional information about the data with respect to their sum, age at scan alone, in line with the hypothesis that birth triggers functional re-organisation that is not just a continuation of the pre-natal maturational patterns. In contrast, the relative displacements of the individual nodes within the connectome were more often better accounted for by the age at scan alone, indicating differential sensitivity of local and global neonatal connectome re-organisation to maturational factors.
