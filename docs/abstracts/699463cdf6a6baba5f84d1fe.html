Introduction

Understanding how the brain organises natural sound categories remains a central challenge in auditory neuroscience. While distributed activity patterns in auditory cortex can decode categories, the global arrangement of these categories and how acoustic and semantic structures jointly shape their organisation is unclear. Prior approaches using representational similarity analysis reveal whether categories are distinguishable but not which representational axes organise the space. We addressed this by deriving low-dimensional functional gradients from high-depth fMRI data, capturing principal axes of population activity in auditory cortex. We then projected acoustic (gammatone filter-bank) and behavioural similarity spaces into this shared framework to test how the brain's representational axes align with low-level acoustic or higher-level semantic structure. Our approach provides a unified coordinate system where all categories can be directly compared, revealing how categories differ in their global organisational structure rather than local activation patterns.

Methods

Three participants completed 8 fMRI sessions (56 runs across 8 sessions, ~4.7 hours each) while performing a category-specific one-back task on 30 natural sound exemplars (10 categories, 3 exemplars each). We derived functional gradients using diffusion embedding applied to functional connectivity matrices and generated session-level beta maps for each exemplar. Random Forest classifiers were trained using 28-folds of cross-validation (6 sessions training, 2 testing) to classify sound categories from: (1) region-of-interest beta values, and (2) gradient-embedded beta maps. We compared three parcellation schemes: csurf auditory regions, Schaefer-1000 whole-cortex, and a frontal-auditory hybrid.

Acoustic structure was modelled using gammatone filter-bank features (3 PCs explaining 93.7% variance). Semantic structure was derived from behavioural similarity judgments (3 PCs explaining 78.8% variance). Both acoustic and behavioural PC spaces were projected into gradient space via linear regression with one-vs-rest feature importance (obtained from the aforementioned classifier) as targets. We compared category representational geometries across brain, acoustic, and behavioural spaces using representational dissimilarity matrices and examined category-pair-specific relationships.

Results

Gradient-based models of auditory cortex achieved significantly higher classification accuracy (56-71% across participants; Figure 1A, 1B) compared to ROI approaches when applied to auditory regions alone, but showed reduced performance for whole-brain parcellations. This auditory-specific enhancement demonstrates that category information is distributed across multiple continuous functional axes rather than discrete regions. Figure 1C shows the accuracy of the gradient approach at the category level. Feature importance analyses revealed no single gradient was sufficient for categorisation; instead, each category recruited distinct combinations of gradients (Figure 1D, E). Confusion matrices showed systematic category-pair confusions reflecting underlying representational similarities. Acoustic features (gammatone PCs) explained substantial variance in gradient feature importance (R²=0.38 average across participants; Figure 2A, B), while behavioural semantic judgments explained somewhat less but still meaningful variance (R²=0.31 average; Figure 2C, D). Critically, representational dissimilarity matrix comparisons revealed heterogeneous category-pair relationships: some pairs were organised primarily by spectro-temporal similarity, others by semantic distinctions, and many by combinations of both. Despite individual differences in gradient topography, the projected category geometry was remarkably consistent across participants, indicating a shared representational structure realised through idiosyncratic spatial patterns. Crucially, the spectral tonotopy map of each participant was explained sufficiently by the functional gradients (R²=0.99) but did not correspond to a single axis of functional organisation in the auditory cortex, but rather was explained by a combination of many.

Conclusion

Natural sound categorisation in auditory cortex emerges from distributed representations across continuous functional gradients that integrate bottom-up acoustic structure with top-down semantic information. Category structure cannot be reduced to any single organisational dimension but instead reflects flexible combinations of spectral and behavioural features that vary across category pairs. While acoustic structure exerts a somewhat stronger influence than semantic associations, both contribute substantially to cortical category geometry. This heterogeneous integration may enable the auditory system to adaptively prioritise different information types depending on context and task demands. Our gradient-based framework moves beyond localist accounts by revealing how perception and cognition are linked through the geometry of cortical organisation, providing a new approach for understanding natural sound processing in the human auditory system. The consistency of category geometry across participants despite variable gradient topographies suggests fundamental organisational principles underlying auditory categorisation. Additionally, that spectral tonotopy required multiple axes of functional organisation to be explained indicates that acoustic structure is not the primary means by which the auditory cortex is organised, consistent with recent claims.
