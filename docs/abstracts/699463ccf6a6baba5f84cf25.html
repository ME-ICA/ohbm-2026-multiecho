Introduction

How does the brain organize its activity over hundreds of hours of naturalistic TV watching? While latent-state models have revealed that brain activity partitions into discrete, recurring states during continuous perception1-4, it remains an open question whether the brain generates novel activity patterns for new narrative events or relies on a reusable "toolkit" of processing states. To address this, we analyzed fMRI data from a single participant who watched all six seasons of the television series Friends. We sought to determine the balance between brain states unique to a narrative episode and those that recur across the entire series, reflecting stable modes of processing.

Methods

We analyzed fMRI data from 286 distinct viewing runs (~11 min each; TR=1.49s), which comprised 139 unique television episodes, from the cneuromod dataset5. After parcellating brain activity into 156 regions, we fit a sticky hierarchical Dirichlet process hidden Markov model (sHDP-HMM) independently to each run. This approach is well-suited for capturing transient brain states without pre-specifying their number1,2. To ensure robustness, we ran 100 initializations per run and identified consensus state patterns that were reproducible across these initializations. A final clustering stage grouped these consensus patterns across all runs, yielding a single, series-wide set of brain states. We then quantified recurrence by measuring how many of the 139 episodes each state appeared in.

Results

This analysis yielded 41 distinct brain state clusters across the entire dataset. Strikingly, 36 of these 41 clusters (87.8%) were classified as recurring states, appearing across a wide variety of episodes. In stark contrast, zero clusters were episode-specific. The single most frequent state appeared in 118 of the 139 unique episodes (84.9% recurrence), while the top five recurring states were each present in over 94 different episodes. This high rate of recurrence was statistically robust: three states appeared significantly more often than expected by chance (p<0.05), whereas only one appeared significantly less often.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that brain dynamics during extended narrative viewing are dominated by a limited, reusable repertoire of processing states. Despite being inferred from short, independent viewing runs, these same states reliably recurred across distinct narrative episodes, extending prior observations of stable brain state dynamics during movie viewing and continuous cognition2-4. The absence of episode-specific states suggests that comprehension does not depend on generating novel neural configurations for each new context. Instead, the brain appears to flexibly deploy a set of recurring states. While these states can be considered "context-invariant" with respect to any single episode, they likely reflect the engagement of domain-general processes (e.g., attention, memory encoding) or the repeated appearance of common narrative themes, a question for future research. This hierarchical organization points toward a fundamental principle of neural efficiency during real-world experience.
