Introduction

Brain age refers to the predicted age of an individual based on a model using brain-related data. Brain Age Gap (BAG) is the measured difference between a model’s brain age prediction and the chronological age of the individual,1 and it has been recently found to be associated with different pathologies, including cardiometabolic and neurodegenerative disorders.1,2 In recent years, deep learning (DL) methods have emerged as prominent tools for studying brain structure through Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) since structural MRI provides good inter-tissue contrast, enabling brain morphometry studies including those aiming to predict brain age.1 Prior studies have mainly focused on predicting global BAG. Despite its strong associations with different anatomy-modifying pathologies, this approach lacks spatial specificity. Given their capabilities of learning biologically-informed image features, DL models could be used for generating voxel-wise BAG maps from structural brain MRI data. As a consequence, we propose VARAN (Voxel-wise Accurate Regression Aging Network), a DL-based framework that uses Convolutional Neural Networks based on a UNet3 architecture to model voxel-wise BAG from structural T1-weighted (T1w) MRIs. This approach enables us to generate individual and population average regional BAG maps, which in turn allow us to study phenotypic aging patterns for diseased and control populations.

Methods

Data: We used a cross‑sectional subset of T1w MRIs from the UK-Biobank dataset. Samples from N=24,301 individuals were used for model development in a 5-fold age- and sex-stratified cross-validation scheme: in each fold, 19,440 subjects (≈68%) were used for training, and 4,861 (≈17%) for validation. For the final evaluation, 4,196 held-out subjects with complete risk factors (e.g. diabetes, hypertension) and ICD10 data were retained, preserving the cohort’s age/sex distribution. All images were denoised, bias-field-corrected, skull-stripped, and linearly registered to the ICBM template (2009b) using PELICAN,4 and intensity normalized in the range [-1,1]. The inputs were reshaped from 193×229×193 voxels to 192×224×192 to keep the dimensionality of the images within the network’s architecture.

VARAN Model: We used a 3D UNet following the architecture used by SynthSeg.3  To obtain the final predictions, we replaced SynthSeg’s Softmax layer by final 1×1×1 convolution to one channel, passed through sigmoid and scaled to 0–100 years. Deep supervision was integrated into the network on bottleneck using a Multi-Layer Perceptron that produced a single scalar age. The optimizer was SGD (LR=0.005, weight decay=1e‑4) and the networks were trained for 70 epochs using PyTorch’s MultiStepLR with milestones at epochs 20, 40, and 60 and gamma 0.3. We used L1 as loss function defined as L=L1Vox+ 0.2L1Bottleneck, where L1Vox was the voxel-wise L1 distance between the predicted age and target age, and L1Bottleneck was the L1 distance between the predicted age at the bottleneck’s MLP output and the target age. 

Using the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss per fold, we generated 5 BAG maps for each of the 4,196 individuals in our independent test set. We then applied a bias correction step to correct the regression towards the mean following the work from Rajabli1 using each fold’s validation set. Finally, we computed the average of the 5 maps per subject, which we then warped non-linearly to the ICBM template by applying the deformation fields produced by PELICAN4 using the T1w images that were linearly registered to the stereotaxic space. This final step generated a voxelwise per-individual BAG map normalized in the standard space which was used for subsequent steps (see Fig. 1)

Disease-specific ROI BAG maps: To characterize disease-specific regional BAG values, we used the Allen Institute atlas5 in the ICBM space to compute mean BAG per 141 regions-of-interest (ROIs). We used linear regression models (BAGROI ~ Disease + sex) to generate disease specific regional BAG maps followed by FDR correction. Furthermore, we used the model BAGROI ~ sex for the healthy controls to compute disease-specific and subject-level residual maps. The top 10-most-frequent diseases (based on ICD10) from our independent test set were included: essential primary hypertension (I10, N=978), disorders of lipoprotein metabolism (E78, N=731), depressive episode (F32, N=406), migraine (G43, N=281), other anxiety disorders (F41, N=231), other hypothyroidism (E03, N=219), haemorrhoids (I84, N=197), mental and behavioural disorders due to the use of tobacco (F17, N=180), obesity (E66, N=157), varicose veins of lower extremities (I83, N=150). Additionally, we examined PD (G20, N=14) and stroke (I64, N=42) as pathologies of interest. 

SFCN-reg model: To make a fair comparison of VARAN versus a state-of-the-art model, we also trained an SFCN-reg model following the work from Rajabli and colleagues,1 using the same training regime as VARAN (5-fold CV using same folds, same inputs), finalized with a bias-correction step.

Results

On the independent test set (N=4,196), the ensemble VARAN and SFCN-reg models achieved Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 2.30 and 2.46 years, respectively, before bias correction (ΔMAE=0.159 years, paired t=8.08, pFDR=8.1×10⁻¹⁶), and MAE=2.04 and 2.39 years after bias correction (ΔMAE=0.344 years, t=16.92, pFDR=3.4×10⁻⁶²). 9 of the 12 tested ICD‑10 phenotypes produced at least one ROI surviving FDR correction (pFDR≤0.05; 141 ROIs total; Fig. 2A): essential hypertension (140 ROIs; mean β=1.12 years), lipidaemias (140; mean β=0.91 years), mental/behavioural disorders due to tobacco (140; mean β=0.93 years), Parkinson’s disease G20 (139; mean β=2.43 years), stroke I64 (135; mean β=1.43 years), obesity (134; mean β=0.70 years), haemorrhoids (129; mean β=0.59 years), hypothyroidism (124; mean β=0.57 years), and varicose veins (3; mean β=0.22 years); depressive episode, anxiety disorders, and migraine showed no FDR‑significant ROIs. Fig. 2B shows representative residualized ROI BAG maps for No_DX, MCI, AD, PD and stroke participants, where the AD/MCI examples show relatively elevated BAG in limbic regions. In the PD map, olfactory‑system regions show strong effects (anterior olfactory nucleus β=2.79 years, qFDR=6.5×10⁻⁴; lateral olfactory gyrus β=2.78 years, pFDR=6.5×10⁻⁴; piriform cortex β=2.68 years, pFDR=6.5×10⁻⁴; subcallosal gyrus β=2.60 years, pFDR=6.5×10⁻⁴; Fig. 2C). Consistent with the findings of Zeighami and colleagues,2 frontal/orbitofrontal regions are among the highest obesity betas (anterior intermediate orbital gyrus β=0.98 years, pFDR=0.0022; frontal pole β=0.90 years, pFDR=0.0023; lateral orbital gyrus β=0.87 years, pFDR=0.0022; frontomarginal gyrus β=0.87 years, pFDR=0.0022).

Conclusion

VARAN showed competitive results for brain age prediction, on par with or outperforming recent literature.1 Furthermore, our proposed methodology shows a promising pathway towards studying local patterns of brain anatomy changes related to different underlying conditions using single-modality cross-sectional MRI data. The generated maps show significantly higher regional brain age in diseases with known related effects in brain morphometry, which were mainly metabolic- and vascular-related disorders2. Finally, the PD maps showed increased BAG in regions widely reported in the literature to be affected in PD such as the olfactory system and brainstem including substantia nigra, indicating biology-relevant BAG maps generated by VARAN.
