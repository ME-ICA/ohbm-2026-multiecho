Introduction

Normal aging is accompanied by significant declines in higher cognitive functions.1 However, the neurocognitive mechanisms underlying age-related changes in higher cognition are still insufficiently understood. Investigating the neural correlates of specific processes that support higher cognition offers a promising way to better characterize these changes. Relational integration (RI), the ability to jointly consider multiple relations or to integrate single relations into higher-order relations, is thought to play a foundational role for higher cognition.2 At a neural level, left rostrolateral prefrontal cortex (RLPFC) is the core region for RI as it is selectively activated by demands to integrate multiple relations, but not by processing of single relations.3 Given that RI is central to higher cognition and relies on prefrontal brain regions and given that age effects are especially pronounced in higher cognition and prefrontal brain regions are particularly susceptible to age-related degeneration4, RI can be expected to play a critical role in neurocognitive aging. Identifying the neural correlates of RI in older age can thus contribute to a better understanding of the nature of age deficits in higher cognition.

Using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), the present study investigated brain activity during a task that required processing of single relations (control condition) and integrating two relations into a higher-order relation (RI condition) in younger and older healthy adults. Specifically, we investigated whether older adults show different activation patterns during the two conditions than young adults, that is, expecting an interaction effect of age group by condition for activation in task regions, particularly in RLPFC. 

Methods

Younger (25-30 years) and older (70-75 years) healthy participants were recruited via postal mailings based on address lists by the communal registry office. The study comprised a behavioral testing and an MRI session. During the first session, participants completed a behavioral version of the Relational Matching Task (RMT; see Fig. 1) to measure RI performance. Only participants with at least 60% accuracy in each condition were invited to the MRI session. At the beginning of the MRI session, participants completed a shortened behavioral task version to refresh task instructions and then entered the scanner to perform the fMRI RMT version.

The RMT is an established behavioral and fMRI task to measure RI performance.4 In this task, participants see two rows with two elements each, varying in shape and pattern (Fig. 1). In the control condition, participants determine for each row separately if elements match along the instructed dimension (shape or pattern), requiring two separate first-order relational judgements, but no RI. In the RI condition, participants determine whether the two pairs match along the same dimension, requiring integration of the two first-order relations into one second-order relation. Importantly, the conditions only differ in the instructions; visual input is completely identical. 96 stimuli arrays were presented once with RI instructions and once with control instructions. Trial duration was self-paced (max. of 10 sec). The task consisted of two runs, each containing 8 task blocks (4 first-order and 4 second-order task blocks), with a rest block inserted after every two task blocks. Responses were given using a response box, with button assignment of yes/no responses counterbalanced between participants. Data were acquired using a 64ch head-coil on a 3T Siemens PRISMA MRI scanner using a T2*-weighted multi-band echo-planar imaging sequence. For spatial normalization of fMRI data, a high-resolution 3D structural image was acquired using an MPRAGE (magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo) sequence. Data from participants with an accuracy below 70% in any of the conditions, excessive movement during scanning, or incidental findings were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 93 participants (46 young; 47 old).

First, differences in behavioral RI performance during the fMRI task between the younger and the older groups were analyzed using a repeated-measures ANOVA with condition and age group as factors and RMT accuracy as dependent variable. For the fMRI data, we conducted a whole-brain analysis to identify all regions where activity for the two task conditions differs between age groups with the first level contrasts RI vs. rest and control vs. rest (i.e., second-order task blocks vs. rest blocks and first-order task blocks vs. rest blocks, respectively). At second level, a flexible factorial model modelled the interaction between age group (young vs. old) and condition ([RI vs. rest] vs. [control vs. rest]) at a voxel level threshold of p < .05 (family-wise error corrected for multiple comparisons; cluster extent threshold of k=81 voxels), with t-contrasts modelling the difference in condition effects between the two age groups. In a second step, we assessed whether age group differences in RI-related brain activity might be influenced by performance differences by conducting a region-of-interest (ROI) analysis. The significant clusters from the whole-brain analysis served as ROIs, with contrast estimates for the RI vs. rest and control vs. rest first-level contrasts extracted for each ROI (mean-aggregated over all voxels) and each participant. We divided participants into low and high performers (median split of overall accuracy). Next, we calculated repeated-measures ANOVAs on the extracted contrast estimates for each ROI with condition x age group x performance group as factors.

Results

At the behavioral level, a main effect of group, F(1,91) = 49.557, p < .001, η²p = .353, and a main effect of condition, F(1, 91) = 53.175, p < .001, η²p = .369 was revealed. The group by condition interaction was also significant, F(1, 91) = 32.000, p < .001, η²p = .260. The difference in accuracy between the younger and the older group was greater in the RI condition (8.8 %) than in the control condition (1.9 %). This shows that the older group was more strongly affected by RI demands than the younger group. At the neural level, as expected, a significant age group x condition interaction was detected in 11 regions: left and right RLPFC; left and right inferior frontal sulcus (IFS); left posterior inferior frontal sulcus (postIFS); right superior frontal sulcus (SFS); bilateral anterior cingulate cortex (ACC); left and right insula; left and right precuneus/inferior parietal lobule. Across these 11 regions, two patterns of activation differences were identified: In left and right precuneus, left and right IFS, right SFS, ACC, and left postIFS, there was a significantly higher RI activation in the young group than in the older group (p < .05). In contrast, in left and right RLPFC and left and right insula, the interaction stemmed from a significant difference between the two age groups in activation in the control condition (p < .05), with the older group showing significantly higher activation in the control condition than the younger group. Within this second pattern, left RLPFC was the only region where the younger group showed no activation in the control condition but significant activation in the RI condition (Fig. 2). By contrast, activation in the control condition in young participants for right RLPFC and bilateral insula was lower than for older participants, but already significantly different from zero. That is, left RLPFC was marked as the core RI region in being selectively activated by RI demands in young participants. In the older group, this functional specificity was not present, as the left RLPFC was already activated in the control condition and showed only a slight additional increase in the RI condition.

In order to check whether these differences in RI-related activation between age groups are influenced by differences in RI performance, we conducted ROI analyses. We calculated a repeated measures ANOVA for each ROI with the contrast estimate as dependent variable and condition, age group and performance group as independent variables. For right insula and ACC, a significant threefold (condition x age group x performance group) interaction was present (p < .05), and a statistical trend was observed for left RLPFC (p = .053). Importantly, the condition x age group interaction remained significant for all three ROIs (p < .05). Sidak-corrected pairwise post-hoc comparisons revealed that performance level influenced activity only in the older group. Specifically, in the older high-performance group, activity in ACC, right insula, and left RLPFC differed significantly between the control and the RI conditions (p < .001) similar to the pattern observed in the younger group (both low and high performers). In contrast, in the older low-performance group, the difference in activity between the control and RI conditions was not significant for ACC (p = .179) and right insula (p = .06). In summary, in these three ROIs, performance levels appeared to exert additional effects in that older high performers showed an activation pattern more similar to that of young participants. However, the previously identified patterns remain: either the younger group showed stronger activation in the RI condition than the older group, or the older group showed a stronger activation in the control condition.

Conclusion

As expected, we identified regions where RI-related activity differed between age groups, thus revealing an interaction between task conditions and age groups. These regions included areas that have previously also been associated with RI, as well as regions involved in working memory, error monitoring and salience processing. Two distinct patterns of activity differences emerged. First, in ACC, parietal regions, and prefrontal regions posterior to the RLPFC, the interaction effect was driven by significantly higher RI activity in the younger group than in the older group. Older adults were unable to sufficiently activate these regions during the RI condition. Second, in bilateral RLPFC and bilateral insula, the interaction effect stemmed from significantly higher control activation in the older group. According to neurocognitive aging models, older adults overrecruit task areas at low task demand (here, the control condition) to compensate for structural decline; at high task demand, compensatory capacity is exceeded, limiting further up-regularization and resulting in equal or reduced activity compared to younger adults.5 This aligns particularly with the pattern of activation differences observed for the RLPFC and insula. Across all regions showing significant differences in RI-related brain activity between age groups, activation differences between the control and RI condition were reduced in the older group. That is, older adults’ brain activity in task regions seems to be less responsive to differences in task demands, irrespective of whether this stems from an over-recruitment at low task demand or an under-recruitment at high task demands. Importantly, in the younger group, left RLPFC was the only region that was truly selectively activated by RI demands in showing no activation in the control condition, but significant activation in the RI condition. In contrast, older adults exhibited RLPFC activation in the control condition, indicating diminished functional specificity. This finding is consistent with evidence marking the left RLPFC as the core region for RI (for an overview, see reference 2) and with previous developmental work4: In younger children, left RLPFC activity did not differ between items that required processing of a single relation (first-order order items) and items requiring integration of two relations into a higher-order relation (second-order order items). With increasing age, RLPFC activity for first-order items diminished, resulting in increasing specialization of left RLPFC for RI and improved performance in adolescence. In line with this pattern, diminished functional specificity in task areas, particularly RLPFC, in older adults may account for age-related deficits in RI performance and possibly also other higher cognitive functions. In subsequent analyses for this sample, we will incorporate data on structural measures (gray matter volume, structural connectivity) and behavioral performance in models predicting brain activity for control and RI conditions so as to further elucidate the age-specific interplay between brain function, behavioral performance, and brain structure.

Given the global trend of an increasingly aging population, a comprehensive characterization of the cognitive and neural changes associated with healthy aging is of high societal importance. Here, we could show that relational integration – a hallmark feature of higher cognition – is differentially affected in older versus younger age both on a behavioral and a neural level. Understanding the neurocognitive mechanisms underlying age-related deficits in RI can thus provide insight into the widely recognized age effects observed in higher cognitive functions.
