Introduction

Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory (RST) constitutes one of the most influential neuropsychological frameworks for explaining individual differences in personality, motivation and behavior. It is traditionally organised around three core systems: the Behavioral Approach System (BAS), the Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS), and the Fight-Flight-Freeze System (FFFS), which account for approach behavior, conflict processing, and defensive responding, respectively.^1 These systems have been linked to partially dissociable neural substrates, including dopaminergic fronto-striatal circuits supporting reward sensitivity (BAS), septo-hippocampal and prefrontal networks involved in conflict monitoring and anxiety (BIS), and subcortical and brainstem structures mediating acute threat responses (FFFS). Supported by new empirical evidence, recent theoretical extensions have proposed a fourth system, the Constraint, a cortical system related to voluntary control.^2 

However, despite its explicit neurobiological grounding, RST neural architecture has primarily been examined using heterogeneous analytical strategies and isolated region-based approaches, yielding inconsistent and sometimes conflicting findings across studies. Evaluating such an expanded, systems-level theory requires analytical approaches capable of modeling latent neurobiological constructs rather than individual brain regions. Recent advances in latent-variable modeling applied to structural MRI provide a principled framework for capturing distributed covariance patterns among brain regions.^3

Here, we present the best-fitting latent neurobiological model of RST derived from structural MRI and examine its theoretical and empirical implications. Additionally, we further test whether this latent architecture is invariant across sexes, a critical requirement for valid neurobiological comparisons given well-documented sex differences in brain morphology.

Methods

Structural T1-weighted MRI data were obtained from 300 healthy adults (18–49 years; 172 males, 128 females). Gray-matter volumes were extracted from 21 a priori RST-related regions of interest using the Neuromorphometrics atlas and processed with CAT12/SPM12. Homotopic regions were parceled to reduce redundancy associated with high interhemispheric correlations. Based on prior model comparison, a four-factor Exploratory Structural Equation Model (ESEM)^4 was specified to represent latent RST systems, including subcortical BAS and BIS/FFFS factors and a cortical Constraint system differentiated into Constraint Dorsal Stream (CDS) and Constraint Ventral Stream (CVS) factors. Models were estimated in parallel with and without correction for total intracranial volume (TIV) using the power-proportion method.

Sex-based measurement invariance was evaluated within a multigroup ESEM framework following a hierarchical sequence of increasingly restrictive models (configural, metric, scalar, and residual invariance).^5 Following the most updated procedures, model comparisons were evaluated using changes in comparative fit indices (CFI and TLI), complemented by RMSEA, to assess equivalence of factor structure, loadings, intercepts, and residuals across sexes.

Results

The four-factor Exploratory Structural Equation Model (ESEM) provided a well-fitting and theoretically coherent representation of RST-related neural organization (non–TIV χ²(125)=289.2, χ²/df=2.3, SRMR=0.018, RMSEA=0.066, CFI=0.977, TLI=0.961; TIV χ²(124)=302.2, χ²/df=2.4, SRMR=0.029, RMSEA=0.069, CFI=0.952, TLI=0.919). In addition to the canonical subcortical Behavioral Approach System (BAS) and Behavioral Inhibition/Fight–Flight–Freeze System (BIS/FFFS), the model identified a cortical Constraint system that was separable into CDS and CVS. This organization supports a distributed architecture in which motivational and defensive subcortical systems are embedded within and regulated by higher-order cortical control networks.

The dorsal Constraint factor was primarily associated with fronto-parietal and medial cortical regions implicated in cognitive control, goal regulation, and executive functioning, whereas the ventral Constraint factor encompassed ventrolateral and ventromedial regions linked to affective valuation and contextual modulation.^4 This dissociation suggests functionally differentiated roles for CDS and CVS systems in constraining approach- and avoidance-related processes.

Sex-based measurement invariance analyses in both non–TIV-corrected (RST-NTIV-1) and TIV-corrected (RST-TIV-1) datasets demonstrated robust configural (RST-NTIV-1; RMSEA = 0.07; CFI = 0.97; TLI = 0.95; SRMR = 0.03/ RST-TIV-1; RMSEA = 0.07; CFI = 0.95; TLI = 0.91; SRMR = 0.04)) and metric invariance (RST-NTIV-2; ∆χ² = 87.02; ∆df = 68; ∆RMSEA = -0.005; ∆CFI = -0.003/ RST-ICV-2; ∆χ² = 54.32; ∆df = 68; ∆RMSEA = -0.011; ∆CFI = 0.003) across sexes, indicating that the latent RST systems share the same dimensional structure and factor loadings in males and females. Scalar (RST-NTIV-3; ∆χ² = 35.93; ∆df = 17; ∆RMSEA = 0.001; ∆CFI = -0.003/ RST-TIV-3; χ² = 505.09; df = 333; RMSEA = 0.059; CFI = 0.954; TLI = 0.942; SRMR = 0.077) and residual invariance (RST-NTIV: χ² males = 246, χ² females = 198; RST-TIV: χ² males = 226, χ² females = 198) were largely supported when TIV correction was applied, whereas models without TIV correction exhibited greater strain at higher levels of invariance. Importantly, despite well-documented univariate sex differences in regional brain volumes, the latent organization of RST systems remained stable across sexes. Conclusion

These findings provide convergent evidence for a four-system neurobiological architecture of RST that extends beyond the classical BAS and BIS/FFFS distinction to include the Constraint system, revealing a higher-order cortical system that can be differentiated into dorsal (CDS) and ventral (CVS) systems.^4

The evidence of sex-based measurement invariance supports the robustness and generalizability of this architecture and indicates that apparent sex differences in RST-related brain regions are more likely attributable to global anatomical scaling than to fundamental differences in the organization of motivational and defensive systems.

By integrating latent-variable modeling with multigroup invariance testing, this work advances RST from a region-based to a systems-level neurobiological theory and underscores the relevance of establishing measurement equivalence when linking personality theories to brain structure.
