Introduction

Music is widely experienced as pleasurable and rewarding, with highly evocative pieces capable of eliciting intense affective responses such as musical “chills.” Despite growing evidence implicating dopaminergic signaling in musical reward, the dynamic neurometabolic mechanisms underlying these experiences remain poorly understood. Prior multimodal work combining fMRI and PET has suggested dopamine release in the striatum during chills, with dissociable roles of the dorsal and ventral striatum in reward anticipation and consumption, respectively1. Here, we present the first study to apply non-invasive functional magnetic resonance spectroscopy (fMRS) to characterize the spatiotemporal neurometabolic signatures associated with chilling versus neutral music experiences.

Methods

Twenty-four music-loving participants listened to self-selected highly pleasurable music (pleasurable block) and to music selected by other participants (control block). Participants reported subjective chill experiences via button press (Figure 1). Dynamic glutamate fluctuations were measured every 38 seconds using a multivoxel Glu-CEST sequence (voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3 mm³) targeting regions implicated in musical reward processing. In parallel, a single-voxel MEGA-PRESS sequence (voxel size = 30 × 30 × 30 mm³) was used to quantify the excitatory–inhibitory (E/I) balance between Glx and GABA in the right nucleus accumbens (NAcc). Acquisition time was reduced to 3 minutes per song using MegaNet (compared to 9 minutes using conventional approaches). Physiological measures, including heart rate and respiration, were recorded throughout the experiment.

Results

Participants showed significantly increased heart rate and respiration during the pleasurable block compared with the control block (Figure 2A), as well as during chill periods relative to non-chill periods (Figure 2B). MEGA-PRESS analyses revealed a significant increase in GABA/Cr concentration in the right NAcc during chilling music, with no corresponding change in Glx/Cr levels (Figure 2C). Notably, GABA/Cr exhibited an inverted U-shaped relationship with individual music reward sensitivity (Figure 2D). Under pleasurable music conditions, GABA/Cr and Glx/Cr concentrations were significantly correlated, indicating coordinated metabolic modulation rather than reciprocal trade-off (Figure 2E). Glu-CEST analyses further demonstrated increased glutamate levels in core musical reward regions—including bilateral NAcc, bilateral caudate nucleus, and right superior temporal gyrus (rSTG)—during chilling music (Figure 2F). Glutamate concentrations were positively correlated across these regions, particularly between the right NAcc and rSTG (Figure 2G).

Conclusion

These findings provide novel evidence for dynamic, coordinated neurometabolic fluctuations underlying musical pleasure. Contrary to traditional models positing a trade-off between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission, our results support a balanced and tightly coupled excitatory–inhibitory mode during intense musical reward. Moreover, this study demonstrates the utility of non-invasive fMRS techniques—particularly Glu-CEST—for capturing the temporal dynamics of neurometabolic processes during complex affective experiences, opening new avenues for research on reward and emotion in the human brain.
