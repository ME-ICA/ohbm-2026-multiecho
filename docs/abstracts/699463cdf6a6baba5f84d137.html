Introduction

Antipsychotic treatment response in schizophrenia shows substantial inter-individual variability, for which large-scale brain functional circuits are considered a critical neural substrate. Previous studies have demonstrated that resting-state functional connectivity can be leveraged to predict treatment response, most commonly through machine learning approaches that estimate the contribution of individual connections.^1 However, the high dimensionality of connectivity features and the strong interdependence among brain regions limit the ability of these approaches to robustly identify core regions and their distributed circuits, thereby constraining the interpretability of treatment-related neural mechanisms.^2，3

To address this limitation, the present study applied multivariate distance matrix regression (MDMR) to a single-center longitudinal cohort of patients with schizophrenia (n = 37), enabling the identification of core brain regions associated with treatment response, followed by the characterization of treatment-related functional connectivity profiles. The results revealed that the cerebellar Control-B region was significantly associated with changes in treatment response (PFDR = 0.04). Functional connectivity between this cerebellar region and widespread cortical areas was negatively correlated with PANSS reduction (PFDR < 0.05), with the involved regions primarily distributed across the frontoparietal control network, default mode network, attention networks, and the sensorimotor network. Together, these findings provide new evidence for distributed neural circuit mechanisms underlying variability in antipsychotic treatment response.

Methods

Participants

This study included longitudinal data from 37 patients with schizophrenia recruited from Peking University Sixth Hospital (PKU6). All patients received 6 weeks of antipsychotic treatment and underwent resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) and clinical assessments both before treatment (baseline) and after treatment. Only baseline rs-fMRI data were used for the present analyses (Fig. 1A). Follow-up clinical assessments were completed approximately six weeks after baseline (Fig. 1B), with a mean interval of 45.5 ± 4.8 days between assessments. Clinical symptoms were evaluated using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). Treatment response was quantified as the reduction in PANSS total score from baseline to follow-up, which served as the primary outcome measure.

Data Preprocessing

Resting-state fMRI preprocessing included slice timing correction, within-subject realignment of echo-planar imaging (EPI) volumes, rigid-body coregistration of the T1-weighted structural image to the mean EPI image, and spatial normalization of EPI images to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space using the T1 image. After normalization, EPI images were resampled to a voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm³, followed by nuisance signal removal. All preprocessing procedures were implemented using the MATLAB-based open-source toolbox BRANT (Brainnetome fMRI Toolkit) (http://brant.brainnetome.org/en/latest).^4. Data quality was ensured through rigorous screening procedures, including the exclusion of scans with excessive head motion (translation > 3 mm or rotation > 3°), visible artifacts, or poor coregistration and normalization quality.

Functional Connectivity Calculation

Following preprocessing, the brain was parcellated using a composite atlas consisting of the Schaefer 200 cortical atlas, Tian 54 subcortical atlas, and Buckner 17 cerebellar atlas, yielding a total of 271 regions of interest (ROIs). For each participant, mean time series were extracted from each ROI, and pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between all ROI pairs to generate individual-level functional connectivity matrices.

Multivariate Distance Matrix Regression (MDMR)

To identify brain regions whose whole-brain functional connectivity patterns were associated with treatment response, multivariate distance matrix regression (MDMR)^5 was applied. For each ROI, its functional connectivity profile with all other ROIs was treated as the input, and the treatment response measure was included as the explanatory variable. This approach enabled the assessment of whether the overall connectivity distribution of a given brain region was significantly associated with treatment response at the regional level. Only ROIs that remained significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction were retained as core regions for subsequent analyses.^6

Seed-Based Functional Connectivity Analysis and Statistical Testing

For each identified core region, seed-based functional connectivity analyses were conducted to examine its connections with all other brain regions. At the group level, functional connectivity strengths were correlated with PANSS reduction across participants. Permutation testing was used for statistical inference, and FDR correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons. Only functional connections that survived FDR correction were included in the final results and subsequent interpretation.

Results

Using a multivariate distance matrix regression (MDMR) framework, we systematically examined the association between whole-brain functional connectivity patterns and antipsychotic treatment response. At the uncorrected threshold (Ppermu < 0.05), 22 brain regions showed significant associations with treatment response. After correction for multiple comparisons, only 7Networks_RH_SomMot_3 (ROI 118) and the cerebellar Control-B region (ROI 266) remained significant (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 1C). Subsequent seed-based connectivity analyses revealed marked differences in the stability of connectivity-level findings between these two regions. Although ROI 118 exhibited 77 functional connections associated with treatment response at the uncorrected level, none survived FDR correction. In contrast, the cerebellar Control-B region showed 93 significant connections at the uncorrected level, of which 49 connections remained significant after FDR correction (q < 0.05), indicating a substantially more robust connectivity profile (Fig. 1D).

In terms of network distribution, functional connectivity between the cerebellar Control-B region and widespread cortical areas—primarily spanning the frontoparietal control network, default mode network, attention networks, and the sensorimotor network—was negatively correlated with PANSS reduction (PFDR < 0.05). This pattern highlights the central role of the cerebellar Control-B region within treatment-response–related distributed functional networks. Notably, only a single connection deviated from this overall trend, showing a positive association with treatment response between the cerebellar Control-B region and the PFCl area of the right frontoparietal control network, suggesting heterogeneous regulatory patterns across control-related nodes. Furthermore, among all significant connections, only one subcortical region—the left ventral posterior lateral nucleus of the thalamus (THA-VPl-lh)—was identified, and its connectivity with ROI 266 was likewise negatively associated with treatment response, implicating the cerebello–thalamo–cortical pathway as an integral component of treatment-related functional networks.

Overall, although multiple brain regions exhibited connectivity patterns associated with treatment response, the cerebellar Control-B region emerged as the most representative core node due to its robustness at the connectivity level and its extensive network involvement. These findings support a close link between antipsychotic treatment response and the modulation of the cerebellar control network within distributed functional circuits.

Conclusion

Using a single-center longitudinal cohort of patients with schizophrenia, this study systematically investigated the relationship between brain functional connectivity patterns and antipsychotic treatment response based on resting-state functional MRI data. By applying multivariate distance matrix regression at the whole-brain level to identify treatment-related regions and further characterizing their connectivity profiles, our findings indicate that functional abnormalities associated with treatment response are not confined to isolated brain regions or individual connections. Instead, they predominantly reflect alterations in the cerebellar control network and its distributed functional connectivity.

Specifically, the Control-B subregion of the cerebellar control network demonstrated robust associations with treatment response at both the regional and connectivity levels. Functional connectivity between this cerebellar region and the frontoparietal control network, default mode network, attention networks, and the sensorimotor network was negatively correlated with PANSS reduction (PFDR < 0.05).In addition, the ventral posterior lateral nucleus of the thalamus emerged as the only subcortical structure involved, highlighting a potential role of the cerebello–thalamo–cortical pathway in treatment-related functional circuits. Taken together, these findings provide neuroimaging evidence from a distributed network perspective for individual variability in antipsychotic treatment response and underscore the critical role of the cerebellar control network in the neural mechanisms underlying treatment response in schizophrenia.
