Introduction

According to the predictive processing framework, perception arises from a dynamic interplay between top-down predictions, shaped by prior experiences, and bottom-up sensory inputs. When sensory signals are ambiguous, the brain relies more strongly on prior expectations to reduce uncertainty. In emotional contexts, this means that prior affective information and experiences can shape emotional perception.

We previously showed in healthy controls (HC) that mood and brief emotional cues (i.e., primes faces) exert top-down influences that bias the perception of ambiguous emotional expressions in a congruent manner1. In bipolar disorder (BD), such affective top-down influences may be stronger or less flexible, possibly reinforcing mood-congruent perceptions and contributing to mood persistence1,2.

As one of the first 7T fMRI studies in BD patients, this research aims to investigate the neural correlates of affective predictive bias on ambiguous emotional expression and refine our understanding of the brain circuits supporting emotional perception in pathological mood states.

Methods

Nine BD patients and ten HC (ongoing recruitment) completed an affective priming task inside a 7T scanner. Participants categorized target faces (happy, sad, or morphed at 50% happy and sad) as positive or negative, following brief (33ms) supraliminal emotional primes (happy, sad, or no-face control, i.e. blank oval). Targets were presented for 1 second, and participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. Mood was assessed daily in the week preceding the MRI session using smartphone-based Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA). 

Functional images were preprocessed using the standardized fMRIPrep pipeline, with spin-echo top-up for distortion corrections. Normalization to MNI152 space was not applied to preserve the spatial resolution of 7T imaging. A general linear model (GLM) was fitted for a fast event-related design. Regressors included nine experimental conditions (3 × 3 prime-target combinations), six motion parameters (rotations and translations), linear drifts, and a constant. Reaction times (RTs) and response valence (positive, negative) were included as parametric modulators.

Regions involved in emotional processing and ambiguity perception3  were considered for the analyses. These regions of interest (ROI) comprised the amygdala (AMY), hippocampus (HIP), nucleus accumbens (NAcc), ventromedial and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC, dmPFC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), insula, and subgenual anterior cingular cortex (sgACC). They were defined using the Brainnetome atlas4 and registered into each participant’s native T1 space using ANTS. ROIs Percentage signal change (PSC) was extracted for each experimental condition in all ROIs. All the first level analyses were performed with Nilearn5 (Python). 

Results

Based on prior behavioral findings with the same experimental paradigm1, we focused on three main contrasts. 

(1) Emotional ambiguity processing. PSC values for each ROIs were analyzed using linear mixed models (LMM) with fixed effects of Group (HC, BD) and Ambiguity (Ambiguous, Unambiguous), Age and Sex as covariates, and random intercepts for participants. No differences between ambiguous and unambiguous faces were found in all ROIs across participants.

Next, within the morph condition only, we fitted linear models (LM) predicting PSC from Group (HC, BD) and behavioral response to morphs (Negative, Positive). In HC, perceiving a morph as more positive was associated with reduced activation in the insula (t = –2.40, p = .043) and left sgACC (t = –2.41, p = .042), with a similar trend in the right sgACC (t = –2.02, p = .077). In contrast, BD showed a trend toward increased left NAcc recruitment when morphs were judged as positive (t = 1.98, p = .088).

(2) Emotional priming effects. PSC values for each ROIs were analyzed using LMM with fixed effects of Group (HC, BD) and Priming (Prime, No Prime), Age and Sex as covariates, and random intercepts for participants. When ambiguous faces were preceded by emotional primes (vs. no prime), BD patients showed reduced engagement of the right AMY (t = 2.48, p = .018), left sgACC (t = 2.13, p = .040), bilateral HIP (right: t = 2.77, p = .009; left: t = 3.57, p = .001) and NAcc (right: t = 2.15, p = .039; left: t = 2.53, p = .016). In contrast, HC showed an increased engagement in the NAcc (right: t = -2.28, p = .028; left: t = -2.18, p = .036), and no priming effects in the other regions. 

(3) Mood effects on ambiguous perception. Within the morph condition only, we fitted LM predicting PSC in each ROI from Group (HC, BD) and weekly mean and variability (standard deviation) of pleasure, serenity, joy, calmness and irritability.

In HC, higher average weekly serenity was associated with greater right NAcc activation during morph perception (t = 2.59, p = .041). Higher weekly pleasure also showed a trend towards greater bilateral NAcc activation (right: t = 2.02, p = .089; left: t = 2.09, p = .081). Moreover, higher weekly joy was associated with greater left NAcc activation (t = 2.94, p = .026), with a similar trend in the right NAcc (t = 2.31, p = .060), and with a trend towards more positive ratings to morphs (t = 2.23, p = .067). Greater weekly variability in pleasure, joy, and irritability was consistently associated with right NAcc activation during morph perception (pleasure: t = –3.74, p = .009; joy: t = –3.84, p = .008; irritability: t = –2.56, p = .043).

In BD, higher variability in pleasure, joy, calmness, and irritability was associated with decreased activation in the left AMY and insula (pleasure: AMY t = -4.12, p = .009; insula t = -3.55, p = .016; joy: AMY t = -3.82, p = .012; insula t = -3.98, p = .010; calmness: AMY t = -4.38, p = .007; insula t = -4.03, p = .010; irritability: AMY t = -2.67, p = .044).

Conclusion

Overall, BD and HC showed distinct mechanisms in perceiving and resolving emotional ambiguity. When interpreting morphs as positive, HC showed reduced sgACC and insula activation, consistent with decreased engagement of regions involved in conflict and negativity processing, whereas BD showed increased activation in NAcc, associated with reward-processing, potentially reflecting a positivity bias.

Regarding affective priming, reduced AMY, HIP and NAcc activation in BD when ambiguous morphs were preceded by prime faces (vs. no prime) may reflect stronger affective predictions or repetition suppression that facilitate faster resolution of ambiguity, consistent with emotional hyper-reactivity in BD. Alternatively, this may reflect reduced sensitivity to emotional cues, consistent with blunted affect in depression. In either case, BD appeared to rely on primes differently than HC when interpreting ambiguity. 

Finally, mood modulated emotional ambiguity perception differently across groups. In HC, higher weekly positive mood (joy, pleasure, serenity) enhanced NAcc responses, consistent with mood-congruent positive biases, while increased mood variability led to the opposite effect. In BD, higher mood variability decreased AMY and insula engagement, suggesting that emotional instability disrupts limbic and interoceptive processing during ambiguity resolution. 

To our knowledge, this is the first 7T fMRI study to explore the neural mechanisms underlying ambiguous emotional perception in BD. The enhanced SNR of 7T MRI allows a more precise characterization of subtle activations, particularly in subcortical structures (e.g., AMY, HIP) and frontal regions (e.g., OFC, vmPFC) during emotional processing. Ongoing analyses will further characterise connectivity and computational mechanisms underlying affective predictive biases and test whether BD patients exhibit stronger or maladaptive affective priors, reinforcing mood-congruent biases that may contribute to mood persistence, escalation, and emotional dysregulation. Moreover, the present analyses exclusively focused on ambiguous perception. Further analysis will examine classical priming effects, using unambiguous happy and sad targets preceded by congruent or incongruent happy and sad prime faces.

Understanding these neurocognitive mechanisms could inform therapeutic strategies aiming to reduce over-reliance on prior expectations, such as mindfulness-based interventions, and may help identify precise targets for neuromodulation, such as fMRI neurofeedback and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS). Ultimately, these findings may contribute to an earlier and more accurate differential diagnosis of BD and support individualized treatment approaches.
