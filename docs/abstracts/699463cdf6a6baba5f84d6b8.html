Introduction

With a growing proportion of older adults, characterizing normative trajectories of brain function has become increasingly important. Resting-state fMRI functional connectivity (FC) is a widely utilised methodology in the study of large-scale brain organisation and its alterations with age1. However, studies assessing age-related differences in the functional architecture of the brain have yielded heterogeneous results, thus hindering the extraction of a clear age-related biological mechanism. One potential explanation for this phenomenon lies in the sensitivity of FC estimates to preprocessing decisions, including the removal of noise, the resolution of parcellation, and the selection of an atlas2. Translating this to dynamic FC, which captures the temporal variability over time by identifying recurrent connectivity states, each reflecting a distinct configuration of network interactions3, adds another level to this already complex problem. For instance, the utilisation of overlapping versus non-overlapping sliding windows may have a bearing on state detection and the derived temporal features. Consequently, it remains unclear whether commonly used pipelines under- or over-filter dFC signals in ways that systematically affect inferences, e.g. in relation to age. Consequently, the investigation of variability related to preprocessing as a factor of interest is imperative for the interpretation of dFC-based aging markers. The present study therefore set out to systematically investigate how different preprocessing pipelines, applied in different combinations of atlases and sliding window extraction methods, influence dFC connectivity matrices, state architecture, temporal features, and the strength and reproducibility of age associations in dFC.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data of 852 older males and females (374 females; aged 55–85 years) from 1000BRAINS cohort4were acquired on a 3T Siemens Tim-TRIO scanner (TR = 2.2 s, TE = 3.03 ms, flip angle = 9°, voxel size = 1 mm³). The first four volumes were removed to ensure signal steady state, and all time series were low-pass filtered at 0.1 Hz. Three preprocessing pipelines were evaluated: (1) Minimal: fMRIPrep outputs, a standardized preprocessing workflow including motion correction, susceptibility distortion correction, spatial normalization, and confound extraction; (2) Denoised: fMRIPrep and additional regression of motion parameters and their derivatives, along with white matter (WM) and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) signals; and (3) Maximal: fMRIPrep plus regression of motion parameters, their derivatives as well as WM, CSF correction and additional global signal regression (GSR). Dynamic FC was estimated using sliding windows of 14 TRs, implemented either as non-overlapping segments or with 50% overlap3. Time series were extracted using three atlases of varying spatial resolution: the Schaefer 7-network parcellations with 100 parcels, 400 parcels, and 400 parcels combined with 21 subcortical regions. For each window, Pearson correlations generated time-resolved connectivity matrices. The combination of three pipelines, three atlases, and two windowing strategies yielded 18 dFC configurations per subject. Matrices of each set were clustered independently into recurring dFC states using Ward’s hierarchical clustering, which resulted into 3 states for maximal (GSR) preprocessing pipelines and into 4 states for minimal and denoised pipelines. dFC states, which derived from both different pipelines and windowing methods, were matched within each atlas, using a Pearson correlation-based similarity matching, combined with a greedy assignment of centroids to enable general and state-level comparability. Afterwards, we extracted four temporal dFC features for each participant: 1) mean dwell time, 2) number of transitions, 3) total time and 4) mean duration of each state. We performed a three-factor ANCOVA (pipeline × atlas × windowing method; covariate: age) to quantify the proportion of variance in each dFC feature explained by each preprocessing component. Afterwards, employing a detailed assessment of dFC feature differences between different configurations (pipeline × atlas × windowing method), we compared z-standardized dFC feature means against the across-atlas group mean using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Age associations were computed for all features via correlation analysis. Finally, pipelines were additionally evaluated based on their ability to preserve structured network-level organization in dFC state centroids, assessed by visualizing hierarchical state centroids decomposed into large-scale functional networks and quantifying the proportion of positive connection density.

Results

Preprocessing pipelines, choice of atlas and windowing strategies had a substantial influence on all dFC features. Three-factor ANCOVA demonstrated that preprocessing choice accounted for the largest proportion of explained variance in dFC features, up to 23%, exceeding the contributions of atlas choice, windowing method, and their interactions (Fig. 1A). Atlas resolution explained a smaller but consistent proportion of variance (up to 20%), while interactions between preprocessing and windowing method contributed additional (15%), although more modest, effects. Detailed assessment of dFC feature differences between configurations (pipeline × atlas × windowing method) revealed configuration-dependent deviations in dynamic FC metrics. Depending on the pipeline, participants varied in terms of the number of switches across time and the time spent in different states. For example, maximal preprocessing revealed less switches across atlases, while the minimal pipeline in combination with the Schaefer 100 atlas revealed more switches between states, which was even higher in the overlapping window configuration (Fig. 1B). However, across all dFC features the denoised pipeline appeared to be more robust. Variance decomposition further revealed that the maximal pipeline paired with the Schaefer higher-resolution atlases yielded the most variable results. Despite this variability, the GSR pipeline consistently produced the most reproducible dFC state architecture, with state topologies that were stable and interpretable across atlases due to presence of negative correlations. Age associations were significant across multiple dFC features in all pipelines and exhibited consistent directional effects, e.g. the age-related decrease of the total time spent in the State 1 or in increase of the time in State 2 were shown by every pipeline combination, though the magnitude of correlations varied by pipeline and feature. Across pipelines, the denoised pipeline most frequently produced the strongest age effects.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that preprocessing strategy is a primary determinant of variability in dFC features, often exerting a larger influence than atlas resolution, windowing method, or even age itself. No single pipeline showed absence of systematic influence across all metrics, underscoring that preprocessing decisions should be guided by the specific scientific aims rather than by convention alone. The Denoised pipeline, removing motion, WM, and CSF signals, offered the strongest and most consistent age associations, suggesting heightened sensitivity to variability. However, this increased sensitivity did not always translate into stable dFC state solutions across atlases. In contrast, pipelines including GSR produced the most reproducible and interpretable state architectures, but also introduced greater variability in quantitative dFC metrics and stronger dependence on atlas choice, highlighting its potential value for studies focusing on individual differences. In turn, the minimally processed fMRIPrep output yielded moderate feature stability and sensitivity to age, which could be useful for studies of predictive modeling and biomarker discovery, especially when models depend on absolute connectivity magnitudes rather than GSR-altered correlation structures5. Together, these patterns reveal a trade-off intrinsic to dFC preprocessing: pipelines that maximize biological sensitivity may compromise state reproducibility, while those enabling consistent state architecture may attenuate interindividual effects. Consequently, preprocessing should be intentionally aligned with research priorities, whether emphasizing robust state topology, reliable effect-size estimation, or comparability with prior literature. Overall, this work reinforces the need for methodological transparency, pipeline benchmarking, and sensitivity analyses in dFC research, particularly in aging studies where subtle effects are easily overshadowed by preprocessing choices. Clear reporting and purpose-driven pipeline selection will be essential for developing reproducible and interpretable biomarkers of functional brain aging.
