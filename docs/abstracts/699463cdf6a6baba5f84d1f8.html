Introduction

Distracted eating is prevalent in modern environments, particularly among younger populations.1 While behavioral research consistently shows that distraction attenuates taste perception and increases food intake,2 recent functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have shown inconclusive results regarding the suppression of core gustatory areas (insula, orbitofrontal cortex) and possible interactions with taste concentration, all while employing unnaturalistic forms of distraction and largely neglecting tastes apart from sweetness.3, 4, 5

This fMRI study investigated whether naturalistic attentional engagement in the form of contemporary visual media modulates gustatory processing via sensory suppression or reallocation of neural resources, and whether the effects align with previous studies using more controlled cognitive load paradigms.

Methods

Thirty-eight healthy participants performed two consecutive sessions of a distraction task while undergoing fMRI scanning. Attentional state was manipulated using muted food-related (low-distraction) versus film-related (high-distraction) video clips. The high-distraction condition included four entertainment-related clips covering an action scene, a silent film, a cartoon and a roller coaster ride. The low-distraction videos were designed to resemble an eating context and showed dishes being consumed in motion. During video presentation, liquid taste stimuli were administered via a multi-channel gustometer (Fig. 1). The experiment followed a 2×2×2 factorial design with Distraction (high vs. low), Taste (sweet vs. umami) and Concentration (high vs. low) as within-subject factors. Each session comprised eight blocks, one for each possible combination of these factors, in pseudorandomizd order. After each clip, participants rated perceived intensity and pleasantness of the associated taste stimuli. Behavioral and neural group-level analyses included covariates for sex, body mass index (BMI), and hunger level. A region of interest (ROI) analysis using a non-orthogonal taste-localizer contrast from the whole-brain analysis was performed for the anterior and middle insula, the amygdala and a region near the orbitofrontal cortex.

Results

High distraction attenuated perceived intensity (p < 0.001, d = -0.28) and pleasantness (p < 0.01, d = -0.21), independent of taste quality or concentration. No significant modulation by distraction was observed in core gustatory regions (insula, OFC). Instead, distraction increased activation in occipital, thalamic, and cerebellar regions, indicating a shift of processing resources toward visual and attentional systems rather than diminished gustatory signaling (Fig. 2). ROI analysis showed no interesting results.

Conclusion

Distraction reduced taste salience without lowering gustatory cortex activity, consistent with resource-competition models. These  findings demonstrate that video-based paradigms work reliably in fMRI and capture how naturalistic distraction alters taste experience primarily through neural resource reallocation rather than sensory suppression. These results underscore that the impact of distracted eating is behaviorally robust yet neurally subtle, highlighting the need for personalized stimuli and ecologically valid methods to capture real-world eating behavior.
