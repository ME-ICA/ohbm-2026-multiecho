Introduction

Over the last decade, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have become a fundamental tool for medical image analysis due to their ability to extract meaningful patterns from complex data, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans. Despite their strong performance, CNNs remain inherently difficult to interpret, which poses a major challenge in neuroimaging, where understanding the anatomical factors influencing model decisions is essential. Although MRI provides rich and complex anatomical information, understanding how a CNN aggregates regional contributions during classification is challenging, especially for smaller brain regions whose contributions may be insufficiently captured by the model despite their established role as key biomarkers. In this study, we investigated the behavior of our 3D CNN by explicitly incorporating anatomical priors derived from regions of interest (ROIs), with particular attention to the impact of ROI size.1 To isolate this aspect from disease-driven structural changes, we used non–pathology specific altered maps derived from diffusion-weighted MRI, enabling controlled modifications while retaining inter-subject variability, a method previously published.2

Methods

Our dataset consisted of mean diffusivity (MD) maps of 126 healthy controls (HC) with a mean age of 66.3 years, registered in the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template at a resolution of 2 × 2 × 2 mm3.3 We considered three ROIs of varying sizes: the cerebellum (24333 voxels), putamen (1340 voxels), and substantia nigra (SN, 178 voxels). Subsequently, ROI voxels were linearly transformed at different percentages, ranging from 5% to 100% for putamen and cerebellum, and to 300% for SN. Increasing MD values reflect water diffusion anomalies associated with reduced microstructural intergrity.2 To assess the influence of each ROI anatomical a priori on CNN learning, we compared (i) MD-CNN, our CNN trained on whole-brain MD maps with ROI-specific alterations, (ii) ROI-CNN, taking as input MD maps masked and altered on each ROI using an atlas-based mask, and (iii) AG-CNN, characterized by an anatomical gate (AG) module guiding the attention of the CNN,4,5 fed with whole-brain MD maps with altered ROI and a second input consisting of an atlas-based mask of the same ROI. All three CNN configurations were trained using ten-fold cross validation to discriminate between altered and non-altered maps for each percentage at a time, and we evaluated their performance on a hold-out set (representing 20% of the dataset).

Results

Our results revealed a region-size–dependent behavior of the CNN models. For larger regions, such as the cerebellum, AG-CNN performed slightly better than ROI-CNN, closely followed by MD-CNN. In contrast, for smaller regions, ROI-CNN consistently achieved the highest prediction accuracy across all alteration percentages. However, AG-CNN tends to converge toward ROI-CNN as the degree of alteration increases. Consistent with our previous study, the smaller the region, the stronger the alteration intensity required by MD-CNN to perfectly distinguish modified maps from intact ones.2 Furthermore, statistical analysis of our results using Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by a post-hoc Dunn’s test, supported the differences among our three models, which are clearly apparent from their visual comparison.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight a strong relationship between the behavior of each CNN configuration and the ROI size. Restricting MRI inputs to the ROI appears to facilitate and improve CNN learning, particularly when small regions might otherwise be overlooked due to whole-image complexity. However, we lack manual or automatic ROI segmentations to maintain the same framework as the original study.5 When applying this approach to real patients versus HC classification, AG-CNN showed significantly improved results compared to ROI-CNN in the case of the putamen.3 This suggests that pathology-specific patterns may also affect the performance of AG-CNN. Finally, given the architectural complexity of AG-CNN models, a balance still needs to be found between performance gain and computational cost.
