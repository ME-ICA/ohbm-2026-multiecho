Introduction

In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aß) occurs as the first neuropathological change, and is later followed by tauopathy, which causes neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. According to the amyloid cascade model of AD, Aß deposition triggers the first aggregation and subsequent spread of tau pathology, thereby initiating a sequence of downstream pathological events, culminating in neurodegeneration and dementia. 1 In parallel, preclinical studies have shown that aggregates of fibrillar tau have the ability to propagate through self-replication 2 and thereby drive disease progression independent of Aß. 3, 4 It is currently not known over what period Aß acts as the primary driver of tauopathy and, in contrast, when self-perpetuating auto-replication of tau aggregates becomes the dominant mechanism of tauopathy progression. The clinical relevance of this question has become apparent as data from the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ2 Donanemab Trial demonstrated limited clinical efficacy of their anti-Aß monoclonal antibody in subjects with high tau burden. 5 This suggests that self-propagating, Aß-independent tauopathy might be a key limiting factor for the efficacy of Aß-targeting therapies in AD. We therefore aimed to characterize the phase transition from Aß-driven to Aß-independent tau-driven tau accumulation in a clinically informative manner. This is key to a better understanding of phase transitions in AD pathophysiology and critical for the effective administration of Aß-targeting therapy. Ultimately, results may inform future clinical trial design. 

Methods

We analysed cross-sectional 18F-florbetaben and 18F-florbetapir PET (amyloid-PET) and longitudinal 18F-flortaucipir PET (tau-PET) data from the ADNI and A4/LEARN cohort across the AD spectrum (N=640). Regional standardized uptake value ratios (SUVR) were calculated as ratios of T1-registered PET images to reference regions in 200 cortical ROIs of the Schaefer atlas. First, we established a pathophysiologically motivated model of regional tau accumulation, considering 1) baseline regional amyloid-PET to determine Aß-effects on tau aggregation, 2) baseline regional tau-PET SUVRs to determine tau auto-aggregation and 3) template-based functional connectivity (FC) to a given subject’s tau epicentres (i.e. 5% of brain regions with highest baseline tau-PET SUVRs) to model tau spreading across connected brain regions (Fig. 1A). Based on this model, we assessed i) the importance of baseline Aß and the importance of baseline tau for driving tauopathy across continuously increasing baseline tau levels using the Lindemann, Merenda and Gold (LMG) method to calculate predictor importance, ii) how regional patterns of Aß- or tau-dependency of tau accumulation change with higher tau load and iii) if limited effects of anti-Aß therapy on attenuating tau aggregation could be explained by Aß-independent tau self-replication by simulating a placebo-controlled clinical trial.

Results

For our first aim, we show that the importance of baseline Aß for actively driving tau accumulation decreases at higher tau-PET levels, whereas the importance of baseline tau for driving tauopathy strongly increases, suggesting larger tau self-replication effects at higher overall tau burden (Fig. 1B). We identified a gradual transition from baseline Aß-dependent to baseline tau-dependent tauopathy between ~1.25 and 1.5 tau-PET SUVR in the temporal meta-ROI (Fig. 1C). This demonstrates that the tau self-replication effect exceeds the effect of Aß on tau accumulation already early in the disease course when subjects still have low fibrillar tau in AD-relevant cortical regions and still being in close proximity to the threshold to tau-PET positivity (> 1.34 SUVR). Consistently, we found that the effect of Aß in significantly driving tau aggregation fades out once a tau-PET SUVR of ~1.5 is reached in the temporal meta-ROI (Fig. 1D). Beyond this threshold, tauopathy seems to shift into a phase of dominant self-replication, rendering the progression of tauopathy primarily dependent on preexisting baseline tau rather than Aß. For our second aim, we observed that Aß was the main driver of regional tauopathy in inferior and medial temporal regions and lateral parietal lobe at low levels of tau (up to ~1.375 tau-PET SUVR). At higher tau levels, the effect of tau auto-aggregation exceeded the Aß effect across the entire cortex (Fig. 2A). These findings highlight that Aß has a major influence opposed to tau auto-replication only at low levels of tau, specifically targeting inferior and medial temporal and lateral parietal cortices. Thirdly, by simulating an anti-Aß clinical trial, we show decreasing anti-Aß treatment effects on tau accumulation with higher tau load at treatment start (Fig. 2B). These results support the notion that Aß-independent tau auto-aggregation is a significant driver of tau progression, particularly at high baseline tau levels. This could represent a critical mechanism limiting the efficacy of anti-Aß treatments to attenuate downstream tau aggregation and therefore neurodegeneration and cognitive decline.

Conclusion

Together, our results show that tau accumulation becomes Aß independent early in AD, i.e. at a temporal-meta tau-PET SUVR of ∼1.5. Therefore, Aß-targeting therapy should be administered before the phase transition to Aß-independent tauopathy has fully established, to attenuate downstream tau progression and cognitive decline.
