Introduction

Large mammalian cerebral cortices exhibit a complex folded architecture composed of gyri (convex regions) and sulci (concave regions). Folding patterns appear during brain development and are known to be linked to various of its functional and structural properties^1. While previous studies have reported that cortical folding influences neuronal morphology, quantitative, cell-level analyses across all cortical regions through development remain limited.

Pyramidal neurons are the principal excitatory cells of the cortex and play a central role in cortical computation and connectivity. Characterizing how their shape and orientation vary across cortical curvature and developmental stages can provide insights into the mechanisms by which mechanical forces and developmental programs interact to shape cortical organization.

The ferret is a well-suited model for studying the development of cortical folding, as its cortex is smooth at birth and develops its rich folding postnatally. This allows direct investigation outside of the womb of how neuronal morphology evolves in parallel with the emergence of cortical gyri and sulci. The conserved neuroanatomy across individuals facilitates cross-sectional analyses over the period when folds form^2. Histological analysis of brain sections at several stages throughout development can provide high-resolution insights into cellular morphology, cell type and organization^3. 

Methods

Ferret brains were collected at multiple developmental stages (postnatal day 8 (P8), P16, and P32). Coronal sections were Nissl-stained and imaged using a Zeiss Axio Scan.Z1 microscope at a resolution of 0.22 µm/pixel. Images were stored in CZI format. (Fig. 1a)

Individual cells were segmented using a deep learning–based approach. Manual annotations of 3,337 cells across 13 image patches were created using QuPath, with patches selected to sample diverse brain regions, including white matter, grey matter, across gyri and sulci. These annotations were combined with publicly available datasets (TNBC_2018, LyNSeC, NuInsSeg, and IHC_TMA) to train the Instanseg model from scratch, following the brightfield configuration described in the original Instanseg publication.^4 Training was performed for 500 epochs.

Segmented cells were classified into pyramidal neurons, glial cells, and endothelial cells based on morphological features (area, shape descriptors, and intensity). A training dataset was manually labeled in QuPath and consisted of 1,111 pyramidal neurons, 459 glial cells, and 228 endothelial cells sampled across cortical regions. Morphological features were extracted using QuPath measurement functions, and classification was performed using a logistic regression model implemented in scikit-learn. Only pyramidal neurons were retained for subsequent analyses.

For each pyramidal neuron, shape descriptors (including area, major and minor axis lengths, eccentricity, and orientation) were computed using the “regionprops” function from scikit-image.

The cortical surface was segmented from histological images using thresholding followed by manual correction in Napari. Local curvature and surface normal direction were estimated by fitting a smooth spline to the cortical boundary using a custom Python pipeline. Regions were classified as convex (gyri) or concave (sulci) based on the sign of curvature. Each cell was assigned to a cortical region based on its closest point to the cortical surface.

Results

The Instanseg model achieved a Panoptic Quality (PQ) score of 0.81 on our dataset, enabling reliable segmentation of more than 200,000 cells in less than 3 hours per histological slice. The logistic regression classifier achieved a macro-averaged F1 score of 92%, allowing robust identification of pyramidal neurons across developmental stages and cortical regions.

Analysis of pyramidal neuron orientation revealed a non-uniform distribution across the cortex that correlated with local curvature (Fig. 1d) In convex regions (gyri), cells predominantly oriented their major axis perpendicular to the cortical surface. In contrast, concave regions (sulci) exhibited a bimodal orientation pattern, with neurons oriented either perpendicular or parallel to the surface. (Fig. 1f)

Further stratification by cortical depth revealed a clear gradient in sulci: pyramidal neurons located near the cortical surface tended to orient perpendicularly, whereas deeper neurons increasingly aligned parallel to the surface. Quantitative analysis of orientation as a function of distance from the cortical surface confirmed a transition from perpendicular to parallel alignment with increasing depth in concave regions (Fig. 1g).

Developmental analysis showed that these curvature-dependent shape and orientation patterns emerged progressively. At P8, when the cortex is still little folded and curvature values are low, pyramidal neurons were relatively round (low eccentricity) near the surface and became more elongated with depth, but showed no parallel orientation to the pial surface in regions that would later form sulci. By P16 and P32, as cortical folding became pronounced, neurons in sulcal regions became more elongated and increasingly oriented parallel to the cortical surface, especially when close to white matter and U-fiber regions (Fig. 1h).

Conclusion

Our results suggest that pyramidal neuron shape and orientation are strongly related to local cortical curvature and cortical depth during development. Gyri regions favor perpendicular alignment of pyramidal neurons, while concave regions exhibit a depth-dependent reorientation, with neurons in deeper layer aligning parallel to the cortical surface. Importantly, this sulcus-specific organization emerges after early postnatal stages, coinciding with the development of cortical folding.

These findings suggest that mechanical forces associated with cortical gyrification may play a key role in shaping neuronal morphology during brain maturation. Such morphological adaptations are likely to influence connectivity patterns and functional organization within folded cortical structures. Given the known impact of mechanical stress on gradient pattern and signaling^5, future studies should investigate how curvature-driven shape changes affect pyramidal neuron physiology and circuit integration.

A limitation of the present study is that curvature was estimated from 2D histological sections, which cannot fully capture the three-dimensional geometry of the cortex. Future work incorporating 3D reconstructions from serial sections or in vivo imaging would enable more accurate characterization of curvature effects. Additionally, the observed dependence of neuronal shape on cortical depth raises the possibility of interactions with white matter architecture; exploring the relationship between pyramidal neuron morphology and axonal bundle organization such as U-fibers will be an important direction for future research.
