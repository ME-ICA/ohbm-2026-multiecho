Introduction


The acquisition of EMG signals provides a wealth of information. These signals allow, for example, to accurately determine the onset of a motor response before it is translated into movement or whether this response is inhibited and therefore undetectable using conventional tools (force sensors, response buttons, etc.).

However, as with other physiological signals (EEG, ECG), the acquisition of EMG signals during an MRI session is made difficult by the presence of considerable inductive artefact due to the switching of gradient trains during acquisition, particularly during functional Echo-Planar Imaging (EPI) sequences where these switches are particularly rapid and of significant amplitude. Inductive noise can thus drown out the useful signal or even saturate the acquisition system.

Due to its repetitive nature and perfect reproducibility in EPI, inductive noise is therefore stationary, volume after volume, with however some low-frequency modulations due to the participant's breathing or other cable movements.

Work has been done to filter this artefact using signal processing tools designed to oversample the analogue signal in order to detect, model and then subtract the inductive noise . This type of purely software-based solution aims to imperfectly circumvent the problem of asynchrony between analogue acquisition and the MRI acquisition clock, inducing an unstable phase shift between the source and the measurement of the artefact.

We propose a solution based on an innovative hardware approach that allows inductive noise to be optimally identified and subtracted.

Methods

Our solution aims to implement an acquisition chain that is perfectly synchronised with the MRI system. This chain consists of MRI-compatible BIOPAC electrodes and cables (SS1LA / LEAD108C / EL509), two GRASS 511K instrumentation amplifiers and a National Instruments NI-CompactDAQ 9178 box equipped with NI-9215 (16-bit analogue acquisition) and NI-9401 (digital I/O & synchronisation) modules. We have verified that the hardware chain does not produce RF artefacts on the MRI images.

Acquisition is controlled by software developed in the LabVIEW® environment using the DAQmx driver. This software ensures hardware resynchronisation at each TR and generates a separate acquisition clock for each of these volumes (TR), enabling the acquisition of a train of samples for the duration of the TR. Each signal sample is therefore acquired at exactly the same time as the corresponding sample during the previous TR. The average noise is thus calculated on the basis of perfectly synchronous trains (globally over the entire run or over a sliding time window). 

The average noise is then subtracted from the initial signal and the resulting trains are then concatenated to reconstruct the signal.
Resynchronisation at each TR ensures that there is no phase shift noise (which is difficult to filter) between all of these TRs. Figure A illustrates the indisputable benefits of this technique by comparing asynchronous and synchronous superimposition of acquisition trains.


Results

The quality and relevance of the EMG signals was demonstrated through the implementation of a Go/NoGo task using response buttons equipped with FSR-type isometric pressure sensors capable of detecting very low pressures below the button activation threshold. Conclusion

This new instrumental technology enables optimal acquisition and cleaning of EMG signals in fMRI. It is a tool that allows new behavioural indicators to be integrated and analysis models to be refined.

The prospects that are opening up concern both the equipment, with optimised amplifiers or electrodes adapted to the muscles concerned, and the acquisition methods themselves: it is possible to envisage real-time acquisition/noise reduction using, for example, an embedded system that would perform online cleaning, as is already the case in audio, for example.
