Introduction

Neurodevelopmental conditions (NDCs) are associated with poorer outcomes, including education & occupational attainment, interpersonal relationships, and mental health.1 However, heterogeneity is high.1,2 NDC characteristics or risk factor models cannot fully capture this heterogeneity and only partly reflect what shapes the support needs of neurodivergent individuals. The mismatch between the relatively high level of adversity experienced by neurodivergent individuals and their outcomes illustrates that a significant portion demonstrate resilience, defined as better outcomes compared to what would be expected given these risk factors.2 It has therefore been proposed that models aiming to predict outcomes or support needs require integration of resilience factors. One method to investigate resilience is to look at the relationship between outcomes and adversity and taking the residuals from these models as a measure of resilience. However, this residual-based method fails to take uncertainty into account, i.e. are unable to separate noise from the residuals. Additionally, sample specific regressions from small samples cannot be meaningfully applied to new cohorts/external datasets. Normative modelling addresses these limitations but requires a large sample to build a reference model.3 Therefore, we used the large ABCD project to investigate if a risk-by-outcome normative model captures resilience and vulnerability related variability in the deviation Z-score that result from the normative mapping at four timepoints. We validated the deviation scores as resilience/vulnerability index using established factors that were not used in the normative outcome-to-risk factors relationship. Additionally, we validated their predictive utility using a linear mixed model to predict changes in outcome over time from change in risk, Z-deviation, and their interaction. Finally, we performed edge-wise associations between canonical resting-state networks and the deviation scores.

Methods

A set of daily functioning and mental health outcomes were selected at baseline and 1-/2-/3-year follow-ups. The variable selection was guided by the International Classification of Functioning (ICF) core sets for Autism Spectrum Condition (ASC) and ADHD, to ensure NDC relevance.4 The outcome variables include mental health variables (CBCL), Prosocial behavior, parent-child conflict and variables related to school life (KSADS background items and School Risk & Protective Factors). A composite outcome score was created by performing PCA on the outcome variable set and computing a sum score (weighted by proportion variance explained) from the first four principal components, that represented general psychopathology, school performance, academic well-being, and prosocial behavior respectively. Using the PCN toolkit software (https://pcntoolkit.readthedocs.io/), we fitted a normative model that predicts the composite outcome score from a set risk factor predictors, including age, ASC and ADHD characteristic scores (KSADS), past trauma (KSADS), adverse life events, and parent psychopathology (Internalizing & Externalizing T scores from Adult Self Report scale). We used Hierarchical Bayesian Regression to control for site effects. Models were fitted at each timepoint. Out-of-Sample validation was performed on two sets of subjects that were withheld from the model training set: all subjects from two sites (site transfer), and additionally the top 1% of subjects with the highest ASC and/or ADHD characteristic scores (test set), to assess how well the model performs at the higher end of these characteristics. This results in individual Z-score deviations from the mapped risk-to-outcome relationship. 
               We validated these deviation Z scores by looking at associations with established resilience and vulnerability factors. These include emotion regulation factors Positive Affect (NIH toolbox), Reappraisal & Suppression (Emotion Regulation Questionnaire), and difficulty in Emotion Regulation Questionnaire. We also looked at Cognition measures including Fluid, Crystalized, and Total composite cognition scores (NIH toolbox), Matrix reasoning (WISC-V), and Executive Functioning Deficits (Barkley Scale). We additionally looked at socioeconomic factors Parental Education, Total Household Income, and Financial Hardship, as well as family and social support related measures, including the Family Environment Scale, Children’s Report of Parental Behavior, and Peer network Health. We used height as a negative control variable. 
In addition to the full sample, we explored three subgroups defined by extreme or typical deviations. The cutoffs were set at Z < -1, which is the ‘vulnerable’ group, -1 ≤ Z ≤ 1 which defines the typical range group, and a group with Z > 1, representing the ‘resilient’ group. We compute spearman correlations in these subgroups and compare group means via F-tests. This allows us to examine if associations between the validatory variables and deviation scores differ across segments of the deviation distribution, identify asymmetric patterns, potential threshold effects, and subgroup-specific mechanisms of vulnerability and resilience that remain obscured in full-sample analyses.
               As an additional validation analysis, we assessed the relationship between the change in risk and change in outcome over time. We computed the change in outcome (Δoutcome) and change in mean standardized risk (Δrisk) between consecutive timepoints and fitted a linear mixed model predicting Δoutcome from Δrisk, Z-deviation at the earlier timepoint, and their interaction. Timepoint and modelling set (training, test, transfer) were categorical covariates and a random intercept was fitted for each subject. If the Z-deviation scores represent vulnerability/resilience they should moderate the relationship between Δrisk and Δoutcome.
               Finally, edgewise correlations were computed between the Smith10 resting-state network connectivity matrices and deviation Z-scores.

Results

PC1 to PC4 explained between 63.7% and 67.6% of the variation in the outcome data. The variable loadings were highly similar across timepoint (overall mean r = 0.991). The normative mappings were capable of predicting outcome from risk factors predictors at all four timepoints, as shown in their explained variance (training: 0.3619 ± 0.02, test: 0.2762 ± 0.09, transfer: 0.3535 ± 0.04) and their Standardized Mean Squared Error (training: 0.6381 ± 0.02, test: 0.7358 ± 0.09, transfer: 0.6475 ± 0.04). 
Z-deviation scores strongly correlates with the composite outcome score (r = 0.71, p < 0.001), showing that the normative relationships is effectively modeled. The remaining unexplained variance that is captured in the deviation Z scores thus reflect individual differences that go beyond the mapped risk-to-outcome relationship. The Z-deviation scores systematically correlated with our validation factors and showed significant group difference effects between the low, typical, and high Z-deviation groups. Effect sizes differed, ranging from 0.02 < ρ < 0.30 (all pFDR < 0.001), with generally lower effect sizes for the cognition domain variables (mean absolute ρ = 0.10; mean η2 = 0.02 ) and socioeconomic domain variables (mean absolute ρ = 0.06; mean η2 < 0.01) and higher effect sizes for emotion regulation domain variables (mean absolute ρ = 0.17; mean η2 = 0.04) and family environment and support domain variables (mean absolute ρ = 0.17; mean η2 = 0.03). As expected, height did not show a significant association or group level difference at the three Z-score bins (r = -0.01, pFDR = 0.414; F=0.23, pFDR = 0.634, η2 < 0.001).
              Asymmetric patterns of associations across the three Z-deviation group (low, typical, high) were observed in a subset of validation variables. One such pattern is the attenuation and eventual dissipation of association when going from the low to high Z-deviation groups. This pattern was observed in all the Difficulties with Emotion Regulation subscales and Barkley deficits in executive functioning scale, as well as family income, parental support. Another asymmetry pattern of significant associations in the typical and high Z-deviation group, and no association in the low group was observed for Peer Network Health.
The linear mixed effects model predicting change in outcome over time showed a significant negative effect of Δrisk on change in outcome (β = -1.19, SE = 0.02, z = -55.85, p < .001), as well as a significant negative effect of deviation Z-scores (β = -0.43, SE = 0.01, z = -34.95, p < .001). There was also a significant interaction between Δrisk and deviation Z-scores (β = 0.31, SE = 0.02, z = 16.04, p < .001), indicating that the association between changes in risk and changes in outcome varied as a function of individuals’ deviation from the normative model. Timepoint and set effects (train, test, transfer) were non-significant (all p > .24). The random intercept variance was small (σ2 = 0.002), suggesting minimal between-subject variability after accounting for fixed effects. A post-hoc Likelihood-Ratio test revealed that including the interaction Δrisk * deviation Z-score significantly improved model fit relative to a model that omits this interaction effect (χ2(1) = 255.38, p < 0.001). 
              Edgewise correlations revealed 8 significant positive correlations of Z scores with executive control to sensory network connectivity, executive control to the frontal-parietal-perceptual network, between sensory network connectivity patterns, and cerebellum to sensory network connectivity. Two negative associations were found with default mode to sensory and default mode to sensory-motor networks.

Conclusion

We developed normative models that map risk factors to outcomes, specifically selected for NDC relevance. The models capture individual levels of deviation from the normative relationship between the composite outcome and risk factor predictors in an adolescent general population cohort. This deviation is reflected is a Z-score. We aimed to investigate whether these scores capture resilience and vulnerability related variability. We tested this by associating the deviations with multiple variables across several domains that are considered protective or associated with risk for poor outcomes. Additionally, we look at mean differences of these variables across three binned Z-score ranges: low, typical, and high Z scores. Although these variables all showed significant associations and group differences, the large sample size emphasizes the importance of the effect size estimates, which were small to moderate. This fits the complex multifactorial nature of resilience.
               We additionally validated the deviation scores as vulnerability/resilience index by looking at its predictive utility. The linear mixed effects model showed that changes in risk profiles predict changes in outcome over time, which confirms that there exists a dynamic between risk and outcome over time. The deviation Z-score also significantly predicts changes in, which may indicate that resilient individuals generally show less change in outcome (i.e they have more stable outcomes over time), or larger negative changes. Crucially, we found a significant interaction effect between the ∆risk and deviation scores: The negative effect of increasing risk on the change in outcome over time is attenuated by a higher deviation score. In other words, resilient individuals are less affected by increases in risk, while vulnerable individuals show larger declines when risk increases. These results confirm that the deviation scores capture meaningful individual differences in risk sensitivity and thus function as an index of vulnerability or resilience at the individual level. 
               The asymmetric relationships across the different Z-score groups suggest that these factors are only relevant for some individuals, depending on where they lie on the vulnerability to resilience continuum as quantified by our deviation scores. Where some factors might be specific markers of vulnerability (e.g poor emotion regulation abilities), others may only contribute to resilience in those that are vulnerable (e.g increasing family income) or show a ceiling effect. This latter possibility was especially evident for parental support, where resilient individuals received relatively uniform, high levels of. Moreover, associations with parental support were highest of all factors we tested, which confirms that parental support is a foundational resilience factor for adolescents. Together, these asymmetries have important implications for tailoring support and interventions to the individual as uniform effects of these factors cannot be assumed. One limitation should be noted, however. The range of deviation scores differ between the low (-5.59 to -1 ) and high (1 to 2.47) deviation groups. This narrower range in the high deviation group may have limited the detectable effects, which may contribute to the observed, or observed lack of, asymmetric relationships. The coarse brain-derived connectivity patterns of the Smith10 canonical resting-state networks show some initial patterns of connectivity that are associated with our Resilience/Vulnerability index. Further, fine-grained analysis may reveal the neurobiological signatures of resilience in more detail.
              In conclusion, we showed that a risk-by-outcome normative model is capable to capture vulnerability/resilience related variability in the deviation Z-scores. Our model’s outcome and predictor sets were tailored to NDC, which makes it useful for applying NDC cohorts and investigation the bio-psycho-social factors underlying resilience in this population.
