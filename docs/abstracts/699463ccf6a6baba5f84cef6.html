Introduction

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is characterized by substantial heterogeneity. Symptoms are commonly divided into inattention (IA) and hyperactivity/impulsivity (HI), yet these dimensions show moderate correlations and divergent developmental trajectories—HI often diminishes with age, while IA may persist or increase. This suggests that apparent symptom levels may arise from distinct latent dimensions.

The Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) framework suggests a bifactor model, which partitions variance into a general ADHD factor (shared variance across all symptoms) and specific factors (unique variance for IA and HI), is a promising approach. This model can simultaneously explain the correlation between IA and HI while preserving their unique features.

However, two major gaps persist: (1) Systematic evidence for the developmental change of these general and specific symptom dimensions is lacking, often limited by narrow age windows or varied sample types. (2) Their neurophysiological bases are poorly understood. While EEG studies show atypical spectral power or network organization in ADHD, it is unclear whether these alterations are linked to the general factor, the specific factors, or both.

To overcome these limitations, we utilized a large (N=4,311), age-diverse (5-21 years) cohort from the Healthy Brain Network (HBN). Our objectives were to: (1) Systematically compare alternative structural models of ADHD symptoms and establish the optimal model's measurement invariance across age and gender. (2) Investigate the age-related change of the resulting general and specific factor scores. (3) Determine the distinct neural signatures of these factors using resting-state global absolute EEG band power and source-space functional connectivity (FC).

Methods

Participants Data were from 4,311 participants (aged 5–21) from the Healthy Brain Network (HBN) 1. A subset with valid resting-state EEG data (n = 2,284) was used for neurobiological analyses.

Measures The 18-item Strengths and Weaknesses of ADHD Symptoms and Normal Behavior Rating Scale (SWAN) was used for factor analysis 2. The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) was used for convergent validity.

ADHD Factor Structure and Validation Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted in Mplus 8.3 (MLR estimator) to test eight competing models (e.g., single-factor, correlated-factor, bifactor, modified bifactor). Model fit was assessed (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR). Reliability (e.g., ωh, ωs, ECV, FD) was calculated for the best-fitting model. Measurement invariance (configural, metric, scalar) was tested across gender and nine age groups (ΔCFI < 0.01, ΔRMSEA < 0.015). Factor scores were extracted from the final model. Partial correlations (controlling for age, gender) assessed convergent validity with CBCL scores. A General Linear Model (GLM) assessed the main effects and interaction of age and gender on factor scores.

EEG Acquisition and Preprocessing 128-channel EGI data (500 Hz) were preprocessed using EEGLAB 3. Data were filtered (0.1–40 Hz), 60 Hz line noise removed, and bad channels removed. ICA was performed, and ICLabel (probability > 0.6) was used to remove eye/muscle components. Channels were interpolated, and data were re-referenced to the average and segmented into 2-second epochs. Artifactual epochs were rejected (±120 μV amplitude; 3 SD threshold; TBT plugin). Participants required ≥ 60 seconds of clean data.

EEG Band Power Analysis Global absolute EEG band power was computed using Welch’s method for five canonical frequency bands: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–40 Hz). 

Source Space Functional Connectivity (FC) Data were projected into source space using MNE-Python4. Source estimation used sLORETA on the fsaverage template. Time series were extracted for 68 ROIs (Desikan–Killiany atlas). FC was computed using the weighted Phase Lag Index (wPLI) for all ROI pairs in each frequency band, mitigating volume conduction. ROIs were mapped onto 7 Yeo networks5.

Statistical Analysis (Brain-Behavior) Partial correlations (controlling for age, gender) were computed between the three factor scores (General, Specific Inattention, Specific Hyperactivity) and (1) global power in each band, and (2) all source-space wPLI-derived FC edges. For FC analyses, a non-parametric permutation test (5,000 permutations) using the maximum statistic approach was applied to correct for family-wise error rate (FWER).

Results

ADHD Factor Structure Bifactor models provided a superior fit compared to correlated-factor models. Standard bifactor models showed problematic parameters (e.g., negative loadings). A modified bifactor model, comprising one general ADHD factor, one specific inattention factor, and one specific hyperactivity factor, provided the optimal balance of model fit and interpretability (RMSEA = 0.06, SRMR = 0.04, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.93) and was selected for all subsequent analyses.

Reliability and Invariance The general factor demonstrated good reliability (ωh = 0.77), high construct replicability (H = 0.95), high factor determinacy (FD = 0.96), and accounted for the majority of common variance (ECV = 0.65). The specific inattention factor showed acceptable properties (ωs = 0.57, FD = 0.93). The specific hyperactivity factor was poorly defined (ωs = 0.14, ECV = 0.07, FD = 0.81), warranting caution in interpretation. Strong measurement invariance was supported across both gender and all age groups (ΔCFI < 0.005, ΔRMSEA < 0.006), validating the comparison of factor scores.

Factor Score Validation and Age/Gender Effects Factor scores demonstrated robust convergent and discriminant validity against SWAN items and CBCL subscales. GLM analyses revealed distinct age-related change: the general factor (β = -0.23, p < 0.001) and the specific hyperactivity factor (β = -0.24, p < 0.001) significantly decreased with age. In contrast, the specific inattention factor (β = 0.21, p < 0.001) significantly increased with age.

EEG Band Power Correlates After controlling for age and gender, only the general factor showed significant, although subtle, positive correlations with global absolute EEG band power (delta, theta, and beta bands; rs > 0.06). No significant correlations were found for the specific factors.

Functional Connectivity Correlates (FWER-Corrected) A clear neurobiological dissociation emerged: (1) The general factor was associated with significant positive correlations, linking widespread, inter-network connections across delta, theta, beta, and gamma bands. (2) The specific inattention factor was associated with significant negative correlations exclusively in the alpha band, involving connections between the Default Mode Network (DMN) and nodes in the somatomotor, limbic, and ventral attention networks. (3) The specific hyperactivity factor was associated with a single significant positive correlation exclusively in the theta band, connecting the frontoparietal network with the limbic network.

Conclusion

This study validates a modified bifactor model of ADHD symptoms in a large, age-diverse cohort, establishing its strong measurement invariance across development. Both behavioral and neurobiological findings support the validity of this hierarchical framework.

Our results reveal a clear neurobiological dissociation between the general and specific dimensions of ADHD. The general factor maps onto global, broadband dysregulation, reflected in elevated global absolute EEG band power and widespread, cross-frequency functional connectivity. This pattern suggests excessive neural integration and may be consistent with delayed network segregation or maturation.

In contrast, the specific factors map onto localized, frequency-specific circuit dysfunctions. The specific inattention factor was uniquely linked to reduced alpha-band connectivity of the Default Mode Network (DMN), consistent with theories of impaired DMN inhibition during rest. The specific hyperactivity factor was linked to enhanced theta-band connectivity between frontoparietal and limbic networks, suggesting heightened motivational or emotional influence on executive control circuits.

Several limitations should be noted, including the low reliability of the specific hyperactivity factor, the cross-sectional design, and the spatial resolution limits of EEG.

In conclusion, these findings bridge behavioral psychometrics and electrophysiology, providing a mechanistic explanation for the heterogeneity of ADHD. The general factor appears to reflect global, cross-diagnostic dysregulation, whereas the specific factors capture distinct, local circuit-level dysfunctions. This dimensional framework offers a promising path for identifying biomarkers that transcend traditional diagnostic categories.
