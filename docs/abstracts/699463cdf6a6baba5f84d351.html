Introduction

Treatment-resistant schizophrenia (TRS) represents a severe and biologically distinct subtype of schizophrenia, yet the neural mechanisms underlying treatment resistance remain poorly understood. Large-scale resting-state fMRI studies consistently implicate dysconnectivity across default mode, salience, frontoparietal, thalamic, and hippocampal circuits in schizophrenia, but most investigations analyse heterogeneous patient groups without differentiating TRS from non-treatment-resistant schizophrenia (NTRS). Evidence specifically comparing TRS and NTRS remains limited, and existing findings are inconsistent or focus on narrow sets of regions or isolated seeds rather than broader network-level assessments.1,2 Few studies, to our knowledge, have combined multiple seeds with ROI-to-ROI analyses in a way that systematically examines connectivity differences among clinically defined TRS, NTRS, and healthy controls (HC). Since thalamocortical, limbic, and higher-order network changes are central to current models of schizophrenia,3,4 a precise, multilevel examination of functional connectivity may help identify network features associated with treatment resistance. To address this gap, we examined resting-state connectivity across multiple a priori seed regions representing major networks implicated in schizophrenia, in a balanced cohort of TRS, NTRS, and healthy controls; using complementary seed-to-voxel and multivariate ROI-to-ROI analyses.

Methods

Seventy-five participants (age 18–65) were included: 25 treatment-resistant schizophrenia (TRS; 16 male, 9 female), 25 non-treatment-resistant schizophrenia (NTRS; 17 male, 8 female), and 25 healthy controls (HC; 18 male, 7 female). All participants were right-handed. Diagnoses followed ICD-10 criteria confirmed by trained psychiatrists. TRS was defined as persistent psychotic symptoms despite ≥2 adequate antipsychotic trials; all TRS patients were receiving clozapine, whereas NTRS patients were clinically stable on non-clozapine second-generation antipsychotics. Symptom severity was assessed in patient groups using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). HC had no psychiatric or neurological disorders and no first-degree family history of psychosis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with institutional ethics approval. Resting-state fMRI was acquired on a Philips Ingenia 3T scanner using a multiband-accelerated (MB=3) SENSE EPI sequence (32-channel coil; TR = 1000 ms; TE = 25 ms; flip angle = 65°; voxel size = 3×3×3 mm; 300 volumes; scan duration ≈5 min). Slice-time correction was omitted due to simultaneous multi-slice acquisition. High-resolution T1-weighted structural images were acquired using a 3D-Turbo Field Echo (TFE) sequence with 0.75-mm isotropic voxel size. Preprocessing was performed in CONN 22.v2407 and SPM12,5 including realignment with susceptibility-distortion correction (Realign & Unwarp), ART-based outlier detection (framewise displacement >0.9 mm or global BOLD change >5 SD), coregistration to T1, direct segmentation and normalization to MNI space (2-mm isotropic), and 8-mm FWHM spatial smoothing. Functional data were denoised using the aCompCor approach, regressing 5 white-matter and 5 CSF components, 12 motion parameters (6 rigid-body + first derivatives), ART outlier regressors, linear trends, and session effects, followed by band-pass filtering (0.008–0.09 Hz). Effective post-denoising degrees of freedom ranged from ~26–45 across subjects. Seven a priori ROIs were defined based on established schizophrenia-relevant networks: posterior cingulate cortex (default mode), anterior cingulate cortex (salience), left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (frontoparietal), and bilateral thalamus and hippocampus (Harvard–Oxford atlas). First-level analyses estimated Fisher-z-transformed seed-to-voxel connectivity maps and ROI-to-ROI correlation matrices. Group-level comparisons evaluated TRS vs NTRS, TRS vs HC, and NTRS vs HC using two-sided general linear models with random-effects inference. Age and sex were included as covariates in all group-level models. Seed-to-voxel maps were thresholded at voxel-level p<0.001 (uncorrected) and cluster-level p-FDR<0.05 using Gaussian Random Field theory. ROI-to-ROI inferences employed CONN’s multivariate Functional Network Connectivity framework, using an omnibus MVPA test and cluster-level false discovery rate correction (connection threshold p<0.05 uncorrected; cluster p-FDR<0.05). All subjects met motion quality-control criteria, and mean framewise displacement did not differ between groups.

Results

Seed-to-voxel analyses revealed widespread connectivity differences in TRS and NTRS relative to healthy controls, whereas the direct comparison between TRS and NTRS did not yield any significant clusters.

In TRS relative to controls, the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC, salience network) showed a mixed pattern of reduced and increased connectivity. Reduced ACC connectivity was found with cerebellar and vermis areas (peak MNI −6 −60 −24, cluster p-FDR ≈ 1.7×10⁻⁴), with putamen and amygdala regions (peak MNI −16 −2 −18, cluster p-FDR ≈ 8.1×10⁻⁴), and with right hippocampal and parahippocampal areas (peak MNI +22 −20 −20, cluster p-FDR ≈ 2.2×10⁻³), along with additional hypoconnectivity in frontal and sensorimotor regions (clusters surviving p-FDR < 0.05). In contrast, ACC connectivity was increased in TRS with right middle frontal cortex (peak MNI +40 +34 +28, cluster p-FDR ≈ 3.8×10⁻³), occipital visual areas (peak MNI +12 −98 −10, cluster p-FDR ≈ 0.018), a dorsal parietal region (peak MNI −20 +4 +28, cluster p-FDR ≈ 0.020) and an occipital fusiform cluster (peak MNI −12 −100 −12, cluster p-FDR ≈ 0.032). With the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC, default mode network) seed, TRS showed a large cluster of reduced connectivity across bilateral intracalcarine and lingual cortices (peak MNI +10 −70 +12, cluster p-FDR < 0.001). The left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex seed (LPFC, frontoparietal network) showed reduced connectivity with the left occipital pole in TRS (peak MNI −30 −94 −14, cluster p-FDR ≈ 0.035), and the right thalamus seed (thalamocortical system) showed reduced connectivity within intrathalamic and brainstem regions (peak MNI +4 −18 +4, cluster p-FDR ≈ 0.022).

In NTRS compared with controls, the left thalamus seed showed increased connectivity with right precentral and inferior frontal regions (peak MNI +50 +4 +26, cluster p-FDR ≈ 0.045) and with right posterior superior and middle temporal regions (peak MNI +48 −36 +16, cluster p-FDR ≈ 0.050).

ROI-to-ROI analyses further indicated reduced functional connectivity between the left and right hippocampus (limbic network) in TRS relative to controls (cluster p-FDR = 0.028). In NTRS, connectivity between the LPFC and left hippocampus was reduced (cluster p-FDR ≈ 0.021), and interhemispheric hippocampal connectivity was also reduced (cluster p-FDR ≈ 0.033), while LPFC–PCC connectivity was increased (cluster p-FDR ≈ 0.021). No ROI-to-ROI effects survived correction in the TRS versus NTRS comparison. All reported clusters met voxelwise p < 0.001 and cluster-level p-FDR < 0.05 thresholds.

Conclusion

Our findings show that treatment-resistant schizophrenia and non-treatment-resistant schizophrenia differ from healthy controls in partly overlapping but also partly distinct network patterns. TRS showed mainly reduced connectivity from salience, default mode, frontoparietal, thalamic and hippocampal regions to posterior sensory and limbic areas, together with weaker connectivity between the left and right hippocampus. NTRS showed increased thalamic connectivity to right frontal and temporal regions, along with reduced frontoparietal–hippocampal connectivity and increased frontoparietal–default mode connectivity. Although the direct comparison between TRS and NTRS did not produce significant results, the two groups showed different patterns relative to controls, suggesting that treatment resistance may reflect a different configuration of network changes rather than simply a more severe version of the same alteration. These results highlight the importance of examining multiple large-scale networks when studying treatment resistance in schizophrenia and provide a basis for future work using longitudinal or multimodal approaches to understand how these connectivity patterns relate to illness course and treatment response.
