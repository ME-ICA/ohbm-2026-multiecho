Introduction

Essential tremor (ET) is a prevalent movement disorder that interferes with daily function. For patients refractory to medication, lesioning or neuromodulation of the thalamus—historically via radiofrequency ablation or Gamma Knife radiosurgery, and more recently using MR-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS)—has become an important treatment option. At the structural level, these treatments target the dentato–rubro–thalamic tract, reflecting the classical cerebello–thalamo–motor loop.1 However, advances in resting-state fMRI suggest that tremor involves a broader, distributed network that extends beyond the motor system, with reproducible alterations reported in visual, cerebellar, and attention regions. These findings raise the possibility that tremor emerges from large-scale network dysfunction rather than a single circuit.2 To systematically characterize the tremor network before and after thalamotomy, we performed a systematic review and a network-based meta-analysis integrating coordinate-based and label-based evidence across functional and structural modalities.

Methods

A systematic search identified studies reporting structural or resting-state functional abnormalities in ET patients undergoing thalamotomy with either gamma knife, radiofrequency or MRgFUS. From each paper, all reported regions were extracted. For coordinate-based studies, peak MNI coordinates were mapped to the Brainnetome Atlas3, Harvard-oxford, SUIT and assigned to Yeo 7 networks4. For label-based studies, we matched Brainnetome and Schaefer to Harvard–Oxford and SUIT regions, then HO was matched to Yeo 7 networks using a voxel-wise weighted correspondence matrix derived from overlap proportions in MNI space5. Studies reporting multiple analyses (e.g., ET vs. controls and pre- vs. post-treatment) were counted as separate experiments. Within each experiment, repeated identification of the same region was counted once to avoid inflation. (Fig 1)

Across all experiments, we calculated the frequency of involvement for each region and network. A Chi-square goodness-of-fit test evaluated whether the observed network distribution differed from chance, based on expected frequencies derived from a whole-brain 246-region reference Brainnetome atlas. Networks with |z| > 1.96 were considered significantly over- or under-represented. (p < 0.05).

Regions within the top 25% frequency were binarized and used as seeds for network analysis. For these seeds, normative functional connectivity matrices were generated in Lead Mapper v2.5.3 using rs-fMRI–based connectivity. Pearson correlation matrices were clustered using pheatmap (Euclidean distance), with k = 4 chosen via elbow analysis. A weighted network graph (r ≥ 0.30) was computed using qGraph. Betweenness and closeness centrality were calculated following Rubinov & Sporns’ recommendations.

Results

From 51 eligible studies, 20 met inclusion criteria, yielding 55 experiments and 207 occurrences across 112 unique Brainnetome regions or 304 occurrences across 85 unique Harvard-Oxford regions. The most frequently reported regions (HO atlas) included the right and left precentral gyrus (n=19, 14), brainstem (n=13), right postcentral gyrus (n=12), left superior frontal gyrus (n=9), and bilateral lateral occipital cortices (n=8). Across Yeo networks, the highest frequencies occurred in the Visual (n = 91), Somatomotor (n = 68), Frontoparietal (n = 47), and Ventral Attention (n = 45) networks. (Fig. 2a)

A Chi-square test confirmed a non-random distribution of regions across networks (χ² = 51.64, p < 0.001). Somatomotor (z = 3.46), Visual (z = 2.88), and Dorsal Attention (z = 2.34) networks were significantly over-represented, whereas Limbic (z = −2.97), Ventral Attention (z = −2.91), and Frontoparietal (z = −2.67) networks were under-represented. (Fig. 2b)

Unsupervised k-means clustering (k = 4) revealed four spatial–functional subgroups of regions involved across studies. Cluster 1 is predominantly sensorimotor–occipito-parietal regions, including bilateral postcentral gyri, right middle frontal gyrus, right lingual gyrus, right superior parietal lobule, the left occipital pole, and the right anterior parahippocampal gyrus, representing a mixed somatosensory–visuospatial integration network. Cluster 2, a cerebello-thalamo–motor module, characterized by bilateral cerebellar lobules (I–IV, VI, VIIIa/b, and Crus II), the right thalamus, and the left precentral gyrus, reflecting the recognized tremor-related cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuitry. Cluster 3 is a posterior insular–occipital subgroup, including the left insula, right intracalcarine cortex, and left lateral occipital inferior gyrus, suggesting a sensory-interoceptive and visual integration component. Cluster 4 comprised a fronto-parietal executive–visuospatial network, including bilateral superior frontal gyrus, right precentral gyrus, right precuneus, and the left lateral occipital superior gyrus, consistent with higher-order motor planning and attentional control. Together, these four clusters formed a coherent multi-network architecture involving somatosensory, visuospatial, cerebello-thalamic, interoceptive, and executive control systems, highlighting the distributed nature of the regions recurrently associated with tremor pathophysiology. (Fig 2c)

Nodes in the right parahippocampal gyrus, right inferior lateral occipital cortex and right lingual gyrus showed the highest betweenness centrality, indicating that they acted as major bridges between the tremor related networks. In contrast, cerebellar lobules I–IV, VI and Crus II, together with the right thalamus and superior lateral occipital cortex, exhibited low betweenness and lower closeness centrality, consistent with a more peripheral role in the tremor-related network (Fig. 2d)

Conclusion

This network-based meta-analysis demonstrates that tremor-related abnormalities and post-thalamotomy network changes are not restricted to the classical cerebello–thalamo–sensorimotor circuit but also involve prominent visual and fronto-parietal systems. Across studies, regions segregated into four reproducible functional modules—somatomotor, cerebellar–thalamic, visual–occipital, and a fronto-parietal integrative module—indicating that tremor engages distributed large-scale networks rather than a single motor related pathway. The connectivity architecture further revealed strong cerebello–visual coupling and highlighted medial temporal and visuospatial hubs—particularly the parahippocampal, lingual, and lateral occipital cortices—as bridge nodes integrating multiple large-scale systems. These findings support a distributed cerebello–visuomotor–frontoparietal architecture underlying tremor pathophysiology and suggest that effective neuromodulation may depend on modulating multiple large-scale networks rather than targeting an isolated nucleus or tract.
