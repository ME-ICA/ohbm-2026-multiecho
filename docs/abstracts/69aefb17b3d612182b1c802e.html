Introduction

Identifying early biomarkers for dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) remains a critical challenge, as up to 80% of patients are initially misdiagnosed1 due to heterogeneous symptoms and compensatory neural mechanisms that mask early deficits. Pupillometry provides a non-invasive, scalable approach to index locus coeruleus (LC) function and compensatory cognitive effort, both of which are altered in prodromal DLB. 2,3 Despite this potential, pupillometric research in DLB and prodromal DLB is extremely limited. This study evaluated whether baseline pupil dynamics can distinguish mild cognitive impairment with at least one core symptom of DLB (MCI-LB)4 from healthy aging, providing a preliminary test of feasibility for developing pupillometric biomarkers of DLB.

Methods

We collected data from 55 participants (17 MCI-LB; 38 healthy controls), aged 58 to 81 (Mage = 68.17). The present analyses focus on two components:

  1. Baseline pupil recordings collected before the first session.
  2. Task-evoked pupil data taken during two consecutive n-back tasks.

Baseline data

Each participant completed a 4-minute baseline pupil recording prior to the first session. During this period, participants fixated on a central point while a cross appeared 10 times. This segment contained no task, allowing assessment of spontaneous pupil fluctuations.

Task-evoked data

Participants performed two visuospatial n-back tasks (2-back and 3-back) to assess pupil dynamics across different working-memory loads, see Figure 1. Each task contained 10 blocks of 10 trials and each block started with a 10-second baseline fixation period.

Preprocessing

Pupil diameter was recorded with EyeLink (sampling rate: 1000 Hz). Preprocessing included blink detection with interpolation from 100 ms before to 100 ms after each blink, removal of physiologically implausible values, and smoothing with a 3rd-order Butterworth low-pass filter (5 Hz cutoff). 

Statistical analyses

For baseline analyses, an age-matched subsample was created using nearest-neighbour matching with the MatchIt package in R,5 yielding 17 MCI-LB and 17 healthy controls. Group differences in pupil size variability (standard deviation across baseline) were assessed using linear regression.

For task analyses, linear mixed-effects models were used to examine effects of pupil metrics extracted from block-level baselines (mean pupil size, pupil size variability), diagnostic group, task load (2-back vs. 3-back) on task performance. Models included random intercepts of subject ID and session order to account for repeated measures and the study design.

Results

Across the age-matched baseline sample, individuals with MCI-LB showed significantly greater baseline pupil size variability compared with healthy controls (β = 4.43; t(31) = 2.08; P = .046), indicating differences even in the absence of task demands.

For the task-evoked data, linear mixed-effects models revealed a significant interaction between diagnostic group, task difficulty, and mean pupil size (β = 0.043; t(1032) = 1.99; P = .047). Specifically, the relationship between mean pupil size and cognitive performance (accuracy on n-back task) differed between MCI-LB and healthy participants and further depended on task load (2-back vs. 3-back).

Conclusion

The present study provides preliminary evidence that pupillometry can sensitively index early neurocognitive changes associated with DLB. At baseline, individuals with MCI-LB showed significantly greater spontaneous pupil variability than healthy controls. Because baseline recordings were collected in the absence of any cognitive demands, these fluctuations likely reflect underlying instability in arousal and autonomic regulation, consistent with early locus coeruleus (LC) dysfunction and attentional fluctuations, a core clinical feature of DLB4. Given that the LC is among the first structures affected in DLB, elevated variability may represent a direct physiological marker of impaired neuromodulatory control.

In contrast, the task-evoked results revealed patterns consistent with compensatory cognitive mechanisms. The relationship between baseline pupil size and task performance differed between groups and shifted as task difficulty increased. 

Together, the present study highlights the potential of combining baseline and task-related pupillometric measures to capture both LC-related dysfunction and compensatory recruitment, offering a promising pathway toward earlier identification of individuals at risk for developing DLB.
