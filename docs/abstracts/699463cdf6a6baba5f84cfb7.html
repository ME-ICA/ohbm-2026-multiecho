Introduction

The human brain undergoes rapid, wide-ranging changes throughout the paediatric period. As a result, paediatric neuroradiology faces the unique challenge of characterising and quantifying neurodevelopmental abnormalities. Recent increases in the availability of open source magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data have enabled the development of growth charts for the brain1,2. These normative trajectories of brain development promise future clinical translation, by mimicking the World Health Organization’s growth charts for height and weight, allowing us to map individuals against a normative reference population. Indeed, prior work has assessed deviations in brain phenotypes from normative growth trajectories in individuals with neuro-psychiatric disorders using these trajectories1,2. However, neuro-psychiatric conditions are heterogeneous and have limited well-defined radiological signatures against which to assess the validity of results. In contrast, paediatric neurodevelopmental disorders, including certain epilepsies, have clinically described radiological abnormalities for which robust, quantitative markers could transform clinical practice. We therefore tested whether brain growth charts can quantify known MRI pathology in three conditions with radiological abnormalities: (i) Rasmussen’s encephalitis, a disorder with progressive atrophy of one hemisphere of the brain - severe in some individuals, more subtle in others; (ii) Hippocampal sclerosis, which leads to focal, localized atrophy of the hippocampus; and (iii) DHDDS, caused by mutations on the DHDDS gene, leading to distributed effects across the brain. 

Methods

First, we constructed paediatric normative growth charts of tissue volume in the cortex and subcortex to use for benchmarking individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders. We aggregated a paediatric sample of 14 studies, with a total of 18,000 MRI scans from healthy controls, spanning from 40 post menstrual weeks to 35 postnatal years (Fig 1A). All scans were preprocessed using SynthSeg+ 3, and scans that did not pass automated quality control (QC) were excluded (SynthSeg QC<0.65). We used generalised additive models of location shape and scale (GAMLSS) to chart regional cortical and subcortical volume development with age, controlling for sex, and random effects of MRI imaging sites (Fig 1B). We additionally collected data from three epilepsy cohorts (Fig 1C): Rasmussen’s encephalitis (NScans=212, NSubjects=58, Age=2-17y), hippocampal sclerosis4 (HS); NSubjects=82, Age=16-34y), and DHDDS (NSubjects=10, Age=1-15y). We then estimated subject-specific deviations from normative development (i.e. Z-Scores) in the three disorder cohorts (Fig 1D). Here, we highlighted exemplary subject-specific effects in disease-relevant regions: In Rasmussen’s we investigated cortical atrophy, in hippocampal sclerosis we quantified hippocampal atrophy, and in DHDDS we assessed differences in total intracranial volume. For individuals with Rasmussen’s and HS, which demonstrate progressive atrophy in one hemisphere, we further contrasted the disease effects in the ipsilateral vs contralateral hemisphere. Next, we estimated regional group-level effects in each disorder, by estimating the median Z-Score per region for the cross-sectional datasets. For the Rasmussen’s cohort, which included longitudinal data, we estimated the group-level effect as the predicted regional Z-Score at age 12, using a linear mixed effects model, accounting for random effects of the subject. Lastly, since DHDDS is assumed to have a distributed effect throughout the brain, we assessed the relationship between group-level deviations from normative development in DHDDS and the cortical and subcortical gene expression profile of the DHDS gene in the Allen Human Brain Atlas5 (AHBA).

Results

We assessed individual-level effects of each disorder across the cortex and subcortex. We first highlight deviations in disease-relevant regions. 

For hippocampal sclerosis, we estimated individual deviations from the norm in the ipsi- and contralateral hippocampus, respectively (Fig 2A). We find that the median ipsi-lateral Z-Score is significantly lower than the contralateral one (t=-9.04, P<0.001), underlining a clear disease effect (Fig 2A, right). We further assessed the asymmetry of deviations (i.e. the difference between the ipsi- and contralateral Z-Score, ΔZ) and find that 93% of subjects had more negative Z-Scores in the ipsilateral hippocampus compared to the contra lateral one (Fig 2B). As expected, hippocampal atrophy was most pronounced, but we also observed widespread atrophy across multiple other subcortical regions, along with increased volume of the lateral ventricles (Fig 2C).

In Rasmussen’s, we observed disease-related decline in cortical volume (Fig 2D). Rasmussen’s is known to be a progressive disorder. Indeed, here we observed a progressive decline in Z-Scores in the ipsi- (t=-3.6, P<0.001), but not the contralateral hemisphere, leading to significantly more pronounced atrophy with age in the ipsilateral hemisphere (t=3.65, P<0.001; Fig 2D). Further, 98% of scans showed more negative Z-Scores, i.e. greater decreases in cortical volume, in the ipsilateral hemisphere (Fig 2E). This pattern extends to the overall regional cortical and subcortical group-level deviations from normative development: Across the brain, subjects demonstrated clear decreases in volume in the ipsi- compared to the contralateral hemisphere, as evidenced by lower Z-Scores (Fig 2F). 

DHDDS cases overall had decreased total intracranial volume (Fig 2G). Regionally, we observed strong atrophy across the brain, more pronounced in the cortex than the subcortex, including in particular in cingulate regions (Fig 2H). Lastly, we assessed whether group-level deviations from normative development in DHDDS develop along the gene expression profile of the DHDDS gene. Indeed we find a positive correlation between the ranked AHBA-derived and the ranked DHDDS gene expression (𝜌=0.42; P<0.001), indicating greater disease effects in regions with lower DHDDS gene expression (Fig 2I). 

It is worth highlighting that across disorders the deviation scores observed here were extreme in many cases, with a large percentage of individuals in each disorder falling into the bottom 1% (Z-Score < -2.33) or even the bottom 0.1% (Z-Score < -3.09) of the distribution.

Conclusion

Children with epilepsy and other neurodevelopmental disorders often have MRI findings on visual radiological review, yet quantifying and monitoring these in the rapidly developing brain remains a challenge. Here, we assessed the feasibility of using normative models (‘growth charts’) of brain development to monitor individuals with such disorders. We provide proof-of-concept that indeed, growth charts of the brain are sensitive to known radiological atrophy. This opens up the opportunity of using such growth charts for early detection, characterisation and monitoring of individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders relative to a normative control cohort. Tools for disorder-specific or even individually-tailored trajectories could help clinicians understand the marked phenotypic variability within these disorders and to distinguish expected development from improvement/deterioration to support clinical decision-making. In summary, our findings pave the way for allowing individualized characterisation and monitoring of neurodevelopmental trajectories, enabling clinicians to track disorder progression and intervention responses over time.
