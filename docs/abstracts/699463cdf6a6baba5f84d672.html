Introduction

Economic decisions often occur in social contexts, requiring individuals to consider both their own outcomes and those of others. The Ultimatum Game (UG) is a two-player economic decision-making task widely used to investigate fairness-related and norm-based behavior: one player (the proposer) receives a fixed amount of money and decides how to split it with another player (the responder). The responder can either accept the offer, in which case both players receive their proposed shares, or reject it, resulting in no payoff for either player. In the classic “one shot” version, each responder interacts with a certain proposer only once.

If the aim were just to maximize one’s income,  the responder should accept any non-zero offer; however, a large amount of studies consistently showed that unfair offers are frequently rejected. These rejections are generally interpreted as reflecting sensitivity to inequity, adherence to social norms, and negative emotional responses, overcoming monetary maximization. Neuroimaging studies have linked these responses to activity in different prefrontal areas involved in value-encoding and conflict monitoring. 1 A growing body of evidence showed that the characteristics of the proposer may influence responders’ decisions: unfair offers are more often rejected when made by humans rather than computers, or when proposers are perceived as socially or morally distant. The neural mechanisms underlying these modulatory effects remain poorly understood. 

In this fMRI study, we investigated whether empathic concern toward the proposers can modulate acceptance of unfair offers and examined the neural bases underlying this modulation. This work aims to clarify how empathy interacts with fairness considerations in economic decisions, and to identify the neural mechanisms that may lead individuals to accept inequitable outcomes when the proposer is perceived as socially or emotionally deserving.

Methods

Twenty-one healthy right-handed female volunteers (mean age 22.8 ± 3.1 years) participated in the study after providing written informed consent. All had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no neurological or psychiatric disorder history. Handedness was assessed via the Edinburgh Inventory. The study  was approved by the local Ethics Committee.

Participants performed a one-shot UG as responders during fMRI scanning. Each participant played 36 trials in which human proposers were presented with name, age, and socioeconomic status, and 18 trials against a computer (control condition, C), for a total of 54 trials, divided in 4 runs. Human proposers were presented as belonging to one of two predefined social categories:

• Retirees living on a minimum pension (R)

• Affluent professionals (M)

Each proposer made a single offer involving the division of 10 €. Offers were of three types: Fair (4–5€), Mid-value (3€), Unfair (1–2€). Each type was presented 18 times. On each trial, the sequence of events was (Fig. 1): fixation (5 s), proposer presentation (8 s), offer (8 s). Both proposer and offer type were presented in a pseudo-random order. Finally, the participants were prompted to accept or reject the offer by pressing a button (6 s). The next trial started after a further 9s interval. The E-prime software was used for stimulus presentation and response recording. Participants received a fixed 10 € fee plus 10% of their in-task earnings.

After scanning, the participants completed the 4 sub-scales of the Italian version of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI; 2 Fantasy -F, Perspective-Taking -PT, Empathic Concern -EC, Personal Distress -PD) and a questionnaire on their emotional closeness towards each proposer. Behavioral data were analyzed by means of a logistic regression.

Functional images were acquired with a Philips Achieva MR system at 3T and a gradient-echo echo-planar sequence (TR= 2000 ms; TE= 35 ms; FOV 240x240x120 mm; in plane acquisition matrix 80x80, voxel size: 3.0x3.0x4.0 mm; 30 axial slices). High resolution T1-weighted anatomical images (voxel size 1x1x1 mm; FOV 256x256x170 mm; TR= 10 ms; flip angle 8°; TE= 4.60 ms; acquisition time 282 s) were acquired to assess brain structure. All preprocessing and statistical analyses were performed using SPM25 running in MATLAB, following standard steps (realignment, coregistration, slice-timing correction, normalization and smoothing with a 6×6×8 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel). For each participant, event-related responses were modeled within the General Linear Model (GLM). The presentation of the proposer identity (subdivided in R/M; accepted/rejected) and the presentation of the monetary offer (subdivided in Fair/Mid-Value/Unfair; R/M; accepted/rejected) were modeled as separate regressors of interest. The six realignment parameters were included as nuisance covariates. For each subject, condition-specific effects were tested and the resulting contrast images were entered into random-effects group analyses (one-sample t-tests and linear regression models) to assess the influence of proposer category, offer type, and individual empathy scores (IRI scales). A double statistical threshold (single-voxel statistics and spatial extent) was used to achieve a combined (i.e., corrected for multiple comparisons) significance level of α < 0.05, as computed by the 3dClustSim AFNI routine with the “-acf ” option. (https://afni.nimh.nih.gov/pub/dist/doc/program_help/3dClustSim.html). 

Results

Behavioural results: 

Logistic regression analysis of acceptance rates revealed a significant interaction between Offer and Proposer; namely, significant differences were found between Fair and Unfair offer acceptance rates for Proposers M and C, as compared to Proposer R (Fig. 2A).

Our volunteers felt significantly more emotional closeness (p<0.001) towards Proposer R (7 ± 2.66) than towards Proposer M (0.33 ± 5.35; p<0.001) and Proposer C (-3.14 ± 4.70; p<0.001). The difference between Proposer M and Proposer C was not significant (p = 0.06).

fMRI results:

Proposer presentation phase: 

When comparing Proposer R vs Proposer M, we found a global MNI peak activation located in the right angular gyrus (AG; MNI peak at 60, -46, 26; t=7.19; k= 144), together with other clusters present in bilateral posterior cingulate cortex (PCC; MNI peak coordinates at 15 -52 26; t=5.88; k= 185) and bilateral Medial Frontal Gyrus (MFG; MNI peak at 21, 41, 38; t= 4.97; k= 181). 

Offer presentation phase:

A single cluster emerged for the Unfair > Fair Offer comparison, with the peak located in the left anterior cingulate cortex and extending superiorly into the supplementary motor area, bilaterally (ACC; MNI peak at -6, 29, 26; t=5.13; k= 196).

The contrast Accepted > Rejected Offers revealed a bilaterally and symmetric increase of BOLD activity in the Superior Parietal Lobule (SPL; MNI peak at -24, -64, 42 ; t= 7.37; k=133), and in the fusiform gyrus (MNI peak at -24, -85, -10; t= 6.03; k=43). However, when Accepted Offers were compared to Rejected Offers using the EC scores of the Responder as a regressor, we found positive correlations in two clusters: one located in the right middle occipital gyrus (MOG;  MNI peak at: 36, -64, 18 ; t=5.11, k= 29) and a second one in the right MFG (MNI peak at: 27, 20, 54 ; t= 5.09; k= 27). Negative correlations emerged in a single cluster in the left superior parietal lobule (SPL; MNI peak coordinates: x: -27 y: -55 z: 38 ; t=5.76; k= 38), indicating that individuals with higher Empathic Concern showed reduced neural responses in these posterior–parietal regions when accepting vs rejecting offers (Fig. 2B)

Conclusion

The present study investigated the neural mechanisms underlying social decision-making in the Ultimatum Game.

When the proposer was an old retiree in need, as opposed to a younger wealthy professional, we observed a prominent activation in the right AG This result is in line with previous studies that consistently implicated AG in perspective-taking and mentalizing, suggesting that differences in proposer-related features engage processes related to social inference and the evaluation of others’ intentions. 3 The concurrent involvement of PCC and MFG is also in agreement with the interpretation that proposers that are perceived as more empathically engaging recruit self-referential and evaluative mechanisms, possibly reflecting internal simulations of social outcomes and norm-based reasoning during strategic decision-making. 

The presentation of unfair, as opposed to fair, offers, revealed a significant cluster in left ACC, extending superiorly into the supplementary motor area (SMA) bilaterally. The ACC has traditionally been conceptualized as a key cognitive region involved in conflict monitoring and cognitive control. 4  Furthermore, its extensive connectivity with limbic regions and prefrontal control areas highlights its broader role in behaviour modulation. More recent accounts have proposed the ACC as a central node in prediction error signaling. 5 Within this framework, the increased ACC engagement observed for unfair compared to fair offers may reflect a violation of social norms, specifically a prediction error relative to an expected norm of fair monetary distribution by the proposer. Thus, ACC activity may indicate the detection and evaluation of socially salient norm violations rather than cognitive conflict alone. The extension into SMA suggests an interaction between evaluative and action-related processes, potentially reflecting the preparation, inhibition, or regulation of behavioral responses elicited by unfair offers.

Outcome-related processing further revealed distinct neural signatures. The contrast of accepted vs. rejected offers was associated with increased activity in the bilateral inferior parietal lobule, including the angular gyrus, as well as in the fusiform gyrus. Importantly, individual differences in Empathic Concern (EC) modulated neural responses during outcome evaluation. Positive associations between empathic concern and activity in lateral occipital regions may reflect enhanced processing of salient offers, whereas negative associations in the superior parietal lobule could indicate a reduced reliance on top–down attentional control. 

Collectively, these findings highlight the involvement of a distributed neural network encompassing parietal, medial frontal, cingulate, and occipital regions in the evaluation of proposer-related features, fairness, and outcome evaluation in the Ultimatum Game. The modulation of neural responses by empathic concern underscores the importance of individual psychological traits in shaping the neural dynamics of economic decision-making. Overall, the present results support models of social decision-making that emphasize the interaction between cognitive evaluation, affective processing, and individual differences in empathy during norm-based social exchanges.
