Introduction

Central to the development and function of the brain is its blood supply. Blood perfusion delivers oxygen and nutrients for all cells, making it a fundamental feature of brain organization. In this study, we comprehensively characterize blood perfusion in the brain at multiple levels of description. We first investigate how blood perfusion co-localizes with micro- and meso-scale features, including neurotransmitter signaling, cell types, and cortical layers. At the macro-scale, we investigate how blood perfusion maps onto regional differences in metabolism and hub connectivity. We further investigate how blood perfusion changes through the lifespan and in multiple neurodegenerative diseases. Finally, we show that inter-regional coordination of blood perfusion can be used to map arterial territories. Collectively, these experiments establish blood perfusion as an important biological annotation for future multi-scale exploration of brain anatomy and function.

Methods

Data from a total of 1,305 participants in the HCP Lifespan studies (2.0 Release) are analyzed in this study.2-4 Specifically, data from 627 participants (337 females) from HCP-D (5–22 years) and 678 participants (381 females) from HCP-A (36–100 years) are included. The ASL data acquisition parameters and the preprocessing pipeline are described in detail in the accompanying references.2-5

To derive a common blood perfusion map, we z-score blood perfusion maps of individual participants and concatenate them into a single data matrix. The standardization at the participant level mitigates perfusion level offsets attributed to biological sex differences. We next apply principal component analysis (PCA) dimensionality reduction to the data matrix. The first principal component accounts for 50.71% of the variance in blood perfusion across the sample. This first component reflects the main regional pattern of blood perfusion shared across participants and is shown in Fig.1a.

All code used to perform the analyses is available on GitHub at https://github.com/netneurolab/Farahani_Blood_Perfusion.

Results

We use pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (ASL) magnetic resonance imaging as part of the Human Connectome Project–Development and the Human Connectome Project–Aging to derive quantitative maps of brain blood perfusion. Data from 1,305 participants (5–22 and 36–100 years; 718 females) are included in this study.

We first derive a normative map of cerebral blood perfusion that is representative of all participants. The resulting perfusion map is highly regionally heterogeneous (Fig.1a). We find elevated perfusion in early sensory regions and in a compact set of transmodal areas, including dorsolateral frontal cortex and highly myelinated POS2, a finding that is consistent with the notion that the heavily myelinated zones have greater cytochrome oxidase enzymatic activity.1 At the cellular and molecular level, cerebral blood perfusion co-localizes with granular layer IV, biological pathways for maintenance of cellular relaxation potential and mitochondrial organization, and with neurotransmitter and neuropeptide receptors involved in vasomodulation, including 5-HT1b, vasoactive intestinal peptide, and endothelin receptors. At the regional level, blood perfusion aligns with cortical arealization and is greatest in regions with high metabolic demand and resting-state functional hubs.

Looking across individuals, blood perfusion is dynamic throughout the lifespan. The overall pattern reveals age-related reduction in perfusion, with a steeper decrease during development compared to aging (Fig.1b). 

Age-related reductions in perfusion during development (5–22 years) are significantly greater in transmodal compared to unimodal cortex. More specifically, there is a positive association between the age-related decline in cerebral blood perfusion and age-related decline in cortical thickness (Fig.1c). This reduction in blood perfusion is likely due to synaptic pruning that happens in transmodal areas at this age range. These results highlight the dynamic coupling between vascular changes and micro-architectural remodeling during development.

Age-related reductions in perfusion during aging (36–100 years) are greater in border areas between major arterial territories, in line with the fact that these areas are more vulnerable to embolism (Fig.1d). We further show that elevated mean arterial pressure, body mass index, glucose, and HbA1c, reminiscent of the metabolic syndrome, exacerbates blood perfusion reduction in the aging cohort, beyond the effect of sex and age.

We further elaborate on how the non-uniform distribution of cerebral blood perfusion contributes to the biological vulnerability of underlying brain tissue in pathological conditions. Atrophy in late-onset Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal lobar degeneration with TDP-43 type C pathology, and dementia with Lewy bodies is most pronounced in areas with inherently lower perfusion, highlighting the utility of perfusion topography as an indicator of transdiagnostic vulnerability.

Last, we investigate how blood perfusion is coordinated among brain regions, and the extent to which this network organization can be used to recover the underlying arterial territories (Fig.2). We derive arterial territories by calculating a blood perfusion covariance matrix across participants and deriving its first two gradients using diffusion map embedding. The first gradient separates the territories of posterior and anterior brain circulations. The second gradient captures the transition from anterior brain circulation to border-zone regions. These results show that ASL measurements of blood perfusion can be used to infer inter-regional dependencies in blood perfusion in a data-driven manner. We further construct a semantic space for how different vascular systems support cognition by cross-correlating cortical maps from the Neurosynth meta-analytic atlas with the two gradient maps. This semantic space serves as a crude ‘lookup table’ to match vascular territories to cognitive function.

Conclusion

In this report, we analyze arterial spin labeling (ASL) data from 1,305 participants sourced from the HCP Lifespan studies and investigate how blood perfusion fits within the multi-scale organization of the brain. 

Across regional, cellular, and molecular scales, blood perfusion aligns with energetic and architectural demands of neural tissue. At the regional scale, areas with the greatest glucose and oxygen consumption and those exhibiting stronger resting-state functional connectivity are highly perfused. We also find elevated perfusion in early sensory regions, potentially reflecting metabolic costs of encoding sensory input. At finer resolution, perfusion corresponds to layer IV density, consistent with this layer being the most vascularized in mammalian cortex. More specifically, perfusion map aligns with In6a and In6b PVALB+ interneurons, which are characterized by high-frequency spiking patterns and greater mitochondrial volume compared to other brain cells. Cerebral perfusion also correlates with biological pathways related to ion transmembrane transport, highlighting the energy-intensive nature of maintaining resting membrane potential. Moreover, perfusion map co-localizes with receptor densities of specific molecular signaling pathways. The perfusion map aligns with vasoactive intestinal peptide and endothelin neuropeptide receptors, both involved in regulating vascular diameter. The perfusion map also aligns with 5-HT1B neurotransmitter receptor, consistent with the fact that these receptors are directly expressed on arteries and regulate vasoconstriction. In sum, these results highlight the close link between blood perfusion and the cellular and molecular environment of brain regions.

In addition to the cartography of cerebral blood perfusion, we investigate its dynamics across the lifespan. Cerebral perfusion increases in infancy and early childhood in response to heightened synaptogenesis and myelination and reaches a maximum at 4–8 years of age. Within the HCP-D age range (5–22 years), we capture the decline phase for blood perfusion following this extremum, which may be driven by synaptic pruning. Decrease in blood perfusion is more pronounced in transmodal cortex, likely due to the greatest overproduction and slowest elimination of dendritic spines in these areas.

Incorporating the HCP-A dataset, we also examine changes in blood perfusion during aging (36–100 years). We find greater age-related perfusion reduction in arterial border-zones, consistent with their reduced vascular density and diameter and increased embolic vulnerability. We show that increase in mean arterial pressure, BMI, glucose, and HbA1c, and a decrease in high-density lipoprotein (HDL) exacerbate blood perfusion reduction. Elevated glucose can lead to microangiopathy, atherosclerosis, and endothelial dysfunction, whereas high HDL exerts a protective effect on vasculature by suppressing vascular-LDL accumulation, inflammation, and endothelial damage. The tight coupling between plasma biomarkers and cerebral blood perfusion underpins the importance of considering body-brain interactions in the aging cohort.

To evaluate whether low baseline perfusion confers vulnerability to pathology, we examine the correspondence between disease atrophy patterns and the regional differences in blood perfusion. Regions with lower baseline perfusion show greater atrophy in late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD), dementia with Lewy bodies, and TDP-43C pathology. In contrast, early-onset Alzheimer’s disease shows no such association, possibly due to its weaker link with vascular risk factors compared to LOAD.

Last, we show that ASL data can be used to delineate arterial territories, including posterior circulation, anterior circulation, and border-zones. We further functionally annotated the arterial territories using an independent meta-analytic atlas. The resulting semantic space opens the possibility to link vascular impairments with physiological processes.
