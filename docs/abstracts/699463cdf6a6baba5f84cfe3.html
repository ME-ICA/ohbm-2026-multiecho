Introduction

Inherited Retinal Dystrophies (IRDs) are a heterogeneous group of genetically inherited disorders marked by the gradual degeneration of photoreceptors, ultimately leading to progressive vision loss. As retinal degeneration advances, compensatory changes may emerge across sensory, neural, motor, and psychological domains. However, the extent to which IRDs shape cortical responsiveness and the mechanisms underlying adaptive coping remain only partially understood. Previous electroencephalography (EEG)-based studies, for instance, have found weak or absent event-related potentials (ERPs) in response to stimuli presented within blind portions of the visual field, indicating reduced or absent functional processing in visual areas deprived of retinal input1.   

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) combined with EEG is a non-invasive technique for directly assessing cortical reactivity and investigating patterns of brain reorganization2. In this context, time–frequency analysis can quantify cortical synchronization dynamics across specific frequency bands in response to the TMS pulses as a function of time. When spectral power changes are calculated relative to a pre-stimulus baseline, the event-related spectral perturbation (ERSP) can be measured3.  

Despite its potential, TMS-EEG has never been applied to IRD patients. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to employ this approach to characterize cortical reorganization in patients with genetically confirmed IRD.   

Methods

Nine IRD patients affected by retinitis pigmentosa (RP) (5 females, mean age: 43.67, std age: 13.79) and eighteen healthy controls (HC) (13 females, mean age: 42.42, std age: 14.48) underwent a TMS-EEG session. The average symptom onset among patients was 20.78 years. Data were collected in accordance with protocols approved by the local ethics committee.   

TMS was delivered in a counterbalanced order over the left or right occipital visual cortex (localized using electrode positions O1 and O2, respectively) and over the primary motor cortex (identified by eliciting a twitch in the contralateral index finger) of the dominant hemisphere (with the exception of one IRD patient), to assess cortical excitability and connectivity. As shown in Figure 1a, a total of 80 single-pulse TMS per site was delivered at 110% resting motor threshold (rMT). EEG signal was recorded using a 64-channel system, while participants fixated on a central cross on a black background. EEG data were sampled at 5000 Hz with reference at the mastoids and ground at AFz. 

Preprocessing involved different steps, as shown in Figure 1b. Data were first high-pass filtered (>1Hz), and a Notch filter (49-51 Hz) was applied to remove line noise.  Then, data were epoched from -300 to 700 ms and baseline corrected considering -100/-2 ms interval.   After an initial manual artifacts rejection, the source-estimate-utilizing noise discarding (SOUND) algorithm was applied, followed by an independent component analysis (ICA). Signal-space projection-source-informed reconstruction (SSP-SIR) spatial filtering was subsequently applied to remove muscle artifacts and residual noise in the first 50 ms following the TMS pulse. Data were down-sampled to 500 Hz. A low-pass filter (<70Hz) was then applied, after which the data were re-referenced and baseline-corrected. Finally, bad trials were removed using the TBT approach.  

We analyzed TMS-EEG data through three different steps: 1) TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs) and global mean field power (GMFP), 2) time-frequency approach (i.e., ERSP), and 3) brain connectivity. TEPs were calculated for each participant and then averaged to obtain the grand average. Time-frequency analysis was performed to compute the ERSP. For each trial, a continuous wavelet transform (CWT) using the Morlet wavelet spanning 3–50 Hz was applied. The ERSP values were averaged across trials and baseline corrected by dividing the absolute ERSP by the mean spectral estimate from −300 to −100 ms, then expressed in decibel4. Finally, the global ERSP was obtained by averaging the ERSP over all channels.    

To assess brain connectivity, we used the weighted phase lag index (wPLI), which was computed for the theta, alpha, beta, and gamma frequency bands using the previously extracted wavelet coefficients5. We performed a seed-based analysis to evaluate how stimulation caused a phase shift in the brain network. For each stimulation site, electrodes O1, O2, and FC3 were used as seed channels, respectively, and their connectivity with all other electrodes was evaluated. wPLI values were then averaged every 50 ms within each frequency band. In addition, wPLI was computed for the pre-stimulus time window (−300 to −100 ms).  

Group differences in ERSPs and wPLI were tested in each targeted brain area using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p < 0.05), and a false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons.   

Results

Overall, TMS-EEG results revealed altered evoked activity in IRD patients following cortical stimulation, pointing to a reorganization within the affected cortical circuits.   

TMS-evoked potentials (Figure 2a). The grand average TEPs and the GMFP are shown in Figure 2a. We observed greater TEP amplitudes in IRD patients compared with HCs, particularly in the presence of severe visual impairment. A similar pattern was found not only following the stimulation of visual areas but also following the stimulation of the motor cortex. This may be associated with compensatory mechanisms and a global network reorganization, not only confined to the visual network. Conversely, no significant differences in TEP component latency between the two groups were found.   

Time–frequency responses (Figure 2b). ERSPs showed statistically significant group differences within specific time windows and frequency bands. Specifically: 1) in the case of left occipital (O1) stimulation, differences were found between 100 and 360 ms in the lower beta band, and between 192 to 400 ms within the alpha band; 2) following right occipital (O2) stimulation, significant ERSPs were primarily concentrated in the beta band until 236 ms post-TMS and in the alpha band from 106 to 336 ms; 3) stimulation of the primary motor cortex (M1) resulted in differences in the alpha band throughout the entire post-stimulus time window and in the beta band in a later time window (140-400ms). These findings indicate enhanced neural synchronization in patients compared to HC, reflecting a more widespread and sustained cortical response to TMS.  

Functional connectivity (Figure 2c). Compared to HC, phase-based functional connectivity dynamics showed an increase in IRD patients following the stimulation of all three target sites. In the alpha and beta bands, we observed a stronger post-stimulation hyper-synchronization, which is widespread and long-lasting following M1 stimulation, whereas it is limited to the time window between 50 and 100 ms following the right occipital stimulation. Furthermore, HC returned to baseline wPLI values more rapidly. Interestingly, seed-based wPLI values showed no statistical difference in the baseline time window when comparing patients with HC, suggesting that these connections may remain unchanged at rest.    

Conclusion

In this study, we explored, for the first time, how the brain adapts in IRD patients by means of a TMS-EEG approach, which enabled us to assess the activation of both local and long-range networks following stimulation of specific target sites.  

Compared to HC, IRD patients exhibited increased brain response following cortical stimulation in the absence of any external visual input. Remarkably, this enhancement was observed regardless of whether the stimulation targeted occipital or motor areas, possibly reflecting network-level plasticity triggered by progressive sensory deprivation. This supports the idea that visual loss can induce adaptive changes in non-visual cortical regions as well.  

Time–frequency analyses further highlighted patterns of hyper-synchronization in IRD patients. Post-stimulation increases in alpha and beta ERSPs were observed following both occipital and motor cortex stimulation, with M1 activation eliciting the most prolonged and widespread effects.   

Connectivity analyses revealed a globally increased phase-based coupling in patients. Notably, wPLI values before stimulation did not differ between groups, indicating that the observed changes are stimulus-evoked rather than existing at rest. Collectively, these findings highlight a dynamic and adaptive reorganization of cortical networks in IRDs.  

The enhanced synchronization and connectivity likely support compensatory processes across different domains, potentially facilitating visuomotor integration and behavioral adaptation. These results underscore the utility of TMS-EEG as a non-invasive tool to probe frequency- and time-specific markers of cortical plasticity in patients with progressive visual loss.  

The insights gained from our study enhance our understanding of sensory-driven plasticity and may inform the development of rehabilitation or neuromodulation strategies to improve functional networks.  In future studies, we plan to increase the sample size and to re-evaluate the patients 18 months after the initial assessment. From a methodological perspective, graph-based analyses will be performed to examine local and global network segregation and integration in IRD patients compared to HC.
