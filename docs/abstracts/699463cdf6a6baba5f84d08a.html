Introduction

Parkinson’s disease is associated with motor symptoms and non-motor symptoms such as neuropsychiatric symptoms and cognitive decline. Disruptions in the structural connectome are related to the symptom profile of Parkinson’s disease.1 However, effect sizes connecting structural connectivity to symptom severity are generally modest. Studies in healthy subjects have suggested that network communication models, that quantify inter-regional communication capacity via anatomical connections, can explain variance in activity fluctuations as measured with functional MRI.2 Examples of communication models include shortest path length, communicability and navigation. By taking into account putative paths of polysynaptic communication these models may better explain behavioral outcomes compared to the structural connectome, which only contains information about directly connected regions.  

Our aim was to study network communication in Parkinson’s disease and its association with neuropsychiatric and cognitive symptoms. We hypothesized that communication models better distinguish individuals with Parkinson’s disease from healthy matched subjects, compared to the structural connectome alone. We also hypothesized that lower communication efficiency is associated with more severe cognitive and neuropsychiatric symptom severity.

Methods

We used diffusion MRI data from 79 individuals with Parkinson’s disease and 29 matched healthy controls from the COGnitive Training In Parkinson Study.3 Using anatomically-constrained probabilistic tractography, we constructed structural connectomes based on diffusion MRI.4 We used two atlases with different granularities to assess stability of the findings (Schaefer 300P7N combined with Melbourne Subcortical Atlas S3 – 350 nodes; Desikan-Killiany atlas combined with aseg subcortical segmentation – 82 nodes). We thresholded the structural connectomes using Orthogonal Minimal Spanning Trees, resulting in connectomes with on average 1.15% (Schaefer-MSA)/2.46% (DK-aseg) density. Next, we constructed connectivity matrices of network communication using node-to-node shortest path length, communicability and navigation as measures of network communication,2 resulting in four connectivity matrices per atlas per subject (see Figure 1). We assessed differences in structural connectivity weights and network communication between healthy individuals, and individuals with PD in incremental stages of cognitive decline (normal cognition, mild cognitive impairment and dementia) using linear regression analyses. We used Pearson correlation to assess the associations between structural connectomes and communication models, and clinical variables. Lastly, we performed analyses with the Network-Based Statistic5 to distinguish subnetworks that showed differences between groups.

Results

The groups were well-matched on demographic variables. Healthy subjects showed better performance on neuropsychological tests and experienced less severe neuropsychiatric symptoms. As expected, neuropsychiatric and cognitive symptoms increased with incremental stages of cognitive decline (see Figure 2a). 

Healthy subjects showed higher network strength, global efficiency and average clustering coefficient compared with individuals with Parkinson’s disease (psS-MSA < .01; psDK-aseg < .05). Surprisingly, no group differences were present for communication model efficiency (all ps > .05; Figure 2b). Moreover, no group differences were present between cognitively impaired and cognitively intact individuals with Parkinson’s disease. Correlation analyses showed significant negative correlations only between structural connectome strength and efficiency, and communicability, and a neuropsychiatric symptom severity composite measure (ps < .05), but not with age, cognitive function or motor symptom severity.

To assess subnetworks showing differences between groups, we performed Network-Based Statistic analyses. We identified subnetworks that showed higher structural connectivity in healthy subjects compared with the Parkinson’s disease group. These networks included nodes in default mode, somatomotor, frontoparietal, limbic and subcortical subnetworks. For shortest path length, we identified one large, widespread subnetwork that showed lower communication efficiency in the Parkinson’s disease group, consisting of nodes in all Schaefer subnetworks. However, we also identified small subnetwork with higher communicability in individuals with Parkinson's disease, consisting of striatal nodes.

Conclusion

The structural connectome is disrupted in PD. Efficiency of polysynaptic communication is more intricately impacted by the disease, with subnetworks showing decreased but also subnetworks of increased communication efficiency. These results suggest that network communication is routed through indirect, polysynaptic paths that can compensate for the disrupted direct connections between nodes. Future studies should replicate these findings in larger Parkinson’s disease samples. Moreover, studies may assess how these compensation effects in communication efficiency in Parkinson’s disease impacts structure-function associations using functional neuroimaging modalities, to get a better understanding of the impact of Parkinson’s disease on these polysynaptic connections.
