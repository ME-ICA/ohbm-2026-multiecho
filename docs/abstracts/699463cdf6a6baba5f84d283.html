Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) is a promising treatment option for drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) patients ineligible for resective surgery. ANT DBS has been shown to reduce seizure frequency (SF) by 50-70% after five stimulation years.1,2 However, the clinical outcome of ANT DBS is highly variable between individuals, with around 30% of patients showing little or no SF reduction.1 SF reduction and possible side effects of the treatment depend on DBS programming parameters such as the location of the active contact,3 but we continue to lack markers to guide individualized DBS programming.

The PerceptTM PC (Medtronic Inc.) is the first commercially available neurostimulator able to both stimulate and record local field potentials (LFPs) from the chronically implanted DBS electrodes. LFP recordings combined with simultaneous noninvasive functional brain recordings such as magnetoencephalography (MEG) allow investigations of functional connectivity patterns between the thalamic DBS targets and various cortical regions, which could be useful for elucidating mechanisms underlying DBS effects and optimizing stimulation settings. This study measured simultaneous resting state MEG and thalamic LFP signals in DRE patients implanted with the PerceptTM PC neurostimulator, to assess the feasibility of such recordings and to characterize the spatial and spectral properties of the coherence between the ANT and various cortical regions during wakefulness and sleep stages.

Methods

16 DRE patients implanted with the PerceptTM PC neurostimulator participated in the study. The patients formed a heterogeneous cohort with a high degree of variability with respect to, e.g., epilepsy etiology, seizure onset zone, and anti-seizure medication. 5 patients were excluded from the final analysis due to unreliable LFP-MEG signal alignment or poor LFP signal quality.

Approximately 30 minutes of simultaneous resting-state MEG-EEG and LFP signals were recorded for each participant. The DBS stimulation was turned OFF during the recording, and the participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed and try to fall asleep. MEG was recorded with a 306-channel neuromagnetometer (Triux, MEGIN Oy) at the BioMag laboratory (Helsinki University Hospital). In total 6 bipolar LFP channels (3 per hemisphere; 250 Hz sampling rate) were recorded in the indefinite streaming mode (Fig. 1).

Spatiotemporal signal space separation (tSSS; correlation limit=0.90) implemented in the MaxFilterTM software (version 3.0, MEGIN Oy) was applied to the MEG data to suppress external magnetic artefacts and to compensate for head movements. Cardiac and ocular artefacts were suppressed using ICA for the MEG data, and using template subtraction for the LFP data. The LFP and MEG signals were synchronized in time using either transcutaneous stimulation artefacts, cardiac artefacts, or tapping artefacts induced in the beginning and end of the recordings. The two data sets were aligned using a linear transformation, to correct for the possible clock drift between the devices.

The data was divided into awake and sleep (NREM1 and NREM2) segments by visually inspecting the MEG-EEG signals. The segments were divided into 15 second non-overlapping epochs, and epochs containing artefacts or epileptiform discharges were excluded from the final analysis. Coherence was computed between every LFP channel and gradiometer sensor. The statistical significance was assessed using non-parametric permutation tests, where the indices of the LFP epochs were randomly permuted against the MEG epochs to destroy the temporal dependence between the epochs, but preserve the temporal fine structure in both data sets. Dynamic imaging of coherent sources (DICS)4 was used to compare coherence patterns for different LFP channels in source space.

Results

The sensor level coherence peaked in the theta frequency band (4-8 Hz) for both the left and right ANT. The permutation test also indicated significant coherence values in the alpha (8-13 Hz) and lower beta (13-20) frequency bands during both the awake and NREM1/NREM2 sleep stages. The peak theta band coherence decreased in most sensors when transitioning from the awake stage to NREM1 sleep, and the peak almost disappeared during NREM2 sleep (Fig. 2).

The theta band coherence was strongest in the frontal and temporal gradiometer sensors and seemed to be shifted towards the ipsilateral hemisphere. Similarly, the coherence in the alpha and beta frequency bands was strongest for the ipsilateral frontal sensors. The spatial coherence pattern remained highly similar when transitioning from the awake stage to NREM1 and NREM2 sleep, although the overall theta band coherence amplitude was reduced.

To determine if LFP-MEG recordings can distinguish different thalamocortical coherence patterns between different ANT LFP channels, we used DICS to examine the cortical sources coherent with LFP channels at different ANT depths. The analysis was performed for the theta frequency band since it showed the most prominent sensor-space coherence. The highest theta band coherence was generally found for the deepest LFP channel (0-1), while the most cranial LFP channel (2-3) had the lowest coherence values, which aligned with the sensor space analysis. DICS found coherent sources in medial prefrontal areas and medial temporal areas for every ANT LFP channel, which matches the anatomical connections of the ANT. The 0-1 and 1-2 LFP channels were coherent with more widespread brain regions including lateral prefrontal and lateral temporal areas. Thus, different ANT LFP channels have partially overlapping but at the same time distinct coherence patterns, with a high degree of interindividual variability.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that simultaneous MEG and LFP recordings with the PerceptTM PC neurostimulator are feasible. The results indicated significant thalamocortical coherence in the theta frequency band that depended on the participants' vigilance state. The theta band coherence was strongest during wakefulness and decreased when progressively transitioning to NREM1 and NREM2 sleep. Different ANT depths connected to distinct but still highly overlapping cortical networks that mainly included ipsilateral prefrontal and temporal regions. The thalamocortical coherence was generally strongest in the deepest LFP channels and lowest for the most cranial LFP channel. However, the anatomical lead locations may be highly variable between subjects. Future analyses should take into account the individual anatomical locations of the leads, to better understand the relationship between thalamocortical coherence and precise ANT location.
