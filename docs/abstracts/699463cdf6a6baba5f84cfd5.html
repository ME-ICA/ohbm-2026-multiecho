Introduction

Myalgic encephalomyelitis / chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a chronic complex illness of largely unknown aetiology and pathophysiology. ME/CFS symptoms are broad-reaching and vary between patients, but most commonly include persistent fatigue, muscle and joint pain, cognitive difficulties (commonly described as ‘brain fog’), and unrefreshing sleep.1 The hallmark feature of ME/CFS is the disproportionate worsening of these symptoms following even minor physical or cognitive exertion, known as post-exertional malaise (PEM). 

Despite its debilitating nature, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying ME/CFS remain poorly understood, including in the context of cerebral blood flow (CBF) and metabolic regulation. Previous neuroimaging studies have reported mixed findings, but trend toward reduced CBF, altered functional connectivity and inefficient task-related activations, but the reproducibility of findings in this area has been hampered by heterogeneous cohorts, small sample sizes, and methodological variability.2 Further exploration of perfusion dynamics, particularly under exertive conditions and subsequent recovery, is warranted.  

As part of the single-site multimodal MELOPIS (Myalgic Encephalomyelitis / LOng COVID and POTS Imaging Study), we employed pulsed arterial spin labelling (pASL) at 7T MRI in an ME/CFS cohort to probe CBF dynamics using a novel grip force task. By investigating a post-task recovery period alongside resting and task-related perfusion, we aimed to capture subtle cerebrovascular alterations that may contribute to the symptomatology of ME/CFS. 

Methods

All imaging took place at the Melbourne Brain Centre Imaging Unit (MBCIU), on a Siemens MAGNETOM 7T Plus MRI system with a combined single-channel transmit and 32-channel receive head coil. CBF was imaged using a pulsed arterial spin labelling (pASL) proximal inversion with control of off-resonance effects (PICORE) sequence (TR=3500 ms; TE=11.0 ms; bolus duration=700 ms; inversion time=1800.0 ms; 30 axial slices; flip angle=90°). Participants were of female sex, aged 18-70 and diagnosed with ME/CFS (n = 13) or healthy controls (n = 10). Inclusion criteria were based on the Canadian Consensus Criteria for ME/CFS patients, and all participants were screened prior to formal participation by a study clinician. Recruitment was achieved through public advertisement, ME/CFS registries, cardiologist clinics, physicians and/or clinical fellows. 

 

Arterial spin labelling (ASL) provides absolute quantification of CBF using an endogenous tracer (labelling of brain arterial blood water) and is used to examine cerebral blood flow in a direct, reproducible and non-invasive manner. For these reasons, it is a commonly employed method for investigating disease state-dependent cerebral perfusion.3 

Participants were asked to complete a task, depicted in Figure 1A, concurrently with the ASL sequence, comprising a simple paradigm in which participants squeezed an MR-compatible dynamometer (Cambridge Research Systems, Grip Force Transducer) with their right hand to 20% of their maximum force (measured with a separate device immediately prior to scan; Baseline Evaluation Instruments, Smedley Digital Hand Dynamometer) for 4 seconds, followed by a 4-second release period. This pattern was repeated 18 times. A visual feedback system provided live feedback to the participant, displaying a ball that moved upwards when force was applied, and downwards when force was released. The task period was preceded by a 150-second resting baseline and followed by a 300-second ‘recovery’ period. During both of these periods, participants fixated on a white cross on a black background. This motor task was selected to characterise CBF dynamics before, during and after a controlled cognitive-motor exertion in ME/CFS patients. The task was developed using Python in the PyCharm IDE. Behavioural force data were collected at a sampling interval of approximately 8 ms throughout the task period, and accuracy in the task was measured as the mean absolute error (MAE) of the difference (in Newtons) between the target force and actual applied force within each sampling window. An illustrative trace of this behavioural data is shown in Figure 1B. 

pASL data were processed using the FSL BASIL toolbox (FSL 6.0.7).4 Preprocessing included motion correction using MCFLIRT and brain extraction using BET. Label and control images were subtracted and quantified using a single-compartment kinetic model, producing CBF maps in units of mL/100g/min. CBF maps were analysed in native space for all reported data, except for the mean group maps which were registered to MNI standard space for visualisation. Regional CBF analyses were performed by BASIL; Harvard-Oxford cortical and subcortical standard space subregions were transformed to the native ASL space; voxels with a regional probability fraction > 0.5 were assigned to the corresponding subregion. Between-group differences in native-space GM, WM and regional CBF were assessed using Welch’s t-tests, with effect sizes reported as Cohen’s d. Between-group differences in task performance were assessed using Welch’s t-tests. Due to the preliminary nature of these data, analyses focused on estimation of effect sizes and directional group differences, rather than null-hypothesis significance testing; therefore, no multiple comparisons correction was applied.

Results

Participant demographics

Participants included 14 patients with ME/CFS and 10 healthy controls (all female sex). Groups did not differ significantly in age (ME/CFS: 40.5 ± 12.2 years; controls: 37.8 ± 10.8 years; p = 0.58); they showed a trend toward higher BMI in the ME/CFS group (25.8 ± 5.6 vs. 22.3 ± 2.4; p = 0.052), but this was not significant. As expected, the ME/CFS group reported higher fatigue severity over the previous six months (2.9 ± 1.0 vs. 1.6 ± 1.1; p = 0.0048, on a 4-point scale where 0 is least fatigue, 4 is most fatigue) and higher fatigue frequency (3.6 ± 0.8 vs. 1.6 ± 1.2; p = 0.00017, on the same scale as above) compared with controls, as measured by the DePaul Symptom Questionnaire (DSQ).


 

 

Grip force performance

Analyses indicated worse task performance (as characterised by the difference between target force and actual applied force during ‘squeezing’ phases of the task) in patients (MAE: 16.9 ± 5.1) than controls (MAE: 13.3 ± 3.7), however group differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.052).  

CBF in ME/CFS patients and controls across rest, task, and recovery periods

From visual inspection (Figure 2), mean CBF patterns were broadly similar between groups. The standard space-registered and smoothed images retained clear anatomical contrast, allowing visualization of cortical and subcortical regions. 


 The groups were indistinguishable via visual inspection of the CBF maps from the task period (Figure 2A). However, it is notable that the ME/CFS group appears to demonstrate higher CBF during the recovery period (Figure 2B), particularly in the task-relevant visuo-motor integrating left parietal lobe. 

Grey matter (GM)

Whole-brain grey matter CBF during the rest period was similar between groups, with controls showing 38.7 ± 9.4 mL/100g/min and patients 39.5 ± 10.4 mL/100g/min (Cohen’s d = −0.08). During the task period, CBF increased slightly in both groups (controls: 38.9 ± 8.6; patients: 40.1 ± 11.1; d = −0.12). Following task completion, recovery CBF diverged; controls demonstrated lower CBF when compared to rest (−0.67 ± 3.8), while patients displayed a slight increase (+0.73 ± 5.6), (d = −0.28). 

White matter (WM)

Whole-brain white matter perfusion showed a similar pattern to GM. At rest, mean perfusion was 27.4 ± 5.8 in controls and 28.6 ± 6.1 in patients (d = −0.20), increasing slightly during task (27.9 ± 5.5 and 29.0 ± 6.4; d = −0.19). In the recovery period, controls decreased slightly (−0.31 ± 2.8) while patients increased (0.67 ± 4.6), corresponding to a small-to-moderate effect size (d = −0.24). As in grey matter, recovery dynamics in white matter were opposite in direction between groups, despite the overall small magnitude of the differences.

Regional perfusion differences in recovery and rest

Exploratory statistical analyses of regional (Harvard-Oxford cortical and sub-cortical segmentations) CBF differences were performed to further examine CBF differences between post-task recovery and pre-task rest periods. Reflecting the visual inspection of mean CBF maps (Figure 2B), the inferior parietal lobe (IPL) displayed directional differences in CBF. In the parietal operculum (PO) and supramarginal gyrus (SMG), ME/CFS participants displayed higher perfusion and controls displayed lower perfusion in the recovery period compared to rest (PO: mean of +2.79 ± 1.85 vs. −4.99 ± 2.48; Cohen’s d = 0.97; mean number of voxels: 90.5 vs. 88.8; SMG: +4.18 ± 2.22 vs. −2.75 ± 1.64; d = 0.75; mean voxels: 102.5 vs. 84.3). Similar patterns were observed in the temporooccipital division of the middle temporal gyrus (+3.48 ± 2.03 vs. −1.79 ± 1.21; d = 0.64; mean voxels: 157 vs. 175), and the subcallosal cortex (+3.68 ± 2.14 vs. −2.34 ± 1.87; d = 0.62; mean voxels: 183.0 vs. 183.9).

Conclusion

Visual inspection of standard space-registered CBF maps indicated broadly similar perfusion patterns between groups during the pre-task rest and task period. The images retained recognisable anatomical features, despite the limitations of low SNR and resolution. Importantly, native space statistical analyses corroborated these visual impressions; resting and task-related CBF was similar between groups in both GM and WM. These findings are in contrast to some previous literature2 identifying resting hypoperfusion in ME/CFS patients, perhaps attributable to the small sample size at this stage, and the relatively short 150-s rest period. During task completion, both groups displayed modest increases in CBF, reflecting the metabolic demands of the task activity. Following task completion, recovery CBF diverged with small effect sizes. Controls exhibited small decreases relative to pre-task rest while the ME/CFS group showed a small mean increase, across GM and WM. These patterns suggest that ME/CFS may be associated with alterations in cerebrovascular regulation or metabolic recovery, contrasting with the appropriate downregulation of perfusion in controls following the completion of the task. However, given the small nature of these effects, further investigation is required.

Exploratory regional analyses provided further insight into these patterns. Directional differences were observed in inferior parietal and temporooccipital regions, as well as the subcallosal cortex. ME/CFS participants showed increased post-task recovery perfusion in these areas when compared to resting perfusion, whereas controls exhibited decreases in these regions. These parietal areas are implicated in visuomotor integration, attention, and sensorimotor processing, aligning with the task-related demands and suggesting potential regional inefficiencies or compensatory perfusion responses in ME/CFS. Interesting, the subcallosal cortex has been implicated in autonomic regulation, affective processing, and the integration of motivational and homeostatic signals5. Although the task employed in this study was motor in nature, elevated CBF in this region during recovery may reflect broader network-level engagement or compensatory mechanisms related to fatigue, effort perception, or autonomic recovery in ME/CFS. While our findings are preliminary, they suggest that altered perfusion in these regions may accompany the subtle post-exertional changes and warrant further investigation in larger cohorts with complementary imaging modalities.

It is important to highlight the preliminary nature of these findings; the sample size is modest, the fidelity of anatomical localisation following registration of low-resolution ASL data to standard space for more detailed analysis requires further exploration, and regional analyses were exploratory and uncorrected. Nevertheless, the initial observations from the study thus far suggest the potential importance of post-task recovery measures in this cohort, which may yield cerebrovascular dynamics that are not evident in resting or exertive periods in isolation. 

In conclusion, our results suggest that global resting and task-related CBF is comparable between ME/CFS patients and healthy controls, but subtle differences may appear during a post-task recovery period, especially when compared to resting baseline levels. These findings underline the utility of 7T ASL in probing dynamic CBF in this context. As the study continues recruiting a larger cohort, including participants with Long COVID and ME/CFS with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), further analyses of whole-brain, tissue-specific, regional and network perfusion will be undertaken, together with a comprehensive examination of associations with symptom severity and task performance.
