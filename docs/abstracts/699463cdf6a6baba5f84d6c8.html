Introduction

Assessing someone else’s pain represents a deeply challenging inferential process, that relies on something inherently invisible and intangible. To date, scientific research struggles to identify biomarkers capable of reliably quantifying pain. For instance, while individuals often rely on facial expressions during everyday interactions, these signals are not systematic and generalizable indicators of pain, and can be suppressed, exaggerated, or even simulated entirely. Altogether, pain assessment can be conceptualized as an inferential process inherently imbued with uncertainty, and vulnerable to prior beliefs and expectations. Predictive coding models offer a theoretical framework to investigate these influences, by formalizing uncertainty as the inverse of priors’ precision. Early evidence suggests that uncertainty may heighten one’s own pain1, while leading to underestimation of the pain of strangers2, although results remain controversial. On a neural level, regions like the anterior insula (AI) and middle cingulate cortex (MCC) have been associated with pain, pain empathy3, but also with expectancy/uncertainty across pain, economic and perceptual tasks4. This opens the question as to whether the neural responses to others’ pain indeed reflect pain empathy, or a much more complex mechanism of inference under uncertainty. Across multiple experiments, we used predictive coding models to systematically investigate, at the behavioral and neural level, the role of expectancy and uncertainty in taking other-oriented, pain-related decisions, such as assessing the intensity of the pain experience, or distinguishing between real and simulated pain expressions.

Methods

This project involves two paradigms designed for fMRI, each based on behavioral experiments. 

Task 1 investigated how expectancy and uncertainty affects pain-related decision-making for others, at both behavioral and neural levels. In Experiment 1 (see Loued-Khenissi et al.2, as behavioral version of the same task), 36 participants were engaged in a decision-making task, where they were informed that another person was about to undergo a painful stimulation at a given probability. They then chose between a gamble to avoid pain altogether or a sure option to reduce it. After receiving feedback about the other’s experience, they provided a subjective pain rating on a visual scale. The information about pain intensity and probability was used to estimate both average expected pain (EP) and uncertainty (UN, quantified as the variance of possible outcomes). Task 2 investigated the role of expectancy and uncertainty in the assessment of both spontaneous or simulated pain facial expressions (from a database5, independently validated by a pilot study with 20 participants). Here, we followed the paradigm from Yoshida et al. 1, and displayed as predictive cue a visual analogue scale showing ten pain-ratings associated with the same painful event. The average rating provided by these cues represented EP, whereas their standard deviation represented UN. Across two subsequent studies (Experiments 2-3, N = 30 each), participants were first shown the predictive cue, followed by one video-clip. Participants then rated how painful and authentic the observed pain was. In Experiment 3, we also included a small percentage of validation trials in which no video followed the predictive cue. Instead, participants rated how certain they felt about the expected pain implied by the cue distribution. These validation trials provided a reliable sanity check of the efficacy of our manipulation, allowing us to verify whether certainty ratings decreased linearly with UN. 

General statistical approach: We used a model-based approach, where information about EP and UN was estimated in each trial based on predictive information, and then modeled as a function of behavioral and neural responses. Subjective ratings were analyzed through linear mixed-effects models, with participants’ identity specified as random factor. Neural activity was acquired through a 3T Siemens Prisma MRI scanner. Functional images were analyzed with SPM12 software (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) through standard preprocessing pipeline, first-level general linear models, where the parametrical modulation of relevant expectancy parameters were specified as parametric modulators, and subsequent group-level analyses. Effects were considered significant if surviving p < 0.05 FWE whole-brain cluster correction for multiple comparisons (with an underlying height threshold corresponding to p < 0.001 uncorrected).

Results

Behavioral responses. On a behavioral level, both paradigms identified a clear effect of EP. In Experiment 1, higher EP led to more frequent risk-averse behavior, involving selection of the sure option (vs. gamble), whereas in Experiments 2-3 it led to higher pain ratings, consistently across real and simulated pain expressions. Experiment 1 also found an effect of UN, leading to higher risk-averse choices and longer Reaction Times, while no effect of UN emerged in Experiments 2-3.

Validation Trials. The analysis of validation trials from Experiment 3 showed that participants felt less confident in trials where cues were associated with higher UN, thus validating the efficacy of the experimental manipulation. Based on the linear dependency between predictive cues and subjective certainty ratings (in validation trials) we obtained a subject-specific estimate of these ratings also in the main trials, and used them as predictors instead of UN. Estimated certainty ratings influenced the assessment of spontaneous (but not simulated) facial expressions in interaction with EP. Notably, when EP was low, higher subjective certainty predicted higher perceived authenticity of the real pain expressions.

Neural activity. On a neural level, results from Experiment 1 revealed that UN influenced the anticipatory activity prior to the exposure to others’ pain, by increasing at the level of AI, MCC, and striatum. Acquisition of neural responses for the paradigm of Experiments 2-3 is still ongoing.

Conclusion

The present study investigated how expectancy and uncertainty influence other-oriented, pain-related decisions, using predictive coding models. We employed two paradigms relying on either probabilistic decision-making or visual stimuli showing a distribution of previous pain assessments. Despite their difference, they both allowed to assess the role of EP and UN in pain-related responses. At the behavioral level, we found convergent evidence of EP in pain decisions, but no systematic effect of UN was observed, even if the validity of our approach was insured (Experiment 3). Some aspects of authenticity ratings were instead explained by subjective certainty, thus possibly suggesting that our predictive coding manipulation (albeit valid) might not capture entirely the complexity of the uncertainty construct. At the neural level, UN was associated with anticipatory activity occurring before pain-related events at the level of structures traditionally associated with pain and pain empathy (AI and MCC) as well as the striatum. Overall, this study provides novel evidence of the cognitive and neural mechanisms by which expectancy and uncertainty shape the inferential process of assessing someone else’s pain.
