Introduction

Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is increasingly recognized as a network disorder, yet the network mechanisms underlying epileptic activity remain poorly understood. Interictal spikes produce both focal activations and widespread deactivations 1,2, but whether these patterns follow specific network architectures is unknown. Understanding the network topology underlying spike-related activity could reveal critical insights into epilepsy pathophysiology and inform therapeutic targeting. Drawing on recent network mapping approaches that identify brain networks from heterogeneous pathology patterns 3, we applied functional network mapping to characterize brain networks associated with spike-related BOLD changes in drug-resistant TLE patients.

Methods

We analyzed simultaneous EEG-fMRI data from 16 drug-resistant TLE patients. Spike-related BOLD response maps were identified using general linear model (GLM) analysis with spike onset informed regressors, modeling multiple hemodynamic response functions to account for variable neurovascular coupling. For each patient, peak activation and deactivation clusters were identified and expanded to 300-vertex ROIs. Overlap maps were computed across patients to identify convergent spike-related regions. To map the network architecture of these regions, we computed their functional connectivity with all cortical vertices in 72 healthy controls using resting-state fMRI, thresholding at the top 20% of connections to identify convergent network patterns.

Results

Spike-related BOLD changes showed distinct spatial patterns. Activation overlap was concentrated in temporal regions, with strongest convergence in the lateral temporal cortex including superior and middle temporal regions. In contrast, deactivation overlap showed prominent convergence in posterior cortical regions, particularly the parietal and occipital areas. Network mapping in healthy controls further revealed the functional architecture underlying these patterns. Regions showing convergent functional connectivity with spike activation sites exhibited a distributed pattern, encompassing temporal and parieto-occipital regions, suggesting that activation sites are embedded within multiple overlapping cortical networks. Notably, regions connected to spike deactivation sites showed highly specific convergence within the default mode network (DMN), including posterior cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal cortex. This spatial dissociation indicates that while spike-related activations propagate through diverse cortical systems, deactivations preferentially target the intrinsic connectivity architecture of the DMN. 

Conclusion

Our findings reveal that spike-related brain activity follows specific network architectures. Specifically, activations propagate through distributed cortical networks while deactivations selectively target the DMN, demonstrating network-specific rather than random organization. This network perspective advances our understanding of how interictal spikes affect distributed brain systems and may inform network-based approaches to surgical planning and therapeutic targeting.
