Introduction

The relation between spontaneous and task-evoked brain activity is one of the major open questions in neuroscience. While most studies have focused on the similarity of spatial patterns between rest temporal correlation and task activation maps, a few studies have focused on the question of whether spontaneous activity patterns represent information states, as captured by multi-voxel patterns of activity. For instance, using multivoxel pattern analysis and fMRI, Kim et al.1 showed that spontaneous activity patterns in category-preferring visual areas (e.g., fusiform face area, FFA, for face stimuli) are more like those evoked by the preferred, rather than non-preferred visual stimuli. Also, resting activity patterns resembling task activation patterns for scrambled stimuli containing contours and oriented lines, but not whole objects, reactivated more commonly in areas V1/V2; conversely, activity patterns for whole objects reactivated more frequently at rest in occipital extra-striate cortex. Livne et al.2 showed that activity patterns evoked by frequently performed hand movements are more represented at rest in motor areas than uncommon movements. Recently, Zhang et al.3 extended this analysis to show that whole-brain co-activation patterns associated with familiar hand movement are preferentially re-activated during rest.

Overall, these findings show that task-evoked patterns are selectively “re-activated” at rest both in visual cortex for visual stimuli and in motor/association cortex for movements. A recently developed theoretical framework proposes that these reactivations represent information states that work as “priors” for anticipating upcoming stimuli and actions.4 If reactivations represent priors, they shall not be confined to specialized areas, but be widely present across cortex. To find evidence in favour (or against) this hypothesis, we re-analysed Kim et al.’s1 dataset searching for re-activation for whole objects beyond visually-related areas.

Methods

Experiment. The experiment is described in Kim et al., 2020.1 Sixteen healthy participants underwent 2 sessions of MRI acquisition in 2 separate days, during which they underwent resting-state, localizer, and event-related scans as described in Fig.1a. During the localizer and event-related scans, participants were presented with visual stimuli belonging to 9 categories: 7 whole-object categories (face, body, mammals, chairs, tools, scenes, words) and 2 scrambled categories (grid-scrambled and phase scrambled).

Experimental Design. A total of 5 resting-state runs (fixation; 300 TRs) were collected before any stimulus was presented to record spontaneous activity (Fig. 1a). During 4 localizer block-design runs (350 TRs), participants were presented with stimuli in a block-design way. Each run contained a randomized sequence of 8 blocks (7 stimulus category and 1 fixation block), each block containing 20 images of a single category. During 16 event-related runs (315 TRs), participants were presented with a total of 96 stimuli for each of the 9 categories.

Imaging parameters: acquisition was performed through a Siemens 3T Prisma MRI scanner for both structural (T1-w MP-RAGE, 1x1x1mm voxels and T2-w fast spin echo, 1x1x1mm voxels) and fMRI scans (gradient echo-planar sequence, 2.4x2.4x2.4 voxels, TR=1.0s).

Preprocessing: As described in Kim et al., 20201 each participant’s anatomical image was linearly registered to MNI152 atlas. fMRI data followed standard preprocessing steps including slice timing correction and removal of distortion. Anatomical surfaces for each subject’s T1 MRI (FreeSurfer automated segmentation) were generated. Left and right hemispheres were resampled to 164,000 vertices to perform surface processing of functional data. Standard nuisance regressors (white matter, ventricles, 6 movement parameters, global signal) were used, together with motion scrubbing.

Analysis. The quantification of similarity between stimulus-evoked activation pattern and resting-state signal was computed at the subject level. For each participant a GLM was used to determine the average whole-brain prototypical activation pattern (AP) for each stimulus category (Fig.2b left). Through a searchlight approach a sphere of radius 10mm around each vertex was defined. Within each sphere, the stimulus-evoked AP for a category was correlated across vertices with the resting-state AP on each single frame of the resting scans (Fig.1c). This results in an evoked-AP-to-rest correlation timeseries for each category in each sphere (Fig.1c). To quantify the task-rest AP similarity, the correlation coefficients of each sphere were organized in a frequency distribution and the 90th percentile (U90 value) was used as an index of the degree to which each evoked AP is represented at rest (Fig.1c). Higher U90 values reflect a higher number of resting-state AP that are spatially similar to the evoked AP. The U90 value computed within each sphere was assigned to the central vertex. The whole procedure led to obtaining, for each subject and category, a vertex-wise map of U90 values that represents the alignment between category-specific and spontaneous AP (Fig.2a).

Results

Our methodology allowed computing category- and subject-specific whole-brain reactivation maps (U90 maps). These maps showed high reactivation in several brain regions beyond stimulus-specific, visual processing areas (Fig.2a top left). 

We first addressed the specificity of maps for different categories. While each participant had regions showing high reactivation only for a specific category (e.g., tools), we also observed a set of brain areas displaying higher U90 values for all the visual stimuli presented, regardless of the category. These "common reactivation" areas clearly emerge from mean U90 maps across categories (Fig.2a top right). 

To remove inter-individual variability, we averaged individual maps - both the category-specific maps and the mean map across categories. This yielded group-level, category-specific reactivation maps (Fig.2a bottom left) and “grand average” reactivation map (Fig.2a bottom right). The grand average map shows a “backbone” of areas reactivating all visual stimuli (independent of category), with prominent peaks in the primary visual cortex and in dorsal parietal regions.

To better highlight the category-specific reactivations, we produced a contrast map (“deltaU90 map”) by subtracting grand average values from the group-wise category-specific U90 maps (Fig.2b left). These maps allowed identifying brain regions reactivating preferentially a specific category. Interestingly, this analysis detected not only previously identified category-preferring regions (e.g., FFA for faces; parahippocampal place area for scenes), but also regions outside the visual processing hierarchy, including motor and prefrontal areas. For example, the reactivation map for “tools” (Fig. 2b left) displayed reactivation spots not only in occipito-temporal regions, but also in precuneus, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, motor cortex and dorsoparietal cortex. 

We asked whether category-specific reactivation maps would align with the corresponding activation maps (“activation patterns”, AP). To this aim, we computed the Pearson correlation between the prototypical AP of each category (Fig.2b right) and category-specific deltaU90 maps (Fig.2b left). The highest correlation was consistently observed between deltaU90 and AP maps corresponding to the same category (Fig.2c, Fig.2d). However, the alignment was partial, as deltaU90 maps showed a tendency to include a more widespread set of regions compared to AP, which were more confined in posterior regions.

Conclusion

According to the previously mentioned theoretical framework4 spontaneous activity may subserve a predictive role, anticipating upcoming stimuli and keeping the brain prepared for future interactions with the environment. In line with this theory, one would expect spontaneous reactivations of task-evoked patterns involving many areas across a perception-cognition-action axis instead of remaining isolated within specific stimulus-processing areas.

While a thorough assessment of this theory is well beyond the scope of our work, our findings support one of its main predictions by showing that re-activations of visual stimuli occur in a distributed set of regions throughout the cortex. 

Our findings raise many questions that should be addressed in future research, such as: the relationship between reactivation networks and the common resting state networks, whether the “backbone” of areas presenting non-specific reactivations has any functional significance or reflects systematic confounders, the temporal coordination of reactivation in different regions, and whether a similar phenomenology arises with other sensory modalities.
