Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease disrupts large-scale brain network dynamics, yet most studies rely on static connectivity measures that overlook temporal fluctuations. Dynamic functional network connectivity (dFNC) provides richer insight but is often limited by inconsistent state estimation across individuals.^1 This work introduces a constrained ICA framework that enforces state correspondence across subjects, enabling individualized dFNC fingerprints associated with mild cognitive impairment (MCI).

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were decomposed into subject-specific functional networks using spatially constrained ICA (scICA), which aligns components with known templates while preserving individual variability.^2 Dynamic connectivity was estimated using a sliding-window correlation approach. ICA applied to the resulting dFNC matrices identified five canonical states in 1,000 UK Biobank participants.^3 These states were used as biologically informed priors in a state-constrained ICA (St-cICA) applied to OASIS-3, BLSA, and ADNI datasets. St-cICA produced subject-specific dFNC states and weighted timecourses used to compute occupancy rates, dwell times, and state-specific connectivity profiles.

Results

The five UKB-derived states captured distinct functional configurations, ranging from negative sensory-network states to high-connectivity patterns linking sensorimotor, visual, default-mode, cognitive-control, and cerebellar systems. Compared to cognitively normal (CN) individuals, MCI participants showed stronger coupling between sensorimotor and default-mode networks and reduced connectivity within visual and cerebellar networks.^4 CN groups exhibited stronger default-mode and subcortical–visual interactions, as well as higher occupancy rates across several states. MCI individuals demonstrated consistently longer dwell times, reflecting reduced neural flexibility.^5

Conclusion

The constrained dFNC framework identifies robust, state-specific connectivity alterations associated with early cognitive impairment. Patterns of occupancy and dwell time highlight reduced neural adaptability in MCI, providing a dynamic fingerprint of Alzheimer’s-related network dysfunction. This scalable approach supports subject-level dFNC profiling across multisite datasets and contributes to the development of early neuroimaging biomarkers for Alzheimer’s disease.
