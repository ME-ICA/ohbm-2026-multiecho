Introduction

The global demographic shift towards an aging population necessitates novel, holistic approaches to understand and mitigate NeuroCognitive Decline (NCD)1. Traditional analyses focusing on single-risk-factors have consistently demonstrated limited predictive power due to the complex, non-linear interactions within the environment-lifestyle-biology nexus—the exposome2. Moving beyond these limitations, the Exposome provides a crucial, cumulative paradigm for assessing individual neurocognitive health trajectories. We hypothesized that individuals stratify into distinct, high-dimensional exposure patterns, or exposotypes, and that these specific topological patterns are strongly and differentially predictive of the individual's rate of NCD. This study aimed to (1) identify and characterize these exposotypes using an unsupervised topological clustering method, (2) quantify their differential risk on the NCD trajectory, and (3) determine the rank-based, heterogeneous priority of specific modifiable factors driving transitions between neurocognitive health states. This methodology establishes a critical new framework for precision exposomic intervention in neuroscience.

Methods

Population: The study analyzed a prospectively followed cohort3,4 of n = 1118 adults 40+ years old (569 females; mean age = 71.04, std = 10.99; mean education = 13.91 years); cognitively unimpaired (CON = 444, 39.71%); with mild (MCI = 393, 35.15%) and major neurocognitive disorders (ADRD = 281, 25.13%)

NCD estimation: Neurocognitive aging was estimated using NeuroCognitive Age (NCA), a multimodal biofunctional marker designed to quantify the discrepancy between an individual's chronological and neurobiological age (DNCA). NCA is calculated as a weighted mean of two components: Brain Age and Cognitive Age. Brain Age is estimated from T1-weighted MRI morphometric features (including ≈100 volumetric, cortical thickness, and surface area measures) using a Support Vector Regression (SVR) model trained on n = 6909 neurotypical adults (CON, 18-100 years old). Cognitive Age is similarly derived from a comprehensive battery of neuropsychological scores (MoCA, Verbal Fluency, BNT, RAVLT, Stroop) using an SVR model trained on n = 5254 (CON, 18-97 years old adults). The final estimate is rigorously controlled, incorporating residual bias estimation and correction steps (see5 for more details).

Exposomic data: A set of 24 modifiable exposomic factors was curated to avoid multicollinearity. These factors spanned four key domains: Biological/Metabolic (e.g., Blood Sugar Control, Inflammation, Blood Pressure), Lifestyle/behavioral (e.g., Sleep Quality, Physical Activity, Alcohol intake), Psychological/Social (e.g., Social Support, Psychological Wellness, Life's Purpose), Environmental (e.g., Pollution and Noise Exposure). All variables were standardized (Z-scores) before analysis to ensure equivalent contribution to the clustering algorithm.

Exposotype identification: We employed a two-level Self-Organizing Map (SOM), an unsupervised artificial neural network, to overcome the limitations of linear modeling in high-dimensional space. The SOM projected the 24-dimensional exposomic data onto a 7 x 7 (49 nodes) 2D topographical map. This grid size was formally determined by simultaneously minimizing the quantization error (to ensure data fidelity) and the topographical error (to accurately reflect the high-dimensional structure). The SOM grouped participants into distinct nodes based on the similarity of their multivariate exposomic profiles (exposotypes). Subsequently, an unsupervised k-means clustering technique was applied to the SOM's weight vectors to aggregate these 49 nodes into three clinically relevant, hierarchical clusters representing exposome ranks: Rank 1 (R1, Low risk), Rank 2 (R2, Intermediate), and Rank 3 (R3, High risk). This ranking was validated based on the significant divergence of the NCD outcome (∆NCA) across the three clusters (p < .001; Figure 1).

Priority analysis: The relative importance of each factor in driving the transition between ranks was calculated using the difference in the factor's mean value between the nodes defining the origin and destination ranks, scaled by the factor's global standard deviation. This was performed for two critical "Phases": the De-escalation Phase (risk mitigation: R3 to R2) and the Optimization Phase (optimal health: R2 to R1). Divergence was analyzed by comparing these factor priorities across sex and age subgroups.

Results

Exposome stratification and NCD: The SOM successfully partitioned the cohort into 49 distinct exposotypes, which aggregated into three robust Ranks (Figure 1B): R1 (Low-risk: 45% of population; mean ∆NCA = +1.7, min-max [-10.8;+6.7], 80.7% CON); R2 (Intermediate: 23% of population; mean ∆NCA = +5.5, min-max [-3.9;+12.9], 67.1% MCI); and R3 (High Risk: 32% of population; mean ∆NCA = +9.2, min-max [+4.8;+20.1], 78.1 ADRD). The difference in ∆NCA across ranks was highly significant (F(2, 1115) = 89.2, p < .001). R3 was characterized by a specific exposomic signature marked by poor Social Support (Z~-2 S.D), high C-Reactive Protein (CRP)-derived Inflammation and Fatigue (mean Z>+1.5 S.D.), while R1 showed optimal scores across all psychological and physical domains (Figure 1C). 

Priority factors for rank transition: The analysis of factor contribution (relative importance) revealed a distinct prioritization scheme for each phase, confirming the need for phase-specific interventions (Figure 2A).

- For the De-escalation Phase (R3 to R2), the top three modifiable factors were Social Support (Relative Importance: 19.5%), Cognitive Stimulation (11.5%), and Nutrition Quality (10.2%). These factors—largely socio-environmental—represent critical leverage points to stabilize the highest-risk group and reduce the ∆NCA acceleration.

- For the Optimization Phase (R2 to R1), the top three factors required to achieve optimal health were distinct, being more internal and behavioral: Sleep Quality (14.8%), Psychological Wellness (8.8%), and Life's Purpose (8.2%). These results suggest that fine-tuning intrinsic lifestyle factors is key for achieving cognitive resilience (optimal ∆NCA).

Heterogeneity in priority by Sex and Age: Significant and clinically meaningful divergence in factor prioritization was observed across demographic groups, underscoring the limitations of general guidelines. 

Analysis of the most significant between-sex divergences (|Male-Female| >= 3) reveals a fundamental difference in the hierarchy of necessary interventions for neurocognitive health (Figure 2B). For Women, interventions must prioritize socio-emotional well-being and life quality optimization. Social Support is the most powerful lever, showing a divergence of -8.8 points. Progression to low risk is heavily dependent on Sleep Quality, which displays a divergence of -5.9 points. Psychological Wellness (-4.9) and Life's Purpose (-3.4) confirm this preponderance of internal and social levers. Risk mitigation for Men is predominantly dictated by addressing toxic exposure and reducing high-risk health behaviors —Alcohol Intake (+3.6), Smoking Status (+3.5) and Physical Activity are the most effective intervention domains for moving them out of the very high-risk cluster. These behaviors remain influential factors for men in the optimization phase (R2 to R1).

The most effective levers are intrinsically dependent on the stage of life (Figure 2C). In individuals aged 70 and over, risk de-escalation is dominated by factors related to cognitive reserve and environment. Preventing social isolation (Social Support) is the most powerful factor for moving out of high risk, displaying a notable divergence of +9.0 points in favor of the older group. This population also benefits from targeted intervention on vascular control and physical function. The divergences of Blood Pressure (+3.0) and Physical Activity (+3.0) confirm the necessity of social and physical support to maintain resilience in older age. For the group under 70, interventions must focus on managing the burden of lifestyle and internal energy. The major obstacles to risk improvement are exhaustion and stress: Fatigue and Psychological Wellness present divergences of -3.5 and -3.0 respectively. Furthermore, Smoking Status displays a divergence of -3.0 in the optimization phase.

Conclusion

Our findings confirm, through a robust topological analysis, that the neurocognitive exposome is not a unitary construct but comprises distinct, quantifiable exposotypes, stratified into ranks that predict clear and divergent neurocognitive trajectories. The use of the SOM was pivotal in achieving this non-linear stratification, revealing relationships and interactions that linear models would overlook, and enabling the robust estimation of factor contribution to inter-rank transitions. Crucially, the prioritization of modifiable factors is highly phase-dependent—with de-escalation requiring changes primarily in external/social factors, and optimization depending on the refinement of internal/behavioral factors—and profoundly demographically heterogeneous. The highly significant divergence in factor priority observed between age and sex subgroups directly challenges the efficacy of current generalized guidelines and strongly validates a personalized intervention strategy. This framework represents a critical advancement in exposomic research, moving beyond simple correlational analysis to identify causality-relevant, actionable points of intervention. The most compelling translational implication of this study is the capacity to model personalized intervention pathways. By accurately mapping an individual's current high-dimensional exposotype onto the established SOM topology, we can conceptualize the necessary steps for improvement. The transition from a high-risk state (R3) to an optimal state (R1) is thus framed as finding the minimal topological distance or "shortest path" on the map. This path conceptually identifies the minimum necessary set of factor changes that, when implemented, are predicted to transition the individual's exposomic profile to a more favorable rank. This allows clinicians to target the most sensitive and relevant factors, maximizing patient compliance and impact. Ultimately, this study provides a novel, data-driven methodology for precision public health and neuroscience, directly translating complex, high-dimensional exposomic data into actionable, individualized intervention targets for NCD mitigation, opening the door for truly personalized neurocognitive aging prevention.
