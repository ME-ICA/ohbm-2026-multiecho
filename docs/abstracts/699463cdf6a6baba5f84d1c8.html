Introduction

Understanding the directional organization of spontaneous brain activity is essential for linking intrinsic neural dynamics with large-scale cognition and for explaining failures in neurological disease. Classical theories propose that sensory and attentional systems broadcast information toward higher-order integrative networks, yet empirical evidence quantifying directionality across macro-scale circuits remains limited. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging signals display scale-free dynamics and long-range temporal dependencies that are not captured by standard linear approaches. To overcome these limitations, we adapted a multivariate fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (mfOU) process, originally developed for financial volatility modeling, to characterize both fractal temporal structure and asymmetric “spillovers” of influence among cortical and subcortical systems. We hypothesized that brain networks would segregate into orchestrators (net transmitters) versus integrators (net receivers) in a manner consistent with hierarchical functional organization, with unimodal and attentional systems acting as broadcasters and transmodal/limbic systems acting as information sinks.

Methods

We analyzed 7T resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data from 173 healthy adults in the Human Connectome Project. The brain was parcellated using Yeo’s 7-network cortical atlas and Tian’s subcortical atlas (hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, nucleus accumbens). For each region, blood oxygenation level dependent time series were assessed for stationarity via Augmented Dickey-Fuller tests and for fractality via the change-of-frequency estimator. We fit an N-dimensional mfOU model per subject using Minimum Distance Estimation, extracting (i) Hurst exponents, (ii) diffusion coefficients, (iii) speed of mean reversion, (iv) contemporaneous correlation matrices, and (v) lagged-correlation–based asymmetry matrices.

Spillover analysis was performed on the mfOU-implied cross-lag dependence structure, using the asymmetry matrix and the model’s autoregressive kernel as inputs to the forecast-error variance decomposition (FEVD). For each region i, FEVD partitions the variance of the h-step-ahead forecast error of its fractional dynamics into contributions from all other regions j, yielding pairwise spillovers Sij. From these, we derived transmitted, received, and net spillover indices that quantify each region’s directional influence balance. Network-level transmitter/integrator communities were identified via consensus k-means clustering (1,000 repetitions). Group-level effects were assessed with Friedman tests and Bonferroni-corrected comparisons. Robustness was evaluated using structural similarity metrics (SSIM) and comparison to Vector Autoregressive (VAR) models, which served as a Markovian baseline to determine whether the observed spillover architecture and transmitter/integrator roles persisted under short-memory dynamics.

Results

All regions exhibited stationary dynamics (all Augmented Dickey-Fuller p<0.01) with heterogeneous fractal signatures. Subcortical structures consistently showed Hurst exponents <0.5, reflecting rougher temporal profiles, whereas cortical networks clustered around H≈0.5 except for the limbic system (H>0.55). 

Directional spillover analysis revealed a strong region effect bilaterally in the subcortex (left χ2=687, right χ2=692; both p<0.0001). Among subcortical structures, the anterior thalamus, caudate, and putamen displayed the largest net transmitted spillover values, whereas the globus pallidus exhibited the strongest received spillover (all Bonferroni-corrected p<0.001). Clustering analyses confirmed this pattern, identifying a coherent orchestrating subcortical group composed of anterior thalamus, caudate, and putamen, and an integrator group composed of posterior thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala, nucleus accumbens, and globus pallidus.

For cortical networks, we observed a significant effect of network on spillover magnitude (left χ2=718, right χ2=731; both p<0.0001). Along the orchestrator-integrator axis, the dorsal-attention network exhibited the highest net transmitted spillover, whereas the limbic network showed the strongest net received spillover (p<0.0001). Consensus clustering revealed two large-scale communities: (i) an orchestrating cluster composed of dorsal-attention, ventral-attention, visual, and somatomotor networks, and (ii) an integrator cluster composed of default-mode, frontoparietal control, and limbic networks. These communities were observed in more than 95% of clustering iterations and were highly consistent across subjects (SSIM=0.96-0.99). 

VAR models reproduced the same cortical and subcortical orchestrator/integrator division, indicating that the hierarchy of directional influence is stable across modeling choices, although only the mfOU model captured the empirical signals’ power-law decay of autocorrelations and long-memory temporal structure.

Conclusion

Multivariate fractional modeling reveals a robust, hierarchical integration-orchestration architecture in intrinsic brain activity. Sensory, motor, and attentional systems function as the main broadcasters of information, whereas transmodal and limbic systems act as integrators that accumulate and transform incoming activity. Subcortical nuclei exhibit a parallel segregation, with the anterior thalamus, caudate, and putamen acting as principal drivers and hippocampal-amygdalar complexes serving as attractors of network influence. These findings provide quantitative evidence supporting theoretical models of hierarchical information flow, where orchestrating systems gate environmental inputs and integrator systems shape internal models of the world. Beyond foundational insights, this fractional framework offers a powerful tool for clinical neuroscience: by capturing temporal complexity and directionality, mfOU-based spillovers may help identify network-level signatures of neurological impairment, including aberrant driver–receiver balance in disorders such as stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, and gliomas. The approach thus bridges theoretical neurobiology, advanced time-series modeling, and translational connectomics.
