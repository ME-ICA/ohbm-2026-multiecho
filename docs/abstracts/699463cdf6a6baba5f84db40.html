Introduction

Neural bursts, brief episodes of heightened oscillatory activity, are increasingly studied as fundamental building blocks of brain function, with relevance to perception, cognition, and disease1,2. As such, detecting and characterizing these bursts in electrophysiological recordings has driven rapid methodological innovation in neuroscience research. However, the growing number of analysis techniques can be overwhelming, making it difficult for researchers to select the most appropriate method for their specific goals. Thus, the current work aims to provide a structured and practical guide for neuroscientists to measure and report neural burst data.

Methods

We provide a high-level overview of the most widely used single- and multi-channel detection methods, outlining their theoretical foundations, practical implementations, and pragmatic trade-offs. To summarize this information, we present a decision-making framework to help researchers align method selection with their research question and experimental design.

We further provide practical tutorials and worked examples to illustrate the respective performance of the featured methods (see https://github.com/lindseypower/BurstDetection_Tutorials/tree/main). All examples are based on ten minutes of resting-state, eyes-open MEG data from the Open MEG Archive (OMEGA)3. MEG data were cleaned and single or multi-channel time series were extracted for burst detection analysis. For single-channel analysis, the same beta-band (15–30 Hz) signal from the right sensorimotor cortex was used for all methods. Bursts were detected from the extracted one-dimensional time series using five standalone burst detection methods: amplitude thresholding, PAPTO, eBOSC, cycle-by-cycle analysis, and sliding window matching. For multi-channel analysis, three methods were employed: CDL, microstates analysis, and HMM. For each method, 6 spatiotemporal patterns (e.g., states, atoms) were extracted from multi-channel, beta-band MEG data. Comparative analysis was conducted across methods.

Results

Figure 1 presents a general decision-making framework designed to help narrow the field of available methods to those most appropriate for a given context. Starting at the top of the tree, users follow each branch by answering a series of guiding questions. Branching paths lead to recommended method classes (coloured boxes) that best align with key analytic considerations such as number of channels, spatial specificity, waveform assumptions, and desired output features. This framework is not intended as a prescriptive rule set, but rather as a flexible guide for navigating the methodological space.

Comparative analysis of single-channel methods demonstrates that method outcomes reflect each method’s implicit definition of a burst. Amplitude-based procedures (amplitude thresholding, PAPTO, eBOSC) favour high-power transients, whereas waveform-centric procedures (Cycle-by-Cycle, Sliding Window Matching) often capture lower-power but shape-consistent events. Methods that use Morlet transforms tend to select wavelet-like waveforms, while others admit flatter or more symmetric oscillations. Simpler algorithms generally yield more detections; more elaborate models emphasize specificity. Overlap is greatest among methods that share similar computational assumptions.

Across multi-channel methods, spatial patterns co-occur with frequency profiles associated with both broad and narrowband beta power. Several of the CDL and microstate solutions concentrate on central sensors, indicating sensorimotor contributions and aligning with the expected spatial distribution of resting-state beta bursting. Other solutions concentrate on posterior sensors, indicating occipital contributions which may reflect a higher-frequency component of the dominant occipital alpha oscillations commonly observed at rest. HMM states, derived in source space, are less directly comparable but similarly show posterior and central involvement typical of resting-state networks. 

Conclusion

The methodological landscape for burst detection is both expansive and nuanced. Selecting an appropriate approach for a given research or clinical application requires careful consideration of factors including signal characteristics, analytic goals, and computational constraints. Aligning these considerations with study objectives will help ensure transparent, reproducible, and conceptually coherent research. By doing so, investigators can more effectively harness burst detection to advance our understanding of the transient and dynamic nature of brain activity, and the neural signatures that underpin cognition, behaviour, and disease.
