Introduction

Ultra-low field MR imaging has emerged as a powerful technology for improving global access to neuroimaging. Its portability, low cost, and reduced energy requirements make it a suitable option for point-of-care applications and deployment in middle and low-resource settings, where high-field scanners remain unavailable, impractical, or scarce. These systems have also been increasingly adopted in paediatric research, enabling studies of early brain development in communities that have been largely marginalised in neuroimaging research.1 By lowering barriers to MRI access, ultra-low-field MR imaging offers a pathway towards more inclusive neuroscience and clinical care worldwide. 

In parallel, network neuroscience has revolutionised our understanding of brain organisation, by modelling it as a complex network of interconnected regions. Structural network approaches, including morphometric similarity, have provided critical insights into brain development in health and disease. Among these, Morphometric Inverse Divergence (MIND) networks represent a recent advance in this field, leveraging distance-based metrics to capture subtle shape variations and structural dependencies across the cortex.2 MIND, as well as other brain modelling approaches, have shown promise in characterising individual differences, clinical phenotypes, and development across the lifespan,3 but their applications to date remain limited to high-field MRI data. 

This gap raises an important question: does ultra-low-field MRI capture morphometric network features consistent with high-field benchmarks? Addressing this question is essential for extending network neuroscience to resource-limited settings, as well as harmonising output in multi-site studies using various scanner technologies. In this work, we systematically evaluate the robustness of MIND networks derived from ultra-low and high-field images of the same adult brains. 

Methods

Design: We recruited 23 healthy adults (20–69 years), balanced for age and sex (≥2 males and ≥2 females per decade). Participants underwent two imaging sessions within 36 days. Session 1 was performed at King’s College London's Centre for Neuroimaging Sciences (CNS) on a 3T GE SIGNA Premier system, acquiring T1-weighted MPRAGE and T2-weighted sequences (1 mm3). Immediately after, participants were scanned on a 64 mT Hyperfine Swoop ® system using a custom isotropic T1-weighted protocol (2.3 mm3). Session 2 was undertaken at Evelina Newborn Imaging Centre (ENIC) on an identical Hyperfine Swoop ® system with the same acquisition protocol and software version. This design allowed us to isolate scanner effects while controlling for protocol consistency. Scanners were labelled as GE (3T), HFC (Hyperfine at CNS), and HFE (Hyperfine at ENIC). 

Pre-processing: Low-field T1-weighted MR images were super-resolved to 1mm3 using SynthSR 4  and processed alongside high-resolution GE scans using FreeSurfer recon-all (v7.2.0) for cortical reconstruction and segmentation. Structural similarity MIND networks were generated using five morphometric features: cortical thickness, mean curvature, sulcal depth, surface area, and gray matter volume. These networks are constructed by estimating the similarity between pairs of brain regions, calculated through the Kullback-Leibler divergence of their multivariate morphometric feature distributions.2  

Analysis: We obtained 23 x 3 MIND networks in total (three per subject: GE, HFC, HFE) (Fig 1). Scanner agreement was assessed by computing Pearson’s r and Lin’s Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) for each corresponding edge across all pairs of scanners. These edge-wise values were then averaged to obtain a mean ± SD for each scanner pair, reflecting overall linear similarity and concordance. Reliability under fixed scanner conditions was quantified using Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC (3,1).

For each subject and scanner pair, we computed the symmetric percent difference (SPD) on the upper‑triangle edge weights to quantify proportional differences between networks. For a high‑field (HF) and a low‑field (LF) edge value, SPD was calculated as: SPD = 100 * ( xLF - xHF) / ((xLF + xHF) / 2)

To assess network sensitivity to individual differences, we calculated differential identifiability on the MIND similarity networks for each pair of scanners. Differential identifiability was defined as Idiff = (Iself – Iothers) * 100),5 where Iself the mean correlation of scans from the same subject across LF-HF or LF-LF pairs, and Iothers is the mean correlation between different subjects. A higher Idiff indicates stronger subject-level identifiability.

Results

For each scanner pair, we computed Pearson’s r and Lin’s CCC for each edge across subjects (using the vectorised upper triangle), then summarized the edge-wise values (mean ± SD) to reflect overall agreement at the network level. Correlations and CCC values between both LF-derived networks were particularly high (HFE–HFC: r = 0.954; CCC = 0.950), indicating near-perfect agreement. Comparisons between LF and HF networks also demonstrated strong consistency (GE–HFC: r = 0.833; CCC = 0.775; GE–HFE: ρ = 0.828; CCC = 0.760) (Fig 2A). The intraclass correlation coefficient indicated substantial reliability across scanners, with ICC (3,1) = 0.719 (95% CI: [0.53, 0.86]). Symmetric percent differences (SPD) were modest, with median differences of –13.18% (IQR = 27.70) for GE–HFC and –14.16% (IQR = 28.01) for GE–HFE, suggesting minimal bias and acceptable network variability across scanner pairs of different field strengths (Fig 2B). Negative medians reflect slightly higher edge weights in HF networks, but the relatively narrow interquartile ranges suggest that discrepancies are not widespread. SPD between the pair of networks from LF scanners was negligible, with a median value of -1.15% (IQR = 14.19%)

Figure 2C illustrates the identifiability matrices, where diagonal values represent within-subject correlations and off-diagonal values represent between-subject correlations. All measures were Fisher z-transformed to enhance visualization. Identifiability was high within the LF scanner pair (HFC–HFE), with all but two subjects’ networks correctly matched to themselves. For identifiability across LF and HF, Iself remained higher than Iothers on average, although the difference was reduced compared to within LF scanners. 

Conclusion

Overall, these findings collectively indicate that morphometric brain networks derived from ultra-low-field MRI exhibit strong correspondence to high-field acquisitions. The combination of high correlation and agreement coefficients, substantial ICC values, and modest SPD supports the robustness of weighted network topology across field strengths, with clear overlap to the high-field reference standard. Importantly, identifiability analyses demonstrate that ultra-low-field MRI captures meaningful, subject-specific information that remains stable across sessions. While subject-level identifiability was higher within low-field strengths compared to across field strengths, the subject identifiability diagonal remained visually distinct, indicating some preservation of individual-specific patterns. These results challenge assumptions about the limitations of low-field imaging and highlight its potential for reliable individual-level connectivity analyses. 

This supports the broader adoption of ultra-low-field MRI for scalable neuroimaging applications. Robustness remains to be validated in younger cohorts or those with brain abnormalities. These efforts will contribute to widening the inclusivity of advanced MRI analysis methods geographically and demographically, benefiting the quality of international research and ultra-low-field MRI consortia.
