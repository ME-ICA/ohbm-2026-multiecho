Introduction

The sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the autonomic nervous system are essential to human thermoregulation. Within the central nervous system, autonomic thermoregulatory processes are most likely established from a combination of dynamic and nested interactions between distributed brain networks 1. These interactions are likely to occur not only at the 2nd order (conventionally between pairs of regions), but also at a higher order (i.e., similarities in the co-influences of regions across the network)2. The latter class (henceforth higher order connectivity or HOFC) has been relatively understudied but can provide insight into how brain regions work cooperatively (or antagonistically) across the entire network to support complex function, and how different effects distinguish sympathetic from parasympathetic conditions. Here, i) we acquired fMRI data while participants were exposed to a whole-body thermoregulatory challenge that alternatively evoked sympathetic responses (moving core body temperature away from the expected basal state) or parasympathetic responses (allowing core body temperature to return toward the expected basal state); ii) from the fMRI time series, we estimated HOFC by emulating a previously published pipeline3; iii) these HOFC values were submitted for linear SVM-based classification to distinguish between the two conditions. Our results (the first of their kind) showed a majority of salient features for classification were region pairs characterized by higher HOFC (i.e., played more cooperative roles across the network).  

Methods

The Human Investigation Committee of Wayne State University authorized all study procedures. Consent was obtained from 61 adults (mean age: 25.1 + 3.4 years, age range: 20 - 31 years). Body mass index (BMI) and body fat % were in the normal range (BMI = 22.7 + 2.1 kg/m2, body fat % = 24.0 + 4.3%), subjects were unmedicated and free of neurological or psychiatric disorder. fMRI was acquired using a 3T Siemens Verio with a 12-channel volume head coil (TR: 2.6s, TE: 29ms, FOV: 256x256mm2, acquisition matrix: 128x128, 36 axial slices, voxel dimensions: 2 x 2 x 3mm3). During fMRI we used an oscillating thermal challenge; subjects wore a specialized whole-body garment through which they were exposed to cold or neutral temperature water. Body temperature was monitored using an MRI-compatible GaAs crystal sensor located at the tip of an optical fiber cable (temperature was recorded at 30 s intervals). The paradigm consisted of two 5-minute cooling periods interspersed within warming periods (5 min) that allowed the system to return to homeostatic baseline (25 minutes). 

MR images were preprocessed using the FSL pipeline (Version 6.0.7.18). From each subject’s functional scan, and for each image, we calculated the raw average fMRI signal in each of 246 cortical regions of the Brainnetome atlas and the 16 regions in the brainstem as outlined in the Harvard AAN atlas. Next, time series were formed from extracting the BOLD data from each of the 262 regions. Onset vectors (from changes in body temperature) were used to identify time intervals indicating Sympathetic (body temperature falling) and Parasympathetic (body temperature rising) conditions. Next, in each condition we estimated 2nd order undirected functional connectivity for each participant (HOFC, based on zero lag correlations between all pairs of regions)3. These conventional 2nd order features represent the condition evoked synchronization of activity between pairs of brain regions. Each correlation coefficient was transformed (Fisher’s z) to stabilize the variance and normalize the feature distribution. This transformation ensures that differences in the transformed space more consistently represent the strength of the connectivity relationships across the distribution of values. The vectorized upper triangle of the FC matrices were classified using linear kernel support vector machines (SVM) which allow for interpretation of the feature weight vector post hoc4 (Figure 1). SVM’s were scored using the area under the receiver operator curve (AUC)5. This metric measures the likelihood of the SVM to rank a random member of the positive class as over a random member of the negative class. Because this metric is independent of thresholds, it is more robust than a raw accuracy score when measuring a model's generalization. Optimal hyperparameters were determined by use of 5 repeats of 5-fold stratified cross validation. The parameters with the greatest mean AUC were then subjected to a 200 repeat 5-fold CV to validate the performance and provide a distribution of scores. This distribution of scores is then used to confirm that the model’s accuracy was statistically significantly greater than a baseline model. Next, we generate a distribution of feature weights by retraining the SVM on 1000 resamples of the training data and extracting weight vectors each iteration. We can then use a 95% confidence interval to determine which features are stable (which feature weights have the same sign 95% of the time or more). Features with relatively larger weights have greater impact on model performance. We then average the weight for each feature and used a modified z-score (Mean Absolute Deviation, MAD) to determine which feature weights have magnitudes which are significantly larger than the rest (MAD >= 3.5). Features which were both outliers and stable were deemed significant and are the focus of the present analysis.

Results

Our best performing SVM scored an average AUC of 0.785 with a standard deviation of 0.0058 across all folds of a 200 repeat 5-fold stratified CV. In addition, the minimum AUC was measured at about 0.583. As all scores were well above the expected baseline of 0.5 our model performance was statistically significant warranting further investigation of the specific features that had the largest impact on performance. We extracted weight vectors from SVM’s fit on 1000 resamples with replacement of the training data to generate a distribution of each features weight. The sign of a features weight indicates the experimental condition (Sympathetic of Parasympathetic) that the SVM associates larger feature values (HOFC coefficient's) with. Therefore, features were determined to be stable if the weight of the feature maintained the same sign in 95% or more of the resamples. 5,851 features were stable, and we averaged the weight of each feature. We then calculated the modified-Z score (Mean Absolute Deviation, MAD) for each feature and to determine which features had outlier weight magnitudes (|MAD| > 3.5). Roughly 1% of features (332) yielded a MAD value that was greater than or equal to 3.5 and these were determined to be statistical outliers, that is highly discriminative. Each outlier was expectedly stable. These 332 features were characterized in the remaining analyses. Figure 2 presents these results. 

Significant features were divided into categories based on the average sign of the feature’s HOFC in both conditions and on which condition evoked greater HOFC. With this we determined that the vast majority (~95.5%) of significant features belong to one of two types: 1)  Type 1 (108 out of the 332 significant features) were those which on average showed evidence of greater cross-network cooperation during the parasympathetic condition (greater HOFC and positive sign during the parasympathetic condition); 2) Type 2 (207 out of the 332 significant features) were those which on average showed evidence of greater cross-network cooperation during the sympathetic condition (greater HOFC and positive sign during the sympathetic condition).  

As seen in Figure 2, a disproportionate number of Type 1 features were associated with regions in the occipital lobe, with high density of connections between regions within the occipital cortex, and between regions in the occipital and parietal cortices (~3.46% and 3.41% respectively). In comparison, a disproportionate number of Type 2 features were associated with regions in the sub-cortical nuclei, with a high density of connections between regions in the subcortical nuclei and the insula (5.10%).  Figure 2c and 2f illustrate the density of significant features in each gyrus pair.

Conclusion

We provide unique evidence for HOFC features that distinguish between brain network configurations evoked during whole-body thermoregulatory challenge. Our results revealed that regions in the sub-cortical nuclei had a disproportionate presence in the salient features associated with the sympathetic condition. Conversely, regions in the occipital lobe had a disproportionate presence in the salient features associated with the parasympathetic condition. These results underline the importance of capturing complex higher order features of brain network interactions in the human brain during thermoregulatory challenge. The indicate context-dependent brain-wide influences of the sub-cortical nuclei (during whole body cooling) and the occipital lobe (during whole body rewarming).
