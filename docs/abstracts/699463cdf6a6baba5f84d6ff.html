Introduction

Intrinsic neural timescales (INT) quantify how long information persists in local neural activity and provide a principled index of temporal hierarchy across the cortex[1-3]. While prior work has established robust INT gradients at rest [4-6], it remains unclear how cognition dynamically reshapes this temporal organization. Understanding task-evoked modulation of INT is crucial for linking large-scale dynamics with flexible behavior and for determining whether the temporal hierarchy is preserved or reconfigured during active processing. Here, we systematically characterize whole-brain INT across seven HCP tasks and compare them with resting-state INT. We further test whether task-evoked INT remains constrained by the underlying structural connectivity (SC), thereby assessing how anatomical architecture shapes functional timescales under varying cognitive demands. Additionally, we incorporate criticality-based modelling frameworks to evaluate whether the observed task-evoked modulation of INT can be explained by the general shifts in distance to criticality[5,7].

Methods

We analyzed fMRI data from 339 Human Connectome Project (HCP) participants across rest and seven tasks (Memory, Gambling, Motor, Language, Social, Relational, Emotion). INT was computed for each cortical region as the area under the curve of the autocorrelation function [4-6]. Group-level INT maps were derived for each task and contrasted with the rest. To test structure–function coupling, regional INT values were correlated with nodal strength derived from individual structural connectivity matrices.

 To situate the empirical findings within a dynamical framework, we incorporated a structure-constrained dynamics model informed by criticality theory. Empirical structural connectivity matrices were used to couple the regions within the model, providing the anatomical constraints that shape large-scale neural interactions. Two dynamical formulations were implemented: (i) a Kinouchi–Copelli branching-process model[8], and (ii) a whole-brain Hopf bifurcation model[9], each characterizing how regional activity slows and the influence of structural constraints changes as the system’s distance to criticality varies. These models served as a theoretical reference for interpreting task-evoked modulation of INT and INT–SC coupling.

Statistical significance was assessed using t-tests and permutation testing, with multiple-comparison control applied across tasks.

Results

Across tasks, INT maps showed robust and systematic reconfiguration relative to the resting state (Figure 1A). Compared with rest, INT decreased during Memory (t = –0.65, p = 0.52) and Language (t = –2.60, p = 0.0096), whereas Gambling (t = 3.23, p = 0.0013), Motor (t = 5.38, p < 0.0001), Social (t = 14.67, p < 0.0001), Relational (t = 7.20, p < 0.0001), and Emotion tasks (t = 5.70, p < 0.0001) elicited significant INT increases. Despite task-specific modulation, the large-scale temporal hierarchy was preserved, with sensory areas consistently exhibiting shorter INT and association networks exhibiting longer INT (Figure 1B).

INT–SC coupling was significantly positive across all conditions and increased during all tasks relative to rest (r = 0.78, p < 0.0001). Task-evoked coupling values ranged from r = 0.60 to 0.74 (Figure 1C), with Emotion showing the strongest coupling (r = 0.74, p < 0.0001), followed by Memory (r = 0.68, p < 0.0001), Gambling (r = 0.67, p < 0.0001), Relational (r = 0.66, p < 0.0001), Language (r = 0.64, p < 0.0001), Social (r = 0.62, p < 0.0001), and Motor (r = 0.60). Although coupling remained positive for all tasks, indicating preserved anatomical constraints, the lower coupling observed during certain tasks—especially Motor and Social—suggests reduced structural dominance over timescales during cognitive engagement.

Modelling analyses based on the Kinouchi–Copelli and whole-brain Hopf frameworks reproduced two central predictions of criticality theory: (i) intrinsic timescales increase and peak at the critical point, and (ii) SC–INT coupling decreases toward zero as the system approaches criticality, reflecting diminishing structural constraints near the critical regime. Within this dynamical framework, the persistence of positive SC–INT coupling across all tasks indicates that the brain remains in a pre-critical domain where structural constraints continue to shape functional timescales. In contrast, task-evoked increases in INT reflect how close neural dynamics operate to the critical regime, with larger INT values corresponding to states that lie nearer to criticality.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that INT undergoes distinct and task-specific modulation across cognitive domains, yet the global temporal hierarchy of the cortex remains stable. Task engagement consistently enhances structural–functional coupling, suggesting that the brain relies more strongly on its structural architecture when sustaining temporally extended computations. Within dynamical frameworks, positive SC–INT coupling suggests that neural activity under tasks remains in a pre-critical domain, whereas task-evoked increases in INT reflect how closely neural dynamics approach the critical regime. Together, this work provides the first comprehensive characterization of task-evoked INT reorganization and establishes INT as a powerful metric for understanding how cognition reshapes large-scale neural dynamics.
