Introduction

Structural MRI can capture cortical folding in humans, consisting of bumps called gyri separated by grooves (or sulci). While the main sulci are generally identifiable in all individuals, their shapes vary greatly. Folding occurs mainly in utero, and the shapes of the folds thus fixed could contain a number of neurodevelopmental biomarkers. In this work, we introduce Champollion, a self-supervised deep learning model trained on 42,000 subjects from the UkBioBank1 database to characterize the variability of folding.

Methods

To generate an input for the deep learning framework, the MRI is first affinely registered to the MNI space and a folding skeleton is extracted using BrainVisa/Morphologist. This skeleton is made of surfaces following the middle and deepest points in the folds, orthogonally to the brain hull. The skeleton is meant to be invariant to sulcal opening (since it has no width) and age (mostly reflected in the folds by their opening), effectively capturing each brain’s folding signature.

Inspired by how neuroanatomists use sulcal atlases to study folds, Champollion breaks down the cortex into regions of interest (ROIs), each of which is modeled independently. Each ROI is defined to encompass one or few of the main folds as defined in the BrainVisa/Anatomist nomenclature. The pipeline is made label-free using a small external dataset of 60 subjects with manually labelled sulci, which are used to define bounding boxes in MNI space containing the sulci of interest for each new subject. In this work, the whole brain was covered using 56 overlapping ROIs. The model produces representations of each ROI that (i) are inherently interpretable because they are directly linked to an ROI, (ii) can be studied using simple linear models to link local variability to biological or clinical factors.

A training strategy common to all ROIs was adopted following co-optimization across four ROIs, using a linear evaluation of the local representations for four tasks related to sulci, chosen to capture the typical variability of cortical folding. These include (i) classification of types of sulcal interruptions, (ii) detection of the presence or absence of a sulcus, and (iii) regression of continuous shape descriptors (Figure 1). The tasks were selected to ensure both the relevance of the coded representations and their generalization: they have already been associated with behavior and pathology, they cover cortical regions of varying size and complexity, and they were performed on external databases whose population and acquisition protocols differ from those of the training data.

Model pretraining uses a self-supervised strategy based on the Barlow Twins cost function. For a given batch of images, two views of each image are generated by random data augmentations. The cost function pushes views from the same image to have similar representations, thereby building augmentation-invariance and abstraction, while minimizing redundancy between latent variables.

Results

Optimisation enabled us to demonstrate the superiority of a configuration designed for brain folds over conventional configurations for natural images. We selected a lightweight convolutional network with 12 layers and 3 million parameters, which had one distinctive feature: the 'global pooling' layer, which is typically used at the output of convolutions, was replaced by a 'flatten' layer followed by a dense linear layer. As our data exhibited spatial normalisation, it was beneficial to maintain a correspondence between descriptors and spatial position. We coupled this with mild positional data augmentations (translation and rotation): the global translation equivariance typical of convolutional networks was replaced by local invariance, which better reflected our data. The impact of the positional augmentations was demonstrated in an exhaustive ablation study. When coupled with cutout-type masking, significant final gains were observed on optimisation tasks compared to a conventional crop-resize-based configuration (+20.9% R² on the shape regression task). The final performance on the four tasks described in Figure 1 are as follows: #1: 93.9% AUC; #2: 82.8% One-vs-Rest AUC; #3: 91.1% AUC; #4: 55.5% R². Finally, we used a simple decoder to verify that the shape of the folds was correctly encoded, even in the most complex regions. This demonstrated the generalisation of Champollion to all cortical regions (Figure 2).

Conclusion

We designed a deep learning model for characterizing local cortical folding shapes in any ROI and showed its ability to retrieve hand-labeled patterns in multiple brain regions through a simple linear classification in the representation space, demonstrating its semantic richness. Furthermore, a visualization of the reconstructed folds confirmed the fine-grained encoding of the main folds’ shapes. While manual pattern annotation is time-consuming and limited to simplified, human-defined descriptors that cannot fully capture the complexity revealed by self-supervised latent spaces, we hope that Champollion will pave the way for a more systematic and comprehensive study of cortical folding patterns as biomarkers.
