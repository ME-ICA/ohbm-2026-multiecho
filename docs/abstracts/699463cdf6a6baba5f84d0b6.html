Introduction

Co-pathology is a common feature of neurodegenerative diseases that complicates diagnosis, treatment, and clinical management. Misdiagnosis rates of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) are around 25–30% even in specialized dementia clinics and can exceed 50% in primary care1. Meanwhile, comorbidity is common in aging, with 70% of patients aged ≥80 years harboring multiple neurodegenerative pathologies simultaneously2. Misdiagnosis can make it difficult to select the right patients for a drug trial, while comorbid neuropathologies can mask the positive effects of a putative therapy3. The first step toward mitigating these issues is to develop biomarkers. However, sensitive, specific, and scalable biomarkers are not available for most neurodegenerative neuropathologies.

Plasma proteomics enables robust measurement of thousands of candidate biomarkers in a single blood draw. Despite great promise as biomarkers, proteomics are high-dimensional, complex, nonlinear, and contain burdensome technological artifacts. In this study, we attempt to overcome these limitations by applying AI to the Global Neurodegenerative Proteomics Consortium (GNPC)4, the largest neurodegenerative proteomics dataset to date. We propose Proteomics-AI for Dementia Diagnosis (ProtAIDe-Dx), a deep multi-task model to resolve differential and multiple neurodegenerative diagnoses (Fig. 1A).

Methods

We included 17,187 GNPC patients and controls (age = 70.3±11.5 years, 53.2% female) with SomaLogic 7k plasma proteomics data. We predicted controls and five major late-life neurodegenerative conditions: AD (n=2,287), PD (n=1,637), FTD (n=175), ALS  (n=435), and stroke  (n=317). For external validation in BioFINDER-2, we selected 1,786 participants (age = 68.6±12.1 years, 49.2% female) with plasma SomaLogic 7k proteomics and neuroimaging data. 

A 10-fold cross-validation was employed on GNPC, with 8 of 10 folds used as training, one fold as validation, and the remaining one as test (Fig. 1A). When generalizing the model to BioFINDER-2, we preserved BioFINDER-2 as the test set to avoid leakage, given that BioFINDER-2 is a participating site in GNPC. We employed a feature selection step with differential abundance analysis and XGBoost to reduce input dimensionality (Fig. 1A), using nested cross-validation to avoid leakage. The architecture of ProtAIDe-Dx was a multilayer perceptron (Fig. 1A). We specifically chose a multi-task, joint-learning approach to allow the model to signal disease co-pathology (i.e., positivity for multiple disorders). Baseline models were Random Forest, XGBoost, and TabPFN5. 

We compared the probability of AD diagnosis from GNPC individuals to factors known to be related to AD, namely cognitive performance and APOE ε2 and ε4 allele carriage. We also evaluated whether baseline proteomics embeddings from ProtAIDe-Dx’s last layer were able to differentiate longitudinal clinical dementia rating (CDR) progressors (advancing from CDR 0 to >0, N = 218) from non-progressors (CDR 0 at all visits, N = 1,445).

In the external BioFINDER-2 cohort, we examined whether proteomics embeddings could improve differentiation of biomarker-confirmed AD (n=231), PD (n=111), FTD (n=39), and stroke (n=20) patients by training SVM models with different feature sets. We then explored whether baseline predictions of ProtAIDe-Dx could help differentiate longitudinal cognitive decline with linear mixed-effects models. We also assessed the correspondence between predicted probabilities and imaging/CSF biomarkers. Lastly, we provided a proof-of-concept application, showcasing how ProtAIDe-Dx could be integrated into clinical management.

Results

ProtAIDe-Dx achieved a median balanced classification accuracy (BCA) above 90% for ALS (95%) and PD (92%), 83% for controls, 81% for AD, 72% for FTD, and 70% for stroke (Left panel, Fig.1B). It significantly outperformed Random Forest across all tasks, outperformed XGBoost in AD (FDR-corrected p = 5e-4), FTD (FDR-corrected p = 3e-4), and stroke (FDR-corrected p = 0.004) classification, and significantly outperformed TabPFN in FTD classification (FDR-corrected p = 0.047). All models except Random Forest achieved AUCs > 0.8 for all tasks other than stroke prediction (Right panel, Fig.1B). Sanity checks revealed that APOE ε4 (Fig. 1C) and worse MMSE scores (Fig. 1C) were linked to higher AD probabilities, while ε2 was protective, and ProtAIDe-Dx baseline embeddings predicted longitudinal CDR progression (AUC 0.74 ± 0.05, Fig. 1E).

For differentiating biomarker-confirmed etiologic diagnoses (Fig. 2A), Model 3, which combined ProtAIDe-Dx with common clinical biomarkers, significantly outperformed ProtAIDe-Dx alone (Model 1) and clinical biomarkers alone (Model 2). Notably, baseline predictions from ProtAIDe-Dx significantly stratified longitudinal cognitive decline trajectories. Specifically, individuals predicted as AD exhibited faster cognitive decline than those predicted as Control, PD, or stroke (FDR-corrected p < 0.05; Fig. 2B1) in GNPC, whereas baseline clinical diagnoses failed to differentiate (unadjusted p > 0.05). Similarly, in the external BioFINDER-2 cohort, MCI patients predicted by ProtAIDe-Dx as AD declined more rapidly than those predicted as controls (FDR-corrected p = 0.0015; Fig. 2B2).

We observed strong correlations between predicted probabilities and conventional AD-related imaging/CSF biomarkers (Fig. 2C). Specifically, when the AD probability exceeded 0.9, most patients were tau-positive by TauPET or CSF p-tau217 and showed cortical thickness below the diagnostic threshold. Likewise, when the stroke probability exceeded 0.7, most patients exhibited elevated white matter hyperintensities. As a proof of concept, we showed an exemplar case presenting with subjective cognitive decline but intact cognition. ProtAIDe-Dx indicated a high probability of both AD and PD, which was confirmed by advanced imaging and CSF biomarkers (Fig. 2D).

Conclusion

In summary, ProtAIDe-Dx represents a pioneering attempt toward the development of scalable, minimally invasive, and multi-disease plasma proteomics diagnostic tools for neurodegenerative diseases, with state-of-the-art performance across six different conditions and promising clinical utilities. Overall, it establishes a robust benchmark for AI-driven proteomics tools, paving the way for precision medicine in neurodegenerative diseases.
