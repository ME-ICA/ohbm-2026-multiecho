Introduction

Preclinical and postmortem studies have consistently shown upregulation of neuroimmune markers, including microglia markers such as IBA1 and CD68, following chronic opioid exposure. Persistent microglia activation can impair synaptic plasticity and cognitive function, disrupt affective and motivational processes, and may contribute to clinical symptoms such as anhedonia, negative affect, and stress. As such, it has been theorized that microglial activation and other neuroinflammatory processes contribute to cognitive deficits, mood disturbances, and other clinical symptoms among patients with opioid use disorder (OUD), potentially impeding treatment. In our prior work, we reported the first in vivo evidence that patients with OUD exhibit upregulated levels of a neuroimmune marker, 18 kDa translocator protein (TSPO) availability, using positron emission tomography (PET) imaging1. While our initial findings are promising, TSPO expression is not specific to glial cells and thus, it remains unknown whether OUD patients exhibit microglia disruptions in vivo. Building on our initial study, we applied a novel, cell-specific PET radiotracer, [11C]CPPC, to investigate in vivo microglia levels among OUD patients enrolled in outpatient methadone maintenance therapy (MMT) contrasted with a cohort of demographically-matched non-opioid-using comparators. [11C]CPPC binds selectively to colony-stimulating factor-1 receptor (CSF1R), a marker expressed almost exclusively on microglia in the human brain, thereby facilitating investigation of microglia-specific disruptions in OUD. Consistent with our prior work1, we hypothesized that OUD patients would exhibit higher levels of [11C]CPPC binding relative to well-matched comparators suggesting a neuroinflammatory phenotype and microglia activation/proliferation. 

Methods

Patients with OUD (n=4 of 5 planned; aged 58.5 ± 19.1yrs; 50% female; 75% African-American) were recruited from MMT clinics in the Detroit, MI metropolitan area. OUD patients were maintained on an average of 65.3mg/day (range: 28-100mg/day) of methadone (PO). Demographically-matched non-OUD comparators (n=3 of 5 planned; aged 48.0 ± 10.4yrs; 67% female; 33% African-American) were recruited locally. Study participants were screened for immunological, neurological, and psychological contraindications via blood chemistries (e.g., CBCs), detailed medical and substance use histories, psychiatric interviews, and physical examinations. Study participants underwent a dynamic 75min [11C]CPPC PET scan on a Siemens Vision 600 PET/CT scanner with 213ps time-of-flight and continuous bed motion (CBM) capability allowing dynamic multi-pass image acquisition at multiple levels of the body (e.g., brain and heart). Prior to tracer injection, a low-level CT scan (100 keV, 80 mA) encompassing the head and heart was acquired for attenuation correction. The [11C]CPPC tracer was injected (0.14mCi/kg) as a slow bolus over 30s using a Medrad Stellant CT injector system. Coinciding with tracer injection, a dynamic image sequence (12 x 10s, 6 x 30s; total 5min) of the heart was acquired to quantify the bolus component of the arterial input function. Throughout the remainder of the PET scan, a multi-pass protocol using CBM (14 x 5min; total 70min) moved each participant’s head and upper torso in and out of the 26.3cm axial field-of-view, to collect dynamic emission data from the brain and heart from 5-75min post-injection. All PET images were corrected for attenuation, tracer decay, and scatter, and reconstructed using OSEM iterative reconstruction. Applying Logan graphical analysis in PMOD 4.5 (t*=30min), parametric images of [11C]CPPC total volume of distribution (VT; ml/cm3) were estimated from the dynamic brain emission data and the image-derived arterial input function corrected for [11C]CPPC metabolism. Parametric VT images (Figure 1) were spatially normalized to MNI template space for identification of regions of interest (ROI). Using a repeated measures analysis of variance (rmANOVA), group differences (OUD vs. non-OUD comparators) in regional [11C]CPPC VT were evaluated across seven ROIs: superior and middle frontal gyri (SFG and MFG, respectively), insula, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), superior parietal lobule (SPL), occipital cortex, and thalamus. As this is a pilot study, effect sizes and group mean differences are emphasized, rather than statistical significance. Exploratory bivariate Pearson correlations examined relationships between regional [11C]CPPC VT and subjective measures of positive/negative affect and perceived life stress (p<0.05; uncorrected). 

Results

Regional [11C]CPPC VT values were higher in OUD patients by 18% (on average across ROIs) relative to non-OUD comparators and associated with a ‘large’ effect size (partial η2=0.16; Figure 2). In each ROI examined, OUD patients exhibited higher mean [11C]CPPC VT values than non-OUD comparators with the largest group differences observed in cortical regions, e.g., SFG (5.44 vs. 4.20ml/cm3; 30% higher) and MFG (5.72 vs. 4.58ml/cm3; 25% higher), and smaller group differences in subcortical regions, e.g., thalamus (5.88 vs. 5.42 ml/cm3; 8% higher). Of note, age and BMI were not correlated with regional [11C]CPPC VT (ps>0.05) and no effects for biological sex or race were observed (ps>0.50; partial η2≤0.09). Exploratory bivariate Pearson correlations showed that higher levels of perceived stress were related to higher [11C]CPPC VT values in the SPL (r=0.92; p=0.025; Figure 2B). Additionally, worse negative affect was related to higher [11C]CPPC VT values across all ROIs (rs=0.81-0.99; ps≤0.05; a subset of ROIs are shown in Figures 2C-E), whereas positive affect exhibited no significant relationships (ps>0.30). 

Conclusion

Here, we report pilot data from the first application of a microglia cell-specific PET imaging study in OUD, or any SUD population, to our knowledge. Our preliminary findings suggest that OUD patients enrolled in outpatient MMT exhibit 18% higher levels of brain CSF1R availability (i.e., regional [11C]CPPC VT), indicative of higher microglia levels in vivo and associated with a ‘large’ between-group effect size. Power analyses indicate that this effect will reach statistical significance (p<0.05; power=0.80) with a sample size of N=36 (18 in each diagnostic group). Our data suggest that OUD patients in outpatient MMT exhibit microglia activation/proliferation (i.e., ‘M1’ biased) or brain infiltration of peripheral macrophages (or both) which may be related to subjective feelings of negative affect and perceived stress. Together with our prior work1, these findings suggest that patients with OUD exhibit a neuroinflammatory phenotype and contribute to the emerging ‘neuroinflammatory hypothesis of addiction2-3.’ Future research in a larger sample is needed to confirm our pilot findings.
