Introduction

Everyday goal-directed behavior requires the brain to stabilize task-relevant representations while flexibly integrating ongoing sensory information under uncertainty1,2. To examine how such internal task representations emerge when environmental uncertainty is constant, we used a paradigm in which informative sensory feedback allowed gradual stabilization of task-relevant representations with increasing task familiarity 3. Representational similarity analysis and time–frequency measures were used to track corresponding changes in neural representational structure over time.

Methods

To probe the formation of internal task representations under sustained uncertainty, 39 healthy young adults (21 women; age range 19–30 years) completed a visuomotor force-tracing task during EEG recording (Fig. 1A). On each trial, a visual cue indicated the upcoming target force, prompting participants to update their internal force estimate and adjust grip force using somatosensory and visual feedback. Target force levels were scaled to each individual’s maximum voluntary contraction and varied pseudorandomly across trials. Participants performed unimanual (left/right) and bimanual force production over 30 blocks (19 trials each; 1710 trials total), allowing progressive refinement of task representations despite constant environmental uncertainty.

EEG data were source-reconstructed using subject-specific cortical surfaces, and single-trial time–frequency representations were computed using complex Morlet wavelets (3–45 Hz). Spectral features were extracted within canonical frequency bands (theta 4-7Hz, alpha 8-13Hz, beta 15-25Hz, low gamma 30-45Hz) and post-cue time windows (early: 0–0.4 s; late: 0.4–0.8 s). To enable hypothesis-driven comparisons of representational strength and learning-related change across functional subsystems, analyses were conducted within a priori defined ROI families to dissociate task-relevant visuomotor representations from nonspecific activity. These included a visuomotor core network, a premotor integration network, and a bimanual coordination network, as well as two control networks: a default-mode and an auditory network (Fig. 1B).

For each subject, label-level Morlet time–frequency representations were first reduced to band-limited power estimates by averaging across predefined frequency bands and post-stimulus time windows, yielding a trial × ROI feature matrix for each ROI family. From these feature matrices, brain representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) were constructed using correlation distance (1 − Spearman ρ) between trial-wise activation patterns, such that higher dissimilarity reflected more distinct spectral representations across trials.

Model RDMs were constructed to encode hypothesized task structure at the trial level. Categorical models captured discrete experimental factors (i.e., left/right/bilateral task condition; response rule such as force in- or decrease), whereas parametric models encoded continuous trial-wise variables (i.e., parametric change in target force, force produced by left/right hand). Model RDMs were defined independently of neural data and reflected either equality versus inequality between trials (categorical models) or graded similarity as a function of parametric distance. All model RDMs were rank-transformed prior to analysis to ensure scale-invariant comparisons.

Spectral RSA was performed by computing Spearman correlations between the vectorized upper triangles of brain and model RDMs, yielding a single RSA coefficient per ROI × frequency × time window. To quantify learning-related representational change, RSA was recomputed within overlapping sliding trial windows spanning the experimental session, producing a trial-resolved RSA time series (“RSA drift”). A linear regression of RSA values onto trial index was fit at the single-subject level, with the resulting slope (Δρ per trial) indexing monotonic strengthening or weakening of task-related representational structure over practice. These subject-level drift slopes were subsequently entered into group-level statistical analyses to assess systematic learning effects across ROI families, frequency bands, and time windows.

Results

To characterize changes in neural representational structure with behavioral change over time, we quantified trial-wise representational similarity (“RSA drift”) using overlapping sliding windows across the experimental session. At the group level, several task-relevant ROI × frequency × post-cue time-window combinations showed systematic changes in RSA strength over trials (Fig. 2). Specifically, within visuomotor core ROIs, RSA increased over time on task in the alpha band for the COND model in both the early (mean slope β = 1.60×10⁻⁵; one-sample t-test vs. 0: t(38)=2.47, p=0.018) and late window (β = 1.78×10⁻⁵; t(38)=2.67, p=0.011), and for the RIGHT-hand model in both the early (β = 1.91×10⁻⁵; t(38)=2.91, p=0.006) and late window (β = 2.66×10⁻⁵; t(38)=3.83, p=4.7×10⁻⁴). In premotor ROIs, alpha-band drift was most pronounced in the late post-cue interval for COND (β = 1.47×10⁻⁵; t(38)=3.26, p=0.0023) and RIGHT (β = 1.49×10⁻⁵; t(38)=3.07, p=0.0039). Likewise, bimanual ROIs showed robust alpha-band drift for COND and RIGHT in the early window (COND: β = 1.83×10⁻⁵; t(38)=3.47, p=0.0013; RIGHT: β = 1.91×10⁻⁵; t(38)=3.51, p=0.0012) and in the late window (COND: β = 1.56×10⁻⁵; t(38)=2.97, p=0.0051; RIGHT: β = 2.12×10⁻⁵; t(38)=3.51, p=0.0012), indicating progressive strengthening of model-aligned representational structure with time on task.

In contrast, beta-band drift effects were not reliable across networks or time windows (all p>0.05), supporting frequency-specific learning-related changes with the strongest effects in the alpha range. Purely exploratory combinations (theta and low-gamma bands, as well as control ROI families such as default-mode and auditory networks) did not show consistent drift across models or time windows (all p≥0.06), suggesting that the observed effects were specific to task-relevant representations rather than reflecting nonspecific temporal trends. Individual-subject trajectories exhibited consistent directional trends across participants, supporting a learning-related reorganization of representational geometry beyond session-averaged RSA measures.

Conclusion

Repeated task exposure induced systematic, learning-related changes in neural representational geometry that specifically tracked both abstract task structure (left/right/bilateral condition) and behaviorally expressed force output of the acting hand, consistent with progressive refinement of internal visuomotor models. RSA drift effects were confined to task-relevant visuomotor and premotor networks and were absent in control regions, demonstrating functional specificity beyond global spectral change. Converging evidence from both time–frequency power and representational analyses identified the alpha band as a dominant carrier of learning-related reorganization, highlighting a frequency-specific mechanism through which cortical representations align with task demands and emerging behavior. Together, these findings establish trial-resolved RSA drift as a sensitive multivariate marker of internal model formation during adaptive visuomotor control. Alpha-band RSA drift in task-relevant visuomotor networks may present a promising mechanistic target for neuromodulation aimed at selectively enhancing internal model formation during visuomotor learning.
