Introduction

Obtaining a high-quality cortical segmentation is an essential step for any quantitative analysis of cortical microstructure. However, accurate cortical segmentation of 2D post-mortem brain sections and of ultra-high-resolution MRI remains challenging due to heterogeneous tissue contrasts, laminar intensity variation, as well as modality and species differences. Synthetic-data training has been shown to produce accurate 3D MRI segmentation models (Billot, et al, 2023)1, but such pipelines are lacking for 2D post-mortem brain imaging. We present an update to our previously described cortical all-purpose segmentation (CAPS) tool (Wagstyl et al 20232, Funck et al 20253), and describe both 1) a generalizable processes for generating synthetic 2D training data based on multiple species and 2)  a new version of our segmentation tool consisting of a U-NET (Ronneberger, et al 2015)4 trained for cortical segmentation on this new multispecies synthetic data for the marmoset, chimpanzee, macaque, and human. We trained 5 U-NET models based on synthetic data: one multi-species (1) model and species-specific models based on synthetic data for the human (2), chimpanzee (3), macaque (4), and marmoset (5). The accuracy of the models was evaluated on empirical (autoradiography, histology, and ex vivo MRI) and synthetic data from each species, as well as out-of-sample empirical data from the sea lion, owl monkey, cat, and pig.  

Methods

3D Input Label Volumes

For each species, we use 3D volumes containing labels for discrete anatomic regions, including: layers of the cortex calculated with LayNii (Huber et al 2021)5, white matter (WM), cerebellum, brainstem, skull and sinuses, and background. We used the following templates as the basis for the 3D labeled volumes: 

  • BigBrain (Amunts et al 2014) tissue classification for the human at 200μm resolution based on histology.

  • D99 for macaque (Saleem et al 2021)5 at 250μm resolution based on histology and MRI.

  • Juna atlas for the chimpanzee (Vickery et al 2020), at 500μm resolution based on MRI.

  • Marmoset Brain Mapping Project (v3) (Liu et al 2020) atlas for the marmoset at 200μm resolution based on MRI.

3D Spatial Transformation

Random 3D affine and nonlinear transformations were applied to the 3D label volumes with TorchIO. The number of control points were chosen uniformly between 6 and 9 and the maximum displacement was sampled uniformly between 1 and the number of control points. We randomly (p=0.5) split the transformed volumes along the middle of the x-axis by setting all voxels on one side of the volume to 0. 

Division of the Subcortex 

In a random (p=0.5) subset of instances of transformed 3D label volumes, subcortical labels were subdivided into Uniform(2,10) subregions. Subregions were defined by selecting uniform random points within the subcortex and assigning the nearest voxels to these random points to the same subregions. 

Creating 2D Synthetic Sections

For each transformed label volume, we sampled up to n 2D input label sections along randomly selected axes. For each input label section we also created a target label that defined the segmentation task the U-NET would learn. The target labels consisted of cortical GM, GM-pial border, and WM-GM border labels. 

In the 2D input labels, the input layers of the cortex span a range of 50 integer values, monotonically increasing from WM to pial boundaries. These 50 values are randomly binned into Uniform(1,6) pseudo layers. The boundaries of these pseudo layers are randomly sampled from Uniform(1,50). Hence, for each 2D input label we randomly define between 1 to 6 random pseudo cortical layers. 

Synthetic images were created from the labeled sections by assigning random pixel intensities to the pixels of each region, r, ~ Normal(μr,𝜎r), μr~Normal(μr,𝜎r), and 𝜎r~Normal(0,1/SNR). The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was set to 10. 

Numerous random augmentations were applied to the synthetic images to introduce plausible image artefacts, including: adding a random bias field, spiking, ghosting, and lines, regions of the images were occluded, spherical regions were offset by a random constant, adding spatially correlated noise, and degrading image resolution. 

Training

We trained 5 U-NET models with nnUNET (Isensee, et al. 2021) each with 16,000 samples. We trained one model for each of the following 4 species: human, chimpanzee, macaque, marmoset. We also trained one multi-species model based on 4,000 synthetic sections for each of the available species. 

Validation on empirical data

Empirical sections with corresponding manually drawn cortical GM labels were used to validate each of the models. These data were not used either during the synthetic data generation process nor during the training of the model. The manual labels consisted of 77 human autoradiographs (Zilles, K. et al. 2002), 11 macaque autoradiographs (Palomero-Gallgher & Zilles, 2018), 5 chimpanzee histological sections (Welker et al 2025), 30 marmoset sections taken from an ex vivo T2 MRI (Liu et al 2018). Accuracy was quantified using the Dice score between the predicted and manually defined labels. Poor quality segmentations (Dice<0.5) underwent visually inspection to identify common error-types. As a heuristic for out-of-sample species generalizability, we also tested the models on histological sections from the sea lion, cat, pig, and owl monkey (Welker et al 2025). 

Results

The validation on synthetic data showed that while each species-specific model performed similarly well on images from its own species (Fig.1.Top), only the multispecies model was able to perform consistently well across all species with a mean Dice score of 0.98. In the empirical data the multispecies model and humans both performed well (Fig.1.Bottom), though the multispecies (mean Dice=0.87) model outperformed the human mode (mean Dice =0.83) in primates. The chimpanzee model had a notably poor overall performance with a Dice score of only 0.48 across all test species. Visual inspection of low Dice (<0.5) score Chimpanzee model predictions showed notable co-occurance with histological sections containing cerebellar tissue.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that U-Net segmentation models trained on 2D synthetic data can perform remarkably well on empirical data across a variety of imaging modalities and species. Not only do these models generalize to real imaging modalities but also to new species which were not part of the training. 

However, the resolution and quality of the synthetic data has a significant impact on the results. Visual inspection suggested that the chimpanzee model performed poorly because the anatomic labels in the source 3D volume, e.g. the cerebellum, did not accurately reflect the level of anatomic detail seen in real post-mortem images. That the human data was the only one derived from a high resolution 3D histological atlas may explain some of why the human model was able to better generalize to high resolution 2D images from other species. 

Our results show that robust, multispecies and modality agnostic cortical segmentation can be achieved based exclusively on synthetic data. Moving forward we anticipate that, as a free and open tool, CAPS will facilitate the quantitative analysis of 2D post-mortem imaging, encourage the reuse of archival post-mortem sections, and help advance cross-species brain mapping.
