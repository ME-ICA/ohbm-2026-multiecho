Introduction

Choosing between options can be driven by both preferences, resulting from outcome evaluation, and strategic policy considerations, driven by action selection (1, 2). Traditional decision-making theories assume a strict separation between these two processes (c.f., 2). In contrast, the predictive coding framework provides a unified formal model that integrates valuation and action selection (3). It assumes two kinds of hierarchical and interacting predictions and prediction errors: one encodes “policies” and the other “preferences” (1). Here, we pitted these two views against each other, asking whether behavioral manipulations that primarily target action selection or outcome valuation produce distinct or overlapping effects on subsequent choices and preferences. To address our question, we compared approach-avoidance training (AAT), a non-reinforced learning method that targets action policies (4), with evaluative conditioning (EC), a reinforced learning approach that targets outcome preferences (5), in terms of how they influenced participants' choices and preferences. 

Methods

Four experiments were conducted. Experiments 1 (N = 50) and 2 (N = 59) were run online and assessed behavioral effects. Experiment 3 (N = 43) was conducted in the laboratory and included behavioral tasks only. Experiment 4 (N = 57) was conducted in the laboratory and included both behavioral tasks and fMRI acquisition. Response modality differed by setting: participants responded using keyboard buttons in the online experiments (Experiments 1–2) and using joystick movements in the laboratory experiments (Experiments 3–4).

Conditioned stimuli (CSs) differed across experiments. In Experiment 1, CSs were face images. In Experiments 2–4, CSs were silhouette images of everyday objects. In all experiments, stimuli were presented within a colored frame (red, purple, orange, blue, green, or yellow). For EC, unconditioned stimuli (UCSs) were selected from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS). UCS images were non-arousing and had either moderately positive or moderately negative valence.

Across experiments, the main session comprised three phases: pre-test, training, and post-test. Pre-test measures: Participants completed (1) a binary-choice task and (2) a preference rating task, referred to as willingness to play (WTP). Binary-choice task: In each trial, a single image was presented at the center of the screen. Participants indicated whether they wanted to play with the depicted item (Experiments 2–4) or meet the depicted person (Experiment 1). WTP task: Participants rated how willing they were to play with the item (Experiments 2–4) or meet the person (Experiment 1) using a toggle scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 10 (“very much”).

Training phase: Training consisted of alternating blocks of an AAT and EC. Each training implemented three valence conditions for the targeted CSs: positive, negative, and neutral. Positive, negative, and neutral training paired target CSs with positive emotion (EC) or approach movements (AAT), negative emotion (EC) or avoidance movements (AAT), and no movement (AAT) or no emotional pictures (EC), respectively. For each participant, three of the six frame colors were trained exclusively with AAT and the remaining three exclusively with EC. Each frame color was assigned to one of the three valences (positive, neutral, or negative). The mapping between CSs, training modality (AAT vs. EC), and valence was held constant within each participant but counterbalanced across participants via randomization.

In the AAT, each CS was shown centrally, and after 650 ms, a brief high- or low-pitched tone cued an approach vs. avoidance response; responses produced a zoom effect, with approach increasing and avoidance decreasing the image size, with tone–movement mappings counterbalanced across participants. In the EC task, CSs were likewise presented centrally, and after 650 ms, an emotional UCS image appeared simultaneously on the left or right; participants categorized the UCS emotion as quickly and accurately as possible.

Post-test measures: Following training, participants completed the WTP and binary-choice tasks again to assess changes in preferences and choices. In addition, participants completed a competitive choice task in which items trained via AAT were directly compared against items trained via EC.

To analyze the behavioral results, we used generalized linear models with Session, Valence, and Learning as main effects, along with their interactions. The models included by-participant random intercepts and random slopes for Learning, Valence, and Session, and random intercepts for Image and Color. For analyzing choices and preferences, respectively, a binomial model and a cumulative link mixed model (CLMM) were used. The fMRI data were analyzed using SPM 12 in MATLAB. The effects of interest were calculated for each participant and session using GLM, which had main and interaction effects similar to those of the behavioral model plus Task.

Follow-up contrasts showed a significant decrease from Session 1 to Session 3 in EC for n–m (estimate = −0.564, SE = 0.158, z = −3.58, p < .001) and a significant increase in EC for p–m (estimate = 0.490, SE = 0.158, z = 3.11, p = .0019). Similarly, for preferences, the Learning × Valence × Session interaction was significant, χ2(2)=7.24, p = .0268. Follow-up Session 1 vs. Session 3 contrasts showed a significant decrease in EC for n–m (estimate = −0.329, SE = 0.108, z = −3.05, p = .0023) and a significant increase in EC for p–m (estimate = 0.259, SE = 0.109, z = 2.38, p = .0175).

However, in Experiment 2, using silhouette images, the Learning × Valence × Session interaction was significant for choices, χ2(2)=17.17, p < .001. Follow-up Session 1 vs. Session 3 contrasts showed a significant increase in AAT for n–m (estimate = 0.270, SE = 0.127, z = 2.13, p = .033), a significant decrease in EC for n–m (estimate = −0.458, SE = 0.127, z = −3.62, p < .001), and a significant increase in AAT for p–m (estimate = 0.277, SE = 0.127, z = 2.19, p = .029). Similarly, for preferences, the Learning × Valence × Session interaction was significant, χ2(2)=11.86, p = .0027, with follow-up contrasts showing significant increases in AAT for n–m (estimate = 0.241, SE = 0.095, z = 2.53, p = .011), a significant decrease in EC for n–m (estimate = −0.200, SE = 0.095, z = −2.12, p = .034), and a significant increase in AAT for p–m (estimate = 0.266, SE = 0.095, z = 2.79, p = .0053).

In Experiment 3, which benefited from ecologically valid responses produced by a joystick, the Learning × Valence × Session interaction was significant for choices, χ2(2) = 13.82, p < .001. Follow-up Session 1 vs. Session 3 contrasts showed a significant decrease in EC for n–m (estimate = −0.650, SE = 0.150, z = −4.34, p < .001) and a significant increase in AAT for p–m (estimate = 0.356, SE = 0.149, z = 2.38, p = .017). Similarly, for preferences, the Learning × Valence × Session interaction was significant, χ2(2)=7.09, p = .0289, with follow-up Session 1 vs. Session 3 contrasts showing a significant decrease in EC for n–m (estimate = −0.366, SE = 0.117, z = −3.12, p = .0018) and a significant increase in AAT for p–m (estimate = 0.239, SE = 0.117, z = 2.04, p = .041).

Experiment 4 replicated the behavioral results of Experiment 3. Further, the fMRI results showed that the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) was significantly more active in the Choice – Preference contrast (pFDR_corrected<.05), whereas the anteroiri-cingulate cortex (ACC)/ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) showed higher activity in Preference – Choice contrast (pFDR_corrected<.05). Interestingly, when considering the interaction between Training and p-m, the posterior cingulate cortex showed higher activity in Session 3 compared to Session 1 (pFDR_corrected<.05. Fig. 2).

Conclusion

Our findings indicate AAT, which is a non-reinforced learning method focused on changing action biases (4), affected both choices and preferences. Similarly, EC, a form of reinforcement learning focused on altering the values of CSs (5) has affected participants’ choices, as well as their preferences. In considering our results, one should note that changing action biases is closely associated with “action selection” in traditional decision-making theories (2). On the other hand, affecting valuations of different choice possibilities aligns perfectly with “valuation” in these theories (2). Hence, our results challenge the strict functional separation between action biases and values. These results are better explained from the lens of predictive coding theory, where two kinds of hierarchical and interacting predictions and prediction errors are assumed, where one encodes “policies” and the other “preferences” (1). In the predictive coding framework, then, it is reasonable to assume that the effects of a training regimen that focuses on either hierarchy will eventually permeate through the other hierarchy and hence affect both choices and preferences. 

Our results also highlighted the importance of stimulus type and response modality in the effectiveness of different training methods and their corresponding effects. Notably, in Experiment 1, where faces were used as CSs, EC but not AAT affected choices and preferences, possibly indicating the relevance of values and the irrelevance of action biases in making decisions about complex stimuli such as faces. However, simpler stimuli, such as silhouette images of everyday items, are more affected by action biases rather than value alterations. On the other hand, responding via a joystick, which embeds movement relevance, can further make action biases more salient while making values less relevant when deciding whether to interact with an object. These results cannot be explained without adding further assumptions to the predictive coding framework (1) or traditional decision-making theories (2). In the current predictive coding framework, one might claim that adding a general precision weight(s) that affects different layers of a hierarchy can explain the effects of input and output modalities. However, in traditional decision-making frameworks, one should assume bi-directional interactions among several layers to account for the effects of input and output modalities.

Finally, in Exp. 4, our results highlighted the significance of IFG in dissociating between choice and preference tasks. This result is closely aligned with the predictive coding framework's assumption that the dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC and vlPFC) are associated with interacting higher layers of policy and preference hierarchies, respectively (1). Further, the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) dissociates the interaction between training regimens and valence, which corroborates previous results showing the PCC's involvement in action biases (4).
