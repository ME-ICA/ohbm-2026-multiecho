Introduction

Brain white matter hyperintensities (WMH) appear bright on T2-weighted MRI scans1 and are common in older adults, increase in volume with age, cardiovascular risk factors, and cerebral small vessel diseases2. Furthermore, WMH have been associated with cognitive and motor decline, and Alzheimer's dementia. However, the neuropathologies associated with WMH in different parts of the brain are not well understood. This study combined ex-vivo MRI with detailed neuropathologic examination in a large group of community-based older adults to quantify WMH volumes in different periventricular and deep brain regions and examine the association of regional WMH volume with age-related neuropathologies.

Methods

Participants, MRI, neuropathologic examination:

This work included 773 older adults participating in four longitudinal clinical-pathologic cohort studies of aging, Rush MAP, ROS, MARS, and Clinical Core of the Rush ADRC. All participants underwent autopsy. Cerebral hemispheres from all participants were imaged ex-vivo on 3T clinical MRI scanners approximately one month postmortem. WMH were segmented automatically using an in-house approach. All hemispheres were segmented into frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal, and  subcortical areas using multi-atlas segmentation3. Ventricles and gray matter were segmented in an ex-vivo cerebral hemisphere template and were expanded by 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, or 10 mm (avoiding overlap) to define periventricular and juxtacortical regions. The template was then registered to each individual hemisphere and the transformations were used to transfer the periventricular mask to each participant’s native space. The portion of each hemisphere not included in the periventricular mask was considered as deep tissue. WMH volumes were then calculated for the 10 regions (periventricular and deep frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal, and subcortical), were normalized by the participant’s total hemisphere volume, and multiplied by 1000. Following ex-vivo MRI, all hemispheres underwent detailed neuropathologic examination. The assessed pathologies included atherosclerosis, arteriolosclerosis, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), gross and microscopic infarcts, amyloid-β plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, Lewy bodies, and limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy neuropathological change (LATE-NC) (Fig. B). 

Statistical Analyses:

Linear regression was used to test the association of regional WMH volume (dependent variable) with age-related neuropathologies (included in the same linear regression model), controlling for age at death, sex, education, scanner, postmortem intervals to fixation and to imaging (Fig. A). The FSL PALM tool with 10,000 permutations and tail acceleration was used for the statistical analysis4. Associations were considered significant at p<0.05 after family-wise error rate (FWER) correction for multiple comparisons.

Results

Linear regression revealed that atherosclerosis was linked to higher WMH volume in the deep (p=0.0067) and periventricular (p=0.0019) frontal regions, while arteriolosclerosis was associated with higher WMH volume in multiple regions, including deep (p=0.0001) and periventricular frontal (p=0.0001), deep (p=0.0020) and periventricular (p=0.0020) parietal, periventricular (p=0.0083) occipital, and deep (p=0.0005) and periventricular (p=0.0007) subcortical regions. CAA was associated with deep (p=0.0145) parietal and deep (p=0.0420) occipital WMH. Gross infarcts were associated with WMH volume in deep (p=0.0001) and periventricular (0.0053) frontal regions, deep (p=0.0001) parietal, and deep (p=0.0066) and periventricular (p=0.0128) subcortical regions. LATE-NC was linked to higher deep (p=0.0165) temporal WMH volume. Neurofibrillary tangles showed associations with deep (p=0.0089) frontal, deep (p=0.0244) and periventricular (p=0.0051) occipital, and deep (p=0.0001) temporal WMH volumes. Microscopic infarcts, amyloid-β plaques, and Lewy bodies did not show any significant associations. Similar results were obtained when defining the periventricular region in the hemisphere template at different distances from 5 to 10 mm from the ventricles.

Conclusion

This study shows detailed WMH patterns associated with different age-related neuropathologies in community-based older adults. Both vascular and neurodegenerative pathologies were associated with regional WMH volumes. Substantial differences exist in WMH patterns across different neuropathologies. Periventricular WMH were mostly associated with vascular pathologies, while deep WMH were associated with both vascular and neurodegenerative pathologies. Vascular pathologies like arteriolosclerosis and gross infarcts were linked to greater WMH volumes in most brain regions, while CAA showed association only in posterior portions of the brain, and in particular deep tissue (not periventricular) which is in line with the fact that CAA affects cortical and leptomeningeal vessels. Neurofibrillary tangles and LATE-NC were associated with WMH particularly in the temporal and frontal lobes where the two pathologies are known to have a high impact. 

This study combined ex-vivo MRI and detailed neuropathological examination in a large sample of community-based older adults and showed detailed patterns of WMH associated with different age-related neuropathologies.
