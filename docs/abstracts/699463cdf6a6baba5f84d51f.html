Introduction

Structure–function coupling (SFC) has traditionally been defined as the correspondence between structural (SC) and functional connectivity (FC), providing key insights into how anatomical wiring constrains brain function1. However, this framework largely ignores effective connectivity, which captures the directionality of interactions and the flow of information across the brain. As a result, it remains unclear how structural architecture shapes not only whether regions interact, but also how information is transmitted between them. 

In particular, the relative contribution of direct anatomical connections versus global structural communication properties to effective connectivity is unknown. While direct structural connectivity reflects local wiring constraints, communication metrics such as shortest path (SP) length capture the efficiency of indirect routes through which information can propagate across the brain2. Whether these structural properties differentially constrain incoming and outgoing information flow has not been systematically addressed. 

Here, we extend SFC to the domain of effective connectivity by testing how SC strength and structural communicability (inverse SP) predict FC as well as incoming and outgoing directed information flow (i.e., EC). By relating these structural measures to normalized directed transfer entropy, we aim to determine whether directed interactions in the human brain are primarily constrained by local anatomical coupling or by global communication efficiency, and whether these constraints differ across functional roles in the cortical hierarchy. 

Methods

A total of 1,163 healthy middle-to-older-aged adults from the Barcelona Brain Health Initiative3 and the Barcelona Brain Stimularion Lab4 underwent MRI on a 3T Siemens Prisma with a 32-channel head coil, including T1- and T2-weighted structural imaging (0.8 mm isotropic), 10-min resting-state fMRI (TR = 800 ms, 2 mm isotropic, 750 volumes), and multi-shell DWI (99 directions; b ≈ 5/1500/3000 s/mm²; 1.5 mm isotropic)3. rs-fMRI preprocessing included discarding the first 10 volumes to ensure magnetization equilibrium, field inhomogeneity correction, motion realignment, co-registration to T1 space, regression of motion parameters, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals, and low-frequency drifts, band-pass filtering (0.04–0.07 Hz), interpolation of volumes with framewise displacement >0.5 mm (scrubbing), and exclusion of subjects with >50% interpolated volumes. DWI data were corrected for eddy currents and susceptibility distortions and processed using multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution and tractography (MRtrix3). 

Cortical and subcortical parcellation used the HCP MMP 360 atlas plus 14 subcortical ROIs (7 per hemisphere from FreeSurfer aseg), yielding 374 ROIs. Structural connectivity (SC) matrices were constructed as the number of streamlines between ROIs normalized by ROI size. BOLD time series were extracted by averaging voxel signals within each ROI. 

EC was estimated from individual BOLD time series using normalized directed transfer entropy (NDTE), a measure of directed information flow between brain regions, yielding incoming and outgoing flow, and the balance between them (net NDTE), per ROI5. This method is based on an information-theoretical measure quantifying the influence of the past activity of one brain region on the future activity of another, using a fixed time lag (of 10 volumes in this study). NDTE allows bidirectional analysis and is normalized to enable comparisons across regions. 

FC was computed as pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients between regional BOLD time series. Correlation values were subsequently Fisher z-transformed to improve normality prior to further analyses. 

SC was defined as the weighted streamline count between pairs of regions, obtained from whole-brain tractography. Streamline weights were normalized by the size of the corresponding regions of interest to account for differences in ROI volume. At the individual level, structural connectivity matrices were thresholded by retaining only connections with weights greater than the median value, reducing the influence of weak or potentially spurious streamlines. Structural communicability was quantified as the inverse of the shortest path length (1/SP) computed on the resulting weighted structural connectivity graph, capturing the efficiency of information transfer through both direct and indirect anatomical pathways. 

For each ROI, Spearman correlations were computed between SC or 1/SP and the functional metrics, including FC as well as incoming and outgoing NDTE. Correlations were calculated individually for each subject and for each ROI, treating the corresponding rows or columns of the connectivity matrices as vectors, yielding a regional SFC measure per participant. 

Results

The final sample included 857 subjects aged from 40 to 82 (median: 55.26), of whom 460 were women (53.68%).  

We first examined the coupling between SC and 1/SP with FC. Both SC and 1/SP showed widespread positive associations with FC across cortical and subcortical regions. Interestingly, areas with lower SFC regarding direct SC shower greater SFC regarding indirect SC (i.e., 1/SP), and vice versa, suggesting that when direct connections are not good at explaining function, indirect ones are.  

Next, we examined the coupling between SC and 1/SP with incoming and outgoing EC. Only the outgoing information flow were positively associated to structure across all regions, regarding indirect pathways (1/SP). The other associations' maps showed a mixture between positive and negative coupling, with the lateral components of the default mode network (DMN) showing inverse patterns to the rest of the cortex. Higher direct SC predicted higher incoming and outgoing flows except for lateral DMN components, where negative or no association were shown. Regarding indirect pathways, the tendency was predominantly negative for the incoming EC. For most of the areas, greater incoming flow was predicted by lower communicability through shortest path; this is, when the shortest path was longer. This may suggest that the more areas to visit, the more information that arrives. Here, the lateral DMN showed positive association, then with greater information arriving when the path was shorter.

Conclusion

Our findings provide new insights into how structural architecture shapes directed information flow in the human brain. We show that the influence of structural connectivity and global communicability is direction- and region-dependent, with lateral DMN regions following patterns distinct from the rest of the cortex. This suggests that some areas may rely on alternative communication strategies, rather than simply on direct anatomical connections or shortest-path efficiency, to integrate or transmit information. 

These results have important methodological and conceptual implications. First, they indicate that structure–function coupling cannot be universally characterized by a single model of communication; different regions and directions of flow may be governed by different structural constraints. Second, our study emphasizes the value of considering both incoming and outgoing information flow, as they can be differently constrained by structural architecture. Finally, our work highlights the need for the exploration of diverse communication models—such as diffusion-, probabilistic-, or navigation-based frameworks—to capture the complexity of brain network dynamics, moving beyond the sole use of shortest-path metrics or direct connectivity. 

In sum, understanding the region-specific and direction-specific rules of structural constraints is essential for a comprehensive view of how anatomy shapes brain function, and may inform future models of neural computation and network control.
