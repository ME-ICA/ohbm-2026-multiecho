Introduction

Neuropsychiatric disorders often show overlapping alterations in cortical thickness (CT), indicating shared structural vulnerabilities rather than strictly disorder-specific pathology1,2,3. Here, we investigate shared and disorder-specific CT patterns at vertex-wise resolution across ten major neuropsychiatric disorders. We performed a large-scale vertex-wise mega-analysis across ten major neuropsychiatric disorders to (i) map shared CT alterations at high spatial resolution, (ii) quantify the spatial similarity of CT alteration patterns across disorders and identify distinct clusters of disorders with similar CT alteration patterns, and (iii) link the cross-disorder patterns of each group to normative neurotransmitter receptor densities4 and large-scale cognitive systems derived from task-fMRI meta-analyses5. Our goal is to identify distinct cross-disorder cortical thickness patterns that span psychiatric and neurodevelopmental diagnoses and to characterize these patterns in relation to molecular neurotransmitter systems and large-scale cognitive functions.

Methods

We analyzed T1-weighted MRI data from 14,704 individuals, including 7,366 patients and 7,338 controls, across 42 datasets and 102 study sites. Patients were diagnosed with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD, 1,363 patients; 855 controls), anxiety disorders (ANX, 403; 196), autism spectrum disorder (ASD, 1,185; 1,255), bipolar disorder (BD, 501; 312), major depressive disorder (MDD, 1,298; 2,339), insomnia (INS, 232; 102), obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD, 434; 285), schizoaffective disorder (SCA, 285; 170), schizophrenia (SCZ, 1,484; 1,582), or substance use disorder (SUD, 181; 242). T1-weighted images were processed with FreeSurfer 7.1.1. CT surfaces were smoothed on the native cortical mesh (20 mm FWHM), subjected to a two-step quality control based on Euler number and regional outliers, and resampled to the fsaverage-7 template. Datasets were harmonized across scanners using ComBat.
    Controls were matched to patients at the study site level by age and sex. For each disorder, vertex-wise group differences in CT between patients and matched controls were estimated using a general linear model including age, sex, and intracranial volume as covariates. Multiple comparisons were controlled with surface-based cluster-wise Monte Carlo simulation (10,000 permutations; vertex p < 0.05, cluster-wise p < 0.05, Bonferroni-corrected across hemispheres). 

    Shared cortical vulnerability across disorders was quantified using a cross-disorder involvement map, in which each vertex was assigned the proportion of disorders exhibiting a significant CT alteration (0-100%), indicating how consistently that location was affected across conditions. Spatial similarity between disorders was assessed using Pearson correlations between the unthresholded effect-size maps. The resulting similarity matrix was subjected to hierarchical clustering; the number of clusters and cluster stability were evaluated using silhouette scores and leave-one-out analyses. For each cluster, a spectrum-specific involvement map was derived, and regional and network specificity were examined by averaging involvement within Desikan-Killiany parcels and Yeo 7 networks against spin-based null models.

    Molecular alignments were quantified by correlating each spectrum’s involvement map with 30 normative PET-derived neurotransmitter receptor density maps4. Cognitive associations were examined by correlating each spectrum’s cross-disorder involvement map with 400 unthresholded meta-analytic activation maps (NiMARE; Neurosynth/Cognitive Atlas), each linked to a cognitive concept organized into ten domains5. For each domain, the fraction of concepts with significant spatial correlation (spin permutations, 10,000 rotations) was computed, and domain-level enrichment was tested by permuting domain labels while preserving the number of concepts per domain.

Results

Vertex-wise mega-analysis revealed significant CT alterations across all 10 disorders (Cohen’s d range: -0.62 to 0.40; cluster-wise p < 0.05). SCZ, SCA, BD, and SUD showed widespread cortical thinning in frontal, temporal, and cingulate cortices; MDD exhibited more moderate thinning in the insula; ASD, OCD, and INS showed mixed thinning and thickening in orbitofrontal, posterior cingulate, and occipital regions; ADHD and ANX displayed more localized changes in frontal, temporal, and parietal areas.
    Spatial correlations between disorder-specific alteration maps ranged from r = -0.26 to 0.74, indicating considerable variability in the degree of similarity among CT alterations. Hierarchical clustering on the spatial similarity identified two largely independent spectra of disorders (r = 0.11, pspin = 0.12). A psychosis-mood spectrum (BD, MDD, SCA, SCZ, SUD) showed extensive cortical involvement (88% of vertices significantly altered), with complete overlap (100%) in the frontal pole, superior frontal, and cingulate cortex, and limbic (64%, pspin < 0.0001) and default-mode (57%, pspin = 0.049) networks exhibited the highest cross-disorder involvement, consistent with prior reports of a fronto-temporal cross-disorder axis3. A possible second neurodevelopmental-anxiety axis (ADHD, ANX, ASD, OCD, INS) displayed a more localized pattern (42% coverage), with the strongest overlaps in the temporal pole, entorhinal, and isthmus cingulate cortices, and in the visual (23%, pspin < 0.0001) and frontoparietal (21%, pspin = 0.0010) networks. Clustering was based solely on spatial similarity and did not include demographic data, yet the resulting groups exhibited distinct age profiles: patients in the psychosis-mood group were mainly adults (average age = 36.8 ± 14.2 years), while patients in the neurodevelopmental-anxiety group were mostly children and young adults (average age = 17.6 ± 12.6 years), with INS including a subgroup of older adult patients.

    The psychosis-mood axis showed significant associations with μ-opioid MOR, GABA-A α5, serotonergic 5-HT1A, histaminergic H3, glutamatergic mGluR5, and dopaminergic D1 receptor density maps (r = 0.23 to 0.37, FDR-corrected spin permutation, p < 0.05) and was enriched for cognitive domains related to social function (6 out of 10 concepts are nominally significant, |r| > 0.19, p = 0.038), emotion (16/28, |r| > 0.15, p = 0.00080), and executive/cognitive control (17/33, |r| > 0.15, p = 0.0044). The neurodevelopmental-anxiety axis showed a selective molecular profile, with a significant association with the serotonergic 5-HT1B receptor map (r = -0.08, FDR-corrected p = 0.023), and was significantly enriched for the action domain (5/9, |r| > 0.16, p = 0.0044). Together, these results point to distinct molecular and cognitive signatures for each cross-disorder pattern.

Conclusion

A vertex-wise mega-analysis across ten neuropsychiatric disorders identifies at least two largely independent axes of shared CT alteration. Spatial correlations among disorder-specific alteration maps varied widely, indicating that some disorders have very similar CT profiles, whereas others differ significantly. In this context, focusing on individual diagnoses risks misattributing shared alterations to disorder-specific traits and overestimating diagnostic specificity, whereas collapsing conditions into a single global map can obscure important differences across the various dimensions of cross-disorder effects1,3. Grouping disorders based on their similarities in CT alteration patterns instead organizes this variability into coherent axes of cortical vulnerability2.
    Together, these findings support a dimensional perspective on brain alterations in psychiatry, in which disorders cluster along different axes of cortical vulnerability. Along these axes, cross-disorder CT alteration patterns correspond with specific patterns of neurotransmitter receptor densities and cognitive functions.
