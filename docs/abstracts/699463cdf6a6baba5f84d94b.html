Introduction

Major advancements in our understanding of the brain-gut axis have been made via Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI). Functional and structural neural correlates of typical and pathological gastrointestinal functioning have been uncovered in a range of populations, including in persons with inflammatory bowel diseases such as Crohn’s disease (CD). CD is an immune-mediated inflammatory disease that is characterized by chronic, alternating periods of remitting and relapsing inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract resulting in symptoms of abdominal pain, diarrhea and weight loss1. In addition, persons with CD suffer an elevated burden of psychological comorbidity compared to the general population, including higher prevalence of depression and anxiety2. Potentially compounding their risk for psychological comorbidity, there is a heightened prevalence of childhood trauma among persons with inflammatory bowel disease (including CD) compared to the general population3. Experiencing childhood trauma elicits a stress response that results in heightened levels of inflammatory markers such as tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) cytokines, as well as C-reactive protein4. Childhood trauma can also affect brain structure and function but the potential contribution of this to the elevated prevalence of depression or anxiety among persons with CD is unknown; neural correlates of childhood trauma have yet to be explored in this population. Critically, studying the neurobiological associations of childhood trauma may advance our understanding of the pathophysiology of CD and psychological comorbidities separately associated with inflammatory bowel diseases and childhood trauma.

Methods

This research was conducted as part of a parent longitudinal comparative study investigating psychiatric comorbidity in immune-mediated inflammatory diseases. Studies were approved by the University of Manitoba Health Research Ethics Board and all participants provided informed consent. Only participants who underwent brain MRI were included in this sub-study and groups were formed such that neither age nor ratio of men to women differed between the 49 adults with CD and 49 healthy controls (HC). 

All participants completed the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire Short-Form (CTQ-SF), a self-report measure consisting of five subscales that assess childhood maltreatment: physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect. The depression subscale of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-D) was also collected to assess the relationship between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms. 

Neuroimaging data acquisition and Analysis

High resolution anatomical, whole-brain T1-weighted (T1w) images were acquired using a 3D magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence. Resting state fMRI data were acquired using a gradient-echo MB-EPI sequence.

Analyses of fMRI data were performed using CONN (release 18.b) and SPM12. Functional and anatomical data were preprocessed using a modular preprocessing pipeline including removal of initial scans, realignment with correction of susceptibility distortion interactions, outlier detection, direct segmentation and MNI-space normalization, and smoothing. Functional data were smoothed using spatial convolution with a Gaussian kernel of 8 mm full width half maximum. Functional data were denoised using a standard denoising pipeline followed by bandpass frequency filtering of the BOLD timeseries between 0.008 Hz and 0.09 Hz. 

First-level analysis proceeded via generation of seed-based connectivity maps. These were estimated characterizing the patterns of functional connectivity (FC) with 164 HPC-ICA networks and Harvard-Oxford atlas regions-of-interest (ROIs). FC strength was represented by Fisher-transformed bivariate correlation coefficients from a weighted general linear model (GLM) defined separately for each pair of seed and target areas, modeling the association between their BOLD signal timeseries. 

Group-level analyses were performed using a GLM for each individual voxel, with first-level connectivity measures at this voxel as dependent variables and groups (CD, HC) as well as total scores on the CTQ total and Emotional Abuse subscale entered as independent variables. At this level, the relationship between tallies on the CTQ and FC of 8 a priori selected ROI seeds was assessed within each group (i.e. the main effect of CTQ on FC within the separate CD and HC groups), in addition to between-group contrast assessing whether the association between CTQ and FC differed between the CD and HC groups, controlling for HADS-D scores. Based on a recent meta-analysis of neural correlates of childhood trauma, these 8 seeds included the core hubs of the default mode network (medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and bilateral lateral parietal lobe ROIs), as well as the bilateral amygdalae and insulae5.  

Voxel-level hypotheses were evaluated using multivariate parametric statistics with random-effects across subjects and sample covariance estimation across multiple measurements. Inferences were performed at the level of individual clusters (groups of contiguous voxels). Cluster-level inferences were based on parametric statistics from Gaussian Random Field theory. Results were thresholded using a combination of a cluster-forming p < 0.001 voxel-level threshold, and a familywise corrected p-FDR < 0.05 cluster-size threshold.

Results

Behavioural and Self-Report

No significant differences were found for age (HC M=47.6, SD=14.3; CD M=52.9, SD=13.9; t(96)=1.86, p=0.07), sex (HC 30:19; CD 35:14; χ2(1)=1.14, p=0.29), scores on the emotional abuse subscale of the CTQ (HC M=7.37, SD=3.68; CD M=8.29, SD=4.21; t(96)=1.15, p=0.13), or for total scores on the CTQ (HC M=34.47, SD=13.38; CD M=36.9, SD=12.03; t(96)=0.94, p=0.17). For scores on the HADS-D, both group means fell below the clinically meaningful threshold of 11 but the CD group had significantly higher scores than the HC group (HC MHADS-D=1.22, SD=1.56; CD MHADS-D=3.96, SD=3.77; t(63.90)=4.69, p<0.001).

Functional Connectivity (FC)

Results of the initial main effects analysis with all 8 seeds simultaneously testing for an association with the emotional abuse subscale of the CTQ within the CD group produced two significant clusters (F(8, 368) > 3.36; k ≥ 84), with peak voxels in the left frontal pole (k = 166) and the right cerebellum Crus 1 (k = 84). Subsequent planned contrasts for each seed revealed a significant negative association between emotional abuse and FC between the right amygdala and a cluster in the right cerebellum (peak voxel in Crus 1; T(46) > 3.51, k ≥ 191), and between the left lateral parietal lobe and a cluster in the left frontal pole(T(46)> 3.51, k ≥ 205).  

Results of the initial main effect analysis with all 8 seeds simultaneously testing for an association with total scores on the CTQ within the CD group revealed four significant clusters  (F(8, 368) > 3.36; k ≥ 86) with peak voxels located in the left frontal pole (k = 138), right cerebellum Crus 1 (k = 119),  the left temporal pole (k = 90), and the anterior cingulate cortex (k = 80). Subsequent planned contrasts for each seed revealed a significant negative association between total CTQ scores and FC between left insula and two clusters (T(46) > 3.51, k ≥ 106) with peak voxels in the left angular gyrus (k =322) and left middle temporal gyrus (k = 106). The seed in the right amygdala had significant associations with three clusters (T(46) > 3.51, k ≥ 77). Total CTQ scores were negatively associated with FC between the right amygdala and clusters that had peak voxels in the right cerebellum Crus 1 (k = 124) and left cerebellum vermis 4 5 (k = 77). Total CTQ scores were positively associated with FC between the right amygdala and a cluster with a peak voxel in the left temporal pole (k = 212). 

For the initial between-group contrast assessing whether the association between scores on the emotional abuse subscale and simultaneous FC of the 8 a priori seeds differed between groups, a single cluster with a peak voxel in the left frontal pole was detected (F(8, 736) > 3.31, k ≥ 153). Subsequent planned contrasts for each seed revealed a significantly stronger association between emotional abuse and FC for the CD group compared to the HC group for FC of the right amygdala with two clusters (T(92) > 3.40) with peak voxels in the right frontal pole and right paracingulate gyrus. Conversely, the CD group exhibited a weaker association between emotional abuse and FC of the posterior cingulate cortex and a cluster with a peak voxel in the left frontal pole (T(92) > 3.40). 

For total scores on the CTQ, two significant clusters were detected (F(8, 736) > 3.31, k ≥ 70) with peak voxels in the left frontal pole and left temporal pole. Subsequent planned contrasts for each seed revealed a significantly stronger association between emotional abuse and FC for the CD group compared to the HC group for FC of the right amygdala with three clusters (T(92) > 3.40) that had peak voxels in the right paracingulate, left temporal pole, and right middle frontal gyrus. As with the emotional abuse subscale, FC between the posterior cingulate cortex and a cluster in the left frontal pole exhibited a weaker association with total CTQ scores in the CD group compared to the HC group (T(92) > 3.40). 

Conclusion

In the present study, persons with CD exhibited significant associations between resting FC and childhood trauma. Meanwhile, no such associations were observed among the HC group. Differences between the groups relative to these associations are particularly striking given that the CD and HC groups did not significantly differ relative to their mean emotional abuse or total CTQ scores. Prior research has found that neural correlates of childhood trauma as well as inflammatory markers are more pronounced among persons with depression4,5. All of the current analyses, however, were completed while controlling for depressive symptoms, thus the differences in HADS-D scores between the groups are less likely to have influenced the results despite the known associations between depressive symptoms and childhood trauma. Further research should assess the association between childhood trauma-related FC and depressive symptoms in adults with CD. Further, while we focused on the emotional abuse subscale as well as total scores on the CTQ, the other subscales for physical abuse and neglect, as well as emotional neglect and sexual abuse have yet to be explored. While emotional abuse is more common, physical abuse has been found to be more strongly associated with inflammatory markers than other forms of childhood trauma4, and its relationship with brain function and structure in persons with CD should be examined.      

Notably, the associations exhibited between FC and childhood trauma for the CD group (relative to the emotional abuse subscale as well as the total) were primarily driven by seed brain regions involved in affective and interoceptive processing (amygdala and insula) and regions in the default mode network involved in self-referential thought (posterior cingulate cortex). The FC of these areas with other regions of the brain was diffuse, but the most prominent significant target regions included the frontal poles and regions of the cerebellum. Future research should explore task-based assessments of affective processing and emotional regulation to better characterize the nature of FC associations in these brain regions relative to childhood trauma. 

A possible limitation of the current design is the mixed composition of the CD group, including both those in active disease as well as those in remission. Future research can aim to form larger groups of persons with CD who are in active disease to compare to those who are in remission to determine whether disease activity impacts childhood trauma associations with resting state FC. In addition, comparisons should be made to test whether the association between childhood trauma and FC differs between persons with CD who have clinically significant depression compared to depressed individuals without inflammatory bowel disease, to determine the specificity and generalizability of the current findings. Similarly, while the present findings shed light on the neural implications of childhood trauma among persons with CD, inflammatory bowel disease also includes ulcerative colitis. Further research is necessary to assess the neural correlates of childhood trauma in ulcerative colitis to attain a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between these immune-mediated inflammatory diseases and childhood trauma.    

The present study offers novel insight on how childhood trauma relates to resting state FC among persons with CD. Unlike in healthy controls with comparable childhood trauma, persons with CD exhibit significant associations between childhood trauma and FC of brain regions involved in affective and interoceptive processing, as well as self-referential thought. Resting state FC represents a component of the brain-gut axis involved in the link between childhood trauma and Crohn’s disease.
