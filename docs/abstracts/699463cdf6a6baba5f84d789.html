Introduction

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) delivered to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (LDLPFC) is an established treatment for depressive disorders, yet individual outcomes remain variable. In routine practice, LDLPFC is often localized using scalp heuristics such as the conventional 5-cm rule or F3/Beam-F3, which do not account for inter-individual anatomical and functional variability and may stimulate different networks across patients. Neuronavigation enables anatomy-guided placement of predefined stereotactic targets and connectivity-based targeting intended to engage depression-relevant circuits; notably, stronger negative functional coupling between the stimulation site and the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) has been linked to antidepressant response. However, head-to-head trials comparing localization strategies are scarce, and findings are inconsistent, limiting the adoption and dissemination of optimal targeting approaches. Moreover, evidence in bipolar depression is comparatively limited, and target selection faces similar challenges. We therefore conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis restricted to LDLPFC stimulation to compare the relative efficacy and tolerability of competing localization strategies in unipolar and bipolar depressive episodes.

Methods

This systematic review and network meta-analysis was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024521477). We searched PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov from inception to November 13, 2025 (English language). We included randomized controlled trials enrolling patients with a primary diagnosis of unipolar or bipolar depressive episode (sample size ≥5) that delivered high-frequency rTMS or intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) to the LDLPFC. Trials had to compare at least two of the following prespecified LDLPFC localization strategies: (1) sham stimulation; (2) the conventional 5-cm rule or similar distance-based methods (including 5.5-cm, 6-cm, or 7-c); (3) F3-based approaches (including the F3 and Beam F3); (4) anatomy-guided localization, defined as neuronavigated targeting of LDLPFC sites on individual structural MRI using predefined stereotactic coordinates or anatomical landmarks (for example, the BA9/46 areas and the border area); and (5) connectivity-based localization, defined as neuronavigated selection of an LDLPFC stimulation site informed by individual connectivity to a priori depression-relevant circuitry (for example, the functional connectivity with the nucleus accumbens or the agACC).

Primary  outcomes was response rate，secondary outcomes included reduction in depressive symptoms， remission, reduction in anxiety symptoms, and tolerability (all-cause discontinuation). We synthesized direct and indirect evidence using random-effects network meta-analysis and summarized treatment hierarchies using SUCRA. Prespecified subgroup analyses examined bipolar depression–inclusive trials and stimulation paradigm (iTBS vs conventional rTMS). Sensitivity analyses excluded trials at high risk of bias.

Results

We included 76 RCTs (3,870 participants) comparing five LDLPFC localization strategies (sham, 5-cm, F3-based, anatomy-guided, and connectivity-based neuronavigation). For the response rate, all four active strategies were superior to sham. Although between-strategy differences were not statistically significant, connectivity-based targeting showed the highest probability of being most effective (vs sham OR 13.72, 95% CrI 3.19–62.34), followed by 5-cm (OR 5.33, 3.29–8.99), anatomy-guided (OR 4.02, 2.07–8.00), and F3-based (OR 2.86, 1.06–7.97); the same rank order was observed for depressive symptom reduction. For remission, only connectivity-based and 5-cm methods showed clear benefits versus sham. 

In trials including bipolar depression, connectivity-based targeting was the only strategy showing a clear advantage over sham for response, and it also outperformed sham and the other active strategies for depressive symptom reduction. 

For anxiety symptom reduction, only the 5-cm method demonstrated a borderline/significant improvement versus sham (SMD 0.48, 95% CrI 0.00–0.97).

Tolerability (all-cause discontinuation) did not significantly differ between any active strategy and sham in either the full population or BD-inclusive subgroup.

Conclusion

Across LDLPFC rTMS localization strategies, active targeting methods improved depressive outcomes compared with sham and had broadly similar tolerability profiles. Connectivity-based targeting demonstrated the most favorable overall efficacy profile—especially in BD-inclusive trials—supporting circuit-informed neuronavigation as a promising direction for image-guided neuromodulation.
