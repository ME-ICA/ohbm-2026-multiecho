Introduction

Functional MRI (fMRI) is widely used to study brain activity and connectivity, yet the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal is strongly affected by respiration- and cardiac-driven fluctuations. Low-frequency changes in breathing and heart rate modulate arterial CO₂ and cerebrovascular tone, introducing BOLD variations that can bias activation and connectivity estimates.1 These effects are typically modeled using external physiological sensors such as respiratory belts and photoplethysmographs (PPG) to compute respiratory variation (RV) and heart rate variation (HRV). However, collecting high-quality physiological data is challenging, especially in older adults, due to greater motion, and discomfort with monitoring equipment. As a result, large datasets often contain missing or unusable recordings; for example, up to 87% of respiratory traces in the Human Connectome Project (HCP) in Development dataset were unusable.2

Recent work shows that machine learning models can reconstruct physiological waveforms directly from BOLD signals. Yet nearly all existing models were developed in young adults, despite the fact that aging is associated with increased physiological variability, reduced vascular reactivity, altered hemodynamic responses, and distinct motion patterns.3,4 These age-related changes raise important questions about whether models trained in younger populations generalize to older adults or whether age-specific methods are required.

Here, we introduce a deep learning framework for reconstructing RV and HRV directly from resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) in older adults using the HCP-Aging dataset. Our architecture combines convolutional neural networks (CNN) with bidirectional gated recurrent units (Bi-GRU) to capture both local temporal features and long-range dependencies in vascular dynamics. This sensor-free approach is tailored to the physiological characteristics of aging populations and supports improved interpretation and correction of rs-fMRI data.

Methods

rs-fMRI data from HCP-A dataset (ages 36–100 years) were used to train and test a machine-learning framework for reconstructing RV and HRV directly from BOLD signals. 300 scans selected, based on the quality of the fMRI images, and associated respiratory and cardiac signals. 

Preprocessing: Standard fMRI preprocessing included motion correction, normalization, and extraction of mean time series from 630 regions of interest: 580 dynamic functional connectivity–derived ROIs from the Dynamic Brain Functional Atlas (D-BFA), 48 white-matter ROIs from the JHU atlas, and 2 CSF ventricular ROIs. Six head-motion parameters (translations and rotations) were also retained because respiratory activity and pseudomotion introduce oscillatory structure in these estimates.

Model architecture: RV and HRV were reconstructed using an identical hybrid 1D-CNN + BiGRU model. The CNN layers capture localized temporal features, while the bidirectional GRUs model both forward and backward temporal dependencies, enabling integration of past and future BOLD information. For each estimate, the model receives a 65-TR sliding window containing 630 BOLD signals + 6 motion traces (636 channels). Mean-ROI inputs improve SNR and reduce computational complexity.

Physiological rationale: Respiratory depth and rate induce delayed BOLD fluctuations peaking ~16 s after inspiration, while heart-rate–related low-frequency fluctuations peak at ~6–12 s with secondary components at ~30–42 s. Thus, future BOLD samples contain information about current physiology, supporting a bidirectional recurrent architecture. Conversely, respiratory and cardiac dynamics also show temporal continuity, so past samples remain informative. White-matter and CSF regions predominantly reflect non-neuronal fluctuations, enhancing sensitivity to physiological effects.

RV reconstruction: A 65-TR window was mapped to a single RV point using three complementary temporal configurations:
 (1) first-point prediction (future-only input),
 (2) center-point prediction (past + future),
 (3) last-point prediction (past-only).
These configurations ensure full coverage of RV across the entire scan. The RV target was defined as the standard deviation of the respiratory waveform within a 6-s sliding window. Figure below illustrates the RV framework.

HRV reconstruction: HRV estimation uses the same CNN-BiGRU architecture and same inputs, excluding motion parameters when desired. For each 65-TR window, the model predicts the HRV at a near-center time point, leveraging both past and future information. The target HRV was computed as the inverse of the mean inter-beat interval (IBI) from the PPG signal within a 6-s window. The conceptual framework parallels that of RV (not shown due to single-figure limit).

Output coverage: Due to windowing, RV is estimated over the full scan using all three temporal configurations, while HRV lacks predictions for the first and last few TRs. 

Model evaluation. A ten-fold cross-validation strategy was employed. The dataset was partitioned into ten subsets; in each fold, nine subsets were used for training and one for testing, ensuring each subset served exactly once as the test set. Within every training segment, 20% of samples were reserved for validation, and each model was trained for 100 epochs. This rotation maintained strictly independent training and testing data and minimized bias. Model performance across folds was quantified using MAE, MSE, Pearson correlation (r), and Dynamic Time Warping (DTW). Final results were reported as Average ± Standard Error (SE), and violin plots were used to visualize the distribution of performance metrics across unseen test data.

Results

Model Evaluation: To assess RV reconstruction performance, we evaluated three input configurations:

   1) Head motion parameters only,

   2) Mean BOLD signals from 630 ROIs (580 dFC-based gray-matter ROIs, 48 white-matter ROIs,       and 2 CSF ROIs), and

   3) Combined BOLD signals + head motion.

The model achieved the highest accuracy with the combined input, which consistently yielded lower MAE, MSE, and DTW values and higher correlation with the measured RV. This improvement reflects the complementary information provided by motion parameters, which capture respiration-driven pseudomotion, and by BOLD signals, which encode physiological fluctuations with a temporal delay. A Friedman test confirmed that input configuration had a significant effect on RV estimation accuracy (p < 0.001).

For HRV reconstruction, adding motion parameters did not improve performance and in some cases slightly degraded it, consistent with the weaker coupling between head motion and low-frequency heart-rate fluctuations. Therefore, head motion parameters were excluded from HRV estimation. Even without motion inputs, the model accurately reconstructed HRV dynamics across resting-state periods, particularly during segments with strong physiological variability.

The figure below summarizes RV and HRV reconstruction performance, showing the distribution of MAE, MSE, Pearson correlation (r), and DTW across subjects. The model demonstrates consistent and robust performance across metrics for both RV and HRV.

Impact of ROI Selection on Model Performance: To examine how spatial input configuration affects performance, we compared four ROI-based input sets using:

-Configuration 1: 69 structural ROIs

-Configuration 2: 580 dFC-based ROIs

-Configuration 3: 360 multi-modal cortical ROIs + 48 WM + 2 CSF

-Configuration 4: 580 dFC-based ROIs + 48 WM + 2 CSF

For RV reconstruction, six motion parameters were appended to all configurations. Across all metrics—MAE, MSE, r, and DTW—Configuration 4 consistently produced the best performance for both RV and HRV. This configuration integrates functionally homogeneous dFC-based ROIs with WM and CSF regions, allowing the model to capture both neural and non-neural physiological fluctuations more effectively.

Configuration 4 significantly outperformed Configuration 2 (p < 0.001), demonstrating that incorporating WM and CSF BOLD signals provides additional physiologically relevant information. It also exceeded the performance of Configuration 3 (p < 0.01), indicating that dFC-derived ROIs outperform standard multi-modal cortical atlases. Configuration 1 yielded the lowest accuracy, likely due to excessive spatial averaging within anatomically defined ROIs, which can attenuate physiologically meaningful temporal structure. In contrast, dFC-based parcels preserve functional homogeneity, enabling more precise modeling of physiological BOLD fluctuations.

Conclusion

Summary of Key Contributions: 

This study introduces a hybrid deep learning framework for reconstructing RV and HRV directly from resting-state BOLD fMRI data, with a specific emphasis on addressing challenges inherent to aged populations, where external physiological recordings are often absent or unreliable. Building on prior work conducted primarily in young adults, we demonstrate that accurate RV and HRV waveforms can be recovered using only fMRI-derived signals in the HCP-A cohort, achieving performance comparable to models relying on externally recorded physiology.

Our design integrates spatially enriched inputs—dynamic functional connectivity–based ROIs supplemented with white-matter and CSF regions—with a temporally informed 1D-CNN + BiGRU architecture. The CNN layers extract localized temporal fluctuations, while the bidirectional GRUs model longer-range temporal dependencies essential for capturing delayed physiological effects. The addition of head-motion parameters further enhanced RV estimation, consistent with the well-established influence of respiration-driven pseudomotion in fMRI. ROI selection also played a critical role: combining dFC-based cortical ROIs with WM and CSF signals yielded the highest reconstruction accuracy, highlighting the value of physiology-sensitive, spatially diverse inputs

Limitations and Future Directions

HRV Modeling with BOLD-Only Inputs: Head-motion parameters did not improve HRV estimation, likely reflecting the weaker coupling between cardiac pulsatility and gross motion. Future work may incorporate alternative physiological proxies (e.g., venous signal fluctuations or CSF inflow).

Partial HRV Reconstruction Due to Temporal Requirements: Unlike RV—which could be reconstructed using past-only, future-only, or bidirectional windows—HRV required both past and future inputs. Unidirectional windows substantially reduced accuracy, likely due to the sharper, more transient nature of cardiac-induced BOLD fluctuations. In contrast, RV exhibits slower dynamics and influences broader brain regions, facilitating reconstruction. Consequently, HRV cannot be estimated at the beginning and end of each scan, producing unavoidable edge effects. Adaptive windowing or causal temporal models may help address this limitation.

Model Interpretability: Deep learning models remain less interpretable than traditional approaches. Integrating explainability methods (e.g., saliency maps, attention mechanisms, GRU gating analysis) may clarify which regions and timepoints drive reconstruction.

Explicit Physiological Delay Modeling: While the sliding-window framework implicitly captures past–future context, incorporating explicit modeling of respiratory and cardiac hemodynamic delays could further enhance reconstruction accuracy.

Real-Time Applications: Although efficient for offline use, real-time or near-real-time implementations (e.g., neurofeedback, intraoperative monitoring) will require additional computational optimization and engineering.
