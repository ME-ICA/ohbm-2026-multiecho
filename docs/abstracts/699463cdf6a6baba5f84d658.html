Introduction

In electrophysiological data, small differences, such as different timing, duration, and background noise, can be observed when the same functional response is recorded repeatedly. Signal differences of this nature can restrict the effectiveness of commonly used correlation-based methods to identify similarities, often leading to the underestimation of similarities. Mutual information provides a useful alternative metric, but its use is constrained by the lack of standard estimation methods on continuous data, like electrophysiological signals, and an incomplete understanding of the limitations and biases associated with different estimation techniques.

In our work, by analyzing both simulated evoked neural responses and real MEG data, we characterized the performance of different mutual information estimators. We provide guidelines on the parameter setting, reliability of the estimators, interpretation of the estimates, and issues to consider when applying the estimators to electrophysiological data.

Methods

Pearson correlation, estimated as the sample correlation coefficient, was compared with three mutual information estimators: the Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator ^1, which relies on the nearest neighbor algorithm; an adaptive binning estimator, which discretizes the data and estimates mutual information using a standard approach for discrete data; and the kernel density estimator ^2, which estimates probability densities and uses them to estimate mutual information. To limit the variability range of the estimates mutual information values were normalized and the absolute values of correlation were used in the analysis.  

Both Pearson correlation and mutual information define similarity as an order relationship and do not provide absolute measures. Therefore, we introduced adaptive lower bounds in our results. These bounds represented the expected similarity values between a meaningful signal and pure noise and offered a reference for identifying meaningful relationships beyond noise comparison. To simulate the full range of possible estimates, the bounds were defined across 1000 random noise iterations. Each estimator and each comparison yielded different lower bounds. 

Each of the mutual information estimators relies on a distinct parameter choice: the number of bins for adaptive binning, the number of neighbors for Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator, and the kernel bandwidth for kernel density estimator. We started by characterizing the relevant parameter choices, paying particular attention to how they affected similarity estimates on simulated neural signals. We identified optimal parameters that were employed in the subsequent analyses. With Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator the number of neighbors was set to 5, with adaptive binning Doane's rule^3 successfully identified the suitable number of bins and with kernel density estimator Scott’s rule^4 proved appropriate for bandwidth selection.

As the next step, we simulated neural evoked responses as controlled Morlet wavelets which were parametrically altered by means of selected realistic transformations and employed the estimators to compare pairs of simulated responses. The applied transformations included changes in the signals’ sample size and cropping, addition of white noise and sparse high-frequency noise, and differences in amplitude, duration, and timing of the response (Figure 1). To model the variability of the estimates, random Gaussian noise was added to the compared simulated responses across 1000 iterations while controlling the signal-to-noise ratio. 

As the last step, we studied the generalizability of our results to real data by applying the similarity estimators to MEG recordings of evoked neural responses to a picture naming task ^5. In a single-subject recording, we compared evoked response recorded by one reference sensor chosen near a known activation source with the responses recorded by all other sensors.

Results

All the estimators were found to be susceptible to both estimator parameter and signal parameters. In particular, the results of the adaptive binning and kernel density estimators were highly dependent on the chosen estimator parameter, and selecting an appropriate parameter was essential to avoid biased estimates. Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger was less sensitive to its free parameter; however, it had an optimum for the number of neighbors.

Importantly, the similarity estimates varied broadly depending on sample size. Too small sample sizes resulted in the overestimation of similarity for kernel density estimator and Pearson correlation, whereas adaptive binning and Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimators underestimated the similarity. Overall, sampling frequencies over 200 Hz seemed to yield sufficient signal coverage and reasonable results for all the similarity estimators with the length of the simulated responses (0.35 s). For all estimators, increasing the sample size improved the stability of the estimates. However, we found a limit in the sample size beyond which adding more samples did not yield significant improvements. This limit corresponded to sampling frequency of 600 Hz with the length of the simulated responses. This limit is easily achievable in common neuroimaging setups. The stability of all estimators benefitted from high signal-to-noise ratio of the input signals but did not improve beyond 30 dB. 

By setting the comparison against adaptive lower bounds, we found mutual information estimators, especially adaptive binning and Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator, to be better than Pearson correlation in pinpointing relationships between signals with small time shifts, changes in signal duration and presence of high-frequency sparse noise.

The case study on real MEG data validated the behaviors observed in the simulation study (Figure 2). In the MEG case study, rank of similarities across signal pairs seemed to reflect the functional relevance of such similarities. When analyzing the similarity rankings of all the channel data, with respect to a chosen reference channel, channels ranking the highest for Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator and adaptive binning aligned well with experimental activation patterns. Pearson correlation and kernel density estimator also highlighted interpretable patterns but mainly recognizing exact shape-similarities in the time series.

The four estimators displayed unique behaviors but tended to organize as two pairs with comparable behavior: Pearson correlation with kernel density estimator, and Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator with adaptive binning estimator. Kernel density estimator behavior only partially differed from Pearson correlation and was overall harder to tune than the other mutual information estimators. Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator and adaptive binning estimator aligned with one another but diverged from Pearson correlation in that they were better at finding more complex relationships from the data. However, their similarity estimates tended to show higher variance and more pronounced upward biases especially with the lower estimate values. Between these two mutual information estimators, Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator was the more stable with respect to the estimator parameters and the number of samples in the input signals, making it the more practical choice.

Conclusion

Our findings outline the trade-offs between the robustness and sensitivity of the different estimators. While Pearson correlation performs reliably under linear transformations and on clean signals, mutual information estimators can better capture more complex relationships and adapt to realistic signals but vary widely in their stability and accuracy. Employing tools such as adaptive lower bounds and similarity rankings instead of relying on estimated absolute values is crucial to mitigate their biases and obtain more interpretable results. 

We recommend Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator as the most reliable estimator for mutual information in identifying meaningful similarities in electrophysiological signals. Nonetheless, with appropriate parameter tuning, both the kernel density and adaptive binning estimators can also provide valid alternatives.   

Overall, mutual information offers a versatile and powerful alternative to Pearson correlation for analyzing electrophysiological time series, when properly implemented and properly interpreted. We believe mutual information to be a delicate but valuable tool for a range of neuroscience applications, and especially in the study of EEG and MEG neural evoked responses.
