Introduction

Functional connectivity (FC) characterizes ‘function’ as temporal correlations and has been extensively utilized in resting-state fMRI analysis. However, standard FC may overlook the generative mechanisms of brain networks, particularly the balance between spatial constraints and topological homophily. Grounded in this theoretical framework, connectional affinity offers a superior characterization of network topology1. The concept builds on Finn et al.'s "functional fingerprinting", which maps unique regional connectivity profiles2. Margulies et al. subsequently operationalized the affinity as the cosine similarity between these whole-brain (but thresholded) fingerprints, revealing intrinsic functional gradients

To facilitate the application of these advanced metrics, we introduce a user-friendly graphical user interface (GUI) toolbox. This toolbox implements four key affinity-derived metrics: Regional Functional Affinity (RFA), Seed-based Functional Affinity (SFA), Homotopic Functional Affinity (HFA)3, and Global Functional Affinity (GFA). RFA, HFA, SFA methodologically correspond to traditional Regional Homogeneity (ReHo), Seed-based Functional Connectivity (SFC), Voxel-mirrored Homotopic Connectivity (VMHC), respectively. The distinct innovation lies in substituting the time series matrix with the FC matrix (no threshold) —representing topological homophily—as the computational input. As for GFA, we used BrainSpace4 to calculate the neural gradient but set the sparsity as ‘0’. 

Methods

As in Figure 1a, RFA is defined as the Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (KCC) computed among the functional connectivity profiles of the entire brain (i.e., full brain FC map) of a given node (or vertex) and its spatial neighbors. Specifically, KCC quantifies the consistency of connectivity patterns within a local neighborhood (e.g., encompassing 6 one-step neighbors, or 18 two-step neighbors). The resulting score serves as the RFA value for that vertex: lower RFA values signify regional functional diversity, whereas higher values indicate functional uniformity.

As in Figure 1b, HFA quantifies the topological similarity between mirror-symmetric brain regions. Specifically, HFA is computed as the cosine similarity between the connectivity fingerprints of geometrically symmetric vertices, which are rigorously constructed by concatenating intra-hemispheric and inter-hemispheric connection profiles to ensure precise spatial correspondence of functional interactions within and across hemispheres.

As in Figure 1c, SFA is designed to quantify the similarity in whole-brain functional connectivity patterns between a specific seed region and all other voxels. Methodologically, the calculation begins by deriving unique connectivity fingerprints for every voxel from a whole-brain functional connectivity matrix. Subsequently, the average fingerprint of a selected Region of Interest (ROI) is extracted. Finally, the SFA map is generated by computing the cosine similarity between the seed’s transformed fingerprint and the connectivity fingerprints of all other voxels throughout the brain.

As in Figure 1d, GFA aims to elucidate the intrinsic topological hierarchy of the cerebral cortex's macroscale functional organization. By mapping high-dimensional whole-brain functional connectivity patterns onto a low-dimensional manifold, GFA characterizes the continuous functional gradients spanning from primary sensory-motor regions to higher-order association cortices (e.g., the default mode network). The computation uses BrainSpace toolbox4, involving two key steps: First, a dense FCmap-affinity matrix is constructed by calculating the cosine similarity between the connectivity fingerprints of all pairs of voxels (or vertices). Non-linear dimensionality reduction techniques, namely Diffusion Map Embedding, are subsequently applied to this matrix to extract the principal gradients that define the main axes of brain functional organization.

Results

The CCS-FEATHER toolbox is designed to process surface-based functional data derived from the Connectome Computation System (CCS)5, ensuring inputs are rigorously preprocessed (including ICA-AROMA denoising) and registered to the standard 10k fs_LR cortical space. Figure 2a illustrates the software's graphical user interface (GUI). Upon specifying the data directory (figure 2b fields1), the toolbox automatic ally parses the directory structure and populates the list box with subjects meeting the inclusion criteria (figure 2c). Users can then specify group-level processing parameters in field 2. Panel 3 facilitates the selection of Regions of Interest (ROIs) for SFA analysis. The toolbox currently integrates the 17-network parcellation by Yeo et al. as pre-defined seeds; users may select one or multiple networks to compute the functional affinity between the chosen ROI(s) and the whole brain (figure 2d). In panel 4, the desired metrics—RFA, HFA, SFA, or GFA—can be selected via checkboxes. Finally, once all configurations are set, the analysis pipeline is initiated by clicking the "Run" button (labeled 5).

Conclusion

We introduced CCS-FEATHER, a novel and user-friendly toolbox designed to characterize the topological organization of the human functional connectome. Moving beyond traditional functional connectivity (FC) measures that rely on temporal synchronization, our approach is grounded in generative network theory, emphasizing the critical role of topological homophily in shaping brain networks. By operationalizing affinity as the cosine similarity of whole-brain connectivity fingerprints, CCS-FEATHER provides a unified framework to compute four multi-scale metrics: Regional (RFA), Seed-based (SFA), Homotopic (HFA), and Gradient (GFA) Functional Affinity. These metrics offer superior sensitivity to the intrinsic generative rules of the connectome, effectively capturing the interplay between spatial geometric constraints and topological homophily1,3. By streamlining the computation of these advanced markers through an intuitive graphical interface, CCS-FEATHER facilitates large-scale, reproducible research into the generative principles of brain organization and their potential alterations in neuropsychiatric disorders.
