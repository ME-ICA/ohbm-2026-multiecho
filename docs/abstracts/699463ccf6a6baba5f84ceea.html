Introduction

In clinical practice, a child’s development is typically monitored using age-normed behavioural milestones, screening checklists, and anthropometric percentiles such as height, weight, and head circumference. Direct, quantitative assessment of brain maturation is rarely performed, in part because magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is not routinely acquired for developmental surveillance and because pediatric “brain growth charts” are not yet clinically integrated. If feasible, individualized neuroanatomical forecasts could complement existing monitoring by providing a direct measure of expected brain change, potentially supporting earlier detection of atypical trajectories and timely intervention. Quantifying how an individual child’s brain is expected to change over time is essential for distinguishing typical maturation from early deviation. Yet most learning-based “future brain” models are trained to reproduce population-average anatomy at a target age, which can blur subject-specific trajectories, especially when longitudinal data are limited and inter-site variability is high. Here, we propose a longitudinal forecasting framework that predicts a child’s structural MRI at a future age from a baseline scan by leveraging a shared latent space learned across multi-site pediatric datasets and a conditional decoder that explicitly maps age-to-age transitions, conditioned jointly on baseline and target ages.

Methods

Datasets. Structural MRIs were pooled from five pediatric cohorts within the 12-84 month age range: the Baby Connectome Project (BCP)¹ (532 scans, 284 participants) and Calgary Preschool (CP)² (275 scans, 96 participants) longitudinal cohorts; and the PING³ (146 participants), DAUFIN (10 participants), and Pixar4 (62 participants) cross-sectional cohorts. Overall, the combined sample of 502 participants includes 51% boys. Cross-sectional data are used only for the first training stage (representation learning), whereas the two longitudinal cohorts are used to construct 4150 within-subject scan pairs for predicting the follow-up time point.

Preprocessing. Each T1-weighted scan first underwent visual quality control, and scans with excessive motion or other artifacts were excluded. The remaining scans were processed with N4 bias-field correction (ANTs), skull stripped using SynthStrip, and affinely registered to the publicly available 0-8 year pediatric template on NITRC⁵ to standardize anatomy across sites and subjects and promote a stable latent space. Finally, image intensities were normalized with WhiteStripe.

Network. We adopt a two-stage training strategy, summarized in Fig. 1. (1) Representation learning: a variational autoencoder (VAE) is trained on individual pediatric T1-weighted scans to learn a compact latent space. Training uses an L1 reconstruction loss, a perceptual loss, a KL regularization term, and an adversarial loss. (2) Longitudinal forecasting: the encoder from stage (1) is frozen, and a new conditioned decoder is fine-tuned to predict a follow-up scan from a baseline scan using within-subject longitudinal pairs (baseline and follow-up from the same child). The baseline and target ages are embedded and injected into the latent space, and provided as additional inputs to this conditioned decoder, which learns to generate follow-up scans at the desired age. This stage is optimized with an L1 reconstruction loss and perceptual loss between the predicted and true follow-up images, plus an additional delta-image loss that encourages the model to match the change from baseline to follow-up: we first compute an identity “no-change” prediction at the baseline age, then penalize the difference between predicted and true follow-up deltas relative to this identity baseline. No adversarial loss is used during this fine-tuning stage.

Validation. We use 5-fold nested cross-validation with subject-level splits to reflect the forecasting task (baseline age t → follow-up t+Δt) and prevent leakage. Folds are stratified to keep Δt and dataset composition approximately balanced across folds; inner folds are used for model selection (hyperparameters/early stopping) and the outer held-out fold for final testing. Performance is assessed using (i) segmentation-based agreement by running SynthSeg v.2.0 whole-brain parcellations on both true and predicted follow-ups and computing Dice across 18 regions, and (ii) the relationship between predicted and ground-truth regional volumes, where ideal points lie on the identity line (slope = 1, intercept = 0) and linear fit quality is summarized by R², slope, and intercept. The 18 regions considered were: cerebral white matter (WM), cerebral gray matter (GM), lateral ventricle, inferior lateral ventricle, cerebellar WM, cerebellar GM, thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, 3rd ventricle, 4th ventricle, brain stem, hippocampus, amygdala, CSF, accumbens area (acc. area), and ventral diencephalon (DC).

Results

From the top boxplot in Fig. 2, across all 18 regions, the model achieved a macro Dice of 0.82 ± 0.02, indicating good overall agreement between predicted and ground-truth follow-up segmentations. Deep gray and brainstem structures were particularly well preserved (brain stem 0.93, thalamus 0.92, putamen 0.90, pallidum 0.88, ventral DC 0.88, caudate 0.88), as well as the cerebellar cortex (0.89) and hippocampus (0.84). Large tissue classes showed slightly lower but still robust scores (cerebral GM 0.76, cerebral WM 0.79, cerebellar WM 0.82). Lower Dice values were mainly observed for fluid-filled spaces (CSF 0.60, inferior lateral ventricle 0.55) and some ventricular regions (3rd and 4th ventricles ~0.79), which also exhibited higher variability (standard deviations ≈0.05–0.08), consistent with their small size and sensitivity to minor shape differences.

When examining the bottom plots, which display predicted versus ground-truth volumes for each region, the relationship between predicted and ground-truth regional volumes is moderate overall and clearly region-dependent. The best agreement is seen for larger, well-defined structures such as the lateral ventricles (R² ≈ 0.86, slope ≈ 0.89), cerebellar cortex (R² ≈ 0.82, slope ≈ 0.81) and brain stem (R² ≈ 0.82, slope ≈ 0.75), with 3rd and 4th ventricles also showing relatively high R² (respectively 0.80 and 0.75). In contrast, many other regions, including cerebral cortex, CSF, hippocampus and several deep nuclei, exhibit lower linear agreement (R² often < 0.5) and slopes well below 1, together with positive intercepts. This pattern indicates a systematic compression of the volume range (larger regions being under-estimated, smaller ones over-estimated) and a global offset relative to the ideal identity line (slope = 1, intercept = 0), while still preserving volumetric ranking reasonably well for a subset of larger structures.

To understand how these stage-2 predictions are constrained by the first stage, we inspected the reconstructions produced by the initial autoencoder. These show high image-level similarity (structural similarity index values often approaching 0.9, with 1 indicating perfect agreement), yet regions such as the cerebellum and cortical foldings in the cerebral cortex remain more challenging to reproduce, consistent with their comparatively lower Dice scores. This suggests that follow-up prediction in stage 2 is inherently limited by the reconstruction capacity of stage 1, particularly for anatomically complex structures.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the network captures overall anatomy and relative volumetric ranking better for compact, high-contrast structures than for extended tissue classes and fluid spaces with fuzzy boundaries. The mismatch between high Dice and weaker volumetric linearity in some regions implies that the model can reproduce shapes reasonably well while still compressing the true volume range, which is consistent with a tendency toward “average-looking” predictions. For longitudinal applications, this is encouraging in that most regions remain anatomically recognizable and ordered, but it also highlights that absolute volume estimates should be interpreted with caution, especially for CSF and small nuclei. These region-specific biases and range compressions point to concrete targets for improvement (e.g., region-aware losses, stronger age conditioning, or explicit volume-regularization) to better preserve individual developmental trajectories.

Conclusion

The age-conditioned longitudinal forecasting network yielded a macro Dice of 0.82 ± 0.02, with highest concordance for deep gray nuclei and brain stem (up to 0.93) and lower performance for ventricular and CSF regions (down to 0.54), indicating accurate preservation of most anatomical structures in the predicted follow-up scans but more variable recovery of fluid spaces. Volume to volume plots further show that, while ranking of regional volumes is reasonably preserved for several larger structures, the model tends to compress the dynamic range (slopes < 1, positive intercepts), suggesting systematic underestimation of large volumes and overestimation of small ones.

Future work will incorporate a registration-based consistency metric by estimating the deformation from predicted to true follow-ups (expected to be near-identity) and summarizing displacement statistics, stratify performance by age interval (Δt) to quantify how forecasting accuracy degrades with temporal distance, and compare volumetric trajectories (baseline → ground truth vs. baseline → predicted) to assess whether the model reproduces expected developmental trends across regions. Finally, additional analyses will quantify the gain of joint baseline–target age conditioning relative to target-age-only models, both in Dice overlap and volumetric agreement.

In the longer term, such individualized forecasts could serve as a building block for pediatric “brain growth charts”, providing subject-specific expected anatomy against which potential delays or atypical trajectories could be flagged.
