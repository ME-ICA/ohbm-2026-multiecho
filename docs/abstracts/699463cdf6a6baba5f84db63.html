Introduction

Language processing emerges from dynamic interactions among distributed brain regions, with the specific areas engaged depending on the linguistic demands. Producing a single word, generating a full sentence, comprehending speech, or describing a picture each recruits partly overlapping and partially distinct cortical areas1. Functional neuroimaging has greatly advanced our understanding of which cortical regions support these processes and how they co-activate or influence one another. However, the anatomical circuits that enable these interactions remain far less understood. 

In this study, we aim to identify the white-matter pathways engaged during language processing. To do so, we employed a novel method, the Functionnectome2, that integrates task-related fMRI signals with white-matter anatomy to map the functional contribution of underlying tracts. This approach yields the first functional white-matter maps that reveal how anatomically connected regions jointly support language functions.

Methods

A total of 205 healthy young Dutch adults participated in a functional MRI (fMRI) study involving four language tasks: Auditory lexical decision, picture naming, sentence comprehension, and sentence production. Each task included a control condition matched for sensory or articulatory demands, and the resulting contrasts allowed us to isolate neural activity specific to phonological–lexical processing (Word > Quilted Words), semantic–lexical retrieval (Object > Nameless Object), higher-level sentence integration (Sentence > Wordlist), and syntactically structured language production (Sentence > Phrase), respectively.

MRI data were acquired on a 3 T Siemens Prisma scanner using whole-brain EPI with a 32-channel head coil. Preprocessing of the fMRI was performed with fMRIPrep (v22.1.1), which provided standardized correction, alignment, and normalization pipelines. We then applied the Functionnectome framework (openly available:  http://www.bcblab.com) to map task-related functional responses onto white-matter circuits. The Functionnectome projects the fMRI time series from each grey-matter voxel to white-matter voxels based on structural connectivity. The resulting 4D Functionnectome volume represents functional signal weighted by the likelihood that each white-matter voxel is structurally connected to the originating grey-matter region, enabling statistical analysis analogous to classical BOLD fMRI. For both the fMRI and the Functionnectome-derived volumes, we performed first-level and group-level analyses using the general linear model implemented in SPM12.

Results

We analyzed group-level contrast images from fMRI and Functionnectome outcomes. For each task contrast, statistical T-maps for the fMRI were thresholded to identify significant cortical activations (T > 3.13, p < 0.001 uncorrected), followed by cluster-level Family-Wise Error (FWE) correction to control for multiple comparisons (Figure 1). The extent of activation, quantified by the number of suprathreshold voxels, varied across tasks but was consistently greater for the Functionnectome. Picture Naming elicited the most widespread activation (47,417 voxels for BOLD fMRI; 67,700 voxels for the Functionnectome), followed by Sentence Production (38,184; 46,826), Lexical Decision (32,595; 49,067), and Sentence Comprehension (32,424; 58,067). To compare the activation patterns for each task between the left and right hemispheres, we calculated a Laterality Index (LI) for each language task using the following formula LI = (NL-NR) / (NL+NR), where NL and NR corresponded to the number of voxels that show significant activation in the left and right hemispheres respectively. LI values thus ranged from -1 to +1 with positive values indicating left and negative values indicating right lateralization. For both cortical and tract activations, LI values were largely left-hemisphere dominant for Sentence Production (LI=0.23 for fMRI; LI=0.46 for Functionnectome), followed by Lexical Decision (0.21; 0.36) and Picture Naming (0.06; 0.23). In contrast, Sentence Comprehension (0.06; 0.15) exhibited a more bilateral activation pattern.

For the Functionnectome, mean T values were extracted from 68 white matter tracts defined by the Rojkova atlas 3. Across tasks, the cortical and tract activations revealed a pattern of network engagement that varied with linguistic demands. For the Auditory Lexical Decision task, the strongest activations were observed in inferior temporal, fusiform, occipital, and parahippocampal regions, accompanied by high involvement of ventral temporal–occipital tracts such as the inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF) and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF). Picture Naming, which relies more heavily on lexical retrieval and visual–conceptual integration, showed its strongest cortical responses in medial and superior temporal, calcarine, lingual, and frontal orbital regions. This pattern aligned with increased engagement of the ILF, IFOF, frontal inferior longitudinal, and fronto-insular pathways. Sentence Comprehension robustly activated temporal pole, mid-temporal, cingulum, and parahippocampal regions, with corresponding tract engagement in the arcuate posterior and long segments, superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF1-2), uncinate and ventral occipito-temporal pathways. Sentence Production, the most demanding task, showed the strongest activation in Heschl’s gyrus, superior temporal cortex, postcentral gyrus, supplementary motor area (SMA), and frontal opercular regions, alongside heavy recruitment of arcuate and SLF subdivisions, frontal-insular tracts, and orbitofrontal pathways.

Across all tasks, a stable core network emerged, prominently involving the ILF, IFOF, and the arcuate complex, while each task also recruited additional, task-specific tracts and ROIs consistent with its sensory modality and processing demands.

Conclusion

The convergence between cortical activations and white-matter networks recruited during language tasks provides a clear and interpretable neuroanatomical mapping between functional demands and the structural circuits supporting language. The two comprehension tasks (Lexical Decision and Sentence Comprehension) relied heavily on ventral temporal-occipital regions and ventral association tracts (ILF, IFOF, uncinate). These pathways constitute the canonical ventral stream, which is specialized for semantic processing and the integration of sensory input with conceptual knowledge, a profile consistent with tasks requiring word recognition and meaning retrieval. Conversely, the production tasks (Picture Naming and Sentence Production) showed a clear shift toward frontal, motor, and peri-insular regions, and their profiles were dominated by dorsal pathways including the arcuate long and anterior segments and all SLF subdivisions. These circuits represent the dorsal stream, which is primarily dedicated to phonological encoding, motor planning, and sensorimotor integration, essential for sequencing and articulating speech. While Picture Naming maintained strong ventral engagement (ILF/IFOF) for initial visual-semantic transformation, the strong dorsal recruitment underscores the speech motor processes.

Importantly, the recurrent presence of the ILF, IFOF, AF and SLF across all tasks indicates a stable structural backbone of connectivity supporting the entire speech-to-meaning cycle. The robust parallelism between the observed cortical activation patterns and the specific recruitment of the ventral (ILF, IFOF, uncinate) and dorsal (AF, SLF) white matter tracts provides strong neuroanatomical support for the Dual-Stream Model of language4. Together, these findings demonstrate that the observed activation profiles are neuroanatomically coherent and capture how the brain flexibly recruits these two specialized circuits to meet the distinct demands of auditory recognition, semantic access, syntactic integration, and speech production.
