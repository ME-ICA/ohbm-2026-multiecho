Introduction

Alterations to subcortical volumes are widely observed across neurological1,2 and psychiatric disorders, yet traditional gross volumetric measures may overlook finer regional variations more closely related to disease-specific vulnerabilities. When applied to large-scale samples, 3D surface-based features can help derive consensus brain signatures for particular disorders. Bipolar disorder (BD) is associated with smaller gross subcortical volumes where patterns can depend on clinical factors such as age of onset, presence of psychotic symptoms and treatment. Lithium treatment is associated with larger (normalized) volumes, and antiepileptic and antipsychotic medications are associated with smaller volumes. Mapping the influence of such factors may lead to more clinically-informative markers for prognosis and treatment response monitoring. The ENIGMA Subcortical Shape Pipeline (https://github.com/ENIGMA-git) is a standardized protocol to extract vertex-wise radial distance and surface Jacobian metrics across subcortical structures and has been used in international consortium studies of schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, substance use disorders, Parkinson’s disease,2 and 22q11.2 deletion/duplication syndrome. Building on this foundation, we introduce a comprehensive Python-based toolkit (Figure 1) to empower multisite shape feature harmonization, accelerated vertex-wise statistical modeling, interpretable machine learning, and 3D results visualization.

Methods

T1-weighted brain MRI data from 21 independently collected study samples (BD=1,487, healthy controls (HC)=1,886; 8.0–83.0 years, 42% male) from the ENIGMA Bipolar Disorder Working Group (ENIGMA-BD) were processed using our shape analysis toolkit. Bilateral subcortical (gross) volumes (hippocampus, amygdala, caudate, putamen, pallidum, thalamus, and nucleus accumbens) were segmented using the ENIGMA-standard FreeSurfer (v5.3) protocol. The ENIGMA Subcortical Shape Pipeline was used to extract vertex-wise shape features (local radial distance or local thickness) along 27,120 vertices from the aforementioned structures. The 4% of cases failing standardized visual quality control were excluded subsequent statistical analysis.

Our toolkit includes several established harmonization methods to adjust for potential site effects, including ComBat and ComBat-GAM, while preserving variability associated with covariates of interest including diagnosis, age, and sex. The toolkit includes a callable module for efficient vertex-wise mixed-effects modeling using Fast and Efficient Mixed-Effects Algorithm (FEMA) with fast runtime and low memory usage using sparse matrix algebra and parallel computation.3 Computational efficiency of FEMA and traditional MATLAB fitlme were compared for BD vs. HC group comparisons subcortical shape features (27,120 vertices) with diagnosis as predictor and site as random effects while controlling for age, sex, and intracranial volume (ICV).

BD versus HC group differences and associations with medication status at time of scan were assessed for gross subcortical volumes and vertex-wise shape features using multiple linear regression adjusting for age, sex, ICV. Site-as-random effect and ComBat harmonized data were compared to adjust for site effects with all results corrected for multiple comparisons (False Discovery Rate (FDR) q<0.05). 

Machine learning (ML) functionality includes traditional linear support vector machine (SVM) and linear/logistic regression model with an L1 penalty to encourage sparse solutions and a Total Variation (TV) term that promotes spatial coherence in the vertex-wise weight map.2 We tested BD versus HC diagnostic classification and medication treatment at time of scan prediction using ComBat-GAM harmonized features with 10-fold cross-validated grid search to identify the optimal parameters, and model performance was quantified using ROC–AUC and balanced accuracy. Our toolkit includes data imputation modules for robust machine learning analyses to handle missing data or exclusion after Q procedures. Strategies include: 1) imputation maps for each subcortical shape model based on the average shape metrics across all subjects, 2) separate imputation maps for BD and HC groups, 3) within individual hemisphere-flipping where poorly segmented structures in one hemisphere are replaced by properly segmented structures from that individual’s opposite hemisphere. 

The toolkit offers several static/interactive visualizations for displaying statistical or machine-learning results (Figure 1), including both original anatomical correspondence, as well as an unpacked dorsal/ventral visualizations. An interactive 3D mode allows users to explore the surfaces via a web browser (see https://yim-igc.github.io/ENIGMA_BD_Shape_LME_details/)

Results

BD was associated with smaller gross hippocampal (Cohen’s dBD-HC = −0.12) and thalamic volumes (dBD-HC = −0.08) compared to HC (Figure 2A). Antiepileptic and antipsychotic treatment was associated with smaller thalamic volumes (dAntiE = -0.28, dGen2AntiPsych = -0.17), and lithium treatment was associated with larger thalamic volumes when compared to individuals not taking those medications.

Shape analysis revealed more complex differences between BD and HC (Figure 2B). BD was associated with thinner regions in the hippocampus and thalamus. The putamen and caudate showed complex patterns, with some areas displaying greater thickness alongside focally thinner regions. Lithium use was associated with greater thickness in the thalamus and hippocampus, whereas antiepileptic use was associated with lower thickness in the same structures. Compared to the traditional linear approach (fitlme), FEMA showed a 16-fold reduction in modeling time.

Machine learning models trained on gross subcortical volumes did not show significant predictive performance for any task, whereas TV-L1 models leveraging vertex-wise subcortical shape features demonstrated significant balanced accuracy across all three tasks, including BD diagnosis: 55.4%, lithium treatment: 57.2%, and antiepileptic treatment: 55.2%. Models trained without imputation achieved the best results (BD vs. HC classification BAC = 55.4; lithium prediction BAC = 57.2; antiepileptic prediction BAC = 55.2). Using a single population-average imputation map led to small reductions in performance across tasks (BD versus HC classification BAC = 54.7; lithium prediction = 55.2; antiepileptic prediction = 54.6). Diagnosis-specific imputation produced a similar BD versus HC performance (BAC = 54.6) and lithium prediction relative to the single group-average imputation map (BAC = 55.7), with comparable accuracy for antiepileptic prediction (BAC = 54.6). Hemisphere-flip imputation resulted in the same BD versus HC performance (BAC = 55.4) as the non-imputed model and resulted in slightly worse lithium treatment (BAC = 55.1) and antiepileptic prediction performance (BAC = 54.2). Across all strategies, imputation led to slight reductions in predictive performance, likely reflecting the heterogeneity of subcortical geometry in BD, which may be influenced by clinical factors such as medication exposure or severity of illness. 

The patterns of altered thickness from linear statistical modeling (Figure 2B) partially overlap regions contributing to machine learning performance (Figure 2C). Importantly, Logit-TVL1 weight maps appear to vary depending on regularization parameters. Weight maps became more sparse as the TV parameter increased, which reduces the degree of spatial smoothing. Because the optimal parameters from our tests were selected based on validation performance, higher spatial smoothness was not always guaranteed.

Conclusion

Here, we present a scalable Python toolkit to support large-scale 3D surface-based brain geometry modeling. Using the largest subcortical shape data sample of its kind from ENIGMA-BD, we demonstrate a framework to integrate multisite brain data, apply robust site harmonization techniques, run efficient mass-univariate modeling, and interpretable machine learning methods to characterize diagnostic group differences and associations with clinical features such as treatment status. BD was associated with locally thinner shape features that provided modest but significant diagnostic and medication status predictions. Our toolkit module applying FEMA provided significant computational acceleration, essential to empower future large-scale analyses. Imputation experiments highlighted an important consideration for large-scale surface-based analyses when dealing with missing data. Future improvements will include evaluating both predictive performance and the spatial coherence of the ML weight maps when tuning these parameters, and integrating surface-based shape features with complementary biological modalities, such as polygenic risk scores, gene expression gradients, cytoarchitectural profiles, and neurotransmitter distributions to develop multiscale models of BD and related conditions.
