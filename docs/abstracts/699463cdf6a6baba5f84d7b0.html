Introduction

In fMRI research, voxel-wise classification between two or more experimental conditions has become an increasingly useful complement to standard general linear model (GLM)-based analysis. Conventional analysis estimates voxelwise BOLD responses via a GLM by convolving with a canonical hemodynamic response function and applies statistical tests with multiple-comparison correction. In contrast, classification methods impose no such constraints and, therefore, they are less prone to overfit results. They can also capture multivariate structure that is inaccessible to univariate GLM approaches, often yielding greater sensitivity even with small sample sizes. However, classification algorithms may exploit spurious or confounded information as well as systematic noise to optimize their objective functions, requiring careful control and validation.

Our work aimed to classify group-level functional responses of first-order thalamic nuclei to visual, auditory and motor stimuli. These nuclei are fundamental to information processing, being critical for relaying sensorimotor inputs to cortical regions and are central for human language systems that differ in their sensorimotor processes such as reading, speech comprehension, and speech production.1,2 We selected the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) for visual input, the medial geniculate nucleus (MGN) for auditory input, and the ventral lateral nucleus (VLN) for motor input. We use two datasets containing three language task modalities -- word reading (i.e., visual), speech comprehension (i.e., auditory) and speech production (i.e., motor). We hypothesize that classification based on these modality-specific thalamic regions would yield shared patterns that generalize robustly to unseen subjects. This methodological approach may further extend to the classification of higher-level cognition processes that exhibit shared characteristics across subjects.

Methods

We selected two in-house independent language datasets for classification, referred to as the Lang and Lang Sparse datasets [Figure 1]. Both were block design fMRI experiments in which participants were instructed to perform parallel word reading (visual), speech comprehension (auditory) and speech production (motor) tasks. The Lang dataset followed a continuous acquisition scheme with a TR=0.85 s while the Lang Sparse dataset adopted sparse-sampling paradigm3 with a longer TR=2.9 s (effective TR=1.7 s) to reduce the effects of scanner noise and motion-related artifacts accompanied with overt vocal responses. Specifically, a silent period was introduced by turning off the scanner gradients right after stimulus presentation, with image volumes acquired at the end of the silent interval. Another difference between the datasets is that participants in the Lang Sparse dataset were instructed to keep their eyes closed in auditory and motor tasks whereas participants in the Lang dataset kept their eyes open across all tasks. In the Lang dataset, 12 words were presented with equal spaces in each block across all modalities. This design yields 16, 16 and 15 trials per subject spanned over two runs for visual, auditory and motor tasks respectively, resulting in 1392, 1305 and 1392 trials per condition from 87 valid subjects. In the Lang Sparse dataset, we obtained 7 trials per condition per subject across 7 runs, yielding a total of 280 trials per condition from 40 valid subjects.

Both datasets were acquired on a 3T Siemens Magnetom PRISMA Fit scanner using an Echo-Planar Imaging (EPI) sequence. The Lang dataset was processed with SPM12, including realignment, interpolation of corrupted volumes using nearest-neighbor replacement with the Artrepair toolbox, coregistration and normalization to MNI152 space, and spatial smoothing with a 4-mm FWHM gaussian kernel. Preprocessing for the Lang Sparse dataset differed only in the inclusion of a slice-timing correction, which was necessary due to the longer TR (>1 s). Finally, time series were extracted from six thalamic nuclei -- the left and right LGN, MGN and VLN (Krauth et al., 2010’s Atlas4 in MNI152 space, 2-mm resolution) via the MarsBaR toolbox with grand-mean scaling. Linear detrending was applied subsequently. Volumes from 4 s onward within each trial were averaged and concatenated across subjects, yielding three matrices with left and right thalamic nuclei combined. 

Classification between pairwise conditions was performed using a linear SVM with a soft margin (SVC from scikit-learn with C=1.0). We chose a linear SVM classifier for two main reasons: it is relatively robust to small sample sizes and high-dimensional features without overfitting, and its weights are interpretable. A customized leave-one-eighth-subject-out cross-validation was used for train-test partitioning. ROC AUC scores obtained from non-task specific thalamic nuclei were also reported as reference (e.g. for tasks Visual-Auditory associated nuclei are LGN and MGN, and the non-task specific nuclei is VLN). Finally, permutation tests with 10,000 iterations were performed for each pairwise classification to generate null distributions of ROC AUC scores. To better interpret the feature importance, trials were averaged within each class to obtain two representative data points, which were then multiplied with the weights of the linear SVM classifier. The resulting values were averaged across thalamic nuclei for visualization. The sign of each value indicates the side of the hyperplane (decision boundary) on which the data point lies, while larger absolute values reflect greater distance from the hyperplane and thus higher classification confidence. Learning curves were generated using 8-fold cross-validation to assess classifier performance, with train-test splits structured to approximate a leave-one-eighth-subject-out scheme.

Results

ROC AUC scores exceeded 0.75 for all condition pairs in both datasets, with the lowest value observed for the Motor-Visual pair in the Lang dataset (0.7562) and the Visual-Auditory pair in the Lang Sparse dataset (0.7612). ROC AUC scores for the Visual-Auditory pair and the Motor-Auditory pair were 0.8354 and 0.9371 respectively in the Lang dataset, both involving the auditory condition. In the Lang Sparse dataset, the highest ROC AUC scores were observed for the Motor-Visual pair at 0.9471 and the Motor-Auditory pair at 0.8842, both containing the motor condition [Figure 2-B]. This difference may be the result of distinct experimental paradigms used in two datasets. The sparse-sampling paradigm used in Lang Sparse dataset is intended to 1). reduce motion artifacts associated with overt word production; 2). minimize the interferences of scanner noise which usually enhances neural activity in the MGN of thalamus. Comparing ROC AUC scores using task-specific thalamic nuclei combined and the non-task specific thalamic nuclei in classifying the same condition pairs, ROC AUC scores obtained from using task-specific thalamic nuclei combined were higher than using the non-task specific thalamic nucleus across all pairs in two dataset except for one pair, Motor-Visual in the Lang dataset, where the score (0.7562) was lower than the reference score obtained from using MGN alone (0.7842). However, the two scores did not differ significantly based on a paired t-test across K-fold cross-validation (p = 0.076).5

We further examined the influence of the features in the classification outcome. In the Lang dataset, the VLN barely contributed to the distinction between motor and visual tasks and the motor and auditory tasks. In contrast, VLN showed greater contribution for the same two task pairs in the Lang Sparse dataset. This may explain why, in the Lang dataset, the Motor-Visual pair achieved a slightly higher ROC AUC score when using MGN alone than using LGN and VLN combined. Although features from both the LGN and VLN were included as inputs, the classifier relied primarily on the LGN. Note that the non-task specific thalamic nucleus is not entirely irrelevant to the task but less relevant, which is sufficient for this coarse validation. Figure 2-A is an example of feature importance in the Visual-Auditory pair classification for the Lang dataset. Positive values were observed for LGN and MGN combined features for the auditory task and negative values for the visual task, indicating that both thalamic nuclei played a significant role in separating both tasks (visual and auditory).

We also plotted learning curves of the linear SVM classifiers to check for overfitting. From learning curves, we observed that the accuracy scores plateaued after using approximately half of the available training data. Adding more data after this mark barely improved the accuracy scores in the test set, although in some cases the gap between train and test scores was reduced. In the Lang Sparse dataset, the training curves for Motor-Visual pair and Motor-Auditory pair were saturated at an early point of the learning curves, indicating that the model is robust even when the amount of training data is very low. Conclusion

Despite the low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of subcortical fMRI signals, reduced spatial resolution due to smoothing and interpolation, and potential inter-subject misalignment arising from anatomical normalization, we were able to robustly classify visual, auditory and motor stimuli using first-order thalamic nuclei. Across two independent datasets with different fMRI designs but similar language tasks, all pairs of tasks achieved ROC AUC scores above 0.75 and were significantly higher than random using permutation tests. These results indicate that the modality-specific characteristics represented in the thalamic nuclei are sufficiently consistent and strong to support reliable classification. Unlike searchlight-based decoding, our approach recruited spatially distributed regions rather than the local neighborhoods, which is advantageous when activations of local voxels are close to the noise floor and become unstable. The result showed that salient shared patterns can be classified without GLM-derived estimates, which does not allow for a train-test split. A remaining concern is the influence of confounds and systematic noise, which must be addressed regardless of the analytical method. Further work is required to quantify their impact on classification performance. The feature-importance map derived from linear SVM coefficients provides an initial assessment, as one would expect that condition-specific thalamic nuclei should contribute meaningfully to class separation. One caveat we have not addressed is the potential loss of idiosyncratic, fine-scale functional patterns unique to each individual in the process. However, if sufficiently shared characteristics can be identified, they may serve as information constraints for aligning subjects without eliminating the finer-grained information.
