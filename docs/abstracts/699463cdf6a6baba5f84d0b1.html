Introduction

Diffusion-weighted Imaging (DWI) is a pivotal non-invasive imaging modality in clinical diagnosis and neuroscience. By probing water molecule motion to reveal tissue microstructure, DWI is indispensable for stroke detection, tumor characterization, and white matter tractography. However, acquiring high-resolution (HR) DWI is inherently constrained by hardware limits. To maintain acceptable signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), DWI is typically acquired at lower spatial resolutions, which limits the precision of quantitative analyses.

Super-Resolution (SR) techniques have been developed to reconstruct high-resolution images from low-resolution inputs. While Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have achieved visual sharpness, they face critical challenges in medical imaging. The adversarial training paradigm often results in instability and mode collapse, compromising structural fidelity and hindering the recovery of intricate details1. Furthermore, existing 2D-based methods often neglect the volumetric nature of MRI, leading to slice-wise inconsistencies. Recently, Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) have emerged as a superior alternative, offering high-quality generative capabilities with stable training objectives2. By modeling the data distribution through a gradual iterative denoising process, diffusion models can recover fine details with higher structural fidelity and reliability than GANs.

In this study, we investigate a 3D Diffusion Model for DWI super-resolution. Unlike 2D approaches, this architecture leverages volumetric context to ensure anatomical consistency along all axes. Our goal is to enhance resolution without prolonging scan time. We demonstrate that the proposed approach superiorly preserves microstructural integrity compared to GAN baselines, with comparable quantitative fidelity. This work provides a robust framework for high-fidelity medical image enhancement, facilitating more precise diagnostic capabilities.

Methods

Dataset

We utilized the publicly available 3T diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) dataset from the Human Connectome Project (HCP). We selected 150 participants and extracted the 90 volumes acquired at a high b-value of b=3000 s/mm2. The dataset was divided into 100 subjects for training (9,000 volumes), 20 (1,800 volumes) for validation, and 30 (2,700 volumes) for testing.

Data Preprocessing

A preprocessing pipeline was applied prior to training. First, a binary brain mask was used to remove boundary noise and retain only intracranial tissue. Low-resolution (LR) inputs were simulated via bicubic downsampling using MONAI3. We deliberately avoided max pooling, as it selects regional maximum intensities; instead, we relied on bicubic interpolation to preserve global intensity profiles and realistic partial volume effects. Due to GPU memory constraints, we employed a patch-based strategy. Volumes were zero-padded to 160×192×160, and 3D patches of size 643 were extracted with a stride of 32 voxels. This strategy captures local microstructure while managing memory consumption.

Network Architecture

We adapted the MRI-to-CT transformer-based improved denoising diffusion probabilistic model (MC-IDDPM) architecture proposed by Pan et al.4, extending it from conditional CT generation to 3D MRI super-resolution. The model employs a diffusion-based generative procedure that progressively transforms a three-dimensional isotropic Gaussian noise sample into a HR image, guided by the corresponding LR input. By gradually adding Gaussian noise to a HR image over 𝑛 timesteps, the image can be converted into a nearly pure Gaussian noise sample. The original HR image 𝑋0 can then be recovered by progressively denoising 𝑋T. Accordingly, the diffusion framework consists of two stages: a forward process that injects Gaussian noise into the HR image, and a reverse denoising process learned by the proposed Swin-VNet, which removes the added noise from the noisy images to reconstruct the SR image, as illustrated in Figure 1(b).

We integrated residual learning to further stabilize the training and enhance convergence speed. Instead of synthesizing the full HR image, we constrain the model to generate only the residual difference between the HR target and the LR input. This residual map primarily captures the high-frequency information, such as sharp edge definitions, tissue boundaries, and subtle microstructural variations. By focusing the model's capacity on recovering these missing high-frequency details, the learning burden is reduced. The final SR output is obtained by adding the generated residual back to the LR input.

Evaluation

To comprehensively assess the quality of the reconstructed images, we employed a set of quantitative metrics covering both intensity fidelity and structural similarity. For standard image quality assessment, we utilized the Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) and Multi-Scale Structural Similarity Index (MS-SSIM), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Mean Squared Error (MSE) to measure intensity precision and structural integrity.

Given the clinical relevance of dMRI, preserving the directional information of water diffusion is essential for downstream analyses. To evaluate this aspect, we estimated the diffusion tensor at each voxel from the SR images and computed the Mean Angular Error (MAE) to quantify discrepancies in fiber orientation. MAE measures the average angular deviation between principal diffusion directions derived from the reconstructed images and those from ground-truth HR data. Lower MAE values indicate better preservation of white matter directional coherence, emphasizing the biological validity of the reconstruction beyond conventional visual similarity metrics.

Results

A quantitative comparison of our proposed 3D diffusion model against LR and the GAN-based approach5 is presented in Table 1. In the evaluation of Quantitative Anisotropy (QA) maps, which are critical for defining fiber definitions, our method achieved comparable fidelity in DWI but superior in QA. Specifically, we attained the highest SSIM of 0.9773, MS-SSIM of 0.9890, and PSNR of 38.3390. Most notably, in terms of directional accuracy, our method yielded the lowest Mean Angular Error (MAE) of 5.4259°, representing a significant reduction compared to the GAN-based method (5.7057°) and the LR baseline (9.1021°). This lower MAE indicates that the diffusion-based reconstruction aligns more precisely with the ground-truth fiber orientations, suggesting a substantial advantage in preserving the angular integrity of white matter tracts. The visual superiority of the proposed method is illustrated in Figure 2. While the GAN-based approach successfully sharpens the image compared to the blurry LR input, it introduces structural discontinuities. In contrast, our diffusion-based model reconstructs anatomical edges with greater fidelity and minimal noise-induced distortions.

This structural integrity is further supported by the downstream tractography results. We performed fiber tracking on the Corpus Callosum (CC) to evaluate performance in regions with high angular complexity. As highlighted by the red arrows in Figure 2, the GAN-based reconstruction introduces spurious lateral fibers that are not present in the HR reference, leading to irregular and anatomically inconsistent orientations at the bundle periphery. In contrast, our diffusion model better preserves the coherence and continuity of these pathways, producing trajectories that more closely resemble the high-resolution ground truth. This demonstrates that the proposed method provides improved reliability not only for global anatomical structures but also for localized regions requiring precise directional information.

In addition, we evaluated the impact of training patch size on the reconstruction performance of the model. In the DWI, the smaller patch size (64×64×64) achieved higher SSIM (0.9492), MS-SSIM (0.9831), and PSNR (35.0303), along with a lower MSE (0.0003), compared with the larger patch size (128×128×128), which obtained SSIM (0.9435), MS-SSIM (0.9788), PSNR (34.0775), and MSE (0.0004). A similar trend was observed in the QA evaluation, where the smaller patch size resulted in a lower angular MAE (5.4308) relative to the larger patch size (5.6400°). These findings suggest that using smaller patches facilitates better preservation of intricate details and high-frequency structures. A plausible explanation is that smaller patches allow the model to focus more effectively on local variations and microstructural patterns in DWI, thereby enhancing its ability to recover detailed information during super-resolution reconstruction.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the application of a 3D Diffusion Model tailored for the super-resolution of diffusion-weighted MRI. By leveraging a volumetric generation process combined with residual learning, the proposed framework effectively reconstructs high-resolution anatomical details from low-resolution inputs while mitigating the training instability and hallucinations often associated with GAN-based methods. Quantitative evaluations demonstrated that our approach achieves superior performance in standard image quality metrics (SSIM, PSNR). Crucially, the significant reduction in Mean Angular Error (MAE) validates that our model preserves the intricate directionality of water diffusion, which is essential for reliable downstream analysis. This was visually corroborated by tractography results, where the proposed method yielded fiber pathways with improved structural coherence and alignment compared to baseline methods. Ultimately, this work offers a clinically viable solution to the inherent trade-off between resolution and scan time, potentially enabling high-fidelity microstructural mapping in routine neuroscience and diagnostic settings.

The proposed approach enhances the spatial resolution of diffusion MRI without extending scan time, thereby improving image quality and increasing its potential clinical utility.
