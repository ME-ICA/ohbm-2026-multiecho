Introduction

Methylphenidate (MPH) is one of the most prescribed medications for the treatment of attention deficits and has been shown to modulate multiple dimensions of attention. However, the precise mechanisms underlying its cognitive effects remain unclear. MPH is known to increase dopamine levels, particularly in the striatum,1  yet also elicits dose-dependent effects on higher-order cortical regions.2 Additionally, MPH-induced changes in extracellular dopamine in the striatum have been shown to predict connectivity changes between the striatum and the cortex.3 Previous work therefore emphasizes the importance of subcortical-cortical dynamic interplay in mediating the effects of MPH on attention faculties often considered cortico-centric. One specific impact of the drug is the reduction of attention orienting bias, the stable, individual tendency to direct attention toward a particular hemispace in healthy individuals. Alterations in attention orienting bias have been previously reported in attention deficit disorders,4 suggesting that these subtle asymmetries in attention are of clinical relevance. Here, we use pharmacological resting-state fMRI combined with data-driven co-activation pattern (CAP) analysis to quantify dynamic brain states and test their associations with MPH-induced changes in attention orienting preference.

Methods

Fifty-four healthy young adults with stable spatial orienting bias completed the greyscales task 5 (a perceptual task quantifying attention orienting bias) and a resting-state fMRI scan (234 Volumes, TR=2000ms, TE=30ms) under orally administered 20mg MPH and placebo (PL) in a randomized double-blind study (within-subject). T1-weighted scans were acquired for registration purposes. The Preferred Asymmetry Index (PAI), the proportion of preferred minus non-preferred choices in the grayscales task, was used to quantify the magnitude of the orienting bias. Imaging data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep followed by nuisance regression that included the average WM/CSF signals, head motion parameters, using nilearn-based load confounds, with a simple denoising strategy. Time series were band-pass filtered (0.008-0.1Hz). Parcellation included: the 1000-Schaefer, the Tian-V2, and the Buncker-2011 parcellations. Time series were extracted and concatenated to form a time-by-region matrix. Dynamic co-activation patterns (CAPs) were identified using a data-driven k-means clustering. The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating inertia and silhouette scores, yielding an optimum at k=5. For each CAP, the occurrence rate, dwell time, and transition probabilities were calculated. MPH and PL were compared using a paired t-test. Correlations between PAI and dynamics measures were calculated using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. FDR correction was applied to account for multiple tests. Eligible participants signed an informed consent form and were monetarily compensated for their time. The study was approved by the ethics committees of the University of Haifa (approval #368/17) and Rambam Health Care Campus (approval #0539–16).

Results

Behavior: When comparing PAI, we found that MPH significantly reduced orienting bias (t(53)=2.7, p=0.009). Brain: Five CAPs were identified (Fig. 1a). CAP1 and CAP3 emphasize states that show subcortical regions as activated or deactivated, in particular, globus pallidus and nucleus accumbens. In contrast, CAPs 2, 4, and 5 emphasize states that show cortical activation/deactivation. When comparing CAPs between MPH and placebo, we found a significant increase in occurrence rate and dwell time in CAPs 2 (t(53)=–2.48, pFDR<0.05; t(53)=–4.80, pFDR<0.001) and 5 (t(53)=–4.08, pFDR<0.005; t(53)=–2.44, pFDR<0.05), a significant reduction in dwell time and occurrence rate in CAP3 (t(53)=–2.32, pFDR<0.05; t(53)=3.97, pFDR<0.005), and a significant reduction in occurrence rate and dwell time in CAP1 (t(53)=3.73, pFDR<0.005; t(53)=2.21, pFDR<0.05, Fig.1b). Transition probability analysis showed a significant difference for the transition from CAP2 (cortical-dominant) to CAP3 (subcortical-dominant), such that under MPH this transition was less likely to happen (Fig. 1b, mean difference=–0.058; d=–0.513). A paired permutation test confirmed this effect (pFDR=0.00833). Brain-Behavior: Dwell time showed CAP-specific associations with PAI. CAP1 (subcortical-dominant) showed a significant negative correlation in the placebo condition (r=–0.314, pFDR<0.05) such that the lower dwell time under placebo, the higher the degree of asymmetry. CAPs 2 (r=0.319, pFDR<0.05) and 4 (r=0.356, pFDR<0.05) (cortical-dominant) showed a significant positive correlation between dwell time and PAI under placebo, such that the longer dwell time in these CAPs, the higher the degree of asymmetry under placebo. These associations were not evident when testing the potential association between dynamic measures and PAI under MPH. No significant correlations were found for occurrence rate and PAI in any of the CAPs in both placebo and MPH conditions. 

Conclusion

MPH causes significant alterations in brain state dynamics. Under MPH, the balance between subcortical and cortical states is altered, and states are skewed towards cortically dominant states, at the expense of subcortical ones. Importantly, these dynamics are associated with the degree of asymmetry under placebo, but not under the MPH condition, which reduces asymmetry, thereby linking subcortical-cortical dynamics balance with trait-like asymmetry in spatial attention.
