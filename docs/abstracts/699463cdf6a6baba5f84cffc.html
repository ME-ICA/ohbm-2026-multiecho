Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental biological process essential for cognitive functioning, emotional regulation, and physical restoration. In adolescence—a period of rapid neural maturation—sleep becomes especially critical. Yet circadian delay, academic pressures, screen exposure, and psychosocial stress commonly restrict adolescents’ sleep, making insufficient sleep duration a major public-health concern.

Short sleep duration, while not necessarily indicating clinical sleep disorders, reliably reflects broader developmental risk. It has been linked to poorer attention and executive function, lower academic performance, emotional dysregulation, and heightened internalizing and externalizing symptoms. Recent large-scale evidence further highlights sleep problems as among the strongest predictors of adolescent mental-health difficulties.

Mechanistically, inadequate sleep may disrupt synaptic homeostasis and alter the maturation of cortico-subcortical circuits and large-scale networks—systems undergoing substantial remodeling during adolescence. Characterizing how sleep relates to neural network organization is therefore key to understanding vulnerability to later behavioral and psychiatric outcomes.

We leveraged data from the large-scale, longitudinal Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study. We investigated how sleep duration relates to the organization and longitudinal development of resting-state functional networks during adolescence. By integrating multi-wave neuroimaging and behavioral data, we aimed to (1) identify reproducible sleep-related connectivity patterns, (2) examine how these brain–network organizations vary with sleep duration over time, and (3) evaluate whether such patterns prospectively predict developmental and mental health outcomes in adolescence.

Methods

Data were drawn from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study® Release 5.0. The current secondary analyses were exempt from Yale IRB review. All rs-fMRI preprocessing and connectome construction were performed independently by Yale and McGill teams. We used the largest available samples at each wave: 8,843 participants at baseline, 6,127 at 2-year follow-up(Y2), and 1,150 at 4-year follow-up(Y4). Remaining data supported reproducibility analyses.

Two preprocessing pipelines were used.
Voxel-wise (Yale): SPM12 + BioImage Suite with slice-timing correction, motion realignment, nonlinear MNI registration, 5-mm smoothing, nuisance regression (WM/CSF/GM signals, 24 motion parameters, polynomial trends), temporal filtering, and global signal regression. This produced 8,843 baseline and 2,441 Y2 follow-up FC matrices.
ROI-wise (McGill): ABCD minimal pipeline plus: initial frame removal, BBR alignment, FD/DVARS computation with fsl_motion_outliers, respiratory pseudomotion filtering, 18-parameter nuisance regression, censoring with interpolation, and 0.009–0.08 Hz band-pass filtering. This yielded 4,247 baseline, 6,127 Y2, and 1,150 Y4 matrices.
All connectomes were constructed using the Shen268 atlas; pairwise Pearson correlations were Fisher-z transformed. Quality control excluded high-motion scans (FD>0.3 mm), missing/asymmetric edges, or outliers.

Two measurement of Sleep Duration were used:

Parent-reported sleep: habitual sleep duration item from SDSC. This item is coded as 1 = 9–11 h, 2 = 8–9 h, 3 = 7–8 h, 4 = 5–7 h, and 5 = < 5 h, reverse-coded so higher values indicate more sleep.
Youth-reported sleep: MCTQ beginning at Y2; weekly sleep duration computed as weighted mean and rescaled to a 5-point scale.

CPM was used to identify connectivity patterns predicting parent-reported sleep duration at baseline. Using 8,843 baseline connectomes, we conducted 10-fold cross-validation repeated 100 times to extract mask. During training, edges associated with sleep duration at p<.001 (Spearman) were retained and separated into positive vs. negative sets. For each participant, summary network strength was computed as positive minus negative edge strength. Linear regression parameters were trained on all 8,843 subjects. Predictive accuracy (Spearman’s ρ) was assessed via 10,000-iteration permutation testing.

Model robustness was evaluated across sex, household income, and pre-/post-scan sleepiness. Group differences (Δρ) were tested using 5,000-iteration permutation tests with FDR correction (q<.001).

The baseline CPM model was applied to:
(1) baseline connectomes to predict youth-reported MCTQ sleep;
(2) Y2 and Y4 connectomes to predict both parent- and youth-reported sleep at corresponding waves;
(3) independent cohort not used during training.

We tested external validity in 515 adults (20–40 yrs) from the Human Connectome Project with two rs-fMRI sessions. Observed sleep duration was derived from the PSQI. Predicted versus observed values were correlated separately for Rest1 and Rest2.

To examine whether sleep changes tracked neuromarker reorganization, we computed positive, negative, and difference network strengths at baseline and Y2, z-standardized within wave, and calculated Δ values. Linear mixed-effects models tested Δ sleep → Δ connectivity associations, adjusting for age, sex, income, with site as random intercept.

Network-pair coupling (positive-negative balance) was computed across canonical systems. Linear mixed models tested associations with impulsivity (BIS/BAS, UPPS), psychopathology (CBCL), and cognition (NIH Toolbox), adjusting for demographics and site. Partial correlations were derived from t-statistics; FDR controlled at q<.05 (39 phenotypes×55 network pairs=2,145 models).

Linear mixed models tested whether baseline neuromarker strength predicted CBCL internalizing/externalizing symptoms at Y1 and Y2, adjusting for baseline CBCL, demographics, sleep duration change, with FDR correction across all tests.

Results

SLEEP DURATION NEUROMARKER

CPM reliably predicted parent-reported sleep duration at baseline (ρ = 0.25, p < .001). The resulting neuromarker involved widespread within- and between-network couplings. Shorter sleep was linked to stronger medial frontal/sensorimotor–visual and frontoparietal–cerebellar/subcortical connectivity, whereas longer sleep was associated with greater within-motor/sensory and medial-frontal connectivity and stronger links among frontoparietal, salience, and subcortical/cerebellar networks. Motor/sensory regions showed both positive and negative associations, indicating functional heterogeneity. Visualizing motor-only versus non-motor edges revealed a dissociation: motor/sensory positively coupled with heteromodal and subcortical networks but negatively with visual areas. 

Model's subgroup robustness

Model performance did not differ across sex, income, or sleepiness groups.

Sensitivity and Generalizability

The neuromarker generalized across timepoints and preprocessing strategies. Both voxel-wise and ROI-wise data showed significant associations with parent- and youth-reported sleep at 2- and 4-year follow-ups (e.g., ROI: baseline ρ = 0.26; Y2 parent ρ = 0.23, youth ρ = 0.21; Y4 parent ρ = 0.24, youth ρ = 0.20; all p < .001).

Independent subsamples not used in training also showed significant prediction: ROI baseline (n = 610, ρ = 0.21), Y2 (n = 1,443, ρparent = 0.23, ρchild = 0.23), and Y4 parent (n = 201, ρ = 0.30). Voxel-wise Y2 predictions were weaker but significant (n = 290; ρparent = 0.13, ρchild = 0.15). These results demonstrate temporal stability and cross-participant generalizability.

The neuromarker replicated in an independent adult sample despite differences in age and acquisition protocols. Predicted sleep duration correlated with PSQI-derived sleep in both HCP sessions (Rest1: ρ = 0.11, p = 0.0096; Rest2: ρ = 0.13, p = 0.0028; Fig.e), supporting cross-dataset robustness.

LONGITUDINAL NETWORK REORGANIZATION

Changes in sleep duration were reflected in changes in neuromarker organization. Increases in sleep were associated with increases in positive connectivity (β = 0.013, p < .001) and greater positive–negative network difference (β = 0.016, p < .001). Decreases in sleep showed the opposite pattern. Negative-edge strength did not track sleep changes (β = −0.003, n.s.), suggesting that longitudinal reorganization primarily reflects the integrative (positive) network component.

BEHAVIORAL ASSOCIATIONS

Network-pair coupling showed broad behavioral relevance.

Stronger coupling—especially heteromodal–heteromodal and heteromodal–unimodal—was positively associated with crystallized intelligence, vocabulary, reading, and card sorting, with weaker associations for fluid tasks and minimal effects for flanker and sequencing.

Negative associations with urgency and drive emerged for coupling involving frontoparietal, salience, default mode, and subcortical/visual systems.

Most CBCL dimensions showed negative associations with heteromodal–heteromodal coupling, particularly default–salience interactions, with limited associations for anxiety and somatic complaints. Overall, heteromodal networks showed the strongest and most consistent behavioral relevance.

PROSPECTIVE PREDICTION OF PSYCHOPATHOLOGY

Baseline neuromarker strength predicted later symptoms at 1- and 2-year follow-ups.

At Y1, stronger positive and differential connectivity predicted lower rule-breaking and conduct problems (p < .05), whereas stronger negative connectivity predicted higher symptoms. We did not find any other measures can be significantly predicted. Likewise, applying the same predictive model using 2-year brain data to forecast 3-year mental health outcomes yielded no significant associations.

Conclusion

Using large-scale ABCD longitudinal data, we identified a robust, age-generalizable sleep-duration neuromarker that tracks within-individual sleep changes and links heteromodal network coupling to cognition, impulsivity, and mental health.

The large sample and multilevel validation framework provide strong statistical power and demonstrate the neuromarker’s robustness and generalizability across subgroups, analyses, and cohorts.

Longitudinal analyses reveal how sleep variation shapes functional network maturation and show that early neural signatures can forecast later psychological functioning.

Our findings suggest that heteromodal network modulation is a key pathway through which sleep affects broad behavioral and emotional outcomes, highlighting the neuromarker’s potential for early mental-health risk identification in adolescence.

During adolescence, rapid changes in cognition, socio-emotional functioning, and behavior unfold in tandem with major reorganization of large-scale brain networks. Building on Dong et al’s study1, Cortical maturation between ages 9 and 14 follows a characteristic transition from primary visual and sensorimotor regions toward higher-order association systems, including the default mode and frontoparietal control networks. In this context, sleep duration appears closely coupled to the pace of this hierarchical reorganization. Longer sleep is associated with enhanced synchronization between sensorimotor regions and frontoparietal, medial frontal, and salience networks—patterns consistent with more advanced integration of higher-order association circuits. In contrast, shorter sleep duration is linked to stronger coupling between sensorimotor and visual networks, a configuration typically observed at earlier developmental stages and potentially indicative of a sleep-related lag in network maturation.

The present analysis extends the prior work, which only focused on the cortex. In our study, we further found that the frontoparietal and sensorimotor networks show associations with subcortical and cerebellar networks in opposite directions. Longer sleep is associated with stronger coupling between the sensorimotor network and subcortical and cerebellum networks while shorter relates to the frontoparietal netowork to subcortical and cerebellum networks. Multiple studies support that the hyper-connectivity between frontoparietal and subcortical could be compensatory2-3. Long-term lack of sleep could induce such resting baseline strength between the frontoparietal and subcortical and cerebellum networks, which induce decline cognition function and adaptation/resilience. 

The association between stronger positive network strength and longer sleep duration, consistent with evidence that adolescent sleep promotes heightened synaptic pruning and circuit refinement compared to adults4-5. Together, these findings suggest that insufficient sleep may disrupt network-level maturation, increasing vulnerability to later behavioral and mental-health difficulties.

Limitation 

* Sleep duration captures only one aspect of multidimensional sleep health, so incorporating richer sleep measures will be essential for a fuller understanding of neurodevelopment.

* Because the present analyses are correlational, future studies leveraging causal frameworks or interventional designs are needed to clarify directionality.

* Finally, the longitudinal sleep neuromarker itself may evolve with development, necessitating future work on its stability and clinical sensitivity across age and populations.
