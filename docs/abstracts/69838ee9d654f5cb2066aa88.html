Introduction

The glymphatic system plays a critical role in clearing toxic proteins from the extracellular interstitial space. Glymphatic clearance begins with the exchange between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and interstitial fluid (ISF). CSF circulates through the ventricles and the subarachnoid space, then enters the brain parenchyma along periarterial channels, thereby mixing with ISF. This process is driven primarily by arterial pulsation and convective bulk flow of ISF. CSF and ISF then traverse the parenchyma through aquaporin-4 water channels on astrocytic endfeet and drain into the perivenous space carrying metabolic waste. Notably, CSF influx has been shown to increase nearly twenty-fold during sleep compared with wakefulness. In line with this, large global BOLD fluctuations in humans have been found to coincide with CSF inflow during sleep1.

Recently, the glymphatic function has been linked with changes in brain parenchymal electrical resistance2. In the field of MRI, several methods have been proposed to measure electrical conductivity (=1/resistivity). Particularly, the phase data of gradient echo sequences used in functional MRI or spin sequences have been used to assess tissue electrical conductivity which can indirectly monitor variations in ionic concentration. Conductivity estimation from MRI typically relies on the transmit RF field phase (B1 phase), which is linked to conductivity through electromagnetic theory3. Under certain assumptions, conductivity is approximately proportional to the Laplacian of this B1 phase.This study aims to investigate the potential of MR phase data as a imaging marker by assessing whether ion-flux changes associated with glymphatic activation during sleep are detectable in phase signals, and whether these phase variations are coupled to tissue conductivity and glymphatic activity.

Methods

All experiments were performed on a 3T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Vida, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Two healthy volunteers (27 ± 2 years) participated in both resting-state fMRI, balanced steady-state free-precession (bSSFP) and T1 Magnetization Prepared - RApid Gradient Echo (T1 MPRAGE) acquisitions. Scan parameters are as follows. Multi-band 2D EPI (FOV = 256 mm, TR/TE = 625/20 ms, flip angle = 30°, matrix = 96×96, Resolution = 2.5 mm isotropic, Multi-band factor = 4, 48 slices). T1-weighted 3D MPRAGE (FOV = 256 mm, Resolution = 1mm isotropic, TR/TE = 2200/2.52 ms, flip angle = 9°, 192 slices). bSSFP (FOV = 315 mm, TR = 4.00 ms, TE = 2.00 ms, Flip angle = 30°, Resolution = 2.5×2.5×5.0 mm³, slice thickness = 5 mm, 1 slice) Figure1 a).

Segmentation maps were derived from T1-weighted MPRAGE images and aligned to both fMRI and bSSFP data. fMRI magnitude data underwent slice-timing correction, white-matter nuisance regression, 0.009–0.08 Hz band-pass filtering, and no spatial smoothing was applied. To preserve fine-scale spatial features, fMRI phase images were subjected only to slice-timing correction, without spatial smoothing or additional nuisance regression. The bSSFP data underwent only first-order phase correction to mitigate eddy-current–induced phase drift; no additional preprocessing was applied to preserve the original phase characteristics. All preprocessing was performed using AFNI, FSL, and FreeSurfer, and all analyses were conducted in fMRI space. 

The global Blood Oxygenation Level Dependent (gBOLD) signal was obtained by applying the previously derived cortical gray-matter mask to the fMRI magnitude data. Similarly, the fMRI phase signal was extracted by applying the same gray-matter mask to the fMRI phase data. The CSF signal was derived from a CSF region of interest defined in the most inferior slice in the neck direction. For the bSSFP phase signal, the gray-matter mask was applied to the bSSFP phase data. The bSSFP Laplacian signal was computed as the spatial Laplacian  of the bSSFP phase. This Laplacian-derived metric was used as a simple proxy to qualitatively assess the phase-conductivity relationship underlying the phase-based conductivity reconstruction3.

MRI acquisition consisted of alternating 6-minute resting-state fMRI and 6-minute bSSFP scans over a total duration of 1 hour. Each consecutive pair of resting-state fMRI and bSSFP acquisitions was grouped into a single segment, labeled S1 through S5. During segments S1-S3, subjects were encouraged to fall asleep, whereas from S4, subjects were awakened and instructed to remain in the awake state through S5. For data analysis, the fMRI and bSSFP data from segments S1-S5 were first separated by modality and then temporally concatenated prior to further analysis.

To investigate cross-modality signal coupling, cross-correlation analyses were performed. Analyses were conducted on segment-level time windows of approximately 150-200 s, and segments in which the gBOLD-CSF cross-correlation exhibited patterns similar to those reported by Fultz et al5 were identified. For these selected segments, additional cross-correlation analyses were performed for fMRI phase-CSF, bSSFP phase-CSF, and bSSFP Laplacian-CSF signals.

Results

Figure 2 a) - d) presents the original signals, in which percent signal changes are simultaneously plotted for (a) global BOLD and CSF, (b) fMRI phase and CSF, (c) bSSFP phase and CSF, and (d) bSSFP Laplacian and CSF. For both subjects, cross-correlation analyses were performed by cropping the sleep segment (S2) and the wake segment (S4) from the acquired scanning segments. 

During sleep, the gBOLD - CSF cross-correlation showed a strong anticorrelation in both subjects (Subject 1: maximal r = −0.570 at lag 3.1 s, p < 0.05; Subject 2: maximal r = −0.582 at lag 4.4 s, p < 0.05), and the −d/dt gBOLD-CSF cross-correlation additionally demonstrated a strong positive correlation(Subject 1: maximal r = 0.723 at lag −2.5 s; Subject 2: maximal r = 0.702 at lag −0.62 s), consistent with the report by Fultz et al5. In the fMRI phase-CSF cross-correlation, both subjects exhibited a significant positive correlation at positive lag times (Subject 1: maximal r = 0.471 at lag 3.8 s, p < 0.05; Subject 2: maximal r = 0.515 at lag 9.4 s, p < 0.05), and a similar pattern was observed for the bSSFP phase-CSF cross-correlation (Subject 1: maximal r = 0.503 at lag 3.12 s, p < 0.05; Subject 2: maximal r = 0.394 at lag 2.5 s, p < 0.05). In contrast, the bSSFP Laplacian-CSF cross-correlation revealed a negative correlation at positive lag times in both subjects (Subject 1: maximal r = −0.500 at lag 3.75 s, p < 0.05; Subject 2: maximal r = −0.400 at lag 2.5 s, p < 0.05). 

During wakefulness, the gBOLD-CSF cross-correlation yielded maximal r values of 0.524 at lag −6.88 s for Subject 1 and 0.508 at lag −6.25 s for Subject 2, while the fMRI phase-CSF cross-correlation showed maximal r values of 0.487 at lag −11.88 s for Subject 1 and −0.502 at lag −2.50 s for Subject 2. The bSSFP phase-CSF cross-correlation during wakefulness resulted in maximal r values of −0.201 at lag −11.88 s for Subject 1 and 0.187 at lag −0.62 s for Subject 2, and the bSSFP Laplacian-CSF cross-correlation yielded maximal r values of −0.166 at lag 21.9 s for Subject 1 and 0.251 at lag −21.88 s for Subject 2. 

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated cross-modality signal coupling between CSF dynamics and phase-based MRI signals to explore the potential of MR phase data as a marker of glymphatic activity. Although accurate assessment of tissue electrical properties ultimately requires dedicated bSSFP-based measurements, fMRI phase data were used to establish a temporal reference aligned with gBOLD and CSF signals. Reproduction of the gBOLD-CSF coupling pattern reported by Fultz et al5 in magnitude data confirms stable sleep-related dynamics in both magnitude and phase signals. This approach established a reliable, temporally aligned reference for phase-CSF coupling and provided a framework for interpreting bSSFP phase-based analyses with limited temporal correspondence to fMRI.

Across all subjects, cross-correlation patterns during sleep exhibited consistent and reproducible trends across modalities, whereas no such consistent coupling was observed during wakefulness. This clear sleep-wake dissociation supports that the observed MR phase-CSF coupling reflects physiological processes associated with glymphatic activation rather than nonspecific fluctuations. Moreover, the opposing correlation patterns observed between bSSFP phase-CSF and bSSFP Laplacian-CSF signals agree with prior electrical properties tomography studies reporting inverse relationships between MR phase and conductivity4. These findings provide a strong rationale for further explicit conductivity reconstruction, as the observed phase- and Laplacian-based trends are consistent with conductivity-related electrical properties-based dynamics reported in prior studies4.

From a broader perspective, electrical properties-based metrics may provide a promising new class of biomarkers for assessing glymphatic system activity. While BOLD-based analyses have been widely used to study glymphatic dynamics, their inherently indirect and relative nature limits quantitative interpretation. In contrast, tissue conductivity offers a physically grounded and quantitatively interpretable parameter that is directly linked to ionic concentration and mobility. Accordingly, conductivity-sensitive phase metrics may provide a more systematic and quantitative means of characterizing glymphatic function beyond conventional BOLD-based approaches.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be considered. Sleep stages were inferred indirectly by reproducing the characteristic gBOLD–CSF coupling patterns reported in prior work, as simultaneous EEG recordings were not available. Although this strategy provides a reasonable proxy for identifying sleep-related dynamics, future studies incorporating EEG will be essential for precise sleep-stage classification. In addition, the relatively small sample size limits statistical generalizability, and validation in larger cohorts will be required. Nonetheless, the consistency of sleep-specific coupling patterns across subjects and modalities supports the robustness of the observed findings.

In conclusion, this work demonstrates that MR phase signals exhibit sleep-dependent coupling with CSF dynamics and that these relationships are consistent with ion-driven electrical property changes predicted by established physical models describing the link between tissue conductivity and MR phase signals. By bridging fMRI phase, bSSFP phase, and Laplacian-based analyses, this work suggests that MR phase information provides a physiologically meaningful window into glymphatic function. These findings further provide a basis for extending phase-based imaging toward explicit tissue conductivity reconstruction. This work lays the groundwork for future investigations integrating phase-based imaging, electrical property modeling, and electrophysiological monitoring to more comprehensively characterize glymphatic mechanisms in both healthy and pathological conditions.
