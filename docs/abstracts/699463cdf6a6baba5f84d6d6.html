Introduction

Infant brain anatomical MR images are characterised by low signal-to-noise ratio, myelination-related reversal in contrast intensity, and small regional and global size. As a result, automated segmentation tools used successfully in older paediatric participants are not suitable for infant images. Morphometric alterations in neonate brains are associated with pre- and perinatal insults and with future outcomes,1,2 but although manual segmentation by neuroanatomical experts is regarded as the gold standard, this is not feasible for larger datasets. Infant-specific image processing tools are thus needed for large-scale studies. Infant FreeSurfer (IFS),3 the automated segmentation pipeline for image processing in 0-5 year-olds, uses a multi-atlas label fusion segmentation framework based on manually-labelled training data. A more recently-developed tool, BabySeg,4 uses convolutional neural networks to segment brain structures of infants and young children across a diverse range of MR protocols. We assessed the reliability of subcortical segmentations produced by BabySeg and IFS, in comparison to a manually segmented dataset of MR images of neonates with prenatal alcohol exposure (PAE). We also assessed their relative sensitivity to detect regional volume changes associated with PAE.

Methods

Heavy-drinking or control women were recruited during pregnancy. During the first postnatal month (gestational age at scan [mean±SD] : 41.4±2.2 wk) their infants were scanned without sedation on a 3T Siemens Allegra MRI scanner using a custom-built neonatal head coil.1 A multiecho gradient echo sequence was acquired twice with protocol parameters as follows: FOV 144mm, 128 slices, TR 20ms, TE 1.46/ 3.14/ 4.82/ 6.5/ 8.18/ 9.86/ 11.54/ 13.22ms, 1mm isotropic resolution, flip angles 5° and 20° respectively. Using mri_ms_fitparms in FreeSurfer, individual echoes from each acquisition were split, tissue parameters were estimated and image volumes synthesised with a 24° flip angle. Images from 104 infants were manually segmented using Freeview in FreeSurfer, and volumes of bilateral caudate, putamen, pallidum, thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala and cerebellar hemispheres were determined. Automated segmentations were performed using IFS and BabySeg. Bilateral putamen and pallidum, and the medulla, pons and midbrain, were segmented manually and in IFS but their volumes were combined into Basal Ganglia and Brainstem volumes respectively to compare to the corresponding BabySeg outputs. Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS, Version 30. Reliability across segmentation methods was assessed using intraclass correlation (ICC) estimates for consistency and absolute agreement with a single-rating two-way mixed effects model and Cronbach's alpha. Infants (n = 63) who did not have exposure to methamphetamine and who had not participated in our clinical trial of choline supplementation5 were included in additional analyses. Sensitivity to detect alcohol-related differences was assessed using a repeated measures general linear model (GLM) for each region with main effects of diagnostic group and segmentation method, and group by method interactions. Diagnostic groups, assessed in early childhood, were as follows: n = 14 with partial fetal alcohol syndrome/fetal alcohol syndrome (pFAS/FAS); n = 23 with heavy PAE but who did not meet diagnostic criteria for pFAS/FAS (non-syndromal; HE); n = 26 with no/minimal PAE. To examine sensitivity in detecting PAE effects, we performed ANOVA, with ANCOVA to control for potential confounding, on data obtained from each segmentation method separately to identify regions showing volumetric group differences. Variables were included as potential confounders if they were weakly associated with ROI volumes (p < 0.1).

Results

Consistency between segmentation methods was moderate to excellent (> 0.5) in 77% of regions, but agreement poor (< 0.5) in 70%. Pairwise comparisons showed that BabySeg and manual segmentation correlated best with each other, with all regions other than the amygdala bilaterally showing moderate to excellent consistency. Cerebellar, thalamus and brainstem regions generally showed acceptable reliability, while the amygdala showed poor reliability bilaterally across all ICC measures.

GLM analyses showed a significant main effect (all p < 0.001) of segmentation method in all regions segmented by all three methods. A main effect of diagnosis (p < 0.05) was observed in the basal ganglia region bilaterally. An interaction effect of segmentation method*diagnosis was seen only in the left hippocampus. In regions not segmented by BabySeg, a significant main effect (all p < 0.001) of segmentation was similarly observed in all but the pons (p < 0.1). A main effect of diagnosis was observed in the pallidum bilaterally, the midbrain and the medulla (all p < 0.05). Weak interaction effects of segmentation method and diagnosis were observed in the vermis and medulla (p < 0.1). Fig. 1 shows regions where volumetric group differences (p < 0.1) were observed for at least one segmentation method. Post hoc pairwise comparisons showed that, in comparison with minimally/unexposed infants, volumes were smaller in HE and/or pFAS/FAS groups for 4 manually traced, 5 IFS and 9 BabySeg regions. After controlling for potential confounders, group differences remained for BabySeg caudate and basal ganglia bilaterally and the right amygdala, with pFAS/FAS volumes smaller than unexposed.

Conclusion

Our results show moderate region-dependent reliability between manual tracing and two infant-specific segmentation tools, Infant FreeSurfer and BabySeg. The strongest pairwise correspondence across segmented regions was observed between BabySeg and manual tracing. PAE-related volume reductions were detected by all three methods across basal ganglia, limbic and brainstem regions, but these effects were most robust in the BabySeg segmentations. These results demonstrate that BabySeg improves on IFS for segmentation of brain structures in neonatal infants, approaching the reliability of manual tracing, and has good sensitivity to detect prenatal alcohol exposure-related alterations in regional brain volumes, supporting its use in large-scale studies of neonatal brain morphometry.
