Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating condition that can lead to long-term impairment and reduced participation in daily life.1 Beyond motor and sensory deficits, individuals with SCI have up to a 13-fold higher risk of cognitive impairment compared to non-injured controls, even in the absence of known contributing factors, such as traumatic brain injury. Possible causes of cognitive changes following SCI include autonomic dysfunction, psychiatric disorders, medication side effects, and neuroinflammation.1

Among the affected cognitive domains, executive functions—essential for planning, decision-making, and inhibitory control—are most frequently impaired.2 These functions can be effectively assessed through verbal fluency tasks, which require rapid word retrieval under phonemic or semantic constraints, and are known to place high demands on executive control. Although previous research has reported reduced verbal fluency performance in individuals with SCI,2 the neural mechanisms underlying these deficits remain largely unexplored.

As cognitive changes in SCI are as relevant to rehabilitation and community reintegration, understanding their neural basis may aid in developing targeted interventions to enhance cognitive functioning, optimize rehabilitation outcomes, and facilitate successful reintegration into daily life.

Neuroimaging has already revealed cortical reorganization after SCI, but most studies have focused on sensorimotor areas,3 and only little research has examined task-related activity during cognitive challenges.

The present study investigated the neural correlates of executive functions in SCI, using a phonemic verbal fluency task during functional MRI (fMRI). We hypothesized that functional reorganization after SCI extends beyond sensorimotor areas, and involves altered functional connectivity within cognitive control networks underlying verbal fluency performance.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This prospective case–control observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Northwest and Central Switzerland. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

24 individuals with SCI (mean age=46±11.3 years; time since injury=13.1±10.8 years) and 26 age- and sex-matched non-injured controls (mean age=43.1±11.5 years) were included in the study. Inclusion criteria were: age 18–60, and for the SCI group, chronic traumatic SCI (>1 year post-injury) with AIS grade A–D. Exclusion criteria included MRI contraindications, pregnancy, psychiatric or neurological comorbidities, substance abuse, insufficient hand function, and documented evidence of traumatic brain injury. All SCI participants underwent a standard neurological examination using the International Standard for Neurological Classification of SCI (ISNCSCI).

Verbal fluency task

Participants completed a phonemic verbal fluency task adapted from the Regensburger Wortflüssigkeitstest during fMRI. The fMRI task used a block design alternating task and baseline conditions of 30 seconds each. During the task conditions, a letter appeared, and participants silently generated as many words as possible starting with that letter. The letter order was randomized. During the baseline conditions, participants silently repeated the word “Cross”. After the fMRI session, the same task was performed overtly outside the scanner to measure performance. The total number of correct words was used as main outcome. To ensure adequate test-retest reliability, we conducted a pilot measurement before the start of the study, and verified that the intraclass correlation coefficients between two sessions of the verbal fluency task was sufficiently high.

Analysis of clinical and behavioural data

Behavioural and clinical data were analysed in R. Group differences in categorical and continuous variables were tested using χ² and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, respectively.

Image acquisition and preprocessing

MRI data were acquired in a 3T Philips Achieva scanner with a 32-channel head coil. The acquisition protocol consisted of i) a structural 3D T1-weighted scan with TE=3.7 ms, TR=8.1 ms, flip angle=8° and field of view of 256x256x180 mm3, and ii) a BOLD sequence with TE=29 ms, TR=2.9 s, flip angle=90°, voxel size=3x3x3 mm3, field of view of 240x240x159 mm3 and 152 repetitions.

Preprocessing and denoising were performed in the CONN toolbox and SPM12, including realignment, slice-timing correction, outlier detection (framewise displacement >0.9 mm or global signal change >5 standard deviations), normalization, segmentation, and 8-mm full width half maximum smoothing. Denoising included regression of motion parameters, white matter/cerebrospinal fluid signals, outlier scans and high-pass filtering (>0.008 Hz).

Functional connectivity analyses and correlations

We implemented a seed-based functional connectivity (FC) analysis, using 11 seeds (bilateral inferior frontal gyrus, insula, anterior cingulate gyrus, left thalamus, right cerebellum crus I, left precuneus, right caudate, and left putamen), according to a meta-analysis on the neural correlates of phonemic verbal fluency.4 At first level, FC values were computed as Fisher-transformed correlation coefficients between seed time series and all brain voxels. At second level, a general linear model (GLM) was implemented for each single voxel using the group variable (SCI vs. non-SCI) as independent variable and the FC of that voxel per subject as dependent variable. Age was included as covariate. Cluster-level family-wise error correction (pFWE<0.05) and Bonferroni adjustment for multiple seeds (pFWE-BF<0.0045) were applied.

Pearson correlations between FC values and verbal fluency performance were computed separately for each group, controlling for age and applying Bonferroni-corrected FWE thresholds, as above.

Results

Behavioural results

Groups did not differ in age, sex, handedness, or education level. SCI severity ranged from AIS A–D (10 A, 1 B, 2 C, 11 D). SCI participants reported significantly higher pain levels (p<0.001), and received bladder medication, antimuscarinics, and pain medication.

Individuals with SCI produced significantly fewer correct words than controls (89.4±27.2 vs. 105.5±22.0; W=462, p=0.024), corresponding to a 15.3% reduction (Figure 1A).

Group differences in functional connectivity

Group comparisons of seed-based FC revealed significantly reduced connectivity in SCI between the left insula and a large bilateral cluster in the superior frontal gyrus (pFWE-BF<0.001; 428 voxels; peak MNI=–18, 16, 60, Figure 1B). This cluster overlaps with regions implicated in cognitive control, performance monitoring, and top-down regulation. No other seeds showed significant group differences after Bonferroni correction.

Correlation between functional connectivity and verbal fluency performance

Within the SCI group, several functional connections between salience-, sensorimotor-, and frontal control-related regions showed negative correlation with verbal fluency performance, indicating that greater coupling was associated with poorer task output (Figure 1C). Specifically:

1. Right insula (seed) / right postcentral gyrus (PoCG, target): ρ=–0.818, pFWE-BF=0.001.

2. Precuneus (seed) / anterior cingulate gyrus (ACG, target): ρ=–0.734, pFWE-BF=0.004.

3. Left putamen (seed) / right precentral gyrus (PreCG, target): ρ=–0.794, pFWE-BF=0.001.

4. Left putamen (seed) / left PreCG extending towards the supplementary motor area (SMA, target): ρ=–0.736, pFWE-BF=0.002.

No significant correlations were observed in the control group.

Conclusion

In this study, we used task-based fMRI to investigate FC differences during a phonemic verbal fluency task and their correlation with performance in chronic SCI. We found: i) reduced verbal fluency performance in individuals with SCI compared to non-injured controls; ii) lower FC between the left insula and the superior frontal gyrus in the SCI group; and iii) negative correlations between verbal fluency performance and FC of right insula–PoCG, precuneus–ACG, and left putamen–bilateral PreCG in SCI individuals. These findings extend previous behavioural studies by elucidating the neural correlates of executive dysfunction in SCI at a network level.

Functional connectivity alterations and executive functions

The lower FC between the anterior insula and the superior frontal gyrus highlights disrupted communication within the central executive network. The anterior insula integrates interoceptive and cognitive signals, acts as a hub for detecting salient stimuli, and coordinates access to cognitive resources. Its altered connectivity with the prefrontal cortex may underlie impaired regulation of executive processes, including speech planning and phonological processing.

Correlations between FC and verbal fluency

Lower verbal fluency performance in SCI was associated with higher FC between the precuneus and ACG, regions involved in phonological processing and high-order cognitive control. This may reflect maladaptive network interactions, with increased FC indicating over-recruitment or inefficient communication within the central executive network. Similarly, verbal fluency negatively correlated with FC between the right insula and the right PoCG, and between the left putamen and the bilateral PreCG. Sensorimotor regions are known to undergo reorganization after SCI, likely as compensatory adaptations to sensorimotor deficits.3 Our findings align with previous work in other health conditions,5 where excessive cross-network coupling was associated with decreased behavioural performance, and suggest that abnormal communication between sensorimotor and cognitive networks may interfere with executive function, resulting in reduced cognitive performance.

Clinical implications

These results reinforce that cognitive impairment is a relevant concern in chronic SCI, and support incorporating cognitive assessments and cognitive rehabilitation into the standard of care. Interventions targeting network-level dysfunction, for instance non-invasive brain stimulation of the superior frontal gyrus, may improve executive function, hence rehabilitation output and quality of life in individuals with SCI.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, verbal fluency was performed silently during scanning to minimize motion artifacts, which may introduce compliance variability, and limit the assessment of actual performance. This was mitigated by an unannounced attention check, and by repeating the task outside the scanner, respectively, though memory effects cannot be fully excluded. Second, no control task was included, limiting the ability to dissociate changes specific to executive functions from network changes related to a general cognitive impairment. Moreover, given the complex clinical presentation, it was not possible to disentangle the contribution of pain or specific medications on patterns of FC. Last, the presence of a subtle and undiagnosed traumatic brain injury could not be fully excluded.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that cognitive impairment in SCI is linked to altered functional connectivity between executive and sensorimotor networks. Disrupted insula–frontal connectivity and maladaptive sensorimotor–cognitive coupling may underline deficits in verbal fluency, highlighting the broader impact of SCI beyond the sensorimotor domain. Understanding network-level disruptions associated with cognitive deficits in SCI may inform strategies to improve rehabilitation in SCI.
