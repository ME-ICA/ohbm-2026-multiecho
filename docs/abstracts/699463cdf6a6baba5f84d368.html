Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is currently the main method of non-invasively inducing neural activity in the human brain. While all brain areas can be stimulated by TMS, the primary motor cortex remains the easiest to investigate. In fact, TMS effects are often quantified using physiological responses, and electromyography (EMG) can quantify muscle activity induced by stimulation of the primary motor cortex. However, neuroimaging techniques can offer further insight into how the signal propagates.  In this context, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a suitable tool1. Specifically, the new BOREALIS system is technically compatible with TMS, allowing simultaneous use on the same cortical site. In this study, we aimed to investigate the influence of the fNIRS on TMS pulse and the influence of TMS on the fNIRS signal by using both systems simultaneously. Moreover, we performed a pilot study to investigate the hemodynamic responses to the TMS stimulation.

Methods

Twenty-one participants (age: 28.4 ± 6.2 years; 12 females) were recruited. The experiment consisted of 10 minutes of fNIRS resting-state acquisition; the assessment of the resting motor threshold (RMT) with optodes; the recruitment curve (RC) assessment with optodes; 10 minutes of fNIRS resting-state acquisition; the assessment of RMT without optodes; and the RC assessment without optodes. The fNIRS montage covered the primary sensorimotor cortex in both hemispheres. TMS was performed on the left hemisphere, while surface EMG activity was recorded from the right abductor pollicis brevis (APB) muscle. Specifically, the center of the coil junction was positioned over the hand area of the left M1, and the site with the highest averaged APB response was identified as the hotspot for eliciting MEPs in the contralateral APB. Then, the RMT was determined for each participant as the lowest stimulator output intensity required to elicit 5 out of 10 MEPs with a peak-to-peak amplitude ≥ 50μV during muscle relaxation. RC with optodes was assessed by measuring peak-to-peak MEP amplitudes (mV) elicited at stimulus intensities ranging from 90 % to 140 % (in steps of 10 %) of the RMT with optodes, while the RC without optodes was assessed by measuring peak-to-peak MEP amplitudes (mV) elicited at stimulus intensities ranging from 70 % to 140 % (in steps of 10 %) of the RMT with optodes. 

The fNIRS signal was preprocessed and analyzed using MATLAB and the Homer3 toolbox functions. Resting-state data were processed using a different pipeline than task data, differing in both the motion artifact correction method and filtering parameters2. After obtaining the time series of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) changes, channels belonging to the Brodmann Area (BA) were averaged to better interpret the results. Then, a cross-correlation was calculated between the left BA4 (L-BA4) and the other BAs. For the fNIRS data collected during the RC, the hemodynamic response function (HRF) was also estimated. In the L-BA4, the average HRF value in the interval [1s; 7s] was extracted to quantify HbO changes in relation to MEP amplitude through a correlation.

Results

Results showed that the TMS was influenced by the fNIRS system. The height of the optodes required a higher intensity of stimulation (RMT with optodes vs. RMT without optodes: 53.9 ± 7.4 % vs. 39.5 ± 5.8 %; p<0.001). However, it was possible to shift and rescale the RC curve obtained with optodes to match the curve obtained without optodes (Percentage without optodes (%) = (RMT without optodes / RMT with optodes) x 100%) (see Figure 1A, 1B).

Moreover, the fNIRS signal was affected by the TMS procedure. Specifically, positioning the coil on the participant's head introduces significant motion artefacts into the fNIRS signal due to the mechanical pressure that the coil applies to the optodes. However, if the user pays attention to this, the signal quality improves considerably. It is therefore recommended to first locate the coil, wait for the fNIRS signal to stabilize, and then start the experimental condition.   

The fNIRS analyses were therefore limited to ten participants (age: 26.33 ± 5.02 years; 6 females) whose data were of adequate quality thanks to the correct management of optode stability. We analyzed three different conditions: one at 1 mV (RC-120% RMT condition) and two conditions with lower (RC-100% RMT condition) and higher (RC-140% RMT) intensities. The HRF showed an increase in activity as the stimulation intensity increased (from 100% to 140% of the RMT, see Figure 1C) in the stimulated motor area (Left BA4), as well as a positive correlation between changes in HbO concentration in Left BA4 and the amplitude of MEPs in the contralateral hand (see Figure 1D). 

Finally, the results of the cross-correlation between L-BA4 and the R-BA4 indicate that, when moving from the resting state (RS) to the RC-100% condition, there is a decrease in functional connectivity between L-BA4 and R-BA4 (p < 0.001). Furthermore, moving from RS to RC-140%, the lag increases, suggesting that L-BA4 temporally anticipates the R-BA4 signal in RC-140% compared to the RS condition (Lag RS vs. RC: -0.28 s vs. 4.11 s, p<0.001).

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results showed that TMS and the fNIRS could influence each other; however, this influence remained controllable, enabling them to be used simultaneously. Data from the pilot study suggest that TMS pulses of different intensities are able to modulate both the functional state and the temporal dynamics of brain activity in distinct ways. All these results suggest that the simultaneous use of fNIRS and TMS could pave the way for new interesting insights into cortical dynamics.
