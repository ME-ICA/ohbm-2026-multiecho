Introduction

Quantifying brain structure from MRI relies on segmentation pipelines that are not only accurate but reproducible across scanners, protocols, and time. Yet reproducibility remains the weak link in morphometry workflows, especially in longitudinal and multi-site studies where small anatomical changes are easily confounded by scanner-induced variance. Tools like FreeSurfer are widely adopted, but their performance under real-world domain shifts remains poorly characterized.

This has direct implications for clinical and translational research. Many studies interpret 5–10% regional volume changes as biomarkers of aging, neurodegeneration, or treatment effects. But for subcortical structures under 10 mL (e.g., amygdala, hippocampus), segmentation noise often exceeds the biological signal. Without method-specific reproducibility benchmarks, downstream inferences risk misattribution or inflated effect sizes.

Most prior evaluations emphasize accuracy on static, curated datasets. These overlook the instability introduced by scanner variation, acquisition drift, and software changes—factors that dominate in prospective and cross-site designs. Reproducibility, not accuracy, is the limiting factor for morphometric biomarkers in real-world applications.

We address this by benchmarking FreeSurfer 8 and FastSurfer using two complementary datasets: 
(1) SIMON, a 17-year single-subject longitudinal series spanning multiple 1.5T scanners, and (2) SRPBS, a nine-site test–retest 3T dataset with tightly controlled cross-scanner acquisitions. We quantify segmentation variability using Dice, Surface Dice, HD95, and volume error across 17 cortical and subcortical regions. Our findings establish reproducibility limits and pipeline-specific biases, and support metric-driven quality control for individualized and cross-site structural MRI analyses.

Methods

We evaluated segmentation reproducibility using two complementary datasets that capture both long-term intra-subject variation and tightly controlled multi-scanner variability.

Datasets
The SIMON dataset consists of 73 T1-weighted MRI scans of a single healthy adult male, collected over 17 years across multiple clinical sites and 1.5T scanners. This dataset captures the cumulative effects of protocol drift, scanner upgrades, and longitudinal variability in a single individual.⁵

The SRPBS Traveling Subject dataset includes 411 T1-weighted scans from nine healthy individuals scanned at nine different sites on 3T MRI systems. Each subject underwent five consecutive scans on the same scanner followed by four cross-scanner sessions, enabling within-subject disentanglement of hardware-induced variance from biological and stochastic effects.⁴

Segmentation Pipelines
We compared three segmentation methods: FreeSurfer 8.0.0 with SynthSeg, FastSurfer, and the longitudinal FreeSurfer pipeline.

FastSurfer replicates FreeSurfer’s anatomical stream using a deep convolutional neural network trained on FreeSurfer-labeled data, offering significantly faster runtime while preserving anatomical parcellation accuracy.³ SynthSeg, a contrast-agnostic segmentation model trained on synthetic MRI, was applied independently per scan using FreeSurfer-compatible labels.²

FreeSurfer was run using the Desikan–Killiany–Tourville (DKT) atlas.¹ For longitudinal processing, subject-specific templates were created using the “-base” flag to generate an unbiased within-subject average, followed by per-session processing with “-long” to reduce intra-subject variability. This enabled a comparison of segmentation stability with and without temporal pooling.

Regions of Interest
We focused on 17 bilateral cortical and subcortical ROIs commonly used in neurodegeneration, psychiatry, and lifespan studies. Subcortical ROIs included the amygdala, hippocampus, thalamus, putamen, caudate, pallidum, nucleus accumbens, and ventral diencephalon. Cortical regions included the insula, fusiform gyrus, entorhinal cortex, caudal anterior cingulate, medial/lateral orbitofrontal cortex, inferior parietal lobule, superior frontal gyrus, and superior temporal cortex.

Preprocessing and Registration
For surface-based metrics, we applied rigid-body registration using ANTs. Two reference strategies were tested: (1) intra-subject alignment to the first time point (native reference), and (2) spatial normalization to the MNI152 asymmetric template. Each scan was resampled using both linear and nearest-neighbor interpolation to assess the influence of resampling choice on metric sensitivity.

Evaluation Metrics
We computed four reproducibility metrics for each ROI:

  • Dice coefficient (DSC): voxel-wise overlap of binary masks.

  • Surface Dice: proportion of surface points within 1 mm boundary tolerance.

  • 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95): robust boundary discrepancy.

  • Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE): relative volume deviation (non-ICV-normalized) between repeated segmentations.

Metrics were computed across ROIs and sessions, and aggregated per subject (SRPBS) or per timepoint (SIMON) to isolate reproducibility effects due to scanner variability, registration, and segmentation method.

Quality Filtering
We evaluated segmentation quality using Dice and Surface Dice thresholds. ROIs failing specified cutoffs were flagged as unreliable. We assessed exclusion rates at each threshold and the resulting impact on MAPE distributions, to evaluate whether boundary-based filtering improved volume consistency without overly reducing data yield.

Results

We evaluated segmentation reproducibility on 73 longitudinal SIMON scans and 411 test–retest SRPBS scans using three pipelines: (1) FreeSurfer 8.0.0 longitudinal (recon-all -long), which builds a within-subject template for each individual; (2) SynthSeg, a contrast-agnostic segmentation model run cross-sectionally per scan; and (3) FastSurfer, a CNN-based replication of FreeSurfer’s anatomical stream. All metrics were computed on raw (non-ICV-normalized) volumes and binary ROI masks unless stated otherwise.

SRPBS test–retest.
Within same-scanner sessions (5 per subject), all pipelines showed excellent reproducibility for large ROIs: hippocampus ICC = 0.98–0.99, CV < 0.3%; amygdala ICC = 0.96–0.98. Cross-scanner sessions introduced marked variability: hippocampus ICC dropped to 0.86–0.90, and amygdala CV rose to 6.4%. For FastSurfer vs FreeSurfer Long, the mean per-subject hippocampal volume bias was –6.8% (95% LoA: –12.4% to –1.2%). Dice scores decreased 3–7 points across methods for cross-site sessions (e.g., hippocampus Dice 0.850 → 0.782; Surface Dice 0.964 → 0.845). HD95 rose by 0.6–0.9 mm in small ROIs. Because FreeSurfer Long shares template surfaces across close-interval scans, ICC/CV may be artificially elevated.

SIMON longitudinal.
FastSurfer produced smooth subcortical trajectories: hippocampus (L/R) = 4.54 ± 0.19 / 4.82 ± 0.16 cm³, CVs = 3.9%/3.3%. FreeSurfer Long yielded systematically larger hippocampal volumes: +9–11% relative to FastSurfer (raw volumes). Temporal variability was reduced by the longitudinal template, but inter-scan CV remained 2–3% subcortically and >5% in cortical regions. SynthSeg maintained subcortical volume stability (cross-scanner MAPE <4%) but showed weaker cortical surface agreement (e.g., entorhinal Dice ~0.72).

Pipeline comparison.
FastSurfer yielded higher Dice scores across cortical regions (e.g., fusiform: 0.816 vs FS Long 0.728; entorhinal: 0.794 vs 0.709), with minimal net volume bias. Surface Dice was comparable across pipelines (Δ ≤0.01), while HD95 favored FS Long in large ROIs but with greater bias in small structures. SynthSeg showed consistent volumes across sites but lower spatial precision; cortical ribbon structures were slightly underestimated (–3% to –5% bias vs FS Long). Subcortical volumes showed tighter agreement between SynthSeg and FastSurfer than with FS Long.

Structure-specific variability.
Amygdala and ventral diencephalon exhibited the highest cross-site deviations (up to 8–10%), often exceeding expected biological changes. Thalamus and fusiform cortex were more stable (Dice ≥0.85, MAPE <4%) but sensitive to interpolation differences. FastSurfer had the lowest bias and highest overlap across ROI sizes.

Quality filtering.
Surface Dice thresholds were effective in flagging unstable segmentations. A threshold of >0.92 removed 5.0% of subcortical ROIs and reduced 95th-percentile MAPE to 8.6%. Lowering the threshold to 0.90 excluded only 3.8% with similar effect. Traditional Dice filtering (>0.80) removed >50% without improving volume stability. Filtering agreement was qualitatively consistent with outliers based on high MAPE or Dice deviation.

Registration and interpolation.
Interpolation choice influenced volume estimates in small ROIs by up to 1.72%. Nearest-neighbor resampling slightly reduced volume bias but increased HD95 in surface-derived metrics. Resampling to MNI vs subject-native space contributed only ~0.07% variability, indicating that interpolation kernel, not template space, was the dominant factor in metric sensitivity.

Conclusion

Our results show that even modern deep learning–based segmentation tools remain sensitive to scanner and protocol variation. Classical FreeSurfer (Longitudinal) and contrast-agnostic SynthSeg both exhibit domain-driven instability, particularly in multi-site contexts. FastSurfer, despite its speed, consistently provided smoother volume trajectories and lower cross-scanner bias. For example, in the SRPBS dataset, small subcortical structures such as the amygdala and ventral diencephalon showed 8–10% cross-scanner variability across all methods, directly limiting the detectability of biologically meaningful changes.

FreeSurfer Long systematically overestimated subcortical volumes relative to FastSurfer (+7–12% in hippocampus and thalamus), with higher inter-scan variability in cortical regions (e.g., fusiform, orbitofrontal), despite template-based smoothing. SynthSeg demonstrated strong robustness in subcortical volume consistency across sessions but lacked surface-level precision in cortical parcellation, reducing overlap scores in regions with complex sulcal patterns.

Reproducibility differed significantly across datasets. SIMON, a single-site longitudinal series with stable acquisition parameters, yielded substantially tighter inter-scan distributions and higher test–retest ICCs across all pipelines. In contrast, the SRPBS test–retest dataset, which spans nine sites and scanner vendors, exposed method-specific weaknesses in cross-domain generalization. These results emphasize the importance of acquisition consistency when interpreting volumetric changes over time.

Multi-metric evaluation was critical. Dice and Surface Dice revealed complementary spatial behaviors across cortex and subcortex: Dice captured gross overlap, while Surface Dice exposed boundary drift and misalignment. HD95 identified local segmentation failures, especially in thin or irregular ROIs. MAPE (computed on raw volumes, not ICV-normalized) exposed relative instability in small structures, with higher error tails in cross-scanner comparisons. Together, these metrics provided orthogonal insights that would be missed by overlap scores alone.

Our findings have concrete methodological implications. Scanner effects, resampling kernels, and method-specific biases should not be treated as negligible variance—they are often larger than the biological effects under investigation. Template-based alignment alone does not fully mitigate protocol noise, especially in cortical surfaces. Filtering out low-quality segmentations using surface-based thresholds (e.g., Surface Dice >0.92) proved effective in reducing variance while preserving data yield.

All code is available at https://github.com/kondratevakate/brain-mri-segmentation, including pipeline wrappers for FreeSurfer Long, SynthSeg, and FastSurfer, ANTs registration scripts, and metric computation.

This work is not without limitations. The absence of manual segmentation ground truth prevents assessment of absolute accuracy; instead, we focus on reproducibility under repeated acquisition. Additionally, ICV normalization was not applied, as the goal was to benchmark raw pipeline behavior under realistic usage conditions. Still, the relative biases and variance scales we report are directly informative for downstream modeling, particularly in longitudinal or multi-site analyses where biological effects are subtle.

We encourage others to adopt this open benchmarking framework for evaluating segmentation reliability in real-world neuroimaging settings.
