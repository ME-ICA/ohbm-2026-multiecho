Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a noninvasive technique used to map the human motor cortex. It modulates neuronal activity by generating time-varying magnetic fields that induce electric fields (E-fields) in targeted brain regions. Owing to its ability to safely and painlessly induce suprathreshold E-fields within the intracranial space, TMS has been widely adopted in research and clinical contexts, including FDA-approved treatments for treatment-resistant depression and OCD1.

Accurate determination of the motor threshold (MT) is essential as MT varies across participants and is critical for appropriate TMS dosing. A widely used approach is the 5-out-of-10 method, in which stimulation intensity is iteratively adjusted until responses are observed in five of ten trials, typically requiring 50–75 TMS pulses. To reduce the number of stimulations, adaptive methods based on Parameter Estimation by Sequential Testing (PEST) have been developed, enabling MT estimation within 14–16 pulses at a 95% confidence interval and up to 30 pulses for full confidence, with accuracy within 5% of maximum stimulator output (MSO). Bayesian adaptations of PEST and machine learning–based approaches have been proposed2; however, their adoption in experimental and clinical TMS settings has remained limited, in part because they lack intuitive user interfaces and support for user-defined stopping criteria with real-time posterior visualization. 

We introduce the Bayesian Uncertainty Dynamic Algorithm for Parameter Estimation by Sequential Testing (BUDAPEST), a tool that models the MT as an uncertain parameter and sequentially updates its posterior using motor-evoked potential (MEP) responses. We establish its accuracy using simulations and human data, promote practical adoption through an intuitive MATLAB-based GUI with user-defined uncertainty stopping criteria, and evaluate its utility by comparing resting and active MT measurements and assessing session-to-session variability.

Methods

TMS and electromyographic (EMG) instrumentation: Magnetic stimulation was applied using a MagPro X100 system (MagVenture, Farum, Denmark) coupled to a figure-of-eight Cool-B35 coil (2 × 46 mm outer diameter). Coil positioning was guided by neuronavigation (Localite, Bonn, Germany) based on individualized head models generated in SimNIBS from each participant’s T1- and T2-weighted MRI scans. EMG activity was recorded from the first dorsal interosseous (FDI) muscle of the dominant hand using TMS-compatible Ag/AgCl C-shaped electrodes arranged in a belly–tendon configuration, with the reference electrode placed over the ulnar styloid. The skin was prepared to ensure electrode impedance remained below 10 kΩ. EMG signals were amplified (gain = 1000), band-pass filtered between 20 and 1000 Hz, and sampled at 20 kHz (Bittium NeurOne, Oulu, Finland).

Experimental protocol: The resting motor threshold (rMT) was defined as the lowest stimulator output that elicited reliable motor-evoked potentials (MEPs; typically ≥50 µV at rest) in 50% of trials. For the active motor threshold (aMT), participants maintained a mild voluntary contraction by holding a small object between the fingers, generating a baseline EMG activity of approximately 100 µV to ensure sustained activation of the target muscle. During active conditions, an MEP was considered present when the peak-to-peak amplitude exceeded 200 µV3. 

At the start of the experiment, the participants remained relaxed, with continuous EMG monitoring confirming minimal unintended muscle activation. Stimulation was initiated at low intensities over the presumed hand region of the primary motor cortex near the precentral gyrus. Single pulses were delivered at randomly selected neighboring cortical sites, with stimulus intensity gradually increased until MEPs exceeding 50 µV were detected. Surrounding locations were then probed at a fixed suprathreshold intensity to identify a provisional hotspot, operationally defined as the cortical site producing the most consistent and robust MEPs under identical stimulation conditions. 

A 3×5 grid centered on the provisional hotspot (7 mm spacing between points) was defined in Localite. The coil was held tangential to the scalp with the induced E-field oriented approximately perpendicular to the central sulcus. The rMT and aMT were estimated at each site of the grid using BUDAPEST, and hotspot thresholds were validated against the conventional 5-out-of-10 method, used as the reference standard. In addition, session-to-session reliability was assessed for each subject to evaluate the experimental utility of BUDAPEST. Overall, experiments were conducted on five right-handed healthy participants following informed consent.

Bayesian Framework: The user initializes the procedure by selecting a starting stimulation intensity and an uncertainty-based stopping criterion (defined as the posterior standard deviation in %MSO). A Gaussian prior over candidate MT values is centered at the initial intensity (𝑘 = 0.5, width ±30% MSO) and iteratively updated via Bayes’ rule after each binary MEP response. Motor responses are modeled with a sigmoid function, capturing biological variability near threshold and defining the MT at the 50% response probability. Uncertainty is quantified as the posterior standard deviation, with multiple stopping criteria evaluated in simulations and a 2% threshold used in human experiments (corresponding to ±2% MT accuracy). A MATLAB-based GUI displays trial-by-trial estimates and uncertainty, allows user input of responses, and supports serial communication for automated control of MagVenture stimulators4 (Figure 1C).

Results

BUDAPEST demonstrated high accuracy and predictable convergence across all uncertainty-based stopping criteria (Figure 1A). Estimation performance scaled systematically with the stopping threshold, reflecting the expected trade-off between precision and efficiency. A lenient 5% uncertainty criterion converged rapidly (mean 3.6 trials) but yielded higher variability (MAE = 3.84% MSO; posterior STD = 4.60% MSO). A stringent 1% criterion produced the most precise estimates (MAE = 1.27% MSO; posterior STD = 1.69% MSO) at the cost of substantially increased sampling (mean 26.4 trials). The 2% uncertainty threshold provided the most practical balance for experimental use, achieving low error (MAE = 1.93% MSO), moderate precision (posterior STD = 2.56% MSO), and efficient convergence (mean 10.2 trials), and was therefore selected for human experiments.

Using the 2% stopping criterion, BUDAPEST-derived resting MT estimates (n = 60) showed tightly bounded error relative to the 5-out-of-10 reference method, with 50% of estimates within ±2% MSO and all estimates within ±4% MSO (Figure 1B). Active MT measurements (n = 17) exhibited a comparable error range (−4% to +3% MSO), with slightly increased variability consistent with known fluctuations in corticospinal excitability during voluntary contraction. Together, these results demonstrate robust MT estimation across resting and active paradigms. The GUI enabled real-time visualization of posterior updates and uncertainty, supported user-defined stopping criteria, and facilitated automated pulse delivery via serial communication (Figure 1C).

Spatial grid mapping revealed a clear corticospinal excitability gradient across the motor cortex (Figure 2A). MT values were lowest at the hotspot and increased progressively with distance from this location, consistent with E-field attenuation and somatotopic organization. Within each session, hotspot localization was consistent across resting and active conditions, demonstrating spatial reliability of the BUDAPEST approach. Across sessions, minor hotspot shifts (typically one grid point; 7 mm) were observed in some subjects, consistent with expected neuronavigation and coil-positioning variability rather than algorithmic error. Notably, the initially defined “tentative hotspot” did not always coincide with the lowest MT, highlighting the advantage of systematic grid-based mapping when high spatial precision is required.

Across all grid locations and participants, aMT values were consistently lower than resting MT, reflecting increased corticospinal excitability during voluntary contraction (Figure 2B). A significant positive relationship was observed between resting and active MT (R² = 0.55, r = 0.74), indicating that individuals with higher resting thresholds also exhibit proportionally higher active thresholds. Test–retest analysis showed strong session-to-session reliability for resting MT (R² = 0.60, r = 0.78), whereas active MT demonstrated weaker reproducibility (R² = 0.24, r = 0.49; Figure 2C). This reduced reliability of active MT is consistent with prior literature and likely reflects physiological variability in maintaining consistent muscle pre-activation rather than limitations of the BUDAPEST algorithm3. Overall, rMT emerged as the more stable metric for longitudinal and clinical applications.

Conclusion

This work introduces BUDAPEST, a Bayesian adaptive framework for rapid and reliable MT estimation in TMS. We established its methodological foundation and validated its performance through extensive simulations and human experiments. Under a 2% uncertainty stopping criterion, BUDAPEST achieved high estimation accuracy, with errors consistently within ±4% of the MSO and a MAE below 2% MSO. The ability to estimate MT in approximately one minute per cortical site (e.g., ~10 pulses with a 5s inter-stimulus interval) enables new possibilities for scalable and high-throughput TMS applications. Systematic motor mapping, which has traditionally been limited by time and operator burden, becomes not only feasible but practical for routine experimental and clinical protocols. By significantly reducing the number of required stimulation pulses, BUDAPEST also improves participant comfort, a critical consideration in clinical and longitudinal studies.

To facilitate practical adoption, BUDAPEST was implemented in an intuitive MATLAB-based GUI that allows experimenters to define uncertainty-based stopping criteria, providing flexible control over the trade-off between estimation precision and procedure duration. This reduction in experimental burden has important implications for clinical workflows, where faster and more reliable MT determination may enable more efficient therapeutic dosing without compromising accuracy.

We further demonstrated the experimental utility of BUDAPEST by comparing resting and active MT measurements and evaluating session-to-session reliability. The algorithm produced consistent and unbiased MT estimates across conditions, with strong test–retest reliability for rMT. The aMT exhibited greater day-to-day variability, reflecting known physiological sensitivity to muscle pre-activation rather than limitations of the algorithm itself. These findings reinforce rMT as a more stable parameter for standardized and therapeutic applications, while confirming the robustness of BUDAPEST under both paradigms.

BUDAPEST is conceptually related to the Stochastic Approximator of Motor Threshold (SAMT), which has been successfully deployed in ongoing clinical trials and models MT using a sigmoidal input–output relationship5. In contrast, BUDAPEST explicitly models posterior uncertainty and allows user-defined uncertainty-based stopping criteria. In large-scale simulations, BUDAPEST achieved lower mean absolute error at a 1% uncertainty threshold than SAMT while requiring a comparable number of stimulation pulses, providing greater transparency and control over the accuracy–efficiency trade-off.

A key next step is integrating BUDAPEST into a fully automated closed-loop TMS framework by incorporating real-time MEP detection using amplitude-based rules or machine learning classifiers, thereby eliminating manual response classification and enabling objective, reproducible MT estimation. Coupling BUDAPEST with robotic coil positioning systems (e.g., the TMS-Cobot platform by Axilum Robotics) would further enable automated multi-site motor mapping, allowing rapid estimation of MT across a cortical grid (~15 sites in ~15 minutes) with minimal operator-dependent variability. Such automation would enable standardized neurophysiological biomarkers for clinical trials, rehabilitation and neurorecovery monitoring, and longitudinal assessment of cortical excitability in neurological and psychiatric disorders. By reducing manual workload and observer bias while supporting uncertainty-aware decision making, BUDAPEST advances precision neuromodulation and individualized therapeutic dosing in both research and clinical settings.
