Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic noncommunicable brain disorder which is characterized by recurrent seizures. For patients with drug-resistant epilepsy, surgery is the preferred treatment to attain seizure freedom. Accurate localization of the epileptogenic zone (EZ) – the crucial brain region responsible for generating seizures - is critical for epilepsy surgery (Thomschewski et al., 2019, Bartolomei et al., 2017). SEEG, the stereotactic implantation of multiple intracerebral electrodes to record electrical activity from various brain regions simultaneously, plays a crucial role in defining the EZ and understanding seizure dynamics, offering insights into spatial and temporal patterns of epileptic activity (Makhalova et al., 2022, Bartolomei et al., 2017).

The Virtual Epileptic Patient (VEP) is an advanced computational framework designed to assist in estimating the EZN (EZ network) to aid surgical decision in epilepsy (Wang et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2024). It employs personalized brain models combined with machine learning techniques to estimate EZNs and support surgical planning. It begins by constructing a structural scaffold of the patient-specific whole-brain network model using T1-weighted MRI and diffusion-weighted MRI (DW-MRI) scans. Each network node is then assigned a mathematical dynamical model to simulate seizure activity. Using Bayesian inference, key parameters of the personalized model are sampled and optimized based on SEEG recordings of the patient's seizures to help identify the patient's EZN. 

The objective of this project is to systematically investigate the sensitivity of the VEP model to SEEG electrode placement and contact localization. The project is organized into four parts:

Part I simulates missing information from the EZN and Part II evaluates the impact of contact localization errors. Part III examines how electrode implantation depth affects model performance  and Part IV evaluates the importance of individual electrodes.

Methods

Data: Data involved in this study are from 7 drug-resistant epilepsy patients who have undergone surgery and had specific brain regions resected and have been seizure-free post-surgery (Engel Class I). 

Method (Fig.1): For each seizure recorded, the VEP pipeline is employed to predict EZN.

In Part I, we selected the top regions by probability for SEEG manipulation. For the selected regions we removed electrode contacts in six groups: Group 1 = contacts inside the region; Groups 2–6 = contacts at 1–5 mm from the region, respectively. After removing each contact group, we re-run VEP to obtain revised EZN predictions. In the end, we compared the original predictions with reduced-data. 

In Part II, each contact was randomly shifted by a fixed-magnitude vector (tested magnitudes: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 mm), the sensor-to-source mapping is recomputed from the perturbed coordinates, and the pipeline is rerun. For each seizure and each shift magnitude we perform 10 independent perturbations and compare the resulting predictions to the original prediction.

In Part III, using patients whose electrode tips were confirmed in the resected EZ, we simulated progressive shallowing by removing contacts one-by-one from the tip outward; after each removal the SEEG is updated and the VEP pipeline is re-run until no contact remains inside the EZ. 

In Part IV, for SEEG recording of each seizure we removed electrodes one at a time (all contacts on that electrode) to create reduced SEEG datasets, re-run the VEP pipeline on each reduced set, and compared the resulting EZ predictions to the original full-electrode prediction to quantify each electrode’s contribution.

Results

Results:

Impact of Missing Information from the EZ | Part I 

The first question is how well VEP performs when the SEEG implantation fails to cover the EZN. Our results (Fig. A) showed that removing SEEG contacts around predicted EZs leads to degraded VEP performance. As more spatially proximal data were excluded, prediction confidence and correlation with the original output systematically decrease. However, the degree of degradation varied across seizures(Fig. B), suggesting inter-seizure variability in reliance on specific contacts. In some seizures, removing contacts from EZ did not eliminate the corresponding regions from the prediction, and the stable performance persisted even after removal of group 6 electrodes, which was not the case in other seizures, where removal of group 1 electrodes induced a evident degradation of performance. This variability suggested that while proximity to the EZ was generally important, certain seizures might be more resilient to contact removal, possibly due to redundancy in sampling or network-level compensation. 

Sensitivity to Localization Error | Part II 

Localization errors in SEEG contact positions represent an additional, often underestimated source of uncertainty. Our results revealed (Fig. C) that the VEP model was generally robust to mild localization errors (up to 2 mm), particularly in cortical regions. However, we observed that predictions became more variable in patients with EZs located in subcortical areas such, where small shifts might cause contacts to be assigned to different anatomical regions. This pointed out the potential vulnerability of deep structures to even small localization inaccuracies, which need to be considered in both implantation planning and post-processing workflows. This finding demonstrated the importance of accurate post-implantation contact localization, especially for deep targets. 

Electrode Implantation Depth and Detection of Deep EZ | Part III 

What is the impact on EZ detection if electrode implantation depth is not sufficient? In cases where an electrode terminates in the EZ, we observed that progressively removing contacts from the tip outward (simulating reduced implantation depth) can cause the EZ to disappear entirely from the predicted top regions. Although the overall prediction structure remained relatively stable (Fig. D), the loss of deeply implanted contacts had a disproportionate effect on the visibility of deep EZ regions. This indicated the need for careful planning to ensure sufficient electrode depth, particularly when targeting deep structures such as the hippocampus. 

Role of Individual Electrode | Part IV 

On evaluating the impact of individual electrode, our results (Fig. E, F) revealed that while the model’s AUC remained stable, precision and correlation metrics varied more substantially, pointing to a discrepancy between overall ranking stability and localized sensitivity. Some electrodes had a greater impact on the prediction outcome than others, particularly those likely to capture seizure onset activity. This suggested that electrode importance was both patient-specific and seizure-specific, and supported the clinical necessity of a strong pre-implantation hypothesis. 

Together, these results highlighted the robustness of VEP to minor distortions in SEEG input but also underscore its vulnerability to incomplete coverage and localization error. The findings emphasized the importance of strategic implantation planning and accurate post-implantation localization.

Conclusion

Together, these results show that the VEP model exhibits both robustness and vulnerability: it

can tolerate moderate perturbations without losing overall ranking accuracy, but is sensitive

to the removal or mislocalization of critical data sources. Most results show patient

specificity and seizure specificity, which reflects the nature of epilepsy and the uniqueness of

EZN, thus demonstrating patient specific approaches such as VEP and virtual brain twins are

particularly necessary and meaningful for seizure prediction and delineating seizure

networks. These findings advocate for more informed and model-integrated SEEG planning

protocols, where hypotheses are tested against possible electrode sampling errors before

implantation. Moreover, acknowledging and quantifying localization uncertainty —

particularly in subcortical areas — could enhance the interpretability and reliability of

computational predictions used in surgical decision-making.

This work provides a systematic evaluation of how SEEG electrode sampling and localization

accuracy influence EZ prediction within the VEP framework. We show that while the model

is resilient to minor perturbations, it depends critically on the inclusion of well-placed,

functionally informative electrodes. These findings support the integration of modeling tools

into the clinical workflow, both for refining implantation strategies and for evaluating

prediction robustness under realistic constraints.
