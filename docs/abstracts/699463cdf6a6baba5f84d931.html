Introduction

As a core intervention in metacognitive therapy (MCT), the Attention Training Technique (ATT) aims to strengthen attentional control (AC), thereby helping to reduce maladaptive cognitive processes 1. Current neuroscientific findings indicate that core regions of the fronto-parietal control network (FPCN) and dorsal attention network (DAN) are engaged during the execution of the ATT 2–4. However, existing paradigms lack objective behavioral performance markers, limiting both experimental control and the capacity to assess training- and psychotherapy-induced changes. Since pupil diameter changes have been established as an indicator of attentional control processes 5, we addressed this issue by adapting an fMRI paradigm designed to capture the ATT 4 for implementation in an eye-tracking setting, hypothesizing that increased AC demands during ATT practice would elicit greater pupil dilation compared with conditions without explicit guidance of attentional focus.

Methods

The task included two ATT conditions — focusing on a single sound while ignoring all others and rapidly shifting attention between different sounds — alongside two passive listening control conditions that do not require intentional attentional regulation. Pupil size data were collected from 52 participants (M = 23.83 yrs., SD = 3.95; 12 males, 40 females), and cluster-based permutation testing was employed to compare pupillary dynamics between ATT and control conditions. To assess subjective experience, trial-wise effort ratings were collected to determine whether ATT trials were perceived as more effortful than control trials and whether subjective effort was related to pupil dilation. Trait AC was measured using the Attentional Control Scale (ACS; Derryberry & Reed, 2002) to investigate whether ATT-related pupil modulation was associated with individual differences in self-reported AC.

Results

Cluster-based permutation tests revealed significant clusters indicating differences in pupil dilation between focusing and both control conditions, as well as between the shifting and both control conditions (p < .05). However, these effects diminished over the course of the trial. No significant clusters were found when comparing the two control conditions with each other or when comparing focused with shifting attention (p > .05) (Fig. 1). Subjective effort differed significantly between the conditions (p < .001), and higher effort ratings were linked to greater pupil dilation throughout the trial (β = 16.89, SE = 4.40, t(1720) = 3.84, p < .001, 95% CI [8.27, 25.52]). Finally, self-reported attentional control was unrelated to both subjective effort ratings and pupil dilation (all p > .05).

Conclusion

The findings demonstrate that pupil dilation is sensitive to the AC demands elicited during ATT practice, distinguishing both ATT conditions from passive listening. Given that trait AC did not predict pupillary responses, the observed modulations likely reflect state-based rather than trait-based AC. The close correspondence between subjective effort and pupil dilation highlights the sensitivity of pupillometry to momentary fluctuations in cognitive effort. The present work indicates that pupillometry holds potential as a quantifiable measure of ATT success and, moreover, for characterizing AC differences across clinical groups.
