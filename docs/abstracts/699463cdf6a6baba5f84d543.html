Introduction

In many sciences, especially in Neuroscience, complex phenomena are often the result of intricate interactions among many factors. As complexity grows, conventional statistics struggle to isolate signal from noise and to identify properties arising from multiscale interactions. Growing evidence suggests that human cognition, especially higher functions, may be emergent properties of high-dimensional brain–body interactions1,2.

While statistical methods have their shortcomings in high-dimensional problems, Machine Learning (ML) has proven helpful for handling noisier and more complex data. However, ML algorithms too often lack the interpretability of statistics. Various methods reduce high-dimensional data to lower-dimensional representations to reveal patterns that explain complex phenomena. Among these, UMAP3 is widely used to capture the structure of complex biological and clinical datasets. While UMAP improves interpretability, it does not provide a tool for studying and predicting the emergent properties on its own. Talozzi and colleagues4 introduced the Disconnectome Symptom Discoverer (DSD), an interpretable method for predicting patients' symptoms 1 year after stroke by embedding high-dimensional lesion-induced disconnection maps into UMAP and exploring the resulting low-dimensional space with ML.

In this work, we present the EMUSES open-source tool, a self-optimising generalisation of the DSD that allows users to train their own interpretable prediction model on their data, whether it is brain imaging-derived, phenotypic, or any data that can be translated into a spreadsheet or a matrix. EMUSES bridges emergent-property modelling and interpretability by optimising a low-dimensional representation and selecting ML models that best predict the variables of interest. As a proof of concept, we reproduce the experiment of the DSD and also explore the prediction of fluid intelligence based on the (non-imaging) phenotypical data of the HCP Young Adult5.

Methods

Datasets and preprocessing. EMUSES is designed to accommodate a variety of data modalities. For proof-of-concept, we used three datasets representing three common data types: (i) an 8×8 handwritten-digit benchmark (NIST, as implemented in scikit-learn, n=1797) for controlled multiclass classification; (ii) the DSD dataset4 comprising 1333 unlabelled post-stroke disconnection probability maps (NIfTI) to learn the latent space, and 149 additional disconnection maps with reading-comprehension scores for supervised modelling; and (iii) unfiltered, non-imaging phenotypic measures (1067 subjects and 117 measures) from the HCP Young Adult dataset5 to predict fluid intelligence as an example of spreadsheet-like inputs. EMUSES flattens inputs into an observations-by-variables matrix and applies 0–1 scaling when needed to ensure comparable feature scales. For fully labelled datasets (NIST, HCP), we held out 20% of the data as an external test set. For the DSD dataset, labelled observations are excluded from Stage 1 and used only for supervised modelling; thus, the prediction cross-validation is performed on data not used to learn the embedding space.

Training Stage 1: latent-space optimisation. EMUSES treats UMAP and HDBSCAN as a single unit and jointly optimises their parameters with Optuna. UMAP parameters are sampled in an outer loop to generate 2D embeddings, and each candidate embedding is evaluated in an inner loop that tunes HDBSCAN (a density-based clustering algorithm). Because both steps are unsupervised, optimisation maximises a composite score combining three embedding metrics (eigen spread, density variability based on k-nearest-neighbour distances, and Shannon entropy) and three clustering reliability metrics (DBCV, cluster persistence, and noise ratio). Each metric can be assigned a target range and weight, allowing the objective to adapt to dataset noise and preventing unattainable "default" convergence targets. The best UMAP/HDBSCAN configuration is carried forward to Stage 2.

Training Stage 2: feature engineering and supervised modelling. EMUSES jointly optimises feature generation, model selection and model hyperparameters. From the optimised embedding, we evaluate: (1) raw UMAP coordinates; (2) Gaussian-weighted distances (GWD) to training points with optimised bandwidth σ\sigmaσ; (3) PCA and kernel-PCA compressions of the GWD representation; (4) correlation-filtered GWD features (optimised Pearson-|r| threshold); and (5) optional autoencoder features pretrained on the original feature matrix. We use nested 5-fold cross-validation for unbiased estimation: an outer loop reports mean ± SD performance, while an inner Bayesian optimisation selects the feature type, model family and hyperparameters. Candidate models include Random Forests, XGBoost, support vector machines/regressors, elastic-net family models, and custom Gaussian-kernel regressors/classifiers. In multi-outcome settings (as in the disconnectome experiment), each target is optimised independently, ignoring missing labels for that target.

Performance metrics and outputs. After training, EMUSES computes a spatial association map by correlating GWD features with the target variable on a 100×100 grid of the embedding space, yielding a heatmap of latent regions associated with high/low values. It also produces effect-size maps for each HDBSCAN-identified cluster to visualise the input-space profiles driving target-related structure. Final predictors are formed by ensembling the best inner-loop models across outer folds and are evaluated on the held-out test set when an external split is available. EMUSES outputs optimised UMAP/HDBSCAN objects, latent coordinates, cluster labels, trained predictive models, performance statistics, and the full optimisation history, alongside interactive visualisation utilities for interrogating the manifold and model behaviour.

Results

We evaluated EMUSES on three datasets spanning controlled benchmarks, non-imaging phenotypes, and imaging-derived stroke disconnectomes. On the NIST handwritten digits benchmark (8×8 images), EMUSES achieved 99.7% classification accuracy under cross-validation and 99.5% accuracy on a held-out test set (Macro-F1: 99.6% and 99.4%, respectively), suggesting strong generalisation beyond the training data.
On the HCP Young Adult cohort, we predicted fluid intelligence from unfiltered, non-imaging phenotypic variables and evaluated performance on an independent test set. The resulting model reached an R² of 0.145 with a mean absolute error of 15% (relative to the target scale).
To demonstrate applicability to neuroimaging-derived data, we applied EMUSES to post-stroke disconnection probability maps. Reading comprehension prediction yielded an R² of 0.158 with a mean absolute error of 17%, evaluated as held-out performance within each cross-validation fold (20% held out per fold). Beyond classic predictive performance metrics, EMUSES provides interpretable maps of the learned embedding space, along with results for the disconnections associated with reading comprehension in the Results Figure. For clarity, we aggregated the effect size maps by region: blue (mostly the left hemisphere) for regions associated with lower reading comprehension, and red for those associated with higher scores. However, the "high" performance is within this dataset of stroke patients, not against a control population. Disconnections associated with higher performance do not mean that some disconnection profiles improve performance, but rather that patients with these profiles perform better than the rest of the stroke population studied.

Conclusion

We released EMUSES as an open-source tool for studying emerging properties in high-dimensional data with a focus on interpretability, reproducibility, and practical usability. Rather than reducing complex datasets to a latent space and stopping there, EMUSES treats the latent space as an object of inference: it provides statistical readouts that make it possible to locate where a variable of interest varies across the manifold and to translate those latent-space patterns back into the original feature space in a form that can be inspected and communicated. In practice, this is what allows EMUSES to bridge predictive modelling and explanatory work without forcing users into opaque "black-box-only" pipelines.

Across three proof-of-concept experiments, our results illustrate both the scope and practical relevance of EMUSES. With the stroke disconnectome data, it illustrates a core advantage for neuroimaging and clinical research: the latent space can be learned from a large unlabelled cohort to capture the main axes of variability in disconnection patterns, and that learned representation can then be used to train the predictive models on the smaller labelled subset—exactly the situation we face in many real datasets. With the NIST handwritten digits, where the structure is clear and the noise is limited, EMUSES reaches near-ceiling performance, showing that the pipeline can faithfully recover a well-defined latent organisation and generalise beyond the training set. Finally, the HCP experiment shows that EMUSES is not restricted to carefully curated inputs: even with unfiltered, noisy phenotypic data, it can still learn a meaningful representation that supports modelling of complex traits, which is precisely what is needed when studying cognition as an emerging property of many interacting factors.

Finally, EMUSES is designed to be usable and extensible by the community: it automates the otherwise brittle parts of this kind of analysis (joint optimisation of embedding, clustering and prediction choices), enforces statistically conservative evaluation patterns, and tracks the provenance needed for genuine reproducibility (seeds, configurations, optimisation history, and versioned artefacts). The goal is that researchers can apply EMUSES to their own datasets, share results in a way that others can reproduce, and contribute improvements back to a common framework. So emergent-property mapping becomes easier to do rigorously and easier to build on collectively. The framework is available at https://github.com/chrisfoulon/emuses (currently via a command-line interface; a graphical interface is in development), and we aim for it to serve as a shared foundation that others can apply, validate and improve to study complex emerging phenomena with explainable machine learning.
