Introduction

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a non-invasive treatment option for individuals with obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD). The neurobiological effects of rTMS extend beyond the targeted cortical brain region and outlast the treatment duration. These durable effects are thought to modulate dysfunctional brain circuits implicated in the disorder. However, evidence linking network-level plasticity to clinical outcome remains limited, particularly in OCD. In the present study, we analysed data from a recently completed randomized controlled trial (TIPICCO) on rTMS for treatment-resistant OCD. We investigated rTMS-induced changes in functional connectivity (FC) between the stimulation target and its normative connectivity network (hereafter referred to as target-to-network FC), and their association with treatment outcome. Normative networks of stimulation targets in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), pre-supplementary motor area (preSMA), and vertex (as control) were derived using an independent resting-state functional MRI (rsfMRI) dataset. We further examined the treatment-induced changes in FC between the stimulation target and regions of the normative network (target-to-region FC) and assessed their associations with treatment outcome. We hypothesized that rTMS treatment leads to stronger (correlated and anticorrelated) target-to-network FC, and that these connectivity changes are related to treatment outcome. 

Methods

Methods are summarised in figure 1. Stimulated normative networks of rTMS targets within the DLPFC, preSMA, and vertex were mapped using a resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) reference dataset from the Global OCD study1. We separately considered positively and negatively coupled normative networks. The Global OCD study included harmonized imaging data from medication-free adults with OCD, their unaffected siblings, and matched age, sex and education-matched healthy controls across five international sites. We included rsfMRI scans (10 min, TR = 2200 ms, TE = 28 ms, 3.3 × 3.3 mm in-plane resolution, 3 mm slices, 0.3 mm gap) from N=158 controls, N=179 OCD individuals, and N=54 siblings (N=391 total) after quality control and full brain coverage screening. Normative networks were estimated using rTMS targets from the TIPICCO clinical trial2 as seeds which used the exact same MRI scan acquisition parameters as the Global OCD study3.

In the TIPICCO trial2, 61 participants received 16 sessions of rTMS combined with exposure and response prevention over 8 weeks and were assigned to 10 Hz rTMS of the left DLPFC (N=19, 110% resting motor threshold (RMT), left preSMA (N=23, 110% RMT), or vertex as control (N=19, 60% RMT). Task-based fMRI was used to identify personalized DLPFC and preSMA targets, while a literature coordinate was used for the vertex. Participants underwent clinical assessments including the Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) and MRI scans before (T0) and after treatment (T2). A total of N=54 participants completed treatment with both clinical and fMRI data and were included in the present analyses.

All rsfMRI scans were preprocessed using fMRIPrep and subsequently denoised using bandpass filtering (0.009–0.08 Hz), regression of 24 motion parameters, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals, spike regressors for framewise displacement >0.5 mm, and global signal regression.

Target-to-network FC was computed at T0 and T2 as Fisher z-transformed Pearson correlations between averaged BOLD time series of the stimulation target and its positive or negative normative network. Target-to-region FC was additionally assessed, with regions defined by ≥30% voxel overlap between normative networks and established atlases (Schaefer 400P7N, Melbourne S2 subcortical, Diedrichsen cerebellar).

Analyses were performed within each treatment group and separately for positive and negative networks (3 groups × 2 directions). FC change was defined as ΔFC=FC(T2)−FC(T0), and symptom change as ΔYBOCS=YBOCS(T2)−YBOCS(T0). Treatment-induced changes in target-to-network FC were assessed using linear mixed models (LMM), and associations with symptom change using linear regression models (LM) controlling for baseline YBOCS severity.

Target-to-region FC change was evaluated using Bayesian region-based analysis (RBA; AFNI4). This approach provides probabilistic estimates of evidence for multiple regions in a single model, reducing the risk of false positives through multiple testing. Intercept-only models quantified overall FC change, and treatment outcome associations were tested using ΔYBOCS as predictor, correcting for YBOCS at baseline. Positive posterior probabilities (P+) <0.10 or >0.90 indicated moderate evidence, and <0.05 or >0.95 strong credible evidence.

Results

DLPFC stimulation

The DLPFC-stimulated normative network showed positive coupling with the striatum and regions within the frontoparietal control network and dorsal attention network, and negative coupling with limbic and visual systems (Figure 2A). At the network level, high-frequency rTMS to the DLPFC did not alter target-to-network FC for either the positive network (LMM: t = -0.09, p = 0.93) or the negative network (LMM: t = -0.36, p = 0.72). Likewise, network-level FC changes were not associated with symptom change for the positive (LM: t = -0.42, p = 0.68) or the negative network (LM: t = 1.06, p = 0.31).

At the region level, however, we found strong credible evidence (P+ < 0.05) for treatment-induced decreases in FC between the DLPFC target and regions within the dorsal attention network and frontoparietal control network (Figure 2B). Decreases in connectivity with specific parcels, particularly those including the frontal eye fields, premotor cortex, and inferior parietal cortex, were credibly associated with symptom improvement (P+ < 0.05)(Figure 2C). We also found moderate credible evidence (P+ >0.90) that increased connectivity between the target and visual cortex regions following treatment was associated with greater symptom improvement.

preSMA stimulation

The preSMA normative network showed positive coupling with the striatum and salience- and somatomotor network, and negative coupling with limbic, inferior parietal, and occipito-visual regions (Figure 2D). At the network level, rTMS did not result in target-to-network FC changes for the positive (LMM: t = 1.94, p = 0.07) or negative network (LMM: t = -0.09, p = 0.93), nor were network-level FC changes associated with outcome in the positive (LM: t < -0.01, p = 0.99) or negative network (LM: t = 0.34, p = 0.74). 

At the region level, we found strong credible evidence (P+ > 0.95) for treatment-induced increases in FC between the preSMA target and frontal regions including the supramarginal gyrus, frontal operculum, and midline premotor regions (Figure 2E). These changes in region-level FC were not associated with treatment outcome (Figure 2F).

Vertex stimulation

The vertex normative network showed strong local connectivity with weaker positive coupling to posterior visual regions, and negative coupling with frontal and subcortical structures. No treatment-induced changes were observed at the network level (positive: LMM t = 0,13, p = 0.90, negative: LLM t = 0.06, p = 0.96). No associations between FC change and treatment outcome were detected (positive: LM t = 0.01. p = 0.99, negative: LM t = -0.58, p = 0.57). At the region level, we also found no evidence for treatment-induced FC change, nor for FC change related to treatment outcome.

Conclusion

We investigated how rTMS applied to different cortical regions affects functional connectivity between the stimulation targets and their normative networks, and how these changes relate to treatment outcome. Across targets, we observed no treatment-related effects at the whole-network level, suggesting that network-averaged measures may lack specificity to detect neuroplastic changes. In contrast, region-level analyses revealed distinct and target-specific effects.

DLPFC stimulation was associated with decreased target-to-region FC, mainly including the frontal eye fields, premotor cortex, and inferior parietal regions. Several of these decreases were associated with symptom improvement, indicating that therapeutic response may depend on down-regulation of possibly hyperactive attention and control circuits during rest. There was also moderate evidence for an association between increased DLPFC–visual cortex connectivity and better treatment outcomes, suggesting therapeutic engagement of the fronto-visual network.

PreSMA stimulation led to increased connectivity with frontal control regions, including the supramarginal gyrus, frontal operculum, and ventrolateral PFC, but these changes were not associated with symptom improvement. Vertex stimulation showed no connectivity changes or clinical associations. 

Together, these findings indicate that rTMS effects on resting-state network functional connectivity are specific to the targeted region and best captured at the region level. Future research may relate rTMS-induced functional changes at rest to functional changes during task performance. rTMS applied over a non-therapeutic cortical region (vertex) does not induce relevant target-to-network (region) FC changes. Control analyses are needed further separate rTMS-specific effects on network function from other contributing treatment factors, such as concurrent exposure therapy or symptom improvement over time.
