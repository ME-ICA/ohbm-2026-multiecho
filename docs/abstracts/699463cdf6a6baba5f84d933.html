Introduction

Structural MRI enables the visualization of cortical folding, composed of gyri separated by sulci whose shape and, in some cases, topology vary substantially across individuals. This folding pattern, largely established in utero, is a potential marker of neurodevelopmental variability and pathological trajectories.

Recently, Champollion [1] was introduced as a foundation model for sulcal morphology, based on the hypothesis that fine-grained sulcal geometry cannot be adequately captured by general-purpose foundation models. Champollion is a lightweight convolutional network (12 layers, ~3M parameters) trained in a self-supervised manner using a Barlow Twins objective on sulcal skeletons extracted from 42,000 UK Biobank subjects, explicitly targeting cortical folding invariants.

Here, we systematically evaluate the zero-shot transferability of several foundation model families to sulcal analysis, comparing their performance with the one of Champollion. We consider two state-of-the-art 2D vision encoders pretrained on natural images (DINOv2 [2] and EVA-02 [3]), three 3D medical foundation models covering the main architectural paradigms in volumetric imaging, SAM-Med3D [4] (ViT-based), BrainSegFounder [5]  (Swin Transformer), and VISTA3D (convolutional SegResNet), all pretrained on large-scale CT and brain MRI datasets, as well as a geometry-oriented point-cloud encoder, Point-M2AE.

Methods

Structural T1-weighted MR images were first affinely registered to MNI space, and cortical folding skeletons were extracted using the BrainVisa/Morphologist pipeline. The resulting skeletons consist of thin surfaces following the deepest and medial parts of sulci, orthogonal to the cortical hull. By construction, these representations are invariant to sulcal opening and largely insensitive to age-related widening, thus capturing intrinsic folding geometry. Inspired by sulcal atlases used in neuroanatomy, the cortex was decomposed into 56 overlapping regions of interest (ROIs), each encompassing one or a few major sulci defined in the BrainVisa/Anatomist nomenclature. In this study, we focus on four representative ROIs (Figure 1): OrbitoFrontal Cortex (OFC), Intra-Parietal sulcus (FIP), Anterior Cingulate sulcus (LC), and Central sulcus (SC). For each ROI, the output is a sparse 3D binary grid in which active voxels represent the sulcal surface.

All models were evaluated under a unified linear-probing protocol. Feature embeddings were extracted from frozen encoders, optionally reduced using PCA, and fed to linear classifiers or regressors trained on four downstream tasks designed to capture complementary aspects of cortical folding: (i) 4-class classification of fine sulcal interruptions in OFC (ROC-AUC one-vs-all), (ii) binary classification of pronounced interruptions in FIP (ROC-AUC), (iii) binary detection of sulcus presence/absence in LC (ROC-AUC), and (iv) regression of a continuous geometric descriptor in SC (R²).

For 2D vision models (DINOv2, EVA-02), 3D skeleton volumes were injected slice-by-slice. Multiple slicing strategies were explored, including direct slicing with nearest-neighbor resizing or volumetric upsampling followed by slicing. Inputs were extracted along single anatomical axes or combined tri-axially. Slices were injected either as replicated RGB images (2D) or as 2.5D triplets of adjacent slices. We evaluated multiple model scales, alternative output representations (CLS token versus spatial feature maps), density-aware slice weighting, and several aggregation strategies across slices and axes (mean pooling or concatenation). 

Medical 3D foundation models (SAM-Med3D, BrainSegFounder, VISTA3D), originally designed for volumetric segmentation, were used in encoder-only mode. We evaluated different input normalizations and patch-level aggregation strategies on the patch grid (mean pooling versus concatenation). To account for the extreme sparsity of sulcal skeletons, we additionally tested density-guided aggregation strategies based on an independent sulcal density map: patches with zero sulcal density were either excluded from aggregation (masking) or linearly weighted according to their density, in order to reduce the contribution of anatomically irrelevant regions. In addition to raw binary skeletons, we also tested distance-map representations to assess whether continuous encodings reduce the domain gap. Features were extracted from the deepest encoder block for SAM-Med3D, while intermediate encoder stages were also evaluated for BrainSegFounder and VISTA3D.

For the geometric baseline, binary skeleton grids were converted into point clouds and processed with Point-M2AE. We explored isotropic upsampling factors, multiple grouping and neighborhood hyperparameters, alternative token-level feature selections (final stage versus multi-level), and different token aggregation schemes.

Results

For each of the four ROIs, datasets were split into five stratified cross-validation folds and an independent held-out test set. Sample sizes were as follows: OFC (Train/Val = 461, Test=116), FIP (Train/Val = 331, Test=77), LC (Train/Val = 273, Test=68) and SC (Train/Val = 707, Test=176). For a given model, all feature-extraction configurations were evaluated using the mean performance across the five cross-validation folds. To enable fair comparison across foundation models and with the domain-specific Champollion model, the configuration achieving the highest mean cross-validation score was selected for each model and ROI, and only this configuration was evaluated on the independent test set. The resulting test-set scores are reported in Table 1.Across models, cross-validated performances remain low and tightly clustered, with limited separation between configurations. For 2D vision models (DINOv2 and EVA-02), results consistently indicate poor transfer to sulcal skeletons, confirming that slice-based encoders struggle to capture intrinsically 3D sulcal geometry. Only weak effects are observed: tri-axial slicing slightly outperforms single-axis inputs, larger ViT backbones yield marginal gains, and strong dimensionality reduction (e.g., PCA to 32D) degrades performance. For 3D medical foundation models, preserving local spatial structure is beneficial, with patch-embedding concatenation generally outperforming mean pooling, while density-guided masking or weighting shows no consistent advantage. Across all architectures, aggressive PCA compression systematically reduces performance and other design choices yield no robust trends. For Point-M2AE, aside from configurations closely aligned with pretraining hyperparameters, no clear pattern emerges, and representations generalize poorly to sparse binary sulcal skeletons, indicating limited transfer from synthetic point-cloud pretraining.

Final results summarized in Table 1 show that 2D foundation models exhibit the weakest transfer overall, with EVA-02 slightly outperforming DINOv2 but remaining limited. 3D medical models achieve higher scores, particularly SAM-Med3D, highlighting the importance of volumetric inductive biases. However, their performance remains modest, indicating that cortical skeletons are largely out-of-distribution with respect to their MRI/CT pretraining. Point-M2AE performs poorly, likely due to the extreme sparsity of the skeleton volumes (≈3% active voxels). Notably, the simple Flatten+Linear baseline remains competitive on topological tasks (LC, OFC, FIP), demonstrating that raw voxel topology can match, and occasionally surpass, pretrained foundation embeddings, underscoring the limited universality of these representations.

Overall, all tested foundation models remain far below Champollion, which consistently dominates across tasks and ROIs.

Conclusion

These results confirm the need for architectures specifically designed for cortical morphology. Champollion, a CNN pretrained in a self-supervised manner on sulcal skeletons from the UK Biobank, demonstrates that a dedicated model can capture sulcal invariants that general-purpose foundation models fail to represent reliably. As future work, we will extend this evaluation by testing all foundation models on raw structural MR images as well as ROI-centered MRI crops, and by exploring self-supervised fine-tuning strategies (LoRA, partial and full fine-tuning) based on Barlow Twins and volumetric MAE.
