Introduction

Sports participation is associated with numerous benefits for adolescents, but collision sports carry risks from exposure to repetitive head acceleration events (HAE), in which the head accelerates due to a direct impact or indirectly via force applied to the body. These short-duration events induce varying strains in neural tissue, potentially leading to structural damage and pathological sequelae. Repetitive HAE exposure, even without causing an acute injury such as mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), has been linked to neurological changes and negative effects on cognition,1 with adolescents particularly vulnerable due to developmental factors. 

Advanced MRI modalities, including diffusion MRI (dMRI) for structural connectivity (SC) and functional MRI (fMRI) for functional connectivity (FC), enable in vivo investigation of these effects. However, HAE studies often characterize connectivity using SC or FC separately, without fully understanding how they interact. The complex relationship between structural and functional connectivity, known as SC-FC coupling,2 is vital for understanding neural physiology but is not well-explored in the context of HAE exposure. An approach for analyzing structural and functional connectivity is via graph theory, which provides metrics capturing local and global aspects of graphs such as efficiency and clustering, which allow for HAE-induced network dysfunction to be quantified.3

This study longitudinally characterized SC, FC, and SC-FC coupling in adolescent rugby players across a single season (imaged in three sessions: early, mid, post-season), comparing them to healthy non-collision sport controls using a suite of graph theory metrics. We hypothesized that SC alteration leads to FC alteration, and that longitudinal changes correlate with mTBI-related symptoms and cumulative HAE exposure. This is one of the first studies to present SC-FC coupling alteration induced by HAE exposure.

Methods

Participants and Study Design 

This study included 31 male adolescent rugby players (collision sport group, 14–18 years old) scanned at three timepoints (early-, mid-, and post-season) and 16 healthy matched non-collision sport controls scanned once (figure 1A). The collision sport group participated in high school rugby union; the control group was comprised of healthy adolescent males who participated in non-collision sports. For both groups, subjects were considered for inclusion if they had no recent mTBI incidence, no neurological conditions, and no substance use. At scanning sessions,  mTBI-related symptoms were assessed using the Sports Concussion Assessment Tool 5th Edition (SCAT-5). During practices and games, the collision sport group wore personalized instrumented mouthguards (iMG), which recorded cumulative HAE exposure metrics including impact count, angular acceleration, and linear acceleration. All work was conducted under ethical approval from the New Zealand Health and Disability Ethics Committee (Reference: 20/NTB/14). 


 Imaging and Connectivity Derivation
 

All MRI scans were acquired using a 3T MR Scanner (GE SIGNA Premier; General Electric, MI, USA) equipped with a 48-channel head coil.  A multi-shell spin-echo dMRI sequence was performed to provide structural connectivity data, and an axial 2D EPI GRE resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) sequence provided functional connectivity data. Additionally, a 3D T1-weighted GRE and a sagittal 3D T2 FLAIR sequence were acquired. Anatomical scans were assessed by a radiologist for evidence of cerebral edema and were used for segmentation and image registration. Manual quality control was performed on all images for presence of imaging artifacts, with affected images excluded from analysis.

dMRI and fMRI images underwent comprehensive preprocessing. dMRI preprocessing included denoising,  B1 field correction, and motion correction, using FSL and MRtrix, and Syncb0-DisCo for deep-learning guided distortion correction. fMRI preprocessing included skull stripping via freesurfer, slice timing correction via fmriprep, and then bandpass-filtering, spatial smoothing with a 6mm FWHM Gaussian kernel, and confound regression via nilearn.  

SC matrices were generated using Anatomically Constrained Tractography (ACT) and ‘tcksift2’ weighting to produce a biologically meaningful representation of white matter tracts. The AAL116 brain atlas, registered to each subject’s space, was used for parcellation, resulting in a 116 x 116 weighted adjacency matrix for SC. 

FC matrices were derived by calculating Pearson’s correlation (r) between the BOLD signals of the 116 spatial regions in the AAL116 atlas. Functional connectivity matrices were thresholded (5% to 40% target densities), and graph metrics were quantified using Area-Under-the-Curve (AUC) across this density range. 

Global and local graph theory metrics (e.g., efficiency, clustering coefficient, small-worldness) were computed using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox. SC-FC coupling was quantified using Spearman’s rank correlation between SC and FC connections aggregated at the network level, mapped to Yeo’s 7-network parcellation (figure 1B). 

Statistical Analysis 

Group differences in global and local graph metrics were assessed in R with Analysis-of-Variance testing on Linear Mixed Models (LMMs), incorporating group, age, and total intracranial volume (for SC metrics) as fixed effects, and subject repeated measures as a random effect. Correlation analyses tested the relationship between changes in global metrics (early- to post-season) and changes in SCAT-5 scores and cumulative HAE exposure. P-values were corrected for false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and effect size was measured with Cohen’s d. An exploratory k-means clustering analysis was used to group collision athletes based on cumulative and average HAE exposure metrics to identify distinct network topological trajectories.

Results

Symptom and kinematic data

The collision sport group sustained a median impact count of 143 [89 – 324] and a median cumulative rotational acceleration of 259,683 rad/sec² [167,080 – 342,223]. SCAT-5 symptom burden was highest at mid-season, decreasing by post-season, although the change was not significant across pairwise time points.  Although correlations did not survive correction, changes in symptom count were negatively correlated with change in structural characteristic path length (r = -0.57, p = 0.001, before correction) and positively correlated with structural global efficiency (r = 0.48, p = 0.007, before correction).

Global and local metrics

No global graph theory metrics revealed significant differences after FDR correction in either cross-sectional (control vs. collision timepoints) or longitudinal comparisons for either SC or FC. However, longitudinal mapping showed notable trends of change within the collision group, characterized by a convergence from early- to mid-season, followed by divergence from mid- to post-season between SC and FC global metrics. Structurally, characteristic path length showed a consistent increase across the season, while global efficiency and clustering coefficient decreased. Functionally, metrics like global efficiency and clustering coefficient decreased early-to-mid, but rebounded mid-to-post, contrasting with SC, which continued to decline.

Local metric analysis revealed chronic differences, with the collision group demonstrating higher structural strength centrality and local efficiency in the bilateral thalamus compared to controls across all timepoints, despite lacking corresponding functional differences. Longitudinally, the collision group exhibited widespread structural and functional reorganization, including increased SC in the left hippocampus and anterior cingulate gyrus. Functionally, lower functional strength centrality was seen in auditory (Heschl’s gyrus: d = 0.958 (early), 0.723 (mid)) and visual (middle occipital gyrus: d = 0.98 (early), 1.145 (mid)) regions, and lower clustering coefficient and efficiency in the cerebellum than controls.
 

Network coupling

Compared to controls, collision sport athletes at both early-season (d = 0.43, p = 0.047) and mid-season (d = 0.49, p = 0.024) revealed lower coupling in the default mode network. This difference did not persist when comparing controls and post-season (d = 0.28, p = 0.19). Conversely, a significant decrease was observed from early to post-season (d = 0.55, p = 0.028) and from mid-season to post-season (d=0.58, p = 0.024) for the coupling of ventral attention network. However, differences in network organization did not survive FDR correction.  

Kinematic clustering

k-means clustering analysis identified two distinct HAE exposure profiles: "high cumulative, low intensity" (Cluster 1) and "low cumulative, high intensity" (Cluster 2). Comparing clusters revealed distinct longitudinal trajectories in network topology (figure 2). Cluster 2 exhibited greater changes, including widespread functional topological disruption early-to-mid season (functional global efficiency d=  -0.57), with a corresponding increase in segregation (functional modularity d=0.37), which was accompanied by structural change (characteristic path length d=0.36).  This was followed by a substantial functional "rebound" mid-to-post season (global efficiency d=0.42). 

Conversely, Cluster 1 ("high cumulative, low intensity") showed early declines in structural modularity but lesser functional change until late season, indicating a different mechanism of uncoupling between clusters. Structural metrics showed decline in early-mid-season ( modularity d=-0.34, small-worldness=-0.29), while functional networks remained stable. However, at mid-post-season, a functional difference was observed (modularity d=0.38). In general, Cluster 1 showed alteration of structural connectivity at early-to-mid only, followed by a functional network alteration only in mid-post. 

Conclusion

This study aimed to characterize the longitudinal effects of repetitive HAE on the structural and functional brain network architecture of adolescent athletes after a season of rugby, a full-collision sport. We had two hypotheses: that an alteration in SC leads to an alteration in FC; and that longitudinal alterations within collision sport athletes are correlated with mTBI-related symptoms and cumulative HAE exposure. 

Repetitive HAE exposure in adolescent collision sport athletes induces subtle, subclinical alterations in large-scale network architecture, characterized by distinct dynamic responses between structural (SC) and functional (FC) networks. SC metrics showed consistent alterations (e.g., increased characteristic path length), reflecting enduring microstructural vulnerability and decreased efficiency in global integration. Conversely, the late-season recovery or "rebound" observed in FC metrics suggests a functional compensation mechanism or neural plasticity, enabling the brain to sustain efficient functioning despite persistent structural changes.4

Local differences, such as elevation of structural strength centrality in the bilateral thalamus, suggest enduring microstructural adaptations in deep cortical regions.  Correlation trends suggest that alterations in structural topology, seen via increases in global efficiency and decreases in characteristic path length, are systematically coupled with greater symptom burden, suggesting clinical relevance for structural alterations. Longer average paths may be a result of impairment in short-range white matter connections within brain regions, which can lead to cognitive deficits. Trauma-induced increases in efficiency can arise from the maladaptive development or strengthening of long-range connections, possibly reflecting loss of network specialization.5

k-means clustering revealed that impact profile is important to characterizing the neural response to HAEs. Clustering produced two groups, defined as “high cumulative, low intensity” and “low cumulative, high intensity”, who showed distinct trajectories when comparing SC-FC coupling. Our analysis suggests that low cumulative, high intensity impacts lead to early structural and functional disruption followed by robust neuroplastic recovery, while high cumulative, low intensity impacts lead to a gradual structural decline and delayed functional adaptation. These results suggest that both impact intensity and frequency are key determinants of neural response, and that athletes exposed to higher magnitude HAE are at greater risk for neurological sequelae than those exposed to repetitive, low magnitude HAE.

Our study comes with limitations. First, our cohort only included male athletes, limiting its generalizability to female populations. Secondly, the control group were assessed only at a single time point, with the assumption that they would have limited brain changes over the study duration. The small sample size weakened the statistical power, resulting in few significant effects after correction, despite suggestions that effects would be significant in larger cohorts. However, strong effects should not be expected in studies of subclinical cohorts. Future studies will address these limitations by incorporating prospective data collection, including female subjects, and multiple timepoint data collection of control subjects.

These findings highlight the brain’s capacity for adaptive reconfiguration of both SC and FC networks in response to repeated impacts and show that changes in connectivity are dependent on impact profile, not solely on magnitude. Overall, the observed network alterations induced by repetitive HAE exposure mimic those seen in mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), especially in subjects with high symptom burden. These observations align with our hypotheses and underscore the need to monitor brain health in collision sport athletes, even for athletes who are undiagnosed with mTBI.
