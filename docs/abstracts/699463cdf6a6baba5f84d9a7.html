Introduction

Measuring the brain cellular makeup and state is key to understanding healthy and pathological brain functioning. However, accessing this information requires invasive methods that are not worth the risk, except in very severe diseases or in animal research. The MRI community currently develops various approaches to make brain MRI cell-specific and obtain “virtual biopsies”. Diffusion-weighted MR spectroscopy (dMRS) combines two MR techniques for analysing cellular microstructure and metabolism: diffusion-weighted MR and MR spectroscopy. Diffusion-weighted MRI (dMRI) is a powerful tool to non-invasively explore the brain microstructure. The ubiquitous nature of water makes dMRI a sensitive technique, but inherently lacking specificity.  dMRS measures the diffusion properties of intracellular and partly cell-specific metabolites in a chosen region of interest, providing direct access to the intracellular neuronal (via N-acetylaspartate (NAA), and Glutamate (Glu)) and glial (via myo-Inositol (Ins) and Cholinergic compounds (tCho)) spaces. dMRS data can be interpreted with biophysical models to extract mean neuronal or glial morphological features such as the fiber radius (Ra) (and the intracellular diffusivity, Dintra) in the ROI. 

The community debates the further metabolites cell specificity, with hope to disentangle glial subtypes (e.g., astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes). For instance, a few in vivo studies indicate that ADC(tCho) could be a microglial marker, but the lack of validation prevents further interpretation. 

Animal imaging enables the combination of MR methods with invasive approaches (microscopy, transcriptomics, ex vivo MRI etc), forming an avenue to better understand the MR signal and infer more from it. In this work, we inform further the nature of the dMRS signal by comparing it with spatial transcriptomics acquired in the same animals. As dMRS is readily available on human MRI scanners, it constitutes a strategic approach for translation.

Methods

Experimental design: Animals were first scanned in vivo on a 7T Bruker Biospec MRI (Paravision 360.3.5, equipped with a 4 channels receive cryocoil and gradients with Gmax = 660 mT/m). After perfusion fixation, the brains in their skull were scanned ex vivo with advanced MRI methods (including a high resolution T2-weighted structural image). Finally, one single-hemisphere hippocampal slice of each brain was sent for a spatial transcriptomics analysis. 

Animals: We selected eight mouse strains from the BXD x Sm22a-Cre recombinant panel, from which two mice per strain were scanned (16 in total). Mice were anesthetised with 1.5% isoflurane in a melange of air and oxygen and were maintained on a stereotaxic bed with a bite and two ear bars. Breathing was kept at 90-110 rpm and  rectal temperature at 36-37˚C during scanning. Mice were perfused with 4% PFA and 2mM Gadolinium. Mouse brains were kept in their skull (cleaned from any residual tissue), and placed on a holder in a falcon tube filled with Fluorinert.

Acquisition & processing (in vivo): 

(acquisition) Data was acquired with a STELASER sequence (TE=34ms). The voxel (2x6x2 mm3) was placed around the hippocampus and dorsal thalamus (Figure A, blue voxel). Diffusion-weighted spectra were acquired at high b-values time at td=52.3 ms, b=[0.035 3.035 6 10 20 30]ms/µm2, 16 directions). A T2-weighted image (T2-TurboRARE, TE/TR=36/2200 ms, RARE factor=8, spatial resolution = 150x150x400 µm3) was acquired in the beginning of the session to position the voxel and obtain an in vivo anatomical reference image. 

(dMRS processing) After coil combination (phased from the water signal), individual transients were corrected for phase and frequency drifts and outliers (abnormally low signal transients) were discarded prior to averaging as in1. Water residual was removed. Spectra were fitted with LCModel (simulated basis set, including a macromolecules (MM) baseline acquired experimentally). Absolute concentrations were extracted at each b-value for NAA+NAAG (tNAA), Glu, Creatine+Phosphocreatine (tCr), Taurine (Tau), tCho, and Ins, and normalised by the signal at b=0.035 ms/µm2 to generate signal attenuations. The latter were then fitted with a randomly oriented infinite cylinders model, giving the intracellular diffusivity (Dintra) and the cylinder radius (Ra) as output parameters.

Acquisition (ex vivo, anatomical): A high resolution T2-weighted structural image (3D TurboRARE, 60µm isotropic, TE/TR=36/350ms, RARE factor=6, ESP=12 ms, BW=60kHz) was acquired. 

Acquisition & processing (transcriptomics): Following in vivo and ex vivo MRI, 2 brains from each of the 8 strains were used for spatial transcriptomics. The brains were removed from the skull, embedded in paraffin, and a single right hemisphere slice through the anterior hippocampus (-2mm AP, 5um) was assayed using the 10X Xenium spatial transcriptomics platform2.  Spatial transcriptomics slices were cell-segmented with cellpose3 using DAPI and 18S rRNA tissue staining. Per-cell transcripts were integrated and cell types assigned via the Allen Institute’s “mapmycells” automatic cell type mapping tool. 

Co-registration: A synthetic MR image was created using a weighted neuron density map obtained from spatial transcriptomics, and then aligned via TIRL4 to each mouse’s ex vivo T2-weighted MRI. The in vivo T2-weighted image was aligned to the ex vivo high resolution T2-weighted structural image with an affine registration, using the FSL tool FLIRT (12 dof, cost function = mutual info). The dMRS voxel was defined in the coordinates of the in vivo T2-weighted image, and could therefore be projected onto the ex vivo high resolution T2-weighted structural image. 

Results

Figure A shows an example of overlap between the dMRS voxel, the transcriptomics data and the high resolution T2-weighted structural image. 

Signal attenuations as a function of b-value for tCho are shown in Figure B and the corresponding Dintra and Ra in Figure C. 

The mean Dintra and R for the other metabolites are as follow: tNAA (Dintra = 0.35 +/- 0.02 µm2/ms, Ra =  0.08 +/- 0.22 µm); Glu (Dintra = 0.40 +/- 0.03 µm2/ms, Ra =  0.96 +/- 0.45 µm), tCr (Dintra = 0.38 +/- 0.03 µm2/ms, Ra = 1.57 +/- 0.17 µm), Tau (Dintra = 0.45 +/- 0.03 µm2/ms, Ra =  1.32 +/- 0.23 µm), tCho (Dintra = 0.29 +/- 0.03 µm2/ms, Ra =  1.69 +/- 0.21 µm), Ins (Dintra = 0.24 +/- 0.03 µm2/ms, Ra =  1.95 +/- 0.15 µm). 

Figure D shows an example of per-cell transcripts for astrocytes (Astro-Epen), microglia, glutamatergic neurons and gabaergic neurons in a half-hemisphere.  

Figure E shows the Pearson’s correlation coefficient of the linear regression between cell densities of astrocytes (Astro-Epen), microglia, glutamatergic neurons and gabaergic neurons, and the Ra estimate out of the metabolites signal attenuation. Ra(tCho) exhibits a strong negative correlation with the microglial density coming from per-cell transcripts (R=-0.86, Figure F).

Conclusion

This work compares highly specific information coming from spatial transcriptomics and partly cell specific, but in vivo, dMRS data. The correlation between the fiber radius extracted from the diffusion properties of tCho and the microglial transcripts is particularly exciting, as multiple dMRS studies discuss the possible specificity of choline compounds to microglia (and less to other glial cells), without a validation dataset. 

Notably, in healthy subjects, the study of De Marco et al. (2023) challenges human microglia with a lipopolysaccharide (LPS) challenge, and reports an apparent diffusion coefficient change (ADC) for tCho. Another study from Ercan et al. (2016) on systemic lupus erythematous patients also reports similar changes. In a mouse model of demyelination, the study from Genovese et al. (2021) also associates changes in tCho diffusion properties with activated microglia, relying on immunohistochemistry and microscopy to establish ground truth. However, the model displays multiple other glial changes (notably correlated astrocytic and microglial changes), complicating the interpretation. In our work, the cell-transcripts in the voxel for astrocytes and microglia densities are only weakly correlated (R=0.35), reinforcing the association between tCho and microglia. 

Note that on one hand, the lack of correlations between the other per-cell transcripts data and metabolites is not particularly surprising given that the transcriptomics slice represents a very small fraction of the structurally heterogeneous voxel. On the other hand, on the contrary to other cell-type markers, microglia distribution is sparse and homogenous between regions: despite the small transcriptomics volume contributing to the voxel, it is likely to be quite representative of the overall voxel volume. 

Finally, the output of the dMRS modelling with randomly oriented infinite cylinders (Dintra and Ra) falls within the literature range. As reported in previous studies, Ra(tNAA,Glu) < Ra(tCr,Tau) < Ra(tCho, Ins), following the expected metabolites compartmentation (thinner fibers for neuronal compartments, larger fibers for glial compartments).
