Introduction

Several magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have detected brain alterations in the structural similarity between cortical regions in individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD), including both increases and decreases in structural connectivity. These structural changes are based on the divergence between different MRI features, and have been observed during the early stages of the disease as evidenced by studies on individuals experiencing a first-episode of psychosis (FEP). However, the hierarchical, evolutionary, and temporal trajectories of these alterations across the psychosis spectrum remain inconclusive, likely influenced by the clinical heterogeneity of symptoms and the effects of antipsychotic drugs, among other factors.

Methods

MRI data from individuals who had experienced a FEP, confirming an SSD (nSSD = 333, age = 29.81 ± 8.79), along with their healthy controls (nHC = 193, age = 29.04 ± 7.66), were obtained from Programa de Atención a las Fases Iniciales de Psicosis (PAFIP). Individuals with SSD were antipsychotic-naïve or minimally medicated (< 6 weeks of antipsychotics). Participants underwent follow-ups at 1-year (nSSD = 96; nHC = 62), 3-years (nSSD = 136; nHC = 51), 5-years (nSSD = 70; nHC = 76), 10-years (SSD, nSSD = 101; nHC = 91), 15-years (nSSD = 33; nHC = 35), and 20-years (nSSD = 8; nHC = 8). 

For each subject, cortical and subcortical MIND networks were constructed by computing, between pairs of regions, the Kullback-Leibler divergence of multiple MRI features (cortex: thickness, curvature, sulcal depth, area, and volume; subcortex: area and radial distance)1,2. This resulted in two edge-level matrices of size 318x318 and 14x14, bounded between 0 and 1, representing the structural similarity between each pair of cortical and subcortical regions, respectively (Fig. 1). Regional degree was computed as the average MIND similarity of each region. The first two structural connectivity gradients G1 and G2 were derived from cortical MIND using gradient decomposition3. To ensure comparability across individuals, gradient direction was standardized using Procrustes rotation to align each individual gradient with a group-level template derived from an average connectivity matrix encompassing both SSD and HC participants. 

To evaluate the long-term impact of diagnosis, time, and medication on brain similarity, a linear mixed model (LMM) was performed for each region as shown in Figure 2A, where ‘Regional {degree, G2}’ is the regional MIND degree or gradient; ‘diagnosis’ is HC or SSD; ‘sex’ is male or female; ‘age at inclusion’ is the age at study enrolment; ‘eTIV’ is the estimated total intracranial volume; ‘Euler number’ is the FreeSurfer’s Euler index representing motion-induced image artefacts; ‘time’ is the period since the first scan (HC) or treatment initiation (SSD); ‘time:diagnosis’ is the effect of treatment time for SSD individuals; ‘medication’ is the antipsychotic dose expressed in chlorpromazine equivalents; ‘protocol’ represents a change in the protocol during the MRI acquisition; and “(1|subject)” is a random intercept to account for subject-specific variability in overall offsets. For HC, ‘medication’ and ‘medication:time’ were set to zero.

Cortical brain maps of evolutionary expansion, functional gradient, and sensorimotor-to-association ranking were derived from neuromaps4. Brain maps of functional and structural epicenters in schizophrenia—the regions whose connectivity most closely resemble disease-related morphological alteration patterns—were obtained from an ENIGMA study5. Regional correlations were computed between these brain maps and the longitudinal effects of brain similarity—after accounting for their respective regional degree or gradient—by applying a spin test (n = 10,000) where spatial autocorrelation is preserved. Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across features to account for multiple comparisons.

Brain similarity was additionally associated with SSD symptomatology using the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS). Only SSD patients were analyzed due to the absence of symptom data in HC. The long-term effect of brain similarity, treatment time, and medication on BPRS scores was assessed through a generalized LMM (GLMM) with a Poisson distribution. In this model, shown in Figure 2B, and in addition to the previous variables, ‘BPRS’ represents each individual BPRS score, and ‘regional {degree, G2}:time’ captures the interaction between treatment time and brain similarity. ‘protocol’ was not included because all individuals with BPRS scores followed the same protocol. For both LMM and GLMM, all non-binary variables were z-scored, and FDR correction across brain regions was applied.

Results

Since the first gradient (G1) showed a heterogeneous pattern—anchored at one end by the cingulate and superior temporal cortices and at the other end by prefrontal, superior parietal, and occipital regions—we opted to represent the second gradient (G2), which notably captured the sensorimotor-to-association (S-A) transition (Fig. 1).

Although diagnosis did not exhibit any association with brain similarity, time was negatively associated with the degree in the frontal, parietal, and temporal regions, along with the nucleus accumbens and the pallidum, indicating lower connectivity over time in these regions (Fig. 2A left). Conversely, the occipital area, along with the thalamus and putamen exhibited a positive association, indicating an increase of degree over time. These differences between primary and associative areas were more pronounced in G2, where negative associations indicated lower G2 over time in these areas, i.e. a shift toward more basic primary functions, while positive associations indicated an increase of G2 over time, i.e. a shift toward more complex associative functions. Interestingly, this effect turned out to be largely the opposite for the degree in individuals with SSD (diagnosis:time), where the temporal, frontal, and cingulate cortices, as well as the subcortex, were positively associated with the degree, suggesting that treatment time was associated with an increase in connectivity. However, the time effect in G2 strengthened in individuals with SSD, where the frontal and parietal regions were negatively associated with G2, while the occipital and cingulate cortices were positively associated, suggesting that treatment time was associated with a shift toward more complex associative functions. Consistent with these findings, the effect of medication was positively associated with the degree but not with G2, indicating that the use of antipsychotics was linked to higher connectivity. 

Additionally, we found that evolutionary expansion, the functional gradient, and the S-A axis were negatively associated with the effect of time in MIND (degree: rpartial = -0.36; rpartial = -0.30; rpartial = -0.39; pspin < 10-4, respectively; G2: rpartial = -0.27, pspin = 2·10-4; rpartial = -0.52, pspin < 10-4; and rpartial = -0.56, pspin < 10-4, respectively), suggesting that the regions with the strongest degree and G2 reductions over time are high-order association transmodal areas with greater evolutionary expansion (Fig. 2A right). Conversely, this effect was partially reverted in G2 of SSD individuals for the functional gradient and the S-A axis (diagnosis:time; rpartial = 0.21, pspin = 0.019; and rpartial = 0.24, pspin = 0.01). Consistent with this finding, the effect of medication in G2 on evolutionary expansion was also positive (rpartial = 0.30, pspin = 0.002), suggesting that the use of antipsychotics was linked to greater evolutionary expansion. On the other hand, time was positively associated with the functional (degree: rpartial = 0.35, pspin < 10-4; G2: rpartial = 0.20, pspin = 0.005), and structural epicenters (degree: rpartial = 0.22, pspin = 0.001), indicating that regional epicenters exhibited strongest degree and a shift toward more complex associative functions over time.

Finally, cortical degree was widely negatively associated with BPRS (Fig. 2B), indicating that the stronger the connectivity in an individual with SSD, the milder the symptoms. G2, however, exhibited both positive and negative associations broadly distributed across the cortex. Although the degree:time interaction barely showed any significant association, the G2:time effect captured several positive and negative associations.

Conclusion

In summary, we derived structural degrees and gradients from similarity MIND networks revealing that the second structural gradient—G2—captured the sensorimotor-to-association transition. While both degree and G2 showed longitudinal associations with the main effect of time, this association was reversed in individuals with SSD. Accordingly, the medication effect was positively associated with the degree, indicating that the use of antipsychotics was linked to higher connectivity. These regional associations also significantly correlated with brain maps capturing hierarchical and evolutionary features, as well as functional and structural epicenters in schizophrenia, highlighting the regional vulnerability of SSD. Finally, we found that psychiatric symptoms were directly associated with the degree but were time-dependent in G2, indicating that lower SSD symptom severity was associated with higher connectivity and greater change in G2 over time. Together, these findings advance our understanding of the long-term effects of diagnosis, medication, and treatment time on brain structure and psychiatric symptoms.
