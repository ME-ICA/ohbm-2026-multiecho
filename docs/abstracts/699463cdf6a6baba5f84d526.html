Introduction

1. Introduction

1.1 The Parietal Numerical Network: From Functional Specialization to Network Integration

The parietal cortex is central to human numerical cognition. Neuroimaging and lesion studies show that its subregions—intraparietal sulcus (IPS), superior parietal lobule (SPL), and angular gyrus (AG)—support distinct yet complementary processes: IPS encodes numerical magnitude, SPL contributes to visuospatial transformation, and AG mediates arithmetic retrieval and semantic processing (Dehaene et al., 2003; Nieder & Dehaene, 2009; Menon, 2016; Amalric & Dehaene, 2019).

Although their functional roles are well established, it remains unclear how these parietal hubs integrate within the broader connectome, whether their connectivity is lateralized, and how subregions cooperate across the whole-brain gray matter. Understanding these patterns is essential for explaining how numerical cognition arises from distributed rather than localized neural systems.

1.2 Surfing the Parietal Connectome

We “surfed” the human connectome using HCP resting-state fMRI and the Glasser et al. (2016) parcellation to map whole-brain gray matter functional connectivity of the parietal numerical network. Regions of interest included bilateral SPL, IPS, and AG—core “math-related” hubs.

Analyses proceeded in three stages:

(1) Whole-parietal mapping of combined bilateral ROIs;

(2) Hemispheric comparison to identify lateralization;

(3) Subregional analysis to determine SPL-, IPS-, and AG-specific patterns and overlaps.

1.3 Hypotheses

Network integration: The parietal numerical network connects with bilateral visual, sensorimotor, and frontal cortices.

Lateralization: Left parietal regions (IPS, AG) show stronger links to language and semantic systems.

Subregional specialization: SPL aligns with visuomotor circuits, IPS with control hubs, and AG with associative–semantic networks.

Methods

taset

This dataset we used includes group-average structural and functional MRI data for the HCP S1200 data release (March 2017) from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) (Van Essen et al., 2013). The cortical parcellations included are the total 393-area multimodal parcellation HCP_MMP1.0(Q1-Q6_RelatedValidation210.CorticalAreasAndSubAreas_dil_Colors_210P_Orig.32k_fs_LR.dlabel.nii) from Glasser et al. (2016). 

2.2 Definition of parietal numerical regions

Regions of interest were defined according to the HCP MMP1.0 atlas (Glasser et al., 2016), selecting areas implicated in number processing based on prior functional neuroimaging meta-analyses (e.g., Arsalidou & Taylor, 2011; Sokolowski et al., 2017). The selected ROIs included (IPS core regions: L/R_7Pl_ROI, L/R_7IP_ROI, L/R_7PC_ROI, L/R_7AL_ROI, L/R_7Am_ROI, L/R_7Pm_ROI), AG regions (L/R_PGi_ROI, L/R_PGp_ROI, L/R_PGs_ROI), SPL regions (L/R_5m_ROI, L/R_5L_ROI, L/R_5MV_ROI). Collectively, these nodes comprise the parietal numerical network (PNN).

2.3 Functional connectivity computation

For each ROI, we computed voxel-wise (gray matter) functional connectivity maps using Pearson correlation between the mean BOLD time series of each seed and all cortical voxels. To ensure interpretability, correlation values were Fisher z-transformed. Positive and negative connections were ranked, and the top 20% strongest positive and negative values were thresholded to visualize distinct connectivity patterns.

Analyses proceeded in three steps. First, whole-parietal analysis, combined bilateral parietal regions as seeds to produce a global connectivity map. Second, hemispheric analysis, separate connectivity maps for left and right parietal ROIs, with overlap shown as gray zones. Third, subregional analysis, connectivity maps for SPL, IPS, and AG were generated independently, followed by overlap and unique connectivity maps. All visualizations were rendered using the Connectome Workbench and displayed in flat and inflated cortical surfaces.

Results

3. Results

3.1 Findings Consistent with Classical Models

Whole-parietal connectivity

The bilateral parietal numerical network showed strong positive connectivity with multiple association cortices, primarily including bilateral intraparietal and supramarginal regions (e.g., L/R_7AL_ROI, L/R_7Pm_ROI, L/R_PGp_ROI), primary visual cortex (L/R_V1_ROI), postcentral gyrus (L/R_1_UpperExtremity_ROI), temporal cortex (bilateral PGs, L/R_STSva_ROI), and frontal cortex (L/R_8Ad_ROI) (Figure 1). These widespread connections highlight the parietal lobe’s integrative role across sensorimotor, visual, temporal, and frontal systems, consistent with models proposing that quantity processing recruits distributed cortical streams beyond the parietal cortex (Dehaene et al., 2003; Menon, 2016).

Hemispheric lateralization

Comparing left and right parietal connectivity revealed extensive bilateral symmetry, with overlapping regions in intraparietal, occipital, and superior parietal cortices (Figure 2). Subtle lateralized biases emerged: the left parietal cortex exhibited broader coupling with temporal and inferior frontal regions (e.g., MTG, IFG), while the right parietal cortex showed stronger links within occipitoparietal and sensorimotor networks. This suggests bilateral cooperation at rest, yet hemispheric specialization for distinct functions—symbolic and linguistic processing (left) versus visuospatial and magnitude representation (right).

Subregional specialization

Distinct connectivity fingerprints characterized the three parietal subregions (Figure 3). The SPL connected extensively with sensorimotor cortices (pre/postcentral gyri) and dorsal visual areas (V2, MST). The IPS exhibited dense intra-parietal coupling and connections with fronto-parietal control regions (L/R_6a_ROI, L/R_PF_ROI, L/R_PFop_ROI), confirming its role as an integrative hub. The AG showed strong links with language and default-mode network areas (L/R_TE1m_ROI, L/R_31pd_ROI, L/R_10d_ROI), implicating it in semantic and retrieval-based number cognition. Overlaps among SPL, IPS, and AG were mainly localized in the visual cortex (L_FFC_ROI, L_V1_ROI, L_V2_ROI) and adjacent parietal zones (Figure 4), reflecting shared sensory-integration mechanisms supporting quantity processing.

3.2 Findings Beyond the Classical View

Lateralization and bilateral coordination

Although earlier work emphasized left-hemispheric dominance for symbolic number processing (Prado et al., 2011; Arsalidou & Taylor, 2011), our data revealed robust bilateral connectivity across parietal and occipital cortices, suggesting a cooperative, dual-hemisphere architecture rather than strict lateralization. Bilateral parietal regions may jointly underpin complementary facets of numerical cognition—right parietal involvement in spatial and visuomotor mapping and left parietal engagement in symbolic and semantic computation.

Moreover, stronger intra-hemispheric connectivity supports a proximity-based efficiency principle (Vigneau et al., 2011), promoting rapid same-side information transfer and facilitating domain-specific integration during quantity computation.

Functional differentiation within the parietal numerical network

Distinct yet overlapping connectivity among SPL, IPS, and AG indicates hierarchical specialization in numerical cognition. The SPL functions as a visuomotor coordinator, integrating perceptual and action-based representations. The IPS serves as a central computational hub, linking magnitude representation with executive control circuits. The AG operates as a semantic interface, connecting numerical operations to conceptual and linguistic systems.

This fine-grained organization underscores a distributed parietal framework, wherein specialized subregions collaborate dynamically across large-scale networks to support the multidimensional nature of number cognition.

Conclusion

This study based on gray matter functional connectivity mapped the whole-brain connectomic architecture of the parietal numerical network in the resting state. Results reveal that: 

  • 1. Parietal numerical regions exhibit widespread bilateral connectivity with sensorimotor, visual, temporal, and frontal cortices.
  • 2. Both hemispheres contribute to numerical processing, suggesting bilateral cooperation with hemispheric biases rather than strict lateralization.
  • 3. SPL, IPS, and AG display distinct yet overlapping connectivity profiles, reflecting functional specialization and hierarchical coordination within the parietal network.

These findings support a distributed and dynamic connectomic model of numerical cognition, emphasizing that the parietal cortex functions not as an isolated computation center but as a coordinating hub embedded in the broader cortical network.
