Introduction

Hyperoxia is a commonly used gas-challenge method in non-contrast-enhanced MRI studies. By increasing the concentration of dissolved molecular oxygen, hyperoxia alters the longitudinal relaxation time constant (T1).^1 However, hyperoxia also induces physiological responses (e.g. cerebral vasoconstriction), leading to T1 change over time. In ventricular system, relaxation properties of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) are spatially inhomogeneous and vary with depth due to partial volume effects and differing proximity to parenchyma and blood vessels.^4 Therefore, measuring CSF T1 changes during long-term hyperoxia using spatiotemporally resolved imaging method may reveal depth-dependent patterns of T1 evolution.^5

Zero echo time (ZTE) MRI enables three-dimensional acquisition with minimal image distortion and reduced sensitivity to susceptibility variations.^2 Due to its intrinsically rapid acquisition, ZTE enables high-resolution imaging with broad spatial coverage. When combined with a variable flip-angle approach, whole-brain T1 mapping can be done with a sufficient spatial resolution to resolve ventricular CSF across depth.

However, ZTE-based T1 mapping typically uses excitation RF pulses with small flip-angles, making it sensitive to transmit RF field (B1+) inhomogeneity. Spatial variation in the effective flip angle can introduce systematic bias in the estimated T1. To address this, we applied B1+ map–based correction. Finally, we performed in vivo experiments in mice during long-term hyperoxia to evaluate CSF T1 using the proposed ZTE-based T1 mapping.

Methods

In vivo experiments: Four male C57BL/6 (8w) mice were scanned on a Bruker 9.4T MRI scanner under isoflurane anesthesia (1.4–1.6%). A 3D variable-flip-angle ZTE protocol was repeated across 14 time points (5 baseline; 9 during hyperoxia). Flip angles were varied from 0.9 to 3.3° in 0.6° increments. Each 3D T1 map required 8 min 20 s, with 0.15 mm isotropic resolution and 19.5 mm isotropic FOV. B1+ inhomogeneity was estimated using an Bruker B1Map protocol and corrected using a ZTE signal model that explicitly accounts for B1+ inhomogeneity.^3 To validate ZTE-based T1 estimation, reference T1 maps were acquired using a variable–TR RARE sequence (in-plane resolution = 0.15 mm2, slice thickness = 0.3 mm, scan time = 8 min 30 s).

Ventricular CSF was identified from the average baseline T1 map based on its long T1 (> 2500ms) relative to parenchyma. The CSF mask was then divided into shallow and deep compartments using an additional T1 threshold (> 3000 ms), producing separated ROIs. Temporal evolution of T1 changes was calculated as percent change relative to baseline, defined as the mean T1 across the initial five baseline measurements.

Results

Uncorrected T1 maps showed severe spatial non-uniformity consistent with flip-angle variation, whereas B1+ correction improved map uniformity (Figure 1). ZTE-based T1 estimates showed overall good agreement with reference variable-TR RARE T1 maps, with slight underestimation of gray matter and white matter T1 values. Parenchymal T1 values are in physiologically plausible ranges (1500–1800 ms), and CSF exhibited extremely long T1.

Following the onset of hyperoxia, both parenchymal tissue and CSF showed a rapid decrease in T1. The initial decrease was more pronounced in CSF than in parenchymal tissue, with both shallow and deep CSF compartments exhibiting steeper T1 reductions and reaching a similar lower boundary (Figure 2). While the mean temporal evolutions of shallow and deep CSF T1 were comparable, the deep CSF compartment showed larger variability. Over time, T1 values in both tissue and CSF recovered even in sustained hyperoxia.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that temporally resolved, whole-brain ZTE-based T1 mapping can capture depth-dependent CSF relaxation properties during long-term hyperoxia. After B1⁺ correction, ZTE-derived T1 estimates agreed with variable–TR RARE measurements, supporting the validity of T1 mapping method despite slight parenchymal T1 underestimation. CSF exhibited a steeper initial T1 decrease than parenchyma following hyperoxia onset, while shallow and deep CSF showed similar mean temporal evolutions but greater variability in deep CSF. We concluded that the rapid initial T1 decrease reflects increased dissolved oxygen in CSF, whereas the subsequent recovery during sustained hyperoxia reflects delayed physiological responses, including cerebral vasoconstriction. This behavior underscores the need for improved temporal resolution, particularly immediately after oxygen saturation, to distinguish rapid CSF responses from those of parenchyma. Although technically challenging, depth-resolved CSF T1 measurements are feasible in humans, and the present findings may support the interpretation and optimization of human hyperoxia-based MRI studies.
