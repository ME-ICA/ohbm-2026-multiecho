Introduction

We frequently experience mind-wandering (MW), a state where thoughts drift away from the primary task. While initial studies focused on external environmental changes as MW triggers, approximately half of MW episodes cannot be explained by external causes, suggesting the involvement of unconscious processing of internal bodily signals in the spontaneous emergence of thought. A leading candidate for the substrate of interaction between visceral states and cognitive processes is interoception—the process of sensing, integrating, and interpreting signals originating from within the body.1 Our previous research has demonstrated that interoceptive accuracy (IA) modulates thought transition patterns depending on the balance of autonomic nervous activity2, and that experimentally amplifying heart rate variability selectively increases the frequency of self-related thoughts (SRT) in individuals with higher IA3. These findings indicate that interoception serves as a physiological driver that actively shapes the dynamics of spontaneous thought. However, it remains unclear whether these body-driven shifts in thought are accompanied by concurrent changes in the actual neural processing of bodily signals. Therefore, we hypothesized that the experimental manipulation of interoceptive processing would specifically induce SRT, accompanied by an amplification of bodily signal processing. Furthermore, we exploratorily examined how individual differences in IA and trait rumination —the tendency to repetitively dwell on negative feelings and their causes— influence the neural correlates of these thoughts.

Methods

Forty-two healthy participants (19 males, 18–30 years old) were recruited. First, IA was measured using the heartbeat counting task, in which participants sat quietly and mentally tracked their heartbeats without manual pulse checking. Participants then performed a simple auditory attention task requiring responses to tones. The experiment consisted of two blocks: a heartbeat synch block and an asynch block. In both blocks, tones were randomly omitted. Crucially, only the omission of heartbeat-synchronous tones served as the interoceptive manipulation, a procedure known to induce an immediate decrease in heart rate and a concurrent amplification of the heartbeat-evoked potential (HEP), a measure of cortical processing of cardiac afferent signals. Thought probes presented at the end of each trial (approx. every minute) assessed thought categories and content dimensions (e.g., valence, temporality, arousal). Electroencephalography was recorded simultaneously to capture HEP amplitude. Trait rumination was assessed using the Japanese version of the Rumination-Reflection Questionnaire (RRQ)4.

Results

Results revealed a hierarchical influence of bodily signals on thought processes. First, regarding the generation of thought, participants with higher IA reported a greater frequency of SRT specifically during the cardiac-manipulation block. Moreover, higher IA was associated with larger HEP amplitudes during SRT, and this association was significantly enhanced under the cardiac-manipulation condition. These findings suggest that as individuals more precisely detect and neurally amplify cardiac signals, internally focused, self-referential thought is selectively facilitated. Second, regarding the emotional content under the cardiac manipulation, SRT segregated into two opposing clusters: 1. negative, past-oriented SRT (high arousal/high contemplation) and 2. positive, future-oriented SRT (low arousal/low contemplation). Regarding neural correlates, negative, past-oriented SRT was associated with significantly larger HEP amplitudes compared to positive, future-oriented SRT (β = 0.443[0.437; 0.450]). Notably, this effect was characterized by a significant interaction with individual trait rumination (β = 0.236[0.231; 0.241]). Specifically, the amplification of HEP amplitude during negative, past-oriented SRT was strongly driven by individuals with highly trait rumination. This excessive cortical processing of cardiac signals during negative SRT was not observed in low ruminators or during positive SRT.

Conclusion

Our overall findings provide a body-centric mechanism for the generation of MW content, indicating that the conscious experience of the self is fundamentally rooted in the processing of internal bodily states. Furthermore, we propose that in individuals with highly trait rumination, this amplified interoceptive processing functions as a feedback loop that validates and sustains negative affective states, thereby preventing disengagement. Consequently, our results suggest that rumination may be fundamentally understood as an embodied process in which the brain's over-reliance on internal signals—accompanied by a loss of flexible cognitive control in response to external stimuli—locks the individual into a cycle of negative, past-oriented self-focused states.
