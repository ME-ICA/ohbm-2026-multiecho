Introduction

Distinct emotions are difficult to identify reliably, both in neural activation patterns and in behavioral evaluations due to substantial interindividual variability in emotional experience, complicating the use of semantic emotion labels as stable categories.1 To address this, emotions are often described along the dimensions of valence and arousal.2 Valence ranges from negative to positive emotional content and arousal reflects the level of activation/alertness from low to high. The encoding of differences along the valence and arousal dimensions in neural activity is still a central topic of research as emotional processes are represented in widespread, distributed patterns across multiple areas with pronounced heterogeneity between individuals.1 Representational similarity analysis (RSA) offers a robust framework for addressing these challenges, as it enables the investigation of the similarity of brain activation patterns evoked by different emotions.3 This approach is used for semantic neurofeedback (sNF), wherein reliable, individual-level classification of emotional representations is needed.4 SNF is a novel emotion regulation training that uses RSA to compare real-time brain activation patterns with previously localized neural representations of different emotional states. Feedback is provided to participants in the form of a semantic map that visualizes the similarity structure between current and target emotional states, enabling participants to navigate between emotions while in the scanner.4 To investigate whether the arousal and valence dimensions can be used for this application and identify brain regions that preferentially encode valence, arousal or a combination of both, different model similarity structures are compared and correlated with empirically observed representational geometries across multiple brain regions and subjects.

Methods

Five participants were scanned at a 7 Tesla Siemens Magnetom Scanner at Scannexus Maastricht. Anatomical images were acquired with an MP2RAGE sequence (voxel size=0.7 mm isotropic) and functional images for the task runs were acquired with a CMRR multiband 2D echo-panar imaging sequence (voxel size=1.8 mm isotropic,TR=1.5 s, TE= 22 ms). The sample was part of a pilot sNF study with two sessions: session one was performed to localize brain activation patterns of four different emotions, employed as anchor patterns in the real-time sNF runs of the second session. In this work we focused on the analysis of the localizer runs. Participants completed three functional localizers of 490 volumes each. They were instructed to engage in autobiographical memory recall of different  emotions for 15 seconds followed by an equally long baseline. Emotions were selected individually based on ratings of engagement scores and between-emotions distance on a valence/arousal grid. Emotions that lead to high engagement scores and bigger distances on the grid were selected, with one emotion selected for each quadrant of the grid (see figure 1). Emoticons representing these emotions were then presented to the participants as cues. Each emotion was repeated six times per run leading to 72 trials in total. 

Functional data underwent standard preprocessing steps including slice time correction, motion correction, and distortion correction. Single trial beta maps were extracted using the GLMsingle pipeline.5 Cortical brain regions were taken from the Glasser brain atlas and subcortical regions were added from the Brainnetome atlas. Those regions were aligned from the MNI space to the participants anatomy and then projected into the functional space. RSA was performed at region level within BrainVoyager, using correlation distance as dissimilarity metric, resulting in a representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM) for each region and subject. These matrices were then compared to three different model RDMs. Valence-based models included positive correlation between trial patterns of the same valence category but zero or negative correlation between different categories. The same model definition was applied to the arousal-based models. Additionally, a model was constructed to include valence and arousal with equal contribution to the representational structure (see figure 1, B to D). 

These model RDMs were then compared to the empirically measured RDM of each subject and region using Pearson's correlations.T-tests were used to investigate whether the (z-Fisher transformed) correlations between the model RDM and the empirically measured RDMs were significantly different from zero at group level. Results were corrected using false discovery rate (FDR) correction. 

Results

Analysis of the behavioral ratings revealed high emotional engagement for all emotions irrespective of their valence and arousal ratings. The only emotion that was rated as significantly lower was surprise. As demonstrated in figure 1, the valence ratings exhibited smaller standard errors in comparison to the arousal ratings. The emotions were clearly sorted in positive or negative valence ratings, with surprise being the sole emotion placed in the middle of the grid. 

The analysis of correlation between the model and the empirically observed RDMs lead to multiple regions revealing similarity between the data and the various models at the group level. The models encoding valence (MV) showed the highest mean correlation in the right dorsomedial frontal pole (Brodmann area 10, r=0.12, p(uncorrected)=0.172), the ventral bank of the left anterior superior temporal sulcus (r=0.11, p(uncorrected)=0.017) and the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (Brodmann area 9, r=0.11, p(uncorrected)=0.044). The highest individual correlation was observed at a correlation of 0.32 (p(FDR)<0.001) in the dorsomedial frontal pole. At the group level, the t-test did not survive FDR correction. However, at the individual level, multiple regions could be identified.

The models reflecting arousal (MA) demonstrated no mean correlations exceeding 0.1. At the individual level, several significant correlations were observed, with the highest ranging to a correlation of 0.35 (p(FDR)<0.001) in the dorsal subdivision of the lateral intraparietal area

The combined model (MVA) showed the highest mean correlation in the the ventral bank of left anterior superior temporal sulcus (r=0.12, p(uncorrected)=0.015), the posterior-dorsal subdivision of the cingulate cortex (r=0.10, p(uncorrected)=0.002), the lateral occipital area 1 (r=0.10, p(uncorrected)=0.008) The highest individual correlation was observed at an r of 0.28 (p(FDR)<0.001) in the left superior temporal visual area. Once more, no correlation survived the multiple testing corrections. However, the correlations between models and empirical data proved to be the most stable, with uncorrected p-values below 0.005 being attained in the following regions: right superior temporal visual area, left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (Brodmann area 9), left causal/dorsal area V3, the posterior-dorsal subdivision of the cingulate cortex (Brodmann area 31), left middle insular area, left ventral intra parietal complex and right area posterior (Brodmann area 24).

Conclusion

The behavioral results demonstrated that the participants were able to engage with the distinct emotion categories comfortably and effectively. However, the placement of emotions on the valence and arousal grid varied among participants. Emotion placement on the valence scale demonstrated relative stability between participants for all emotions except surprise. This resulted in the consistent classification of emotions as either positive or negative. However, the variation along the arousal axis was more pronounced. This led to emotions being allocated to different categories of arousal by different individuals. These findings align with prior research.1, 2

The RSA results indicated the presence of distinct regions throughout the brain that encode arousal and valence in varied combinations. The observed trends are all situated within the emotional networks of the brain, except for the two areas found in the occipital cortex, which  might be involved in processing of emotionally salient visual stimuli.2   

A fundamental constraint of the analysis employed in this abstract is the extensive number of investigated regions and their small size as well as the small sample size. A different parcellation could yield different results and would be interesting to investigate given the unknown extent of the representational information. The large inter-individual differences are an important consideration but are not crucial for a sNF application as the representational space is fitted for each person individually.4 The reliable detection of similarities between the anchor states and the real-time mental states is of more interest.4 As this dataset encompasses three runs and an additional run for most of the participants during a separate scanning session, the within-/across-run reliability of the RDMs could be further investigated.  

In conclusion, the results show local encoding of valence and arousal information in the brain. However,  the small sample size and the employed fine-grained parcellation might have limited our ability to identify robust representational geometries. Nonetheless, the observed trends, especially at the individual level, are promising and will be investigated further using different methods, such as the searchlight approach, and different similarity metrics, such as Mahalanobis distance.
