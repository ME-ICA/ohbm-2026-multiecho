Introduction

Dietary decision-making often involves weighing immediate palatability against long-term health consequences. Decision-making for foods is mediated through the brain valuation system including ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and ventral striatum, which encode experience and predicted values.^1 Prior research demonstrated that in dieters who successfully control food choices, vmPFC integrates both health and taste values, whereas in non-dieters, vmPFC encodes only taste. Concurrently, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) activity increases with self-control.^2

Building on this foundation, an influential fMRI study by Hare et al. showed that directing attention to health aspects of food improves dietary choice and alters prefrontal value signals in non-dieting participants.^3 These findings have shaped models of dietary self-regulation, yet fMRI constraints limit broader research applications. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) offers a portable method for measuring cortical hemodynamic responses in frontal regions and exhibits quantitative correspondence with fMRI across cognitive tasks.^4 The present study tested whether fNIRS can capture prefrontal neural effects of health- versus taste-focused attentional priming during willingness-to-eat decisions.

Methods

Forty right-handed adults (mean age 25.9 years, SD = 9.4) participated in the study. All participants had normal vision and no history of psychiatric, neurological, metabolic, or eating disorders; none were on a diet or had specialized food-related education. Participants fasted for 4 hours prior to the session and provided written informed consent.

Participants completed a willingness-to-eat task involving 60 trials with food items:^5 30 healthy foods (fruits/vegetables) and 30 non-healthy snacks (cookies, candies, chips, chocolate bars). Each trial presented a food item for 6 seconds, followed by a willingness-to-eat question with four response options ("surely no," "no," "yes," "surely yes"). Three attentional priming conditions were used: priming healthiness (focus on healthiness), priming taste (focus on tastiness), and priming neutral (no specific focus). At the end of the experiment, one trial was randomly selected and participants consumed the food if they had indicated willingness to eat it.

fNIRS data were acquired over the frontal cortex using a NIRScout system with a 16×12 montage (41 source–detector pairs; 30-mm distance) at wavelengths of 758 and 840 nm and a sampling rate of 3.9 Hz. Data were analyzed using SPM-fNIRS following the SPM12 framework. Preprocessing included motion artifact correction, removal of cardiac and respiratory noise, downsampling, detrending, and filtering. A general linear model included predictors for decision, subjective taste, subjective healthiness, healthy, unhealthy, priming healthiness, priming taste, and priming neutral. Planned contrasts tested decision (eat vs not eat), healthy–unhealthy, priming healthiness–priming neutral, priming taste–priming neutral, subjective taste, and subjective healthiness.

Results

General linear model analysis revealed significant prefrontal activation differences across key contrasts. Decisions to eat versus not eat produced significant activation (t = 2.62, df = 39, uncorrected p = .004), peaking in the left inferior frontal gyrus at MNI coordinates [-20, 84, -1]. Healthy versus non-healthy foods elicited significant activation (t = 2.93, df = 39, uncorrected p = .002), with a peak at [-69, 37, 11] localized to the left middle temporal gyrus and left inferior frontal gyrus.

Attentional priming modulated prefrontal activity relative to the neutral condition. The priming neutral–priming taste contrast showed significant activation in the left inferior frontal gyrus (t = 3.43, df = 39, uncorrected p = .001; peak [-8, 84, 22]). The priming neutral–priming healthiness contrast yielded a stronger effect (t = 4.34, df = 39, uncorrected p < .001; peak [29, 81, 15]) with bilateral inferior frontal gyrus activation. No significant effects were observed for subjective taste or subjective healthiness contrasts.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that fNIRS can detect prefrontal cortical differences associated with attentional priming during food-choice decisions. The robust effects in inferior frontal regions during health- and taste-focused conditions closely correspond to lateral prefrontal effects previously reported in fMRI studies of dietary self-regulation. By replicating these key neural distinctions using fNIRS, the present findings provide evidence that fNIRS is a viable complementary method for studying attention-modulated food valuation.

Although fNIRS is limited in its ability to measure activity in deeper brain structures, such as the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, it successfully captured lateral prefrontal activation patterns associated with attribute-focused decision-making. Given its lower cost, portability, and tolerance to movement, fNIRS offers substantial advantages for dietary decision-making research in contexts where fMRI is impractical.
