Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a cornerstone for characterizing brain structure and functional organization1, yet its utility is often constrained by heterogeneous acquisition protocols, missing imaging modalities, and limited sample sizes in disease-focused studies2. We propose a generative pre-training framework that learns a unified multimodal knowledge of brain MRI data and can be efficiently adapted to a wide range of downstream neuroimaging tasks. The framework is pretrained on approximately 1.2 million high-quality three-dimensional brain images from over 44,000 subjects spanning 34 structural and functional modalities, enabling it to capture shared anatomical and functional structure across imaging domains. When transferred to diverse external datasets with varying scanners, preprocessing pipelines, and populations, the framework consistently outperforms specialized approaches on key image-level tasks, including image enhancement (e.g., denoising and motion artifact removal) and cross-modality synthesis, while preserving subject-specific biological semantic information such as age, sex, and brain morphology. In low-data regimes, the generative framework enables synthetic data augmentation that improves model robustness across sites. Moreover, the framework can be adapted to previously unseen clinical MRI modalities, improving outcome prediction in stroke cohorts under extreme data scarcity. This work introduces a unified generative pre-training framework that enhances data quality, robustness, and scalability for multimodal brain MRI analysis.

Methods

We used a large population-scale multimodal brain MRI dataset derived from the UK Biobank3, comprising over 44,000 subjects and approximately 1.2 million three-dimensional images spanning 34 structural, diffusion, functional, and susceptibility-related modalities. Most of the data were used to pretrain the proposed generative framework, with an independent subset reserved for internal evaluation and downstream experiments. All images were processed using a standardized neuroimaging pipeline and spatially normalized to the MNI152 2 mm standard space. Voxel-wise 3D brain images were used as inputs, with non-brain regions excluded to ensure consistency across modalities and acquisition protocols.

The proposed framework adopts a denoising diffusion generative formulation and is pretrained jointly across multimodal MRI data to learn shared anatomical and functional representations. During pretraining, images are progressively corrupted with noise and the model is trained to recover the original signals. Conditioning information included modality index and basic demographic attributes (age and sex), enabling the framework to retain modality-specific characteristics while capturing population-level distributions.

To evaluate adaptability to image-level tasks, the pretrained framework was fine-tuned for image enhancement, including denoising and motion artifact removal. Image-conditioned generation was enabled by modifying the input embedding layer to accept multi-channel inputs formed by concatenating corrupted images with their original noised versions. Fine-tuning was performed using paired corrupted and reference images generated with realistic noise and motion simulations representative of clinical MRI artifacts. At inference, multiple synthetic samples conditioned on the same input were averaged to produce the final enhanced image. Performance was assessed using image-level metrics such as PSNR and 3D-SSIM, as well as semantic-level metrics reflecting preservation of age- and sex-related spatial patterns.

The generalization capability of the framework was further evaluated on cross-modality synthesis tasks spanning structural, functional, and structure–function translation. These included synthesis between different structural modalities, generation of task-related functional activation maps from resting-state functional connectivity, and synthesis of functional connectivity patterns from diffusion MRI-derived scalar maps. The framework was fine-tuned using paired source and target images, with conditioning restricted to the target modality index. Final synthesized outputs were obtained by averaging multiple synthetic samples. Comparisons were conducted against convolutional neural networks, adversarial synthesis models, task-specific MRI synthesis approaches, and an ablated variant trained without pretraining, under matched experimental settings.

Results

The proposed framework effectively enhances brain MRI images while preserving biologically meaningful information. In downstream image enhancement task (denoising and motion removal), the framework was fine-tuned on a limited set of corrupted T1w and FLAIR images paired with their high-quality counterparts. It substantially improved image quality metrics, increasing PSNR from 23.8 to 26.5 dB and 3D-SSIM from 0.92 to 0.96 for T1w, and PSNR from 20.5 to 22.2 dB and 3D-SSIM from 0.87 to 0.92 for FLAIR images, outperforming other methods4. We also found that the framework can capture age- and sex-related spatial patterns. Evaluation on an independent cohort confirmed the framework’s ability to generalize, maintaining superior image-level performance and semantic fidelity across diverse datasets. These results highlight the robustness and reliability of the framework in addressing realistic clinical imaging motion artifacts and noises.

The framework also enables high-fidelity cross-modality synthesis. For T1w-to-FLAIR synthesis, it generated structurally coherent images achieving a PSNR of 21.5 dB and 3D-SSIM of 0.94, closely matching ground-truth scans. FLAIR-to-T1w synthesis yielded PSNR of 23.6 dB and 3D-SSIM of 0.96, with high age- and sex- pattern preservation, outperforming other methods5. External evaluations also demonstrated robust synthesis quality and faithful preservation of subject-specific biological traits. These findings indicate that the framework can effectively impute missing imaging modalities while maintaining clinically relevant information, offering strong potential for large-scale neuroimaging studies.

For functional predictions, the framework accurately translated resting-state functional connectivity into task-based activation maps. The predicted activation maps showed strong agreement with ground-truth patterns, consistently outperforming baseline methods. The predicted activation patterns captured a substantial portion of age- and sex-related variance, indicating that the framework not only achieves spatial accuracy but also preserves biologically meaningful inter-subject variability. This capacity to model functional dynamics from structural connectivity enhances its utility for integrative neuroimaging analyses.

Finally, in structure-to-function mapping, diffusion MRI-derived metrics were translated into seed-based resting-state functional connectivity. The framework achieved the most biologically plausible predictions, with mean correlation scores of 0.24 and 0.29 for the Language and Visual Central Networks, respectively, and higher age-similarity scores than alternative models. This demonstrates its ability to capture individual differences in brain microstructure that are predictive of functional network organization. Collectively, these results illustrate the versatility of the framework across multiple imaging domains, underscoring its potential to support a wide range of neuroscience applications and clinical research.

Overall, the proposed framework provides a unified generative approach for multimodal brain MRI analysis, excelling in image enhancement, cross-modality synthesis. Its capacity to preserve critical biological information, generalize across datasets, and maintain both anatomical and functional fidelity establishes it as a robust and versatile tool for advancing population-scale neuroimaging studies.

Conclusion

The proposed framework provides a unified generative approach for multimodal brain MRI analysis, demonstrating robust performance across diverse imaging domains and downstream tasks. It effectively enhances image quality, including denoising and motion artifact removal, while preserving biologically meaningful information such as age-, sex-, and morphology-related spatial patterns. Fine-tuning on limited corrupted T1w and FLAIR images substantially improved image quality metrics and semantic fidelity, and evaluations on independent cohorts confirmed strong generalization across scanners, preprocessing pipelines, and populations.

Beyond image enhancement, the framework enables high-fidelity cross-modality synthesis, including T1w-to-FLAIR and FLAIR-to-T1w translations, producing structurally coherent images that closely match ground-truth scans and maintain subject-specific biological traits. Its generative capabilities also extend to functional predictions, accurately translating resting-state functional connectivity into task activation maps that capture inter-subject variability and age- and sex-related patterns. Furthermore, in structure-to-function mapping, diffusion MRI-derived microstructural metrics were successfully translated into seed-based resting-state functional connectivity, yielding biologically plausible predictions and capturing individual differences predictive of functional network organization.

These results collectively demonstrate the framework’s versatility, enabling reliable image enhancement, robust modality synthesis, and integrative functional predictions from structural and microstructural data. Its capacity to preserve critical biological information, adapt to low-data regimes, and generalize across diverse datasets highlights its potential to advance large-scale population neuroimaging studies. Overall, the framework represents a scalable and flexible tool for improving MRI data quality, supporting cross-modality analyses, and enhancing downstream clinical and neuroscience research applications.
