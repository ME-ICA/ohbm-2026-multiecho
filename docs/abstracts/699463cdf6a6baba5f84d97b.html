Introduction

Working memory and cognitive load engage coordinated cortical and autonomic processes, yet the precise interactions between event-related potentials (e.g., P300, N200), theta–alpha oscillations, and peripheral physiology (heart rate, photoplethysmography amplitude, and respiratory volume per time), and how these interactions are modulated by controlled respiration, remain incompletely understood (Polich, 2007; Berntson et al., 1997). The P300 and N200 components are established markers of attentional allocation and cognitive control, while frontal midline theta and posterior alpha rhythms index working-memory engagement and attentional gating, respectively (Polich, 2007; Cavanagh & Frank, 2014; Klimesch, 1999). Separately, slow and paced breathing has been shown to entrain neural oscillations and alter autonomic state, producing tonic shifts in alpha/theta power and indices of parasympathetic activity (Zelano et al., 2016; Zaccaro et al., 2018). Here, we investigate brain–body coupling during N-back working memory and assess how cognitive load and brief guided deep breathing reshape the relationships among ERPs, oscillatory dynamics, and autonomic measures.

Methods

Simultaneous EEG and physiological signals were acquired during rest and during working memory tasks with increasing cognitive load (0-back, 1-back, 2-back). Task blocks were repeated following a guided deep-breathing session to assess respiration-related modulation. Data from N = 4 healthy participants have been collected to date.

Physiological measures included respiratory volume per time (RVT) derived from a respiratory belt and heart rate (HR) derived from a fingertip pulse oximeter. Respiratory signals were band-pass filtered (0.08–0.5 Hz, zero-phase Butterworth), and respiratory cycles were identified using a trough-to-trough approach. RVT was computed for each cycle as tidal volume (peak-to-trough amplitude) divided by breath duration, with implausibly short or long cycles excluded. Raw PPG signals were band-pass filtered with a 3rd-order zero-phase Butterworth filter (0.5–3.5 Hz) to isolate the cardiac component. Slow baseline drift was reduced by subtracting a moving median (1 s window) from the filtered signal. Pulse peaks were detected with a minimum peak distance corresponding to a maximum heart rate of 180 bpm and a minimum peak prominence set to 30% of the signal standard deviation. Instantaneous heart rate (HR) was computed from interbeat intervals (IBI) as HR=60/IBI, with each value assigned to the midpoint between successive beats.

EEG was recorded at 500 Hz using a 32-channel EEG Brain Products system. Data were processed in MATLAB using EEGLAB. Auxiliary channels (physiological signals) were labeled as non-EEG and excluded from neural analyses. EEG data were re-referenced to the common average reference and band-pass filtered (0.5 - 30 Hz). Independent component analysis (extended infomax) was used to identify and remove artifact-related components in a conservative manner.

For time–frequency analysis, EEG data were epoched into 2-second segments time-locked to stimulus onset. Theta (4–8 Hz) and alpha (8–12 Hz) band power was computed using zero-phase band-pass filtering and squared amplitude estimates. Power was averaged within predefined frontal, parietal, and occipital regions of interest and compared across task load conditions. Topographic maps of theta, alpha, and beta (13–30 Hz) power were generated at the group level using standardized color scales.

Event-related potentials (ERPs) were computed from epochs spanning −200 to 800 ms relative to stimulus onset with baseline correction. Analyses focused on midline electrodes (Fz, FCz, Cz, CPz, Pz), and component amplitudes and latencies were extracted for N100, P200, N200, and P300 time windows.

Results

Due to the limited sample size (N = 4), analyses were restricted to descriptive comparisons, and results should be interpreted as illustrative summaries of observed response patterns across task loads and experimental conditions. Fig. 2A presents representative cardiac, respiratory, and EEG spectrogram data from one participant.

ERP results are shown in Fig. 2B. In the N-back condition, P3 amplitudes at Pz increased from 0-back to 1-back (4.72 ± 2.52 µV to 5.30 ± 2.28 µV) and decreased at 2-back (4.16 ± 1.37 µV). P3 latencies increased with task load (0-back: 366.5 ± 41.5 ms; 1-back: 393.0 ± 15.7 ms; 2-back: 409.0 ± 58.3 ms), with greater variability at the highest load. Following deep breathing, P3 amplitudes remained relatively stable across load levels, and the load-related amplitude reduction observed in the N-back condition was absent. Mean P3 latencies were comparable across loads after deep breathing, although latency variability was substantially higher than in the N-back condition alone. The load-related decrease in P3 amplitude observed in the n-back condition (Δ2–0 = −0.56 µV) was not present in n-back with deep breathing (Δ2–0 = +0.23 µV). Similarly, the increase in P3 latency from 0-back to 2-back was reduced after deep breathing (N-back: +42.5 ms; DB: +13.0 ms).

Working-memory engagement was associated with increased respiratory volume per time (RVT) relative to rest (Fig. 2C), reflecting enhanced respiratory drive during cognitive task performance. This increase emerged at the 1-back level and did not further differentiate between 1-back and 2-back. In contrast, paced deep breathing induced a global reduction in RVT that persisted into subsequent task blocks, resulting in RVT values below resting baseline across all post–deep breathing N-back conditions. Despite this overall attenuation, the relative pattern of load-dependent modulation was preserved.

Heart rate increased modestly during working-memory tasks compared to rest, with slightly higher values at higher loads. During paced deep breathing, heart rate decreased below both resting and task levels and remained lower during subsequent N-back blocks compared to the N-back condition without breathing.

Time–frequency analyses revealed a monotonic increase in frontal midline theta power (4–8 Hz) with working-memory load across both experimental contexts (Fig. 2D), indicating robust load sensitivity of frontal theta. This relationship was preserved after deep breathing, although inter-subject variability increased at higher loads. Parietal and occipital alpha power (8–12 Hz) showed canonical task-related suppression during N-back alone, whereas this suppression was attenuated when tasks followed deep breathing, suggesting an elevation of tonic alpha levels.

Topographic maps (Fig. 2E) reveal a consistent load-dependent increase of frontal midline theta (4–8 Hz) across both experimental contexts, with a focal maximum at Fz/FCz indicative of neural frontal midline theta rather than diffuse ocular contamination. Posterior alpha (8–12 Hz) shows canonical task-related desynchronization in the N-back only condition, while this suppression is attenuated following guided deep breathing—consistent with breathing-induced up-regulation of tonic alpha. Notably, the breathing condition exhibits a pronounced, spatially widespread increase in beta-band (13–30 Hz) power. This beta enhancement may reflect sustained cortical involvement in the motor control of breathing (e.g., continuous cortical drive to diaphragm and intercostal muscles) during paced deep respiration. Such a motor-origin hypothesis for beta is compatible with the established role of beta oscillations in sensorimotor control and maintenance of ongoing motor states, and with evidence that breathing manipulations can entrain broad neural and autonomic dynamics.

Conclusion

This multimodal investigation successfully demonstrated that voluntary Brainstem-Autonomic Coupling, mediated by a paced Deep Breathing (DB) intervention, modifies cortical resource allocation and arousal states during a cognitive challenge. The DB manipulation shifted the subjects into a lower, more regulated autonomic state, confirmed by a general drop in both Heart Rate (HR) and RVT across all task loads, which contrasts with the sympathetic drive (HR/RVT increase) seen with rising cognitive load alone. Cognitive Load Stability (Theta & ERP): The fundamental neural signature of cognitive load remained robust: Frontal Midline Theta (4–8 Hz) increased monotonically with load in both conditions. Furthermore, the P3 amplitude analysis showed that the typical load-related P3 amplitude reduction at the highest 2-back load was mitigated following deep breathing, suggesting that the regulated autonomic state helped sustain attentional resources under maximal cognitive stress. Moreover, the regulated state caused distinct changes in cortical arousal rhythms. Alpha suppression was attenuated when the task followed deep breathing, suggesting a competition between parasympathetic tone (elevated tonic alpha) and task-driven desynchronization. Furthermore, a pronounced, widespread increase in Beta (13–30 Hz) power was observed, likely reflecting sustained cortical involvement in the motor control of breathing.
