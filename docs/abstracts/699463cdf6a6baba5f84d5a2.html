Introduction

In songs composed in tonal languages like Cantonese, pitch serves a dual function: it is the structural unit of musical melody and the distinguishing feature of lexical meaning1. Uniquely, Cantonese songwriting follows relatively strict “tone-tune alignment” rules, creating a complex acoustic environment where lexical tones parallel with melodic pitch trends2. This integration between linguistic and musical pitch in Cantonese songs imposes a high cognitive demand on the brain to simultaneously decode musical structure and linguistic semantics. However, it remains unclear how the brain allocates neural resources to manage this dual processing load.

In the current study, we utilized functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) to test this dual-pathway hypothesis on Cantonese song processing among native speakers. We hypothesize a dissociation between structural decoding of melody and semantic integration of song processing: For pure melodies, the brain might rely on auditory-motor simulation to track pitch trajectories to process the musical structure3. For songs, in contrast, the presence of lyrics combined with melody would create a unified cognitive “schema”, triggering a semantic enhancement effect as the brain actively retrieves and integrates the associated content4. We also propose that prior knowledge (indicated by familiarity of stimuli in this study) acts as a critical modulator that shifts the brain’s processing strategy to optimize efficiency5. 

Methods

Thirty healthy, right-handed native Cantonese speakers (mean age = 24.5 yrs) with no professional musical training participated in the study. We employed a 3 (Stimulus Type: Song, Solfège, Lyrics, and Story) × 2 (Familiarity Level: Familiar vs. Unfamiliar) within-subject design. 

For Song stimuli, ten excerpts from contemporary Cantonese pop songs were selected. To investigate the role of familiarity, these were divided into two sets based on a priori popularity metrics. To create solfège stimuli, which isolate the melodic component while retaining the vocal source, the singer replaced the original Cantonese lyrics with the corresponding solfège syllables (e.g., do, re, mi). Critically, the melody, rhythm, and overall vocal production style were kept identical to the original song performances. To generate the spoken Lyrics stimuli, the same speaker/singer was recorded reading the lyrics from these ten song excerpts. She was instructed to speak in a natural, prosodically expressive manner. To provide a naturalistic speech baseline, excerpts were taken from the Cantonese version of The Little Prince, which contains a rich mix of narration, dialogue, and monologue, representative of typical spoken language. 

All recordings were made using Audio-Technica AT2035 microphone connected to the Steinberg UR22mkII interface in a sound-attenuated booth at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz and 16-bit resolution. Each stimulus was normalized to 20 seconds in duration and 70 dB SPL in loudness.

Participants first listened to a 20-second auditory stimulus. Immediately following the stimulus, two sequential 2AFC questions appeared on the screen. The first question asked, “Have you heard this specific content before?” (Yes/No), and the second asked, “Do you feel familiar with this content?” (Familiar/Unfamiliar). Participants were instructed to respond by pressing designated keys on the keyboard. After both questions were answered, a jittered silent inter-trial interval (ITI) ranging from 16 to 20 seconds followed. The task consisted of 40 unique trials in total (4 vocalization types: song, solfège, spoken lyrics, spoken story × 10 unique items).

Hemodynamic changes were recorded using NIRx NIRSport 2, which employed two wavelengths of near-infrared light, 760 nm and 850 nm. Data were acquired at a sampling rate of 10.2 Hz using NIRx Arora software. The optode montage was designed and optimized using the fOLD (fNIRS Optodes’ Location Decider) toolbox in MATLAB (Zimeo et al., 2018). A 49-channel montage including eight short separation channels covered the bilateral Middle Frontal Gyrus (MFG), Inferior Frontal Gyrus (IFG), Precentral Gyrus (PrCG), and a part of Superior Temporal Gyrus (STG), as shown in Figure 1.

Preprocessing was performed using the NIRS Toolbox in Matlab. To remove motion artifacts, we applied the Temporal Derivative Distribution Repair (TDDR) algorithm. The data were then band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 0.12 Hz to remove physiological noise and low-frequency drift. 

For the statistical analysis, a subject-level General Linear Model (GLM) was applied. Short-separation channels were included as regressors in the GLM to account for systemic physiological interference. Beta values representing the hemodynamic response for each condition were extracted. Group-level analysis was subsequently performed to examine the effects of Stimulus Type (Song, Solfège, Lyrics, and Story) and Familiarity (Familiar vs. Unfamiliar).

Results

A significant main effect of Vocalization was found on HbO concentration in the right prefrontal cortex. This was observed in both the right SFG, F(3, 522.94) = 4.97, padj-global = .025, and the right MFG, F(3, 704.54) = 4.74, padj-global = .025. Post-hoc contrasts confirmed that HbO activation was significantly greater for song (padj-hier = .012), solfège (padj-hier = .012), and spoken lyrics (padj-hier = .041) compared to the story condition in these regions. 

A significant interaction between Vocalization and Familiarity was found, revealing that the effect of familiarity was different for Song and Solfège. The specific patterns were explored through post-hoc contrasts.

For the Solfège condition, familiarity was associated with a general decrease in activation. HbO concentration in the right STG (estimate = -2.08, t(164.55) = -2.28, padj-hier = .024) and the right MTG (estimate = -1.54, t(528.60) = -2.06, padj-hier = .040) was significantly lower for familiar solfège compared to unfamiliar solfège. Additionally, when unfamiliar, Solfège elicited significantly greater HbO activation in the left PrCG than the Story condition, t(346.11) = 3.22, padj-hier = .046.

For the Song condition, the effect of familiarity was the opposite. HbO concentration in the left MFG was significantly greater for familiar songs compared to unfamiliar songs (estimate = 1.70, t(528.47) = 1.96, padj-hier = .050).

Conclusion

This study provides neuroimaging evidence for a hierarchical mechanism in Cantonese song perception, demonstrating that the brain switches predictive strategies based on information richness and familiarity.

Our results support a dual-pathway model: When processing pure pitch sequences (Solfège), the brain utilizes the dorsal auditory-motor stream (PrCG) for predictive simulation. Familiarity minimizes prediction error, manifesting as neural efficiency. On the other hand, when processing songs that containing rich semantic information, familiarity triggers active engagement of the prefrontal cortex (L-MFG). This reflects the retrieval of “music-speech schemas” to integrate auditory inputs.

In conclusion, within the unique acoustic environment of a tonal language, familiarity acts as a switch transitioning the brain from auditory-motor simulation to high-order semantic integration.
