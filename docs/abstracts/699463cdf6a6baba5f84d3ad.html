Introduction

The world’s population is ageing and this rise in longevity is accompanied by heterogeneous cognitive changes within the population. A deeper understanding of the cognitive and brain mechanisms underlying this variability is essential for the early detection of pathological ageing trajectories. Studying temporal processing offers an effective way to explore cognitive heterogeneity in older adults, as it is a key component of cognition involved in structuring perception, planning abilities, and memory. It is often said that “time passes more quickly as we get older”, but is it true? While this would be most relevant to a retrospective assessment of the passage of time on a large scale, such as over the course of a decade, this phenomenon seems less evident when paying attention to the passage of time of shorter intervals, ranging from a hundred of milliseconds to a few seconds.1 Age differences in response accuracy are often not observed on this timescale. Instead, differences appear in the precision of temporal judgements, with older adults showing greater variability in their duration estimates.2,3 But what are the underlying mechanisms? Several studies have shown that this temporal variability seems to be associated with age-related neuropsychological changes, particularly changes in attention.2 However, to our knowledge, no studies have examined the underlying brain mechanisms associated with these temporal processing changes in elderly subjects. This leaves a substantial gap in the literature, as it is within this timescale that most of our actions, perceptions and mental processes take place, shaping our interactions with the environment. Therefore, a deeper understanding of these mechanisms could provide novel insights into the neurophysiological processes associated with ageing and the heterogeneity of cognitive decline. In this context, the present study aims to shed light on the brain mechanisms underlying age-related changes in temporal processing.

Methods

This work is embedded within a larger-scale research project called TIMES, which aims to clarify the changes in brain connectivity associated with the perception and memory of time in young and elderly people, using multimodal brain imaging (simultaneous EEG-fMRI, T1w, DWI, PET). This study is part of clinical trial NCT06349213 sponsored by Inserm. The present study focuses on the analysis of functional MRI data acquired from fourteen young adults (YA group; 20 – 35 years) and twenty healthy older adults (OA group; 60 – 85 years) while they performed a temporal generalisation task. More participants are currently being included to reach a total of 50 YA and 80 OA. 

In the temporal generalisation task, participants learned a 1500 ms reference duration, shown three times as a grey square displayed for that interval. Then, other squares were presented in random order and remained on the screen either for the reference duration (1500 ms) or for one of the deviant durations (600, 900, 1200, 1800, 2100, or 2400 ms). For each trial, participants had to indicate whether the presented duration was “identical” or “different” to the reference duration. For each subject, the proportion of “identical” responses was calculated for the different durations and was plotted against the tested interval for the OA and YA groups (figure 1A). A non-parametric ANOVA-Type Statistics (ATS) was used on the proportion of “identical” responses, with age group as a between-subject factor and duration as a within-subject factor, using RStudio (v2025.9.2.418). Post hoc analyses were then performed to identify the differences highlighted by the ATS, with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm method. A Gaussian function was also fitted to individual performance to calculate the peak time (measure of temporal accuracy) and the full width at half maximum (FWHM; measure of temporal variability) of the curve for each subject. These variables were then compared between groups using Mann–Whitney tests in RStudio. 

During the execution of the task, T2*-weighted images were acquired using an 3T MRI scanner with an EPI sequence (438 volumes, interleaved slices, multiband acceleration factor of 3, isotropic voxel of 3 mm, TR 1.2 sec) for each participant. A visual quality control procedure was conducted, followed by a preprocessing sequence that includes slice timing correction, motion correction, EPI distortion correction, co-registration of functional images to structural ones, normalisation of functional images using the deformation field from T1w normalisation into MNI space, smoothing (6mm kernel isotropic), and outliers volume detection based on global signal change and framewise displacement, using SPM12 (v7771), FSL (v6.0.7.6) and the artefact detection toolbox (ART toolbox, v7(19), 11). Subsequently, an event-related first-level analysis of the pre-processed fMRI data was performed, and low-frequency drifts were corrected by applying a 128s high-pass filter. As this study focused on the differences in activation elicited by the reference duration between YA and OA, a two-sample t-test was performed between YA and OA activation maps for this duration in the second-level analysis. The YA>OA and OA>YA contrasts were tested and the resulting statistical maps were initially thresholded at p < 0.005 (uncorrected for multiple comparisons) at the voxel level. The voxel-per-cluster threshold was set to k = 20 voxels (corresponding to 160 mm3). To control for activations and deactivations, a one sample t-test was also conducted in each group.

Results

At the behavioural level, the main effect duration was significant (FATS(2.38) = 15.51, p < 0.001), but the main effect group was not (FATS(1) = 1.46, p = 0.23). However, the interaction duration x group tends to be significant (FATS(2.38) = 2.81, p = 0.0501), indicating that the effect of duration may differ depending on the group. Post-hoc analyses of the effect of duration revealed that the proportion of “identical” responses was significantly higher for the reference duration than for the deviant durations of 600 ms (W = 77, p < 0.05), 900 ms (W = 73.5, p < 0.05) and 2400 ms (W = 546, p < 0.001). No difference in the proportion of “identical” responses between OA and YA was found for any of the durations. However, OA tended to respond more “identical” to the 600 ms duration than YA (U = 192, p = 0.066) (figure 1A). Furthermore, no significant difference was found between the peak time of OA and YA (U = 109, p = 0.29), but the FWHM tended to be greater in OA than in YA (U = 192, p = 0.07). At the cerebral level, the YA>OA contrast revealed no significant difference in the amplitude of cerebral activation between the two groups. However, the OA>YA contrast revealed several clusters. The first cluster comprises cerebellar and occipital regions, including lobule VI of the cerebellum, the vermis, bilateral calcarine, lingual cortex and the left middle occipital gyrus (cluster-level pFWE-corr < 0.05, k=653). The second cluster comprises frontal regions including right supplementary motor area (SMA) as well as right middle and superior frontal gyrus (cluster-level pFWE-corr < 0.05, k=205). The third cluster comprises temporal, insular and frontal regions, including right middle temporal gyrus, right insula, and right inferior frontal gyrus opercular and orbital part (cluster-level puncorr < 0.05, k=95). The fourth cluster contains temporal regions including right middle and superior temporal gyrus (cluster-level puncorr < 0.05, k=107). Among all these regions, only the right SMA, the right insula, the right superior frontal gyrus and the right inferior frontal gyrus opercular part are significantly activated in OAs (voxel level puncorr < 0.001) (figure 1B).

Conclusion

The regions identified in the present study converge with those highlighted in recent meta-analyses on temporal processing.4,5 Indeed, the insula and the pre-SMA/SMA are the regions that are most consistently activated during timing tasks. Crucially, our findings extend this literature by revealing age-related differences in these timing-associated regions for the first time. While previous neuroimaging studies have focused almost exclusively on young adults, the current results suggest that OA recruit regions involved in temporal processing to a greater extent, as well as more frontal regions, to perform the task with a similar level of accuracy to YA. Indeed, OA demonstrated preserved temporal accuracy, exhibiting a response pattern similar to YA: both groups were more likely to judge the reference and nearby probe durations as “identical” than the more distant durations. Nevertheless, as demonstrated by other studies, OA tends to be more variable in their temporal judgements, responding more often “identical” to distant probe durations (e.g., 600 ms) resulting in a flatter curve (figure 1A) and greater FWHM.2 Including more participants will strengthen and confirm our preliminary statistical inferences. Presently, these results provide useful insights for further investigation into the relationships between imaging measures, temporal estimation and the heterogeneity of cognitive performance in older adults, with the aim of determining whether changes in temporal processing could serve as an early indicator of cognitive decline.
