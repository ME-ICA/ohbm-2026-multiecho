Introduction

The regional division of the human brain based on cytoarchitectural, functional, or structural signatures, also known as brain atlas or cortical parcellation, is a widely used approach in current neuroimaging research. The numerous existing parcellation schemes1 usually reflect group-level averages to provide robust and generalizable definitions of brain regions. However, such a population atlas can lead to a mitigation of inter-individual variability in a newly measured data as shown in the previous study2. Therefore, data-driven parcellation reflecting individual empirical data can be effective for further analysis in relationship between brain and behavior across individuals. Consequently, we tested whether such an individualized parcellation, as compared to a standard (predefined) parcellation, can more effectively capture individual characteristics of neuroimaging data, leading to enhanced brain-behavior relationships.

In particular, this study investigated the impact of individualizing a cortical population atlas on brain network and their relationship with behavioral measures in different age groups. To this end, we employed MRI as well as dual-task performance data collected from young and old participants. Age-related deterioration in dual-tasking is a well-known phenomenon, and also the neural correlates of dual-task performance have been investigated in the context of aging3. Still, it is an open question about the extent to which individual patterns of inter-regional connectivity are linked to individual dual-task performance. Here, we focus on functional connectivity-driven individualization, in which subject-specific resting-state functional MRI data are used to refine a population cortical atlas, yielding individualized functional parcels that serve as network nodes. Thus, we investigated alterations of brain network architecture induced by the individualization and examined the relationship between network properties and task performance.

Methods

We included 71 participants for analysis comprising 41 young adults (21 females; mean age: 25.6 ± 3.4; range: 21-34 years) and 30 older adults (12 females; mean age: 62.0 ± 5.8; range: 51-71 years). All participants reported no history or presence of psychiatric or neurological disorders and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. 

We used previously acquired data to assess dual-task performance3 and to define task-related regions among the considered whole-brain parcellation. Subsequently, using structural and functional MRI data, we investigated the impact of individualizing the brain parcellation on network properties and their relationships with performance measures.

In the MRI scanner, participants performed a single-stimulus dual-response task. On each trial, a high- or low-pitched tone stimulus (S) was presented, and participants were required to respond as fast and accurately as possible to the tone pitch by pressing the upper or lower of two vertically arranged response (R) keys. This spatially defined button-press response was to be performed with either one hand (left or right, single-task conditions) or both hands concurrently (dual-task conditions), which was varied across blocks of trials. The S-R compatibility mapping was applied to mutually congruent or incongruent conditions. Subsequently, this paradigm comprised two single-task and three dual-task conditions:

  • S-R compatible single task (C).
  • S-R incompatible single task (I).
  • S-R compatible, R-R congruent dual task (CC).
  • S-R incompatible, R-R congruent dual task (IC).
  • R-R incongruent dual task (S-R compatible hand: CI; S-R incompatible hand: II).

A 3T scanner was used to measure T1-weighted MRI (T1w), diffusion-weighted MRI (dwMRI; 60 directions), and functional MRI (fMRI) for resting-state and dual-task paradigms3. Structural MRI data were processed using an in-house pipeline. Subsequently, whole brain tractography with 10 million streamlines was calculated. More details of the structural MRI processing and tracking parameters can be found elsewhere4.

The task-related fMRI was processed with an in-house pipeline and the preprocessing details can be found in the previous study3. The thresholded group-level t map of the neural activation contrast between R-R incongruent and R-R congruent conditions (cluster-extent threshold of k > 224, corresponding to FWE-corrected p < 0.05, with voxel-level inclusion threshold of p < 0.001) elicited activation in a large fronto-parieto-insular network, which comprised 25 clusters of the population atlas. As such, this constituted dual-task-related brain regions and subsequently used for defining the task network.

For individualization of the population atlas (including the 25 task-related parcels), we applied the method of iteratively refining the individual brain parcellation by integrating additional subject-specific fMRI data to improve its functional precision beyond the given population atlas5. This individualization algorithm involves iterative realignment, where initial functional boundaries are adjusted based on individual resting-state functional connectivity patterns until convergence. This method allows for the detection of fine-grained variations in cortical functional organization, accounting for individualized network architecture.

Using two-way mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA), we evaluated main and interaction effects of age and individualization on various graph-theoretical network properties of both whole-brain and task-related structural and functional connectomes. We then applied multiple regression analysis to examine the relationship between the considered network properties and task performance.

Results

Our results reveal the impact of individualizing a population atlas on characteristics of structural and functional brain connectomes and their relationships with dual-task performance as a function of age. We found that individualizing cortical parcellation did not only altered graph-theoretical network properties but also resulted in pronounced age-related group differences through interaction with the individualization. Therefore, using individualized parcellations can provide evidence to improve our understanding of age-related changes in both brain connectivity and cognitive function.

We evaluated three graph-theoretical network properties: modularity (global network segregation), efficiency (global network integration), and clustering coefficient (community strength of regional network nodes) before and after individualization in each age group. For the full (whole-brain) network, the results of Wilcoxon signed-rank (population vs. individualization) tests revealed significant individualization-driven reductions in modularity for both structural connectivity (SC) and functional connectivity (FC), as well as in efficiency for FC only (Fig. 1a-b). Notably, we observed no age-related differences by Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (young vs. old) in either network property for SC, whereas for FC, both modularity and efficiency were significantly lower in the older group after individualization. The same analyses were conducted for the 25-node dual-task-related network. In contrast to the whole-brain connectome, we observed that individualization significantly increased the modularity of SC while preserving the significant age-related differences. The other comparisons yielded similar results to those observed for the whole-brain network (Fig. 1c-d), except for a lack of significant age differences in efficiency for FC. We also examined the clustering coefficients for each brain region using two-factor mixed ANOVA. We found that the individualization effect on a regional clustering coefficient was spatially rather sparse for SC but brain-wide for FC, because of functional (resting-state) data-driven individualization. However, age effects were generally confined to only a small set of regions. Interaction effects were completely absent for SC and present for FC only in four regions (Fig. 1e-f).

Furthermore, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to assess the joint potential of the SC and FC global network properties (modularity and global efficiency) for predicting individual reaction time (RT) per task condition. In both young and old groups, for the whole-brain network, we found significant correlations between predicted and observed RT across all dual-task conditions (CC, IC, CI, and II) only after individualization. Notably, for all dual-task conditions, the individualization approach yielded successful predictions of individual RTs as indicated by significant correlations between predicted and observed RTs, while using the non-individualized population atlas resulted in lower correlation coefficients in all task types than those of the individualized parcellation condition (Fig. 2a-c). F statistic results from the multiple linear regression models indicate that the individualized parcellation resulted in a better model fit for estimating RTs of all task conditions considered here than those of the population atlas (Fig. 2d). This differentiation, however, was only observed for the whole-brain network, whereas using the task-related 25-node network resulted in non-significant regression models for all scenarios (Fig. 2e). Examining the mean absolute error (MAE) of the predicted RTs to assess the prediction accuracy of the whole-brain network models revealed that using the predefined (non-individualized) population atlas led to a higher level of prediction error overall, as compared to using the individualized parcellations (Fig. 2f).

Conclusion

This study shows that individualizing brain parcellation impacts structural and functional connectome leading to changes in graph-theoretical network properties and their relationships to behavioral performance in different age groups. Accordingly, the network properties (modularity and efficiency) of the individualized parcellation explained behavioral performance better than those of the population atlas in all single- and dual-task conditions. These findings provide an answer from a neuroimaging perspective to the question of how individualization elicits changes in network architecture reflecting empirical functional data through a data-driven approach, as compared to a standard (predefined) population atlas in different age groups.

Our results also demonstrate that individualizing brain parcellation differentially affects the integration (efficiency) and segregation (modularity) of functional and structural brain networks. Moreover, by showing that these alterations induced by the individualization strengthen the associations between network properties and behavioral performance, our findings suggest that refining parcellations through a subject-specific data-driven approach, which keeps the backbone of brain parcellation schemes, may provide deeper insights into how individual differences in information processing during a highly demanding task are linked to individual brain network architectures and how this is influenced by ages.

In future research, therefore, it would be highly valuable to examine inter-individual variability of neuroimaging data while preserving individual characteristics in the context of data-driven neural correlates of behavioral and clinical measures obtained outside the scanner. Furthermore, it would be useful to examine whether the connectome and its network properties still exhibit consistent changes when applying different personalization schemes. If so, this would not only support the validity of this study in the future but also demonstrate the generalizability of this approach.
