Introduction

Throughout the history of psychology and cognitive neuroscience, research has largely focused on externally driven paradigms, leaving introspective thought out of reach. These mental contents, known as self-generated thoughts (SGTs), are consistently associated with the Default Mode Network (DMN), a network for internally oriented processing and mind-wandering. Growing interest in the DMN has motivated the development of methods to capture and analyze SGTs.

Approaches such as Experience Sampling enabled access to internal experience but failed to capture its moment-to-moment flow. This limitation led to tasks such as the Think Aloud Paradigm (TAP), in which individuals verbalize whatever arises in their mind, allowing continuous recording of more specific thought content and evolution.

In mental health, rumination is common in internalizing disorders such as depression and anxiety and refers to negative and persistent thought patterns^¹. Christoff et al.^² argue that ruminative states generate rigid cognitive dynamics, reducing flexibility. Raffaelli et al.^³ build on the concept of conceptual scope, describing how widely a person moves through conceptual space during thinking. A broad scope allows shifts toward varied ideas, whereas a narrow one constrains thought within a limited domain. Using TAP and Natural Language Processing (NLP), they showed that higher brooding rumination is linked with a narrowing of this scope, reflected in reduced variability and increased semantic repetitiveness, illustrating how these methods can quantify ruminative rigidity.

Moreover, several studies ^⁴ ^⁵ highlight the need for behavioral paradigms that capture thought dynamics and can be translated to neuroimaging methods such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). To date, no work has integrated clinical populations, TAP, and NLP-based approaches. The present work aims to provide a behavioral foundation for future fMRI studies examining thought dynamics.

Methods

The aim was to form two groups: one composed of individuals with symptoms of depression and/or anxiety, and a control group without such symptoms. Prior to the experimental session, each participant completed a psychological interview via videoconference to determine group assignment. In addition, all participants completed questionnaires assessing rumination, depression, and anxiety. So far, a total of 50 university students have completed the experiment, with 25 assigned to the clinical group and 25 to the control group.

During the TAP, participants were instructed to continuously verbalize any mental content that arose in their mind for 10 minutes, including thoughts, emotions, memories, images, internal or external perceptions, etc.

After completing the TAP, participants performed a valence-rating task in which they listened to 6-second segments of their whole recording and evaluated the emotional valence of each segment on a scale from 1 (very negative) to 9 (very positive).

The TAP audio recordings were automatically transcribed using Whisper and subsequently corrected manually. Word-level timestamps were aligned using the Montreal Forced Aligner and further adjusted in Praat, allowing to obtain the exact moment at which each word was produced. The transcripts were then manually segmented into thought units, defined on the basis of thematic shifts in the discourse, and the onset and offset of each unit were extracted. Using this temporal information, the previously obtained valence ratings were assigned to each individual thought.

Valence ratings were categorized into three affective states: negative (≤ 4), neutral (> 4 and < 6), and positive (≥ 6). This categorization was used to construct transition matrices based on first-order Markov models, which estimate the probability of remaining in or switching between emotional states during the flow of thought.

The semantic content of each thought was represented using embeddings generated with the BERT model (Rioplatense variant). Based on these vectors, three within-subject semantic similarity metrics were computed, using cosine similarity as the similarity measure: all-vs-all similarity (AvAS), in which each thought is compared to every other thought; subsequent similarity (SubS), in which each thought is compared to all posterior thoughts; and adjacent similarity (AdjS), in which each thought is compared to the immediately following one.

The analyses included group comparisons of word count and number of thoughts; linear regression models to evaluate whether group and psychological scales of depression, anxiety, rumination, and worries predicted thought valence; group comparisons of affective transition probabilities; models assessing whether semantic similarity was explained by group and psychological measures; and comparisons between groups of semantic similarity among thoughts within the same affective state to evaluate potential valence-dependent patterns of cognitive rigidity associated with affective symptomatology.

Results

No significant differences were observed between groups in the number of words or thoughts.

The analyses showed that group and all clinical questionnaires significantly predicted the self-reported valence of thoughts during the TAP. Consistent with this, the clinical group tended to report thoughts with more negative valence (b = -1.49, p < .001).

In the analysis of first-order Markov chains, significant group differences were observed in the probability of several types of transitions. The clinical group showed a higher probability of remaining in a negative state (p < .001) and of transitioning from a neutral to a negative state (p < .001), whereas the control group showed higher probabilities of remaining in a positive state (p < .001) and of transitioning from a neutral to a positive state (p < .001).

Regarding the semantic analysis, both group and several clinical questionnaires predicted AvAS and SubS. Participants in the clinical group showed higher semantic similarity in both cases (AvAS: p < .05; SubS: p < .05).

It was also found that thought valence specifically predicted AdjS, indicating that the affective content of a thought influences its semantic similarity to the following one. Specifically, more negative thoughts predicted greater similarity with the subsequent thought (p < .05).

Finally, when examining AvAS within each emotional state, negative thoughts showed significant group differences: negative thoughts in the clinical group exhibited greater similarity compared to negative thoughts in the control group (p < .001). For neutral and positive thoughts, groups did not predict differences in AvAS.

Conclusion

Taken together, the results show that the TAP allows for the detection of clear differences between individuals with and without symptoms of internalizing disorders. Both group and psychological scales predicted the emotional valence of thoughts, indicating that affective profiles directly influence the emotional tone of the ongoing stream of thought.


The transition analysis using Markov chains revealed greater inertia toward negative states and reduced stability in positive content in the clinical group, suggesting that negative affective states tend to persist and propagate more easily.


The clinical group showed higher semantic similarity in both the AvAS and SubS comparisons, indicating a less diverse discourse. Moreover, AdjS was modulated by valence: more negative thoughts predicted greater similarity with the subsequent thought, suggesting a narrowing of conceptual scope in the presence of negative affect, which limits the range of ideas toward which thought can shift.

Finally, when examining AvAS within each emotional state, this interaction between affect and rigidity was most clearly expressed in negative thoughts. In the clinical group, negative thoughts were more similar to one another than in the control group, indicating that under negative affective states, individuals with internalizing disorders tend to return to more recurrent and closely related themes. In contrast, negative thoughts in the control group were more varied.

Taken together, these findings are consistent with models such as those proposed by Christoff (2016), which describe rumination and depressive cognitive styles as processes characterized by a reduced conceptual scope and diminished cognitive flexibility. 

By providing robust behavioral evidence that the TAP effectively captures the dynamics of self-generated thought and how these dynamics are modulated by depression and anxiety symptoms, the present results offer a valuable empirical basis for future fMRI studies examining the neural dynamics of self-generated thoughts in these disorders.
