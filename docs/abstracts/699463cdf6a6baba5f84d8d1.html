Introduction

Prenatal exposure to psychoactive substances (e.g. cannabis) has been associated with lasting changes in brain development, yet the neural mechanisms linking early exposure to later cognitive vulnerability remain poorly understood. Neuroimaging studies have reported alterations in both structural and functional connectivity in exposed youth, but these modalities are often examined separately or modeled as static networks, limiting insight into how anatomical pathways shape ongoing functional interactions. Brain communication emerges from dynamic coordination across distributed regions constrained by underlying structure. Ignoring this interplay may obscure key markers of neurodevelopmental risk. To address these, we proposed a graph-based framework that explicitly models functional interactions as dynamic processes guided by structural wiring. By embedding phase-driven network dynamics within an attention-based representation and conditioning on individual cognitive profiles, the framework captures subject-specific patterns of coordinated brain activity. Applied to a large adolescent cohort, this approach aims to reveal network-level signatures of prenatal drug exposure (PDE) while providing interpretable insights into exposed brain networks.

Methods

Dataset and preprocessing

Data were drawn from the baseline cohort of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study, a U.S. multisite longitudinal cohort.1 The sample comprised 10,199 children aged 9-10 years with resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), and cognitive measures (fluid, crystallized, total intelligence and working memory score), including 601 participants identified with prenatal cannabis exposure.  

Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using standard steps, including motion correction, spatial normalization, and temporal filtering. Subject-specific functional network connectivity (FNC) matrices were estimated among 53 intrinsic connectivity networks identified via the NeuroMark ICA framework, with pairwise interactions computed using normalized dynamic time warping.2 DTI data were processed to reconstruct whole-brain white-matter pathways, and structural connectivity (SC) matrices were generated by quantifying streamline counts between atlas-defined (NeuroMark) networks. All connectivity matrices were normalized and aligned across modalities prior to analysis.

Proposed method

Kuramoto Coupling & Graph Tokenization: For each subject, we started from the SC matrix derived from DTI and applied a Top-K masking and row normalization to retain the strongest anatomical connections per network. This masked SC defined the anatomical support for all subsequent modeling. A Kuramoto layer was then applied directly on this SC graph to simulate oscillatory phase synchronization across brain networks. Node phases evolved through iterative updates driven by neighboring phases weighted by SC strength, producing a subject-specific phase configuration that captured network-level synchronization constrained by white-matter wiring. The final phases were embedded into phase-informed node tokens by combining trigonometric phase encodings with local structural summaries of each node’s SC profile and node degree statistics, followed by a learnable projection.  

Cognition–Phase Graph Attention: The resulting phase-informed graph, defined by the node tokens and the same SC-derived edges, was processed using a Graph Transformer operating under anatomical constraints. Self-attention was restricted to Top-K SC edges and was explicitly biased by both phase synchrony and structural connection strength, encouraging communication between anatomically connected and dynamically synchronized networks. To model inter-individual variability, subject-level cognitive scores were incorporated as conditioning signals that modulate query-key (Q-K) interactions within the transformer via lightweight, low-rank adaptations (MoE adapters), enabling cognition-dependent information routing while keeping value representations shared across subjects.  

Joint Training Objective. Node embeddings were aggregated using global pooling to obtain subject-level representations for PDE prediction. Training was performed end-to-end using a joint objective with three components: (i) a supervised classification loss for prenatal exposure status, (ii) a FNC reconstruction loss, where predicted inter-network interactions from learned embeddings were encouraged to align with empirical FNC, and (iii) a supervised contrastive loss to improve class separability and robustness under the pronounced class imbalance present in the ABCD cohort.

Results

Quantitative Performance: Model performance was evaluated using five-fold stratified cross-validation with identical data splits and inputs across all methods. As depicted in Figure 2(a), the proposed framework consistently outperformed representative baseline approaches across accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Methods based on generic graph attention or transformer architectures showed reduced sensitivity, reflecting limited capacity to capture structure-function interactions when connectivity was treated as static. Multimodal fusion baselines3,4,5 achieved moderate improvements but remained inferior to the proposed approach, highlighting the added value of dynamic coupling and cognition-aware modulation. Overall, results indicate that incorporating anatomically constrained synchronization dynamics yields more discriminative representations for PDE prediction.  

Qualitative Network Analysis. To examine network-level differences, we analyzed the top 3% of attention weights averaged within each group as demonstrated in Figure 2(b). Unexposed participants exhibited broadly distributed connectivity spanning sensorimotor, visual, and cognitive control systems with extensions toward the default mode network, consistent with normative large-scale integration. In contrast, exposed participants showed more localized patterns dominated by interactions among default mode, cognitive control, and cerebellar networks. These group-level differences suggest a shift from distributed integration toward selective engagement of higher-order control circuits in the exposed group, aligning with prior evidence of altered developmental network organization following PDE.

Conclusion

This study presents a graph-based unified framework that models brain network organization through anatomically constrained phase synchronization and cognition-modulated attention. By coupling oscillatory dynamics with graph transformer, the approach captures subject-specific patterns of network coordination that improve prediction of PDE and provide interpretable insights into altered large-scale brain organization. The results suggest that explicitly modeling dynamic structure-function interactions offer advantages over static connectome representations, particularly in developmentally sensitive and heterogeneous populations.   

Future work will include extending this framework in several directions. First, we plan to apply the model to longitudinal ABCD data to examine how structure-function coupling and phase synchronization evolve across adolescence and whether early alterations predict later cognitive or behavioral outcomes. Second, the Kuramoto dynamics will be expanded to incorporate frequency-specific and region-adaptive coupling, enabling investigation of network-dependent synchronization mechanisms. Third, we aim to integrate additional biological constraints, such as cortical thickness, to further ground the model in neurodevelopmental processes. Finally, we also plan to explore causal and counterfactual analyses within the learned graph representations to assess how targeted disruptions of specific networks may contribute to risk trajectories, supporting translational interpretation.
