Introduction

Both brain activity as measured by EEG or fMRI and naturalistic audiovisual inputs often show fractal (scale-free) dynamics characterized by power-law distributions. These distributions are typically quantified by the spectral exponent, or power-law exponent (PLE), using least-squares regression on the log-frequency and log-power transformed power spectrum.1 It has been suggested that this common feature is not coincidental but constitutes a mechanism for facilitating perceptual processing of ongoing naturalistic sensory input streams by matching the brain’s fractal dynamics to the dynamics of our environment. If so, scale-free brain dynamics should adapt to the ever-changing PLE of naturalistic audiovisual stimulation, which is rarely constant (monofractal) over time.2 

To test this, we used a sliding-window approach examining whether the brain’s dynamics show a non-stationary, multifractal PLE structure during movie-watching that is specific to auditory and visual regions, in contrast to the more stable, monofractal PLE observed during rest. We also aimed to see whether the temporal structure of the time-varying PLE is linked to the degree of input processing during movie-watching. Recent fMRI studies showed that the extent of brain activity shared across subjects during naturalistic audiovisual stimulation, as measured by dynamic (sliding window-based) inter-subject correlation (ISC), can serve as a marker of how strongly inputs are processed and tracked.3,4 Building on the multifractal nature of audiovisual inputs and their potential imprint on brain activity, we here asked whether the brain’s PLE time-series correlates with that of ISC in auditory and visual regions during movie-watching as a marker for the degree of input processing. A tight PLE-ISC correlation would indicate that the temporal structure of the brain’s multifractal dynamics is associated with input processing.

Methods

Dataset

Data were obtained from the Human Connectome Project 7-T dataset, comprising resting-state (REST_1) and movie-watching (MOVIE2_HO1) runs from n = 182 participants. Supplementary analyses included data from the MOVIE3 and MOVIE4 runs. We used HCP-preprocessed ICA-FIX–denoised data. Using the HCP MMP 1.0 atlas, we selected 11 visual, 13 auditory, and 13 dorsolateral prefrontal cortex regions for analysis.

Multifractal (sliding-window) PLE analysis of brain activity

For computing time-resolved PLEs for each brain region of interest, we used a 60-s sliding window moving with 1-s steps in the 0.05–0.5 Hz frequency range. The lower frequency limit of 0.05 Hz was chosen to include at least three cycles of the lowest frequency (0.016 Hz) within a 60-second window, while the upper limit corresponded to the Nyquist frequency of the dataset (TR = 1 s). PLEs were derived from log-log linear regression of power spectra obtained via fast Fourier Transform (FFT) of the averaged time series of all voxels of a given region. We avoided very short windows, such as 20- or 30-second windows, to preserve a sufficient number of sampling points for FFT and dynamic ISC.

Dynamic (sliding-window) ISC analysis of brain activity

Following our PLE analysis, we also used a 60-second sliding window with 1-second step increases for our ISC analysis of the BOLD time-series. For each subject, we computed mean time-series across all voxels per region of interest and applied a fourth-order forward-backward (effectively an eight-order) Chebyshev Type I bandpass filter with the frequency band of 0.05-0.5 Hz. We subsequently computed pairwise Pearson correlations between BOLD time series of a given region across subjects for every time window. Finally, we calculated the median Pearson correlation across subjects as a centrality statistic based on correlation coefficients of the lower triangle of the correlation matrix (excluding the diagonal line and upper triangle) for every window per region. Especially when using the pairwise correlation approach to compute ISC, the median, as compared to the mean, can provide a more accurate summary of the Pearson correlation values across subjects.

Formation of two subject groups per region

We applied a two-region group approach to use dynamic ISC as adopted by previous studies.3,4  Instead of grouping subjects based on behavioral data, we formed two groups based on the brain’s relative Rest-to-Movie change in the variability of scale-free dynamics since the brain’s spontaneous or intrinsic activity often shapes stimulus-induced activity, providing a subject-specific baseline of intrinsic (stimulation-independent) fluctuation. To this end, we derived the standard deviation (SD) of the PLE time-series in resting-state and movie runs per subject and region and computed the Rest-to-Movie differences in SD. In a second step, we formed two groups per region: participants with a Rest-to-Movie SD decrease of the PLE time-series were grouped into one group, while we assigned participants with an SD increase to the other group. This two-group approach allowed us to separately compute the region-based PLE and ISC time-series in the movie run for groups one and two.

Results

Higher Mean and SD of the PLE Time-Series During Naturalistic Inputs Compared to the Resting-State

Regarding our first aim, we found that the brain’s scale-free dynamics show a clearly distinct temporal structure of the PLE time-series in auditory and visual regions during movie-watching. This structure reflects multifractal, non-stationary dynamics, in contrast to the more monofractal and temporally stable PLE observed during rest. All p-values reported below were Bonferroni–Holm corrected.

            Mean results: The mean PLE was significantly higher in the movie run than in the resting-state run for both visual (t = –4.5, p = 0.001) and auditory regions (t = –7.47, p < 0.001). No significant difference emerged for DLPFC regions (t = –0.02, p = 0.987).

            SD results: We also observed a significantly higher SD of the PLE during movie-watching in visual (t = –11.58, p < 0.001), auditory (t = –12.9, p < 0.001), and DLPFC regions (t = –3.53, p = 0.004). Figure 1 shows these results and illustrates the increased mean and variability (SD) in sensory regions during naturalistic stimulation.

Correlation Between the PLE and ISC Time-Series During Movie-Watching

Regarding aim 2, we examined whether the brain’s multifractal dynamics are linked to input-driven shared brain fluctuations as indexed by the ISC time-series. We found robust correlations between PLE and ISC in sensory regions during movie-watching, as shown in Figure 2.

            Visual regions: All visual regions showed significant positive PLE–ISC correlations (p < 0.001 for every region), with an across-region average rho = 0.50, indicating a sizeable and consistent relationship between multifractal temporal structure and shared visual processing.

            Auditory regions: Similarly, all auditory regions exhibited significant positive correlations (p < 0.001), with a stronger across-region average rho = 0.72. These results, together with the visual findings, were replicated across MOVIE3 and MOVIE4 runs.

            DLPFC regions: DLPFC regions showed a mixture of positive and negative correlations. These were generally weaker than those in sensory cortex, yielding an average rho = –0.11. The weaker and inconsistent correlations suggest minimal linkage between PLE dynamics and ISC in higher-order control regions. 

Formation of Two Subject Groups – Linking Multifractal Brain Dynamics to the Degree of Input Processing

To further probe the relevance of multifractal temporal structure, we tested whether Rest-to-Movie changes in the SD of the PLE time-series relate to ISC levels during movie-watching. Employing a two-group method from prior fMRI research, subjects showing a Rest-to-Movie decrease in PLE SD formed Group 1, and those showing an increase formed Group 2. We expected that Group 1 showed systematically higher ISC values, reflecting stronger input tracking.

            Visual regions: Group 1 showed a significantly higher mean ISC than Group 2 (t = 5.8, p < 0.001).

            Auditory regions: Group 1 also showed higher ISC than Group 2 (t = 3.14, p = 0.002).

            DLPFC regions: No significant group difference was observed (t = 0.99, p = 0.33). These patterns were replicated in MOVIE3 and MOVIE4.

We then tested whether Group 1 also showed stronger PLE–ISC coupling.

            Visual regions: Group 1 exhibited significant positive PLE–ISC correlations in all visual regions (p < 0.001), with an average rho = 0.59. Group 2 showed a lower average rho = 0.40. The between-group comparison revealed significantly higher correlations for Group 1 (t = 1.94, p = 0.034).

            Auditory regions: Group 1 again showed significant positive correlations across all auditory regions (p < 0.001), average rho = 0.74, compared to 0.65 in Group 2. The group difference did not reach significance (t = 1.44, p = 0.082).

            DLPFC regions: Correlations in DLPFC were weak and mixed (Group 1: rho = –0.04; Group 2: rho = –0.16), yielding no significant group difference (t = 1.09, p = 0.288). These results were replicated in MOVIE3 but did not reach statistical significance in MOVIE4.

Conclusion

Our fMRI study is the first to show that human brain activity exhibits multifractal dynamics during naturalistic inputs, as demonstrated by a non-stationary temporal structure of the PLE. This structure was not only modality-specific with distinct temporal structures in the auditory and visual regions, but also specific to the different clips within the assessed movie runs. Conversely, during the resting-state, in which participants are exposed to highly repetitive auditory input from scanner noise and constant visual input from the head coils and scanner bore, the brain exhibited a monofractal PLE that lacked any meaningful temporal structure and instead oscillated in a stationary manner around a stable mean. We also demonstrated that the PLE time-series tightly correlated with the ISC time-series during movie-watching in both auditory and visual regions. Together, these results suggest that the brain’s multifractal dynamics tracked the movie’s auditory and visual input dynamics, respectively.

       How can we explain this positive PLE–ISC correlation, whereby higher PLE values are associated with greater shared brain activity in response to inputs? First, as shown in the analyses related to our first aim, mean PLE significantly increased during movie-watching. This likely reflects the greater complexity and fractal nature of the movie’s audiovisual features: continuous, naturalistic audiovisual inputs exhibit a richer, nested temporal structure than do the inputs during rest, prompting the brain’s dynamics to strengthen slower frequencies for temporal integration of information, thus elevating the PLE over time. The positive correlation between the PLE and ISC is consistent with previous findings from an electrocorticographic study which showed that steeper spectral power distributions were associated with greater shared brain activity (ISC) during intact movie conditions, compared to scrambled movie conditions.5 Conversely, higher degrees of temporal segregation, and thus lowered PLE values, are required in fast event-related designs, where the PLE decreases, relative to the resting-state.

            Finally, we sought to provide evidence that the brain’s relative Rest-to-Movie change in SD of scale-free dynamics, that is, in the PLE time-series, modulates the mean level of ISC during movie-watching. First, we showed that Group 1 (characterized by a Rest-to-Movie SD decrease of the PLE) exhibited a significantly higher mean ISC during movie-watching. Second, Group 1 also displayed a significantly stronger positive PLE-ISC correlation in visual and auditory regions than Group 2 (characterized by a Rest-to-Movie SD increase of the PLE). We infer that the higher PLE-ISC correlation in Group 1 relates to the previously observed higher mean of the PLE during movie-watching compared to rest. As discussed earlier, higher PLE values correspond to a shift of power from faster to slower frequencies, supporting greater temporal integration necessary for processing continuous, complex inputs.5 Group 1 exhibited a stronger shift toward steeper PLE values during the movie, enhancing temporal integration of continuous inputs and allowing a more faithful imprint of the movie’s audiovisual properties on brain dynamics. This, in turn, is thought to have led to higher ISC, reflecting a higher degree of shared input dynamics encoded across participants in Group 1 than in Group 2. Taken together, our results suggest that the ever-changing temporal structure of our natural environment is tracked by corresponding adaptations of brain activity dynamics, possibly enabling the efficient processing of perceptual inputs and their integration over time.
