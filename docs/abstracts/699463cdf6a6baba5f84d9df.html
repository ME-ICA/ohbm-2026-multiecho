Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a severe neurological condition that compromises the anatomical and functional integrity of the spinal cord. Although SCI produces structural and functional changes at the site of injury, alterations are also observed in the cerebral cortex as a result of the disruption of ascending and descending pathways.1

These changes may affect not only motor control but also somatosensory processing and cognitive functions associated with motor imagery. Studies have shown that individuals with SCI exhibit reduced cortical activity during somatosensory tasks compared to healthy individuals, suggesting a partial reorganization of cortical circuits involved in motor control following injury.2-5

However, a gap remains in the literature regarding the impact of epidural spinal cord stimulation (EES) on motor imagery, movement intention, and the modulation of sensorimotor rhythms in individuals with SCI.

Methods

One chronic paraplegic patient who had undergone implantation of epidural electrodes for motor rehabilitation was evaluated through electroencephalographic (EEG) signal acquisition using a LiveAmp-16 system (Brain Products GmbH, Germany). Data acquisition was performed twice per week under two stimulation conditions: epidural spinal cord stimulation turned OFF and turned ON.

The experimental protocol comprised two distinct motor imagery (MI) task conditions, presented independently: (i) a text-based instruction condition, in which the message “imagine yourself walking” was displayed on the screen, and (ii) a dynamic visual stimulus condition, in which a video of a person walking was presented. Both task conditions were performed under both epidural stimulation states (OFF and ON).

Each combination of stimulation state (OFF/ON) and task condition (text instruction/video) consisted of 20 trials. Each trial followed a fixed temporal structure, including a pre-task period, a motor imagery period, and a rest interval.

Each trial was organized as follows: (i) Pre-task (1 s): two consecutive auditory cues (double beep) were presented 1 second before task onset, serving as a preparatory stimulus. (ii) Motor imagery (20 s): immediately after the auditory cue, participants were instructed to initiate and maintain the motor imagery task for 20 seconds, according to the corresponding experimental condition (text instruction or visual stimulus). (iii) Rest (5 s): at the end of the trial, an auditory command (“rest”) signaled the beginning of a 5-second rest period before the next trial.

Acquisition setup

EEG recordings were obtained using the LiveAmp® system (Brain Products GmbH, Germany), comprising 16 EEG channels and three integrated inertial measurement units (IMUs). Data were acquired in continuous mode at a sampling rate of 500 Hz.

Passive Ag/AgCl electrodes provided by the LiveAmp® system were used and mounted on a cap compatible with the international 10–20 electrode placement system, covering frontal, central, temporal, parietal, and occipital regions. The reference electrode was configured according to the LiveAmp® system standard and located at Cz. The ground (GND) electrode was positioned on the right earlobe region, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Electrode impedances were maintained at ≤ 5 kΩ and were checked using the BrainVision Recorder software prior to data acquisition and reassessed at each change of experimental task. When necessary, manual adjustments were performed to ensure signal quality.

EEG signal processing and analysis

EEG signal processing was performed in the MATLAB environment (MathWorks, USA) using EEGLAB and custom routines. Initially, channels corresponding to the inertial sensors (IMUs) integrated into the LiveAmp® system were removed, retaining only EEG channels for subsequent analyses.

The signals were then visually inspected to identify gross artifacts, followed by band-pass filtering between 1 and 40 Hz. Continuous data were subsequently segmented into epochs time-locked to experimental events, covering a time window from −1 s to 2 s relative to the onset of the motor imagery task. The pre-task period was used as the baseline for spectral normalization.

Spectral analysis focused on sensorimotor rhythms, particularly the μ (8–13 Hz) and β (13–30 Hz) bands. Spectral modulation associated with motor imagery was assessed through the estimation of event-related desynchronization and synchronization (ERD/ERS), calculated from baseline-normalized spectral power.

Results

Figure 1 presents the modulation of sensorimotor rhythms in the μ band (8–13 Hz) during motor imagery (MI) and video observation tasks under pre-stimulation and epidural spinal cord stimulation (Stim) conditions.

The topographic maps (Fig. 1A–D) show a predominantly central distribution of μ-band activity, consistent with sensorimotor cortical regions. In the MI-pre condition (Fig. 1A), a moderate modulation of μ power is observed, whereas the Video-pre condition (Fig. 1B) exhibits a similar pattern but with reduced central involvement.

When epidural spinal cord stimulation was enabled, changes in both the spatial distribution and magnitude of μ-band power can be observed, particularly during motor imagery (MI-Stim; Fig. 1C), characterized by increased modulation over central regions compared with the pre-stimulation condition. In the Video-Stim condition (Fig. 1D), μ-band modulation is also evident, with a more pronounced spatial distribution over motor-related cortical areas.

Consistently, the analysis of μ-band power variation (Fig. 1E–H) reinforces the differences observed across experimental conditions. The comparison between Video-pre and MI-pre (Fig. 1E) reveals greater μ-band modulation during motor imagery associated with visual feedback. Similarly, the comparison between MI-Stim and MI-pre (Fig. 1F) demonstrates that spinal cord stimulation enhances μ-band power modulation across multiple channels, suggesting an effect of epidural spinal cord stimulation on sensorimotor rhythms during motor imagery. Comparable results are observed when comparing Video-Stim and Video-pre (Fig. 1G). The direct comparison between MI-Stim and Video-Stim (Fig. 1H) highlights additional spatial differences, indicating that the effect of stimulation depends on the type of task performed.

Furthermore, power spectral density (PSD) analyses in the frontoparietal region (Fig. 1I–L) show well-defined spectral peaks within the μ band across all conditions. Differences in μ-peak amplitude are observed between MI and video-based tasks, as well as between MI-pre and MI-Stim states, suggesting task- and stimulation-dependent modulation. Taken together, these results indicate that epidural spinal cord stimulation is associated with changes in the modulation of sensorimotor rhythms, particularly during motor imagery, with more pronounced effects over central cortical regions related to motor control.

Conclusion

The results demonstrate that epidural spinal cord stimulation may be associated with modulation of cortical sensorimotor rhythms, particularly in the μ band, during motor imagery tasks in individuals with spinal cord injury. Topographic and spectral analyses indicate that this effect occurs predominantly over frontoparietal cortical regions, consistent with sensorimotor areas involved in movement planning and motor intention.

Furthermore, the findings suggest that modulation induced by epidural spinal cord stimulation is task-dependent, being more pronounced during motor imagery compared with video observation. These results support the hypothesis that epidural spinal cord stimulation can influence spino-cortical circuits, impacting neural mechanisms related to motor intention even in the absence of overt motor execution.
