Introduction

Dedifferentiation is an age-related process marked by reduced neural specialization with activation patterns becoming less selective, more diffuse, and weaker, leading to poorer information processing and cognitive performance1. Entropy, a graph-theoretic measure derived from edge-based functional connectivity, quantifies this loss of specialization across multiple morphological scales, and has been associated with advancing age and declines in fluid cognition2. Although dedifferentiation is thought to contribute to many of the cognitive changes observed in aging, evidence is limited by the lack of racially diverse neuroimaging samples and the frequent practice of treating race as a nuisance covariate. The approach of running a single combined-race model on a majority White population risks obscuring meaningful group-specific patterns of neural aging3.

Given that specialization varies across individuals and over the lifespan, racialized differences in life experiences and contexts may contribute to distinct dedifferentiation trajectories. Social and Structural Determinants of Health (SSDoH) provide one framework for characterizing these environmental and structural conditions. These determinants range from individual factors like social support and socioeconomic status to neighborhood material deprivation and broader systemic inequities and vary substantially across racial groups. However, the collective and individual impact of these factors on whole-brain entropy, including whether they shape distinct dedifferentiation trajectories within each racial group, remains poorly understood. 

The objective of this study is to examine the impact of SSDoH on entropy in a diverse group of older adults through racially stratified analyses. This approach allows us to better characterize how environmental and structural factors impact age-related changes in dedifferentiation and to identify whether distinct neural aging patterns may emerge across racial groups.

Methods

We examined resting state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data from 2,738 adults (ages 50–93, M=65.3, SD=8.6) in the Health & Aging Brain Study–Health Disparities (HABS-HD) study4, a publicly-available, deidentified dataset that includes a diverse group of older adults, including 37.0% White (n=1,012), 27.7% Black (n=758), and 35.4% Hispanic participants (n=968).

MRI data were collected on Siemens 3T Skyra and Vida systems with a multi-channel head coil. High-resolution T1-weighted images were acquired using an MPRAGE sequence (TR = 2300 ms; TE = 2.98 ms; TI = 900 ms; flip angle = 9°; voxel size = 1 mm isotropic). Resting-state BOLD fMRI was acquired (TR = 3380 ms; TE = 30 ms; voxel size = 3.3 × 3.3 × 3.0 mm³; 150 volumes) with participants’ eyes open. For full MRI acquisition parameters, see Peterson et al4.

MRI data were preprocessed using FSL (FSL v6.0.7.13). T1-weighted images were skull-stripped with ROBEX and registered to MNI space using linear and nonlinear alignment. Functional images were motion-corrected with MCFLIRT, aligned to each participant’s T1 using FLIRT, and normalized to standard space without spatial smoothing, slice-timing correction, or intensity normalization. Time series were extracted from the Glasser atlas and denoised using a 24-parameter motion model, eight WM/CSF regressors, and global signal regression. Frames with framewise displacement greater than 0.5 mm were scrubbed, a 0.01 Hz high-pass filter was applied, and ICA-based noise components identified by MELODIC were removed using FSL FIX.

    Entropy was calculated by unwinding functional connectivity into an edge time series of the co-fluctuation between each node pair. These edge time series were clustered into ‘communities’ by temporal similarity in co-fluctuations. The distribution of each node’s participation in each edge-community was constructed, with the Shannon entropy formula used to calculate entropy for each node, which is then averaged to calculate whole-brain entropy. Higher entropy indicates more diverse community participation (i.e., greater dedifferentiation) while lower entropy indicates less diverse community participation (i.e., greater specialization). A detailed description of entropy calculation is shown in Figure 1.

We selected SSDoH variables based on the seven domains identified by the Alzheimer's Disease Research Center as likely to contribute to AD risk and progression5. Twelve variables were identified, representing all seven domains and spanning Bronfenbrenner’s microsystem (immediate environment), exosystem (external environment), and macrosystem (societal and cultural systems) levels.

We performed elastic net regression with repeated cross-validation for each racial group (White, Black, Hispanic) and a pooled sample with race as a predictor variable. Elastic net was used to handle collinearity among SSDoH variables and to improve model stability. All models were run in R (R v4.4.0) using the ‘caret’ and ‘glmnet’ packages on individuals with complete data. Whole-brain entropy was the dependent variable, predicted from SSDoH and covariates (age, sex, and APOE4 status). SSDoH were checked for collinearity and near-zero variance and winsorized to reduce the influence of extreme values. For each model, 30% of data was held out as a validation sample and the remaining 70% was repeatedly split into 10-folds for 100 iterations. Models were trained across a grid of parameter combinations, with optimal parameters selected by minimizing mean squared error (MSE) across folds. These parameters were then refit to the full 70% training data to obtain feature selection and weights, and model fit (MSE and R2). The resulting model was evaluated on the 30% held-out validation sample using R2 and MSE as measures of model generalizability. Feature stability was assessed by identifying the top 10% of models with the lowest RMSE and calculating for each predictor the percentage of those models in which it was selected.

Results

After assessing for near zero variance and high collinearity, “health insurance” and “cost barrier to healthcare” were removed in the model including only White participants. 

For the Combined group (N=2,736), the best-fitting model (ɑ=1, λ=0.00046) identified seven predictors, ranked in order of magnitude as age, Black race, having insurance (negative), Female sex (negative), Interpersonal Support Evaluation List (ISEL) score, Chronic Burden Scale (CBS) score (negative), and having a primary care provider (Figure 2A). The combined model had high feature stability, with age being selected in 86% of top models, and Black race, insurance, and female sex appearing in 70-80% (Figure 2B). The Combined model’s performance when applied to the full training set (n=1,916) returned an r-squared of 0.02 (R=0.14) and a smaller r-squared of 0.009 (R=0.096, MSE=0.00065) when applied to the validation set (n=820).

For the model including only White participants (N=1,012), the best-fitting model (ɑ=1, λ=0.0010) identified four predictors, ranked in order of magnitude as age, income, education, and seeing a doctor recently (Figure 2C). This model had moderate feature stability, with age being selected in 74% of top models, and other final model variables appearing in 30-40% (Figure 2D). This model’s performance when applied to the full training set (n=710) returned an r-squared of 0.03 (R=0.17) and a smaller r-squared of 0.007 (R=0.084, MSE=0.00060) when applied to the validation set (n=302). The alpha value of 1 suggests that this model performed best as a LASSO model.

For the model including only Black participants (N=758), the best-fitting model (ɑ=0.05, λ=0.034) identified one negative predictor—ISEL score (Figure 2E). However, this model had poor feature stability, with ISEL score being selected in only 26% of top models, and no other variable appearing in more than 12% (Figure 2F). As such, this model failed to identify any consistent predictors and had high instability. This model’s performance when applied to the full training set (n = 532) returned an r-squared of 0.009 (R=0.096) and an equal r-squared of 0.009 (R=0.094, MSE=0.00056) when applied to the validation set (n=226).

For the model including only Hispanic participants (N=860), the best-fitting model (ɑ=1, λ=0.0012) identified two predictors, ranked in order of magnitude as age and insurance (negative; Figure 2G). This model had moderate feature stability, with age being selected in 67% of top models, and insurance appearing in 42% (Figure 2H). This model’s performance when applied to the full training set (n=604) returned an r-squared of 0.02 (R=0.14) and a smaller r-squared of 0.008 (R=0.089, MSE=0.00074) when applied to the validation set (n=256). 

Across the racial groups, predictor selection and model performance differed substantially. The model including only White participants selected four sociodemographic/health-access features, while the model including only Hispanic participants selected two, with age being the only shared feature. The model including only Black participants retained only one feature and showed very low stability. The model combining across all participants retained the largest number of predictors across demographic, healthcare access, and psychosocial domains. Across all models (excluding the model including only Black participants which was too unstable to interpret), final models selected small regularization penalties and alpha levels close to one, indicating that sparse LASSO solutions provided the best balance of fit and regularization. All models explained low variance in the full training set (r-squared = 0.009-0.03) with smaller r-squared values on validation set (0.007-0.009) indicating limited generalizability to unseen data. Across all models, coefficients for selected predictors were small in magnitude, consistent with the low variance explained and indicating that no single SSDoH variable exerted a dominant influence.

Conclusion

Across this large, diverse sample of older adults, SSDoH explained a modest but detectable proportion of variance (~1-3% across groups) in entropy. Several predictors emerged consistently across models, suggesting that dedifferentiation is influenced by the cumulative influence of many contextual factors rather than any single determinant. Age was the strongest and most consistent predictor, with healthcare-access variables including insurance and recent medical contact also showing small but relatively stable associations in the Combined group and the groups including only White and Hispanic participants, demonstrating that access-related factors may contribute modestly to variability in neural specialization. One likely reason for the small magnitude of age effects compared to past studies2 is our age range (50+) which excludes meaningful specialization changes occurring earlier in adulthood. Previous research has also focused on majority-White populations, and our diminished age effects could be due to the age–entropy relationship being weaker in non-White populations. Furthermore, previous entropy studies have used task-based fMRI, where cognitive demands may amplify age-related dedifferentiation, whereas resting-state measures may show more modest effects.

    Racially stratified elastic-net models revealed that associations between SSDoH and entropy differed substantially across White, Black, and Hispanic participants, with each group showing distinct patterns of predictor selection and stability. Certain predictors that appeared consistently in the Combined model, such as female sex, often did not replicate within any racial group, demonstrating that pooled analyses can collapse heterogeneous pathways into averaged effects that are not representative of any subgroup. Conversely, several predictors emerged within specific racial groups (e.g., income for White participants) but not in the pooled model, highlighting that stratified analyses can reveal group-specific patterns that are obscured when data are aggregated. Low feature stability in the model including only Black participants suggests that the current SSDoH variables do not capture the key influences on entropy for this group, indicating the need for additional relevant factors (e.g., racial discrimination). These findings illustrate the limitations of relying on majority-White, pooled analyses and highlight the value of large, diverse neuroimaging datasets combined with rigorous racially stratified modeling. Such approaches provide a more complete and accurate characterization of the factors associated with dedifferentiation across different racial groups than pooled models can offer.

    Together, these results show that while individual SSDoH effects on entropy are small, they are detectable and show differential patterns across racial groups, reinforcing the importance of contextual factors for understanding variability in dedifferentiation. The divergence between pooled and stratified models demonstrates that group-specific pathways can be obscured when analyses rely on majority–White samples and combined–race analytic approaches. Stratified approaches reveal variation in predictor selection and stability that may more accurately reflect the underlying heterogeneity in the relationship between environmental factors and neural dedifferentiation. More broadly, this study shows that analyses grounded in large, diverse datasets reveal patterns in entropy not visible in traditional pooled approaches, highlighting the importance of diversity in neuroimaging datasets in order to accurately model dedifferentiation. Future work should integrate richer social and environmental indicators, follow entropy longitudinally, and analyze regional or network-level patterns to better capture how dedifferentiation progresses with age and varies by racial group. These efforts will help clarify pathways through which individual–environment interactions shape neural dedifferentiation.
