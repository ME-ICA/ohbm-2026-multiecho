Introduction

Focal epilepsy is increasingly regarded as a disorder of distributed functional and structural systems rather than isolated focal pathology. Resting-state fMRI and network analyses have shown that epileptic activity disturbs large-scale connectivity, particularly affecting connector hubs that integrate information across networks. Prior work demonstrated that mesial temporal lobe epilepsy exhibits loss of typical hub architecture and the emergence of aberrant hubs, but with widely varying by lateralization and localization, underscoring the need for better characterization of individualized network properties1,2.

Recent evidence indicates that individualized intrinsic connectivity patterns are not merely altered in epilepsy but also track meaningful variation in clinical and cognitive profiles. For instance, in temporal lobe epilepsy, the degree to which an individual’s intrinsic networks align with canonical resting-state systems impacts the integrity of neurocognition 3,4. However prior work solely focused on temporal lobe epilepsy, relied on single network parcellation schemes, and provided mostly global brain indices rather than regionally resolved measures of intrinsic organization.

The current study extends individualized network profiling to a large (pre-surgical) cohort that included left and right temporal lobe epilepsy, along with extratemporal epilepsy and matched healthy controls. We used a SPARK1,2, a bootstrap-stabilized sparse decomposition framework to obtain reliable individualized networks. This framework is able to characterize two complementary dimensions of individualized brain network topology, namely, alignment with established functional system architecture and the maintenance of intact connector architecture. These features were then modeled using a normative framework to assess deviation from expected organization, and multivariate analyses were used to relate individualized network alterations to epilepsy subtype and cognitive status.

Methods

We retrospectively examined 308 presurgical patients with focal epilepsy and 230 age- and sex-matched healthy participants (HPs) recruited from the Thomas Jefferson University Comprehensive Epilepsy Center. Based on a multidisciplinary presurgical evaluation, patients were classified according to seizure onset localization into left or right temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE_L, TLE_R), or extratemporal epilepsy (EXE). All participants underwent resting-state functional MRI scans and image preprocessing followed by individualized functional network extraction. Subject-level intrinsic connectivity networks were identified using SPARK, which applies sparse dictionary learning with bootstrap resampling to derive stable, overlapping functional components at the individual level1,2.

To evaluate how well individualized networks aligned with canonical brain systems, each subject’s spatial maps were compared to six established cortical network atlases (including Yeo 17-networks, Gordon 17 networks, and HCP-ICA et al.) as well as subcortical segmentations. Network correspondence was quantified using Dice similarity computed through the Network Correspondence Toolbox 5. This yielded atlas-specific best-match values for each canonical system for each individual. Global normativity was defined as the average of these best-match values across all canonical networks, providing an index of the overall conformity of each individual’s network architecture to expected functional topology. Complementing this measure, non-normativity was computed as 1 minus the best-match similarity index, computed for each individualized network, averaged across all individualized networks to quantify the extent of topological deviation (i.e., idiosyncrasies). To generate a robust cross-atlas regional indicator of normative organization, correspondence maps were projected to a common Freesurfer cortical representation and non-zero best-match values were averaged across atlases, producing a consensus correspondence profile per subject that indexed regional network normativity.

Beyond network correspondence, we assessed multi-system integration by computing voxel-wise k-hubness, defined as the number of individualized networks in which a given voxel participates. This measure identified connector regions that participate in multiple functional systems, thereby providing a summary of hub architecture at the regional level.

All correspondence-based and hubness-derived measures were transformed into individualized deviation scores (w-scores) using a Bayesian normative modeling framework implemented in PCNtoolkit. For each metric, models were trained exclusively on HPs to learn expected values as smooth functions of covariates including age, sex, head motion, and individualized network number. A cubic B-spline basis was used to capture nonlinear covariate effects.

Group-level statistical inference was performed using non-parametric permutation testing with maximum-T (Tmax) correction to control for multiple comparisons across cortical networks. For each comparison, subject-level w-scores were randomly shuffled 10,000 times under the null hypothesis, and test statistics were recomputed at each iteration to build an empirical null distribution of the maximal T-value across networks, yielding FWE–corrected p-values that ensured statistical significance only reflected effects exceeding chance under the strongest possible null scenario.  To investigate brain–behavior relationships, we applied sparse canonical correlation analysis (sCCA) using the PMA package. This approach simultaneously identified linear combinations of imaging features (set one, e.g., correspondence, hubness) and clinical–cognitive variables (set two, e.g., epilepsy lateralization, neuropsychological performance) that maximized cross-set covariance under L1 sparsity constraints. Sparsity parameters were tuned using permutation-based model selection to avoid overfitting.

Results

Correspondence profile analyses, operationalized through the consensus network normativity index, demonstrated that individualized deviations in canonical network representation are readily quantifiable at both global and regional scales. Patients with focal epilepsy exhibited widespread reductions in cortical normativity, with maximal effects in bilateral temporal cortices, dorsal prefrontal regions, and visual association areas. These spatial patterns showed the most pronounced deviations within Yeo-defined Temp–Par, limbic, Default B/C, and visual networks (Fig 1a). Global normativity analyses converged to indicate that relative to the normative distribution, focal epilepsy as a group displayed significantly lower conformity to canonical network architecture. This pattern was preserved across subgroups (TLE, TLE_L, TLE_R, and EXE). Conversely, all epilepsy groups demonstrated significantly higher non-normativity scores (Fig 1b).

Regional k-hubness was markedly reduced in bilateral temporal cortex and the hippocampus among focal epilepsy patients, pointing to a loss of multi-network integration capacity within the default mode and limbic systems. In contrast, dorsal prefrontal and occipital regions exhibited increased k-hubness, consistent with a re-allocation of connector load to associative and sensory processing territories (Fig 1c). Subgroup analyses revealed syndrome- and lateralization-specific hub disruption. For example, TLE_L patients showed selective reductions limited to the ipsilateral temporal cortex and hippocampus, whereas TLE_R patients exhibited broader reductions encompassing ipsilateral temporal regions and the bilateral hippocampi, with stronger effects ipsilateral to seizure onset. EXE patients demonstrated reduced hubness in bilateral temporal poles and increased engagement within left hemisphere motor networks, suggesting syndrome-specific redistribution of integrative capacity (Fig 1d).

SCCA identified multivariate relationships linking network deviations (set one) to clinical and cognitive expression (set two). After 10,000 permutations, a significant overall association was detected (p = 0.014) between sets. The first canonical variate (r = 0.33) was anchored by a correspondence profile including increased non-normativity, reduced global normativity, and reduced normativity within several systems (e.g., somatomotor, salience, and ventral-attention systems).  This canonical variate was mostly coupled with better cognitive performance (e.g.,  California Verbal Learning Test, coding [processing speed], IQ ). The second canonical variate (r = 0.30) emphasized hubness-based features including increased k-hubness in the right caudate and putamen and reduced hubness in several networks (e.g., control network). This variate mapped onto clinical epilepsy variables that included left lateralization, unilateral or bilateral hippocampal sclerosis, and unclear lateralization (and others, see Fig 2a). Subdomain-level sCCA provided additional resolution on these dissociations by isolating domain-specific relationships. Correspondence-profile features were primarily associated with executive and motor performance, whereas connector(k-hubness) abnormalities aligned with epilepsy subtype, lateralization, and memory performance. Cross-domain dependencies also emerged: language and memory scores covaried with lateralization; executive deficits tracked seizure type; and both memory and executive networks showed interactions modulated by underlying pathology (Fig. 2b).

Conclusion

Using a large presurgical cohort spanning temporal and extratemporal epilepsy, we identified systematic alterations in individualized brain network topology compared to matched healthy participants. Across the focal epilepsies, we observed a systematic disruption of the correspondence profile, reflected in reduced global and regional normativity together with elevated non-normativity. These alterations indicated widespread deviations from expected functional network organization. Importantly, correspondence disruptions extended beyond the seizure onset zone and implicated large-scale functional systems, aligning with the contemporary view of focal epilepsies as distributed network disorders rather than strictly localized conditions. Connector architecture pointed to some specificity in pathologic processes that is consistent with potential differential impacts in some specific epileptogenic regions. For instance, k-hubness analyses revealed that a loss of typical connector capacity, along with a redistribution of integrated hub responsibility across regions, was associated with reductions in temporal lobe epilepsy, with this effect most prominent within the ipsilateral temporal cortex and hippocampus. 

Sparse canonical correlation analysis revealing distinct modes of individualized topology with dissociable clinical associations. Deviations in correspondence, including reduced alignment and increased non normativity within specific intrinsic systems, were linked to poorer cognitive performance, indicating that global departures from canonical topology primarily index cognitive vulnerability. In contrast, regional hubness abnormalities mapped onto seizure lateralization and hippocampal pathology, demonstrating that disruption of connector architecture conveys phenotype specific information that is not captured by correspondence-based measures alone.

Methodologically, this work advances efforts at individualized network characterization in several ways. SPARK allowed us to generate bootstrap stabilized single subject networks, mitigating noise and instability that have challenged previous individualized decompositions. In turn, quantifying correspondence across multiple atlas spaces allowed us to project the results onto a unified template that reduced dependence on variability related to the choice of parcellation scheme, yielding more a reliable consensus on what are the regional indices of normativity.

Of particular clinical significance was our finding that a single analytic framework can produce two complementary but distinct axes of individualized brain network topology. Correspondence based measures derived from resting state fMRI provide a way to stratify patients according to global and regional organization abnormalities that have direct links to cognitive deficits. In contrast, hubness-based measures refine our characterization of network-level disease burden by helping to localize regions preferentially targeted by pathology. Both axes provide leads and hypotheses about the location and nature of functional impairments can make other types of assessments more efficient in their search for disease (i.e., seizure) impact. Most important, both axes provide network-level information that is missing from conventional pre-surgical evaluations, which tend to focus on isolated regions. If one keeps in mind, for instance, that surgical failures often result from seizure foci or connectivity abnormalities that go undetected, it becomes clear that true advancements in surgical prognostication will come only from a better accounting of this broader, network-level brain information.
