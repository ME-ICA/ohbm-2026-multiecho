Introduction

Identifying the neural correlates of chronic pain and their relationship to clinically relevant measures of disease has proven challenging due to high variability in individual patterns of functional brain connectivity. One viable method for addressing this issue is linear mixed-effects modeling (LMEs) paired with repeated measures designs. LMEs, however, remain a rarity in the neuroimaging space, perhaps due to a lack of mainstream toolboxes offering support for this type of modeling. An additional drawback is the compute time required to run multiple mixed-effects models, especially for applications such as whole-brain analyses which can involve tens of thousands of models. FEMA (Fast and Efficient Mixed-Effects Algorithm for large-sample whole-brain imaging data), an algorithm published in Human Brain Mapping in 2024, was recently introduced to enable mass-univariate LME analyses by dramatically reducing computation time while accounting for hierarchical and longitudinal data structures.1 The algorithm has since been recommended as an available method for longitudinal analysis in the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) study.2 Although FEMA shows strong promise, it has seen limited application beyond its original validation. 

Here, we implement FEMA in a large longitudinal chronic pain dataset and compare its results to a conventional LME approach based on MATLAB’s fitlme, evaluating both computational efficiency and concordance of neurobiological findings across two dimensions of the pain experience. 

Methods

Data were drawn from the Multidisciplinary Approach to the Study of Pelvic Pain (MAPP) Symptom Patterns Study,3 including 382 participants with urologic chronic pelvic pain scanned repeatedly (2–4 visits) over 36 months. At each visit, resting-state fMRI was acquired during two naturalistic bladder states: a “fuller bladder” scan following controlled water intake and an “empty bladder” scan after voiding. Participants reported pain at the time of each scan and recalled average pain over the preceding week.

Functional connectivity was computed as Fisher z-transformed pairwise correlations between 400 cortical nodes derived from the Schaefer atlas4 and 36 subcortical nodes derived from the Brainnetome atlas,5 yielding 94,830 edges. Analyses were restricted to participants meeting motion criteria (worst framewise displacement <3 mm; mean <0.5 mm) and contributing at least two visits. Site effects were harmonized using ComBat with biological covariates preserved. A complete-case filtering procedure was applied globally to remove scans with missing imaging values or missing model covariates, ensuring consistent sample sizes across all edges. 

Two linear mixed-effects models were fit separately for pain at the time of scan and recalled pain, adjusting for age, sex, scan type, and head motion, with a random intercept for participant. 

Model 1: f ~ sex + age + MeanFD + scantype + pain at time of scan + (1|pid) 

Model 2: f ~ sex + age + MeanFD + scantype + recalled pain+ (1|pid) 

We ran Models 1 and 2 using both FEMA and a more basic set of scripts that relied on the fitlme function in MATLAB. The fitlme function (using restricted maximum likelihood; REML) estimates the full random-effects covariance matrix via a Cholesky parameterization. The maximum number of optimization iterations was set to the default of 1000. All model outputs were assessed to confirm convergence, and only models that successfully converged were retained for downstream analysis. Degrees of freedom for fixed effects were approximated using the Satterthwaite method, which has been shown to produce consistent Type I error rates and be more conservative than other common approaches for approximating p-values in linear mixed-effects models.

FEMA implementation was based on the same models. A random intercept for participant ID was specified to account for within-subject dependence across repeated visits, with the family and individual identifiers set to the participant level. Model fitting was performed using the FEMA_fit function from the CMIG Tools FEMA implementation. Fixed-effects parameters were estimated using generalized least squares (GLS), while variance components for random effects were estimated using a method-of-moments (MoM) approach. 

For both implementations, adjusted p-values for the fixed effects of pain at time of scan and recalled pain were false discovery rate (FDR) corrected across all edges using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (via the mafdr function in MATLAB), with a significance threshold of q < 0.01. Significant effects were visualized using glass-brain representations, with edge color indicating effect direction and magnitude and node size reflecting the number of significant connections per region.

Results

The results of the two approaches identified highly overlapping results: while not identical, both FEMA and the fitlme approach identified connectivity within sensory-discriminative regions as being associated with pain at time of scan, while past-week recalled pain was associated with distributed patterns across regions implicated in memory, salience, and affective processing (Figure 1). This included a somatosensory region relevant to the painful body region (pelvis) and bilateral thalamus for pain at time of scan, and insula, bilateral prefrontal cortex to bilateral hippocampus, and amygdala to somatosensory edges in the recent average pain analysis. FEMA and fitlme agreed on direction of effects for all overlapping edges.

In addition to arriving at a similar set of findings, FEMA offered a significant reduction in processing time: FEMA completed each whole-brain analysis in approximately 15–20 seconds per model compared to 10–15 minutes using fitlme.

FEMA identified a slightly larger set of significant edges for recalled pain (113 vs. 105 with fitlme), while results for pain at the time of scan were identical across methods. No edges were uniquely identified by fitlme. 

Conclusion

FEMA produces results that closely match those obtained with conventional mixed-effects modeling while dramatically improving computational efficiency as originally posited by its authors. These findings validate FEMA as a reliable and scalable approach for longitudinal whole-brain neuroimaging analyses and demonstrate successful implementation of FEMA on a novel set of data. Applied to chronic pain, mixed-effects models revealed distinct connectivity patterns associated with pain experienced in the moment versus recalled clinical pain, aligning with hypothesized sensory-discriminative and affective-cognitive pain systems. FEMA lowers practical barriers to the widespread adoption of mixed-effects modeling in neuroimaging and facilitates more nuanced investigations of complex clinical phenotypes.
