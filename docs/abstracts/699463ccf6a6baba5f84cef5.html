Introduction

How a stable structural connectome gives rise to flexible and adaptive brain function remains a central question in neuroscience. While structure–function coupling (SFC) has been associated with development and pathology, most studies have treated this relationship as static. Yet brain activity is inherently dynamic, and how the dynamics of structure–function coupling underpin cognitive flexibility—and how they change with aging and disease—remains largely unknown.

Methods

Using multimodal MRI data from over 38,000 participants (44-82 years old) in the UK Biobank, we quantified both static (SFC) and dynamic (DSFC) structure–function coupling across the cortex. We then examined their associations with age, cognitive performance, physical health, and genetic risk factors, including Alzheimer's disease (AD) polygenic risk and APOE genotype.

Results

Dynamic coupling revealed a spatial organization distinct from static coupling. Dynamic structure-function coupling (DSFC) showed a more pronounced age-related decline in higher-order networks (limbic, frontoparietal, and default mode), whereas static structure-function coupling (SFC) primarily reflected decline in lower-order sensorimotor systems. Moreover, both DSFC and SFC in higher-order networks positively correlated with cognitive performance, while DSFC in higher-order networks and SFC in lower-order sensorimotor systems negatively associated with disease burden, Alzheimer's disease (AD) genetic risk, and APOE genotype. Notably, DSFC demonstrated a stronger association with cognition than SFC, and the impact of AD risk genes on DSFC occurred as early as midlife, preceding its effect on SFC. Structural equation modeling further revealed that both aging and APOE genotype contribute to reduced cognitive abilities through diminished DSFC in higher-order networks.

Conclusion

Our results, for the first time in a large-scale dataset of over 38,000 individuals, characterize the aging trajectories of static structure-function coupling (SFC) and dynamic structure-function coupling (DSFC), revealing their complementary role in brain. While SFC captures network stability, DSFC appears to reflect the brain's ability to transiently reconfigure beyond structural constraints, enabling large-scale integration that supports attention and other higher cognitive functions. Aging and genetic vulnerability may erode both this stability and flexibility. Together, these findings identify static and dynamic structure-function coupling as a complementary set of critical mechanisms linking brain architecture, cognition, and healthy aging.
