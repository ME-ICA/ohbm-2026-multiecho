Introduction

Understanding how epigenetic regulation shapes cortical structural development is a central challenge in contemporary systems neuroscience. DNA methylation (DNAm) dynamically modulates gene expression and plays a key role in neural development and behavioral variation (Moore et al., 2013). However, traditional neuroimaging analyses—such as voxel-level or cluster-level association studies—tend to focus on localized effects and may overlook distributed structural influences across the cortex (Eklund et al., 2016; Gong et al., 2018). Recent large-scale studies further demonstrate that cognitive and behavioral phenotypes are better characterized by global or multiregional brain patterns rather than focal regions alone (Zhao et al., 2021; Jia et al., 2020). This suggests that cortical architecture and its developmental trajectories may likewise reflect distributed molecular influences rather than isolated epigenetic effects.

DNAm-wide biological signatures are spatially structured across CpG sites. Therefore, capturing how distributed DNAm patterns map onto cortical structural organization requires computational models that operate beyond region-specific or locus-specific associations. Motivated by concepts from LD score regression (Bulik-Sullivan et al., 2015), we recently developed a DNAm-wide dependence index—here termed DNAm-DI—which aggregates correlation structure across the methylome to derive a pattern-level measure of DNAm influence.

We use the DNAm-DI sparse pattern to quantify DNAm-associated variance (DNAm-Var) in four cortical structural modalities derived from FreeSurfer’s Destrieux/Desikan–Killiany parcellation: cortical thickness, surface area, gray matter volume, and mean curvature. By applying this model at two developmental stages—early adolescence (age 14) and late adolescence (age 19)—we aim to identify cortical regions most sensitive to DNAm-wide patterns and to characterize developmental changes in epigenetic modulation of cortical structure.

Methods

Cortical structural features were extracted using FreeSurfer’s Destrieux/Desikan–Killiany parcellation (aparc.a2009s). For each participant, four complementary modalities were obtained for every cortical region in both hemispheres: cortical thickness (mm), surface area (mm²), gray matter volume (mm³), and mean curvature reflecting local folding morphology. Data were analyzed at two developmental stages, baseline (BL, age 14) and follow-up (FU2, age 19), enabling developmental comparison of DNA methylation–based effects on cortical morphology across adolescence.

To quantify distributed methylation influences, we defined a DNA methylation dependence index (DNAm-DI), derived from the Pearson correlation matrix across all CpG sites. Because DNAm exhibits strong block correlation structures, LD-score–like weighting with power q = 2 systematically underestimates DNAm-associated variance (DNAm-Var). The DNAm-DI extends this idea by raising correlations to a tunable power q, such that correlations near 1 retain substantial weight whereas small correlations rapidly approach zero. This formulation emphasizes CpG sites that are highly interconnected within the methylation network. As q increases (e.g., 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 14), the resulting DNAm-DI values increasingly reflect the true dependency structure of the methylome, and the regression-based DNAm-Var estimates converge toward the variance implied by the underlying correlation matrix. This provides a scalable framework for capturing pattern-level DNAm effects across tens of thousands of CpG sites.

Direct application of the full DNAm correlation matrix produced unstable optimal q values, likely due to its dense and imbalanced block structure. To address this, we constructed a DNAm-DI sparse pattern that retains only a representative subset of CpG sites. Starting from DNAm-DI scores computed at q = 2, the CpG site with the highest DNAm-DI was selected and all CpG sites with correlations exceeding a threshold (e.g., r > 0.5) were removed. This selection–elimination procedure was repeated several times to yield sparse subsets capturing the major dependency blocks of the methylome. For each sparse pattern, we recomputed the correlation matrix and used it in the DNAm-DI regression, which substantially improved stability and accuracy by reducing block-density imbalance and preventing variance underestimation.

DNAm-associated variance (DNAm-Var) was then estimated for each cortical region and each structural modality using a two-step regression analogous to LD-score variance decomposition, with the DNAm-DI (under different sparse patterns) as the predictor. Model calibration was evaluated by inspecting regression intercepts, all of which were approximately 1, indicating no detectable population stratification, confounding, or inflation. Thus, the resulting DNAm-Var values reflect genuine contributions of DNAm-wide patterns to variation in cortical structure.

Results

We estimated DNAm-associated variance (DNAm-Var) across eight cortical structural maps—thickness, surface area, gray matter volume, and mean curvature—at ages 14 and 19, each derived from the Destrieux/Desikan–Killiany parcellation. Across modalities and ages, a consistent spatial organization emerged: DNAm-Var was systematically elevated in transmodal association cortices, including the inferior and superior frontal gyri, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), insula, and inferior temporal regions. For example, at age 19, gray matter volume showed its strongest DNAm-Var in the left inferior frontal sulcus (0.30) and right superior frontal gyrus (0.25), while mean curvature highlighted the right anterior cingulate gyrus and sulcus (0.22) and right lunate/occipitomiddle sulcus (0.21). Thickness similarly emphasized association regions, with the right inferior temporal sulcus (0.23) and left anterior cingulate (0.20) among the top contributors. Even surface area—despite its distinct developmental trajectory—showed maximum DNAm-Var in the left inferior frontal sulcus (0.18) and right superior parietal lobule (0.13). In contrast, early-maturing sensory–occipital areas rarely exceeded DNAm-Var values of 0.05–0.07 in either developmental stage (e.g., cuneus and occipital pole at age 14), reinforcing the specificity of DNAm-wide influences toward higher-order regions.

A second prominent pattern was a developmental intensification of DNAm-Var from ages 14 to 19. Although the topological layout of epigenetically sensitive regions was already present at age 14—e.g., curvature peaks in the right anterior cingulate (0.083) and right posterior collateral sulcus (0.081), and thickness increases in the left occipital pole (0.063) and mid-cingulate regions (0.055–0.056)—the magnitude of DNAm-Var nearly doubled in several regions by age 19. Frontal territories showed the clearest amplification: the inferior frontal gyrus increased from modest values at 14 (e.g., 0.047–0.052 across surface area and volume) to some of the highest effects at 19 (0.17–0.30 across modalities). Insular regions followed a similar pattern, with the superior and inferior circular sulci rising from ∼0.05–0.08 at age 14 to ∼0.12–0.21 at age 19 across curvature, area, and volume. This coherent multimodal escalation suggests that DNAm-wide patterns exert growing influence on cortical architecture precisely in regions undergoing protracted adolescent maturation, such as the ACC, inferior frontal cortex, insula, and temporal association cortex. Regression intercepts remained close to 1 across models, indicating well-calibrated estimation without substantial inflation. Together, these findings indicate that distributed DNAm patterns increasingly shape the structural refinement of association cortices through adolescence, with frontal–cingulate–insular–temporal systems showing the strongest and most developmentally dynamic epigenetic sensitivity.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that distributed DNA methylation patterns exert systematic and spatially selective influence on cortical structure during adolescence. By applying the DNAm-DI–based sparse pattern model to multimodal MRI measures, we show that DNAm-associated variance is not uniformly expressed across the cortex but is instead concentrated within transmodal association regions—including the inferior and superior frontal gyri, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and inferior temporal cortex—that undergo prolonged structural maturation. The developmental increase in DNAm-Var from age 14 to 19, particularly within frontal and cingulo-insular territories, suggests that epigenetic patterning becomes progressively more relevant as higher-order neural circuits refine and consolidate. The convergence across thickness, area, volume, and curvature further supports the robustness of this epigenetic influence across multiple anatomical dimensions. Overall, our results highlight a coherent neuroepigenetic signature whereby DNAm-wide patterns preferentially shape the maturation of association cortex, offering new insight into how molecular regulation contributes to the structural organization and developmental trajectories of the adolescent brain.
