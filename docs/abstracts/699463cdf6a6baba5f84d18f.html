Introduction

Linear registration to a common template space is one of the first steps in many pipelines used for processing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data. Linear registration is a critical prerequisite for downstream processing and analyses such as tissue segmentation and nonlinear registration. To ensure its accuracy, quality control (QC) protocols relying on visual inspection have been proposed1. Despite being widely used, manual QC is time-consuming and subject to inter-rater variability. Consequently, automated quality control approaches for linear registration have been proposed2,3. However, current automatic QC methods have been trained and evaluated on synthetic data, small sample sizes, or preprocessed images, and their generalizability to other datasets is therefore limited. To address these limitations, we propose a 3D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)–based automated QC framework trained on raw T1-weighted (T1w) images from large-scale open-source datasets. 

Methods

Data: T1w MRI samples from large open-access datasets (e.g., the UK Biobank, National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC)) were preprocessed and the processed images were then linearly registered to the T1w MNI ICBM152-2009b template using PELICAN4. The resulting affine transforms were then applied to the raw T1w volumes (using mincresample tool) to obtain linearly registered raw images in template space. Based on our validated QC protocol1, experts visually assessed the quality of the linear registrations for over 100,000 T1w images. Network: we employed a 5-layer 3D CNN based on the architecture proposed by Bottani and colleagues5 (see Fig. 1) to predict the residual misregistration distance (RMD) from linearly registered raw T1w images. At present, 1,515 scans from UK Biobank were used for training, and 2,360 scans from NACC were used for validation and testing. The validation set (Npassed = 2,084) was used to assess RMD estimation accuracy of the model, and the test set (Npassed = 138, Nfailed = 138) to compare RMD distributions between QC-passed and QC-failed images for further classification. To allow the model to learn a broad range of potential misregistration parameters, we characterized linear-registration quality using a residual misalignment distance (RMD)3. Specifically, we defined a residual distance of 0 for QC-passed MRIs and generated additional synthetic misregistrations by applying extra linear transforms to these passed qc images. Affine parameters were sampled within expert-defined ranges (rotation ∈ [−2.2, 2.2] degree, scaling ∈ [0.94, 1.04], translation ∈ [−5, 5] mm along each axis). These ranges were empirically derived from prior visual assessments in which single-parameter perturbations were applied to QC-passed images and parameter values beyond these intervals consistently led to failed QC images. The model was trained for up to 80 epochs using Adam and Mean Squared Error loss, with an initial learning rate of 1e-3 halved every 10 epochs, and early stopping was applied if the validation loss did not improve after 10 epochs. Evaluation: To test the performance of the proposed method, we computed the Pearson correlation between predicted and target RMDs. Additionally, we compute the model’s accuracy for predicting failed QC cases, by estimating the optimal threshold of predicted RMD, following the work by de Senneville and colleagues3.

Results

Figure 2 summarizes the results of the proposed model’s performance. Figure 2.A shows the predicted RMD values plotted against the true synthetically generated RMD values for both the UK Biobank (used for training, plotted in blue) and NACC (used for independent validation, plotted in yellow) samples. Predicted RMD was strongly correlated with the true RMD in both the training (r = 0.96, p < 0.01) and validation (r = 0.80, p < 0.01) sets. However, in the NACC validation dataset, the model tended to systematically overestimate RMD compared with the training set. In the test dataset (Figure 2.B), QC-passed MRIs exhibited significantly lower predicted RMD values than QC-failed MRIs (t = 4.436, p < 0.01). Using the optimal threshold on predicted RMD (RMD = 7.06), we achieved a classification accuracy of 67.39%. 

Conclusion

Creation of over 100,000 QC-labeled MRI scans provides us with a unique opportunity to develop more robust models for automatic quality control of linear registration. Our current results show that the proposed 3D CNN can estimate RMD across heterogeneous datasets (UK Biobank and NACC). In addition, the predicted RMD distributions differ between QC-passed and QC-failed cases, suggesting that RMD could serve as a potential metric to distinguish QC-passed from QC-failed linear registrations. However, the current model systematically overestimates RMD in QC-passed raw MRI images and thus achieves only moderate classification accuracy. To further enhance QC performance and improve generalizability, future work will explore (i) better calibration strategies for the true RMD distribution to mitigate overestimation in QC-passed scans, (ii) alternative definitions and estimation methods for registration misalignment error, and (iii) network architectures that are more sensitive to spatial position and subtle misregistration patterns.
