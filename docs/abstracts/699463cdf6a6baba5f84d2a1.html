Introduction

Autism is marked by substantial heterogeneity in clinical characteristics, neuroimaging and genetic profiles. To characterize this variability, subtyping approaches have been increasingly used. Typically, autistic subgroups are derived from either clinical or imaging measures using clustering methods and validated in a complementary domain. Such external validation alone does not guarantee that the identified subgroups are robust, reliable or interpretable. Replication in an independent sample is needed to provide stronger evidence for the existence and stability of the subgroups1. 

Buch et al. identified four autistic subgroups with distinct gene-brain-behaviour associations using the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) dataset2. However, ABIDE combines data across sites retrospectively, and differences in site protocols or sampling strategies may introduce additional non-biological variability that obscures the interested biological variability. To address this issue, we applied an analytic pipeline matched with Buch et al. to the Longitudinal European Autism Project (LEAP) dataset3, a deeply phenotyped cohort of autistic and non-autistic participants (https://www.aims-2-trials.eu/). LEAP provides MRI, behavioural and genetic data collected according to a prospective protocol, thereby reducing non-biological sources of variability. Here, we aimed to independently validate whether the autism subgroups with specific gene-brain-behaviour relationships found in ABIDE can be replicated in LEAP.

Methods

We used the T1-weighted and resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) images in this study. After data quality control, 486 participants [270 autistic (197 males and 73 females) and 216 non-autistic participants (137 males and 79 females)] aged between 6 and 30 years from the first wave of LEAP were included. For clinical measures, we used the same four scales as Buch et al.: Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule 2 (ADOS-2) with its total severity calibrated severity scores (CSS), social affects (SA) CSS and restricted/repetitive behaviours (RRB) CSS, and verbal IQ. 

fMRI data preprocessing was conducted based on the tools from the FMRIB Software Library (FSL version 5.0.6, https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl), including removal of the first five volumes, motion correction, global intensity normalization, spatial smoothing (6mm FWHM Gaussian kernel), ICA-AROMA for correcting the motion related artifacts, nuisance regression for white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals, temporal high-pass filtering (0.01Hz), linear and nonlinear registration of fMRI images to the 2mm MNI 152 standard space. Following Buch et al., functional connectivity (FC) was calculated using 277 defined 10mm radius spherical regions from 264 cortical parcellations in Power et al.4 plus additional 13 brainstem and subcortical regions. After discarding regions where the temporal signal-to-noise ratio was small (TSNR < 75) in more than 10 autistic participants as in the original study, 243×243 connectivity r maps were converted into z maps using Fisher’s r-to-z transform. 

L2 regularized canonical correlation analysis (RCCA, with a ‘ridge’ penalty) was employed to identify a low-dimensional representation of the relationship between FC and SA, RRB and VIQ. RCCA decomposes the complex relationship between two sets of variables into several distinct dimensions - canonical variates (CVs). RCCA was then performed using a nested cross-validation framework, and the 270 autistic participants were split into training sets (N=256, 95%) and test sets (N=14, 5%) across 1000 iterations. The inner loop was for feature selection and for identifying the optimal parameter combinations (λx, λy and the number of selected FC features), and the outer loop evaluated the significance and the generalizability of CVs in the test dataset.

Further, three brain CVs were used to cluster autistic participants into subgroups. For each of the 1000 replicates, hierarchical clustering (cosine distance, average linkage) was performed exclusively in 256 training samples. We evaluated the cluster performance for 2-7 clusters in each training sets, replicating the procedure in Buch et al.. Finally, the cluster assignments of 270 autistic participants across 1000 replicates were aligned using Hungarian method, and the resulting consensus assignments were adopted as a robust basis for subsequent analyses of autistic subgroups.

We repeated the two-tailed t-test to compare the FC differences between each autistic subgroup and non-autistic group by including age, sex, full-scale IQ, site and mean framewise displacement as covariates. Multiple comparison correction was implemented by false discovery rate correction (FDR) (p < 0.05). Next, we also conducted the gene enrichment analysis to identify gene sets associated with FC alternations in each autistic subgroup, using the public transcriptomic data from Allen Human Brain Atlas dataset (AHBA, https://human.brain-map.org/). Here, partial least squares regression (PLSR) analysis was applied to generate gene rankings based on their association with FC changes in autistic subtypes.

Results

Hierarchical clustering was performed based on three brain CVs from RCCA. The four-cluster solution was also optimal for LEAP autistic participants, balancing model complexity and performance. Across the 1000 replicates, each individual was assigned to the cluster in which they appeared most frequently. The autistic subject number of four subgroups was in Fig. 1c (Subgroup1: 72, Subgroup2: 74, Subgroup3: 81, Subgroup4: 43). Three autistic subgroups’ behavioural patterns (Subgroup1, Subgroup2, Subgroup4) were similar with those identified in ABIDE, except the verbal IQ in Subgroup3 (Fig. 1a, 1b). Among these four autistic subgroups, Subgroup2 presented broadly affected autistic behaviours, including more social difficulties and restricted behaviours, in addition to the reduced verbal IQ. Subgroup1 exhibited the best verbal ability with above-average verbal IQ, but moderate levels of stereotyped behaviour and social difficulties. Subgroup3 showed comparatively fewer repetitive behaviours and moderate social difficulties, while subgroup4 characterized by fewer social difficulties and slightly above-average restricted behaviour. 

Several FC difference patterns between these autistic subgroups and non-autistic participants were found common across ABIDE and LEAP datasets (Fig. 1d). Compared with the non-autistic group, all autistic subgroups showed consistent increased FC between thalamus and other cortical networks. Notably, only Subgroup1 exhibited thalamic hyperconnectivity limited to the sensorimotor cortex, whereas the other subgroups showed thalamic hyperconnectivity involving multiple cortical networks. Subgroup2 displayed consistent increases in FC between brainstem (ventral tegmental area, VTA) and subcortical regions (amygdala and hippocampus). Robust FC decreases within default mode network (DMN), between medial temporal gyrus (MTG) and visual areas, as well as between visual and somatomotor networks were observed in Subgroup1 and Subgroup2. Decreased FC between visual and salience network was only found in Subgroup4. 

Additionally, we observed several LEAP-specific FC alterations (Fig. 1e). Subgroup1 and Subgroup4 showed increased FC between cerebellum and several cortical networks. Subgroup2 demonstrated strengthened FC within DMN and between DMN and salience networks, along with decreased FC between the amygdala and several cortical networks. Subgroup1 exhibited reduced FC between ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC) and insula. Subgroup3 showed increased FC between DMN and amygdala, hippocampus.

Similar to ABIDE result from Buch et al., several autistic related gene sets were significantly enriched (Fig. 1f, 1g). Notably, autism-upregulated genes were enriched among positively weighted genes in Subgroup2 and Subgroup3, whereas downregulated genes were enriched among negatively weighted genes in Subgroup2, Subgroup3 and Subgroup4. These findings suggest that the increased expression of autism-upregulated genes and decreased expression of autism-downregulated genes may contribute to the heightened FC differences between autistic subgroup and non-autistic group. We also observed enrichments across several other neuro-developmental gene sets. Specifically, ADHD-related gene sets were enriched in negatively weighted genes in Subgroup1, Subgroup2 and Subgroup3, and vocal learning-related gene sets were enriched in negatively weighted genes in Subgroup2, which had the poorest verbal performance, validating known existing prevalence of co-occurring clinical presentations across multiple neurodevelopmental conditions. Together, these findings imply that the FC alternations in each autistic subgroup were linked not only to autism risk variants but also to genes implicated in co-occurring conditions.

Conclusion

In this study, we validated the reliability of the autistic subgroups originally identified in ABIDE by Buch et al., now using an independent sample. The replication was performed using a matched analytic pipeline applied to the LEAP dataset, which minimizes non-biological sources of variability and better captures biologically-induced heterogeneity. Clustering based on brain CVs from RCCA revealed four distinct autistic subgroups, three of which were consistent with ABIDE results in terms of clinical measurements (Subgroup1, Subgroup2 and Subgroup4). 

Beyond the behavioural consistency, these four subgroups also demonstrated distinct FC patterns that partially converged with those observed in ABIDE. All these subgroups displayed increased thalamocortical FC, which aligned with the finding reported by Buch et al.. The thalamus plays a crucial role in integrating sensory input to the cortex and coordinating large-scale cortical activity, and thalamocortical hyperconnectivity has been consistently reported in autism. Beyond this shared impacted FC among four subgroups, each subgroup also showed distinct FC difference patterns. Subgroup2, characterized by broadly affected symptoms exhibited greater reduction in FC between visual and somatomotor networks than other subgroups. The visual-somatomotor hypoconnectivity has also been reported in previous LEAP studies, which reflects impacted visual-motor and multisensory integration in autism for the learning motor, communication, and social skills. Subgroup4, which showed the least social difficulties, displayed reduced FC between visual and salience network. Previous work found that stronger visual-salience FC is associated with higher social affect scores, which is consistent with our findings. Moreover, autism and co-occurring condition related genes enriched in our subgroups overlapped with those identified by Buch et al., further supporting that FC alternations in each autistic subgroup are linked to underlying gene expression patterns.
