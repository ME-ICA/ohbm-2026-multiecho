Introduction

Autistic youth and youth with early-onset psychosis (EOP) commonly experience difficulties in social cognition, a domain supported in part by the mirror neuron system (MNS). The MNS supports social experiences by assisting with action understanding, imitation, and appropriate response selection. Prior research has shown that individuals with EOP exhibit decreased MNS connectivity, which has been linked to social dysfunction.10 MNS connectivity in autistic youth has been more variable. Some studies indicate autistic youth demonstrate MNS overconnectivity, also associated with social difficulties,6 whereas other studies report underconnectivity between the MNS and mentalizing networks in autistic individuals.1 Despite these findings, the direct comparison of the MNS in autistic youth and youth with EOP using resting-state functional connectivity has not been fully explored yet. We aim to examine whole-brain functional connectivity of the MNS in autistic youth and youth with EOP compared with typically developing (TD) controls. 

Methods

Resting-state functional MRI data were acquired via a Siemens Prisma 3-Tesla scanner with 64-channel head coil at the UCLA Ahmanson-Lovelace Brain Mapping Center for 23 autistic youth (Mage = 15.86, 83.3% male), 25 youth with EOP (Mage = 16.51, 48% male), and 26 TD (Mage = 16.55, 42.3% male) controls. FSL9 and AFNI2 were used for preprocessing and bandpass filtering (0.008 < f < .08 Hz). ICA-AROMA8 addressed motion confounds. A bilateral inferior frontal gyrus, inferior parietal lobe, and intraparietal sulcus masks were created using the Harvard-Oxford atlas5 to form the MNS mask. Global time series from this MNS mask were correlated with whole-brain signal. Final connectivity maps were normalized using Fisher r-to-z transformations. AFNI’s 3dClustSim3,4 estimated the probability of false positives while ruining paired t-tests for group-level comparisons. Pairwise t-tests were used to evaluate the following group differences in whole-brain MNS functional connectivity maps: autistic youth vs. TD controls, autistic youth vs. EOP youth, and EOP youth vs. TD controls. 

Results

Whole-brain analyses showed underconnectivity of the MNS with the left inferior temporal gyrus (lITG) in autistic youth compared to TD controls, p = .01. When compared to youth with EOP, autistic youth also demonstrated underconnectivity of the MNS with the right middle temporal gyrus (rMTG), p = .01. No significant MNS over- or underconnectivity was observed in the EOP group compared to TD controls, p = .05. 

Conclusion

This study provides a direct comparison of resting-state MNS connectivity between autistic youth and youth with EOP. Although prior findings on MNS connectivity in autistic individuals are mixed, our study supports the pattern of underconnectivity of the MNS. Autistic youth demonstrated MNS underconnectivity with the lITG implicated in facial discrimination and decision making7 when compared to TD controls and underconnectivity with a region (rMTG) associated with social cognition11 when compared to the EOP group. In contrast, there were no significant MNS differences in the EOP group when compared to TD controls. One possible limitation of the study is medication use, which may alter functional connectivity patterns, potentially affecting group differences. Understanding these differences may help clarify distinct neurobiological pathways underlying social challenges in each group.
