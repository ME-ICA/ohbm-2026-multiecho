Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is associated with widespread brain structure abnormalities,1,2 yet how these regional deviations relate to large-scale network organization remains insufficiently understood. Structural covariance analysis offers a network-level framework to study how regional morphometry co-varies across individuals; regions that covary in size typically participate in shared functional networks, highlighting structure-function coupling.3 Graph theory extends this framework by quantifying network topology in terms of segregation, integration, and nodal importance, providing global and regional descriptors of brain organization. In neurodegenerative diseases, weaker interregional correlations may reflect disconnectivity or focal degeneration, whereas stronger correlations may indicate coordinated involvement of regions jointly affected by disease-related processes.

A consistent structural covariance signature of PD has been difficult to establish, likely due to heterogeneity in sample characteristics, methodological differences, and limited statistical power in individual studies. Large datasets with harmonized processing pipelines are therefore essential for characterizing representative network-level patterns. Here, we present findings from the multi-cohort ENIGMA-PD consortium on structural covariance across 110 brain regions. Using permutation testing on global and nodal graph measures, we examined covariance organization across Hoehn & Yahr (HY) disease stages.4 

We hypothesized that morphometric deviations from controls would become more coherent at higher HY stages and relate to worse cognitive and motor performance, expressed globally as lower modularity and higher clustering, and regionally as higher nodal strength and clustering. By assessing both global and nodal network properties, we aim to advance understanding of how structural brain networks reorganize throughout PD progression.

Methods

We analyzed 2,664 T1-weighted MRI scans from 1,678 individuals with PD (311 HY1, 792 HY2, 198 HY3, 74 HY4-5) and 986 controls. Medication strata included 86 medicated and 130 unmedicated HY1 participants, and 488 medicated and 175 unmedicated HY2 participants. In total, 816 participants had available MDS-UPDRS-III OFF motor scores, and 708 had available MoCA cognitive scores. Regional morphometrics were extracted using ENIGMA protocols, including 68 cortical thickness, 14 subcortical volume, and 28 cerebellar volume regions (https://enigma.ini.usc.edu/protocols/imaging-protocols/). Site variability was harmonized with ComBat (https://github.com/Jfortin1/neuroCombat_Rpackage),5 using empirical Bayes pooling across ROIs while preserving age, sex, and disease effects.

Two adjusted morphometric scores were generated. 1) Residualized scores were computed by regressing out age, sex, and ICV across the entire sample, enabling PD (full sample and HY strata) vs control comparisons. 2) W-scores were computed by quantifying deviations of PD participants from a normative model derived from matched controls (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/MatchIt/), supporting contextualized staging of disease-related abnormalities. W-scores were used for between HY comparisons. 

For each group, 110×110 positive-edge Pearson correlation matrices of the adjusted scores were constructed. Weighted graph metrics (Louvain modularity, average clustering, nodal strength, and nodal clustering) were computed using NetworkX. Group differences were assessed using 10,000-label permutation testing with FDR correction.

Because graph measures could only be assessed on group level, the associations between W-score networks and MoCA or MDS-UPDRS-III OFF medication were evaluated using bin-wise subsampling, in which participants were sorted by clinical score, partitioned into equal-sized bins (n = 30), and covariance matrix-derived graph measures computed per bin. The distribution of average clinical scores across bins were then correlated with the respective graph measures. Statistical significance was assessed using 10,000-label permutation testing with FDR correction.

Results

Main results for nodal strength are shown in Figure 1. Across all PD participants, cortical and subcortical clustering was higher relative to controls (cortex: z = 2.42, pfdr = 0.022; subcortex: z = 2.30, pfdr = 0.032), while modularity did not differ. When considering clinical staging, HY2 displayed the most distinct cortical covariance profile, characterized by higher clustering (z = 3.66, pfdr < 0.001) and lower cortical modularity (z = -2.81, pfdr = 0.005). Differences in strength and clustering were widespread across cortical nodes, reflecting a broad reweighting of covariance structure rather than focal regional effects. HY3 exhibited a contrasting pattern centered on subcortical regions, with higher subcortical clustering (z = 3.08, pfdr = 0.004) and higher nodal strength in the thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala, and accumbens. HY1 and HY4-5 did not show robust global distinctions from controls on these measures. Medication stratification within HY2 further differentiated covariance organization: medicated participants showed higher cortical and subcortical clustering (z = 2.45-2.72, pfdr ≤ 0.019) and lower modularity (z = -2.05 to -3.64, pfdr ≤ 0.026) than unmedicated participants, indicating heterogeneity in covariance patterns within the same clinical stage.

Clinical associations based on W-score networks revealed two distinct relationships. Subcortical clustering was positively related to MDS-UPDRS-III OFF severity (r = 0.47, pfdr = 0.025), such that bins with higher motor impairment displayed covariance structures with greater subcortical coherence. Similarly on the nodal level, stronger coherence was associated with greater motor severity in the left/right thalamus (r = 0.458, pfdr = 0.045; r = 0.586, pfdr = 0.014), left/right amygdala (r = 0.492, pfdr = 0.045; r = 0.486, pfdr = 0.045) and left putamen (r = 0.464, pfdr = 0.045). In contrast, higher global modularity was related to worse MoCA performance (r = -0.49, pfdr = 0.041), suggesting that bins with poorer cognition exhibited more segregated co-deviation patterns.

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated consistency of nodal strength patterns across unthresholded and thresholded networks (median r = 0.75; median CCC = 0.60), supporting the robustness of the observed group-level covariance profiles.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that PD is characterized by stage-specific reorganization of structural covariance rather than uniform degeneration. HY2 showed the most extensive cortical covariance pattern, suggesting a stage at which cortical atrophy becomes broadly coordinated across individuals. Differences between medicated and unmedicated HY2 participants point to additional heterogeneity within this stage, potentially reflecting a mix of dopaminergic influences and disease-severity-related factors. By HY3, covariance patterns were more prominently expressed in subcortical and limbic-striatal regions, aligning with networks implicated in motor progression and cognitive vulnerability. Clinical associations further revealed distinct axes of involvement: subcortical coherence tracked motor severity, whereas greater network segregation related to poorer cognition. Together, these results support PD as a condition of shifting covariance architecture across disease stages, reflecting evolving network involvement beyond regional atrophy alone.
