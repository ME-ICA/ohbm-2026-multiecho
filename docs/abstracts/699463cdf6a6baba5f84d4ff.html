Introduction

Previous studies demonstrate that prenatal maternal mental health influences fetal and early-childhood brain development and is linked to later behavioral outcomes. However, whether these prenatal effects persist into later childhood and how they shape long-term developmental brain trajectories remain unclear. This study aims to characterize the impact of prenatal maternal depression and anxiety on longitudinal brain structural and functional development from early to middle childhood

Methods

The study included 549 mother–child dyads from the longitudinal birth cohort.1 Prenatal maternal mood was assessed at 26–28 gestational weeks using three standardized instruments: the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS; 10 items), the State Anxiety scale of the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-S; 20 items). Postnatal maternal mood was measured using the Beck Depression Inventory–II (BDI-II; 21 items) at 3, 12, 24, 36 months, 4.5y, 6y, 8.5y, and 10.5y postpartum, and a weighted average score was calculated to derive a cumulative postnatal mood index used as a covariate in statistical modeling.

Children underwent longitudinal brain MRI scans at 4.5, 6, 7.5, and 10.5 years of age (total 1,247 scans) on a 3T Siemens Magneto Skyra scanner with a 32-channel head coil. High-resolution structural images were acquired using a T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (1 mm isotropic resolution; TR = 2000 ms, TE = 2.08 ms, flip angle = 9°). Cortical thickness, cortical area and subcortical volume measures were extracted using FreeSurfer. Resting-state fMRI preprocessing was performed using FSL v5.0.10,2 including slice-timing correction, motion correction, skull stripping, intensity normalization, regression of motion parameters, white matter, CSF, and global signals, and temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz). Scans with mean framewise displacement >0.5 mm were excluded.3 Functional MRIs were aligned to each subject’s anatomical T1 space and transformed into atlas space using LDDMM.4,5 

To investigate the longitudinal effects of prenatal maternal mood on brain development, generalized additive mixed model (GAMMs) was applied to cortical thickness, cortical surface area (both parcellated using the Desikan–Killiany atlas, 68 regions), and subcortical volumes (16 regions) across ages 4.5–10.5 years. With age as smooth term, GAMMs modeled nonlinear developmental trajectories with random intercepts for subject, adjusting for sex, ethnicity, maternal education level, and adjusted birth weight. Analyses were performed in two stages: first, postnatal maternal mood effects were regressed out from brain measures; second, prenatal mood effects and their interactions with age were modeled on the residualized outcomes.

Regions shown significant prenatal mood associations were selected as seeds for resting-state functional connectivity (rs-fMRI) analyses. For each seed, whole-brain connectivity maps were generated using voxel-wise Pearson correlation of BOLD time series. Linear mixed-effects models were used to examine prenatal mood effects on connectivity strength, controlling for sex, ethnicity, maternal education, and mean framewise displacement (FD). Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

Results

Across 68 cortical regions (DK atlas) and 16 subcortical structures, GAMMs detected select prenatal mood × age interactions on brain development from 4.5–10.5 years after FDR correction. For cortical surface area, significant effects were observed in the left temporal pole for depression × age (F = 14.63, qFDR = 0.009) and in the right isthmus cingulate for state anxiety × age (F = 9.09, qFDR = 0.004). No significant interactions were found for cortical thickness. For subcortical volume, a significant state anxiety × age effect emerged in the left amygdala; other regions did not survive multiple-comparison correction. 

Post-hoc group comparisons (high [≥ mean + 1 SD] vs low [≤ mean − 1 SD] exposure groups) (Figure 1A) revealed trajectory differences not just baseline shifts, with flatter surface-area growth in the left temporal pole under higher prenatal depression, steeper surface-area growth in the right isthmus cingulate under higher prenatal state anxiety, and crossing left amygdala volume trajectories indicating an early volume elevation followed by slower growth under higher prenatal anxiety exposure.

Seed-based connectivity analyses (Figure 1B) further revealed significant mood × age effects on functional network maturation. Prenatal depression was associated with altered developmental trajectories of left amygdala connectivity with medial and inferior temporal cortices and the temporal pole, and with posterior cingulate/isthmus cingulate connectivity to inferior parietal and lateral occipital cortices, implicating disruption of amygdala–temporal circuits and core nodes of the default mode network (DMN). State anxiety specifically moderated age-related changes in connectivity between the posterior cingulate/isthmus cingulate and superior frontal regions, indicating altered maturation of fronto–cingulate networks.

Conclusion

Prenatal maternal depression and anxiety shape long-term trajectories of brain structural and functional development, with region-specific effects on cortical surface area, amygdala volume, and affective and default mode networks. These findings identify the prenatal emotional environment as a critical determinant of childhood neurodevelopmental maturation.
