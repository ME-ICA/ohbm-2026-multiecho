Introduction

It is known that parameters reflecting white matter integrity across the brain vary with age, showing variability across individuals and brain area1 . This holds true for brain wide white matter in general and the visual white matter pathways specifically. Traditionally, clinical research has relied on case control research where the differences in means of the variable of interest are compared across a healthy and ‘diseased’ group, averaging out individual variation. While providing valuable insight on a population-wide level, means of populations are less informative when results are utilized in research or clinical setting on the level of a single individual. By applying a normative modelling approach to the white matter integrity measures of the visual system, we aim to model the inter-individual variation in white matter integrity measures instead of averaging it out and gain insight into the distributional quality across age. Thereby we create the ability to compare individuals to the established norm and quantify the deviation of the individual. 

Methods

We utilised the UK BioBank (2020 release, application number 23668 and 705478) to perform tractography of the optic tracts and optic radiations2. Individuals were included based on having participated in the first imaging assessment (age range 44-82). Patients without any ophthalmologic condition other than cataract, based on self-reported measures and ICD10 records, were included as a healthy sample. Furthermore, exclusion criteria were presence of self-reported neurological or psychiatric disease, potentially affecting the visual system. Finally, we performed data-driven quality control of diffusion scans.

In included individuals we performed probabilistic tractography3 for the optic tracts and two distinct bundles of optic radiations conveying central visual field information and peripheral visual field information, for both hemispheres separately. The seeds and targets for the tractography of the optic tract were the optic chiasm and the LGN. For tracing the optic radiations we leveraged the known retinotopic organisation of the visual cortex, with the aid of the Benson atlas 4, to create tractography targets consisting of the 0-3 degrees eccentricity and 15> degrees eccentricity of primary visual cortex (V1). Seeds for the optic radiations were defined as the LGN. Tractography was performed as seed-to-target and reversed target-to-seed, after which results of regular and reverse seeding were combined into a single tract. To increase the probability of including actual tracts and attempting to reduce partial volume effects, we excluded voxels of the tracts in which less than 5% of all traced fibres for that particular track cross the said voxel.

Tractography results were evaluated on a population-wide level, by visually inspecting the average tractography results of 1000 individuals at a time on the basis of known anatomical features, such as occurrences of hemisphere-crossing tracts and the Meyer loop in the peripherally projecting optic radiation. We extracted FA and NODDI (ICVF, IsoVF and OD) parameters for all tracts, after which the these were modelled against age using Generalised Additive Models of Location, Shape and Size (GAMLLS)5 in R (4.3.3). 

Within GAMLSS, we used the LMS (lambda, mu, sigma) method by Cole and Green, extended by Rigby and Stasinopoulos to model location, skewness and kurtosis. This method has been specifically developed for creating centile curves, and used by the World Health Organization to create growth reference curves.

The automated LMS function in R uses the RS (Rigby and Stanisopoulos) algorithm, iteratively fitting each of the four distributional parameters until convergence, selecting the best fit using the BIC. Additionally, we assessed whether the parameter estimates capture the distribution of the response accurately, by visual inspection of diagnostic plots of the residuals, including normal QQ-plots, worm plots, quantile residuals against fitted values and a density estimate. Further, we used Q-statistics to determine any unexplained structure in the residuals due to inadequate fit in one of the four distribution parameters. Residuals with absolute z-scores greater than 3.5 are considered outliers and were removed, after which the final normative model is fit again. 

To assess the stability of the estimated centile curves, we applied bootstrapping of incremental subsamples of the original full sample to calculate R2 values for different sample sizes, for different centile curves of the normative models for all tracts and parameters separately. Additionally, for each included individual Z-scores for all white matter parameters were computed based on the created models for all tracts, correlation analysis between deviation scores was performed.

Results

In the 2020 release of the UKBioBank dataset 42803 individuals underwent T1-weighted imaging. After filtering individuals on the basis of having no self-reported or ICD-10 coded ophthalmologic disease, self-reported neurological or psychiatric disease potentially influencing the visual system, a sample of 27082 individuals remained. 

Of these 27082 individuals we excluded individuals based on data-driven dMRI quality control measures. Appropriate functions, gaussian or lognormal, were fitted to quality-related data-fields after which the intercept value was calculated as an exclusion point for normal dMRI quality, as well as individuals with missing values in any of these data-fields. 23079 individuals had a normal number of dMRI outlier slices detected and corrected, 25922 individuals had a normal amount standard deviation of apparent translation in the Y axis as measured by eddy and 25940 individuals had dMRI data in which the discrepancy between dMRI brain image and T1 brain image was considered to be normal. After combination of these three quality control criteria, 21828 healthy individuals were left for inclusion.

Of the initial 42803 individuals who underwent T1-weighted imaging, we excluded all 11789 individuals who had no bedpostX processed data available which is required for tractography. For the optic tract 16334  healthy individuals whose T1 and dMRI data was available to us and passed quality checks, plus whose chiasm seed mask was included in the BedpostX mask, were included. For the optic radiations 15838 healthy individuals whose T1, freesurfer and dMRI data was available to us and passed quality checks, were included.

The sample sizes used produced models which were sufficient in robustly estimating the 5th up to the 95th centile curves. Slight differences in centile curves were present between hemispheres for the optic tract and the optic radiations, when comparing the centrally and peripherally projecting radiations within the same hemisphere there are clear differences in distributional ranges of the same white matter parameter. Overall, in the optic tract’s FA gradually declines over age, while ICVF and IsoVF steadily increase with age, all with stable distributions regarding spread and shape. The range of the distribution of OD increases with age, with the higher centiles increasing faster than the lower centiles, the 50th centile shows a slightly increasing OD with age. 
 For both the central and peripheral optic radiations there are little differences between hemispheres, however, differences can be observed when comparing diffusion parameters of the central to the peripherally projecting optic radiations in the same hemisphere. While the directionality of the centile curves is similar comparing both optic radiations, the distributional qualities over age does differ between the central and peripherally radiating optic radiations appear (Figure 1). Large inter-individual variation is visible across all measures and tracts.

Within individuals, no strong correlations were found between z-scores of the optic tract and optic radiations. Within the optic tracts, mild correlations between the same microstructural parameters were found, within the optic radiations there were strong correlations between ICVF hemispheres and eccentricities. 

Conclusion

Using a large adult population sample we attempted to establish normative ranges of FA and NODDI parameters resembling white matter integrity of the post-chiasmal visual white matter tracts. These normative models show age related changes to be similar when comparing the same structures across hemispheres, both in directionality of effects and distributional qualities. Within hemispheres, the centrally (0-3 degrees) and peripherally (15> degrees) optic radiations show differences in distributional qualities. For all structures and parameters studies, there is large inter-individual variation. 

The created normative models enable quantification of the deviation from the norm on an N=1 basis. This information is especially useful in assessing the white matter integrity in the visual system of individuals affected by ophthalmologic disease, in which the input from the retina is altered. Our approach of separately tracking the centrally and peripherally projecting optic radiations, based on the retinotopic maps of the Benson atlas, add clinical value to the constructed models. In clinical practice, visual field defects due to ophthalmologic disease are characterised by their specific location in relation to the affected retina; usually the retinal diseases are located peripherally and progresses inwards, or pathology is located in the centre of the retina as is the case in macular diseases. The ability to quantify deviation scores of individuals enable stratification and assessment of patients, which is useful in (future) clinical trials attempting to restore vision by reviving retinal input signals. Furthermore, using deviation scores from the norm as a biomarker has the potential explain differences in visual functioning between individuals suffering from ophthalmologic disease.
