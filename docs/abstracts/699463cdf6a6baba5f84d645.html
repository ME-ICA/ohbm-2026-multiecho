Introduction

Deep learning models can characterize aging-related structural changes from brain MRI to estimate normative aging trajectories for age prediction, thereby providing a quantitative framework for identifying deviations associated with disease. 1 However, neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric are known to affect brain structure in a spatially heterogeneous manner. For example, the hippocampus and amygdala have been reported to exhibit early and pronounced atrophy in Alzheimer’s disease. 2 These observations suggest that region-specific biomarkers are likely to be more sensitive to disease-related alterations than a global brain age measure. By using convolutional neural network, local brain age estimation can be achieved by restricting predictions to structural features extracted from predefined image patches. 3 In this study, we instead propose a region-of-interest-based approach in which anatomically meaningful structures, such as cerebral cortex or subcortical gray matter nuclei, could be selectively preserved by masking on the structural MRI, rather than being restricted by a rectangular cuboid. The Simple Fully Convolutional Network (SFCN) is a lightweight deep neural network that has demonstrated high accuracy in brain age prediction. 4 By eliminating fully connected layers, SFCN substantially reduces model parameters, enabling efficient training, lowering the risk of overfitting, and allowing flexibility in input size. As a result, we adopt the SFCN architecture to train our regional brain age models, including models targeting the hippocampus and/or amygdala, and contrast their performance with the conventional global brain age models. In addition, we compare two types of prediction strategies, soft classification and regression. To evaluate the clinical relevance of regional brain age estimation, patients with dementia and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) were collected from the OASIS-3 cohort.

Methods

To train the age prediction model, T1-weighted MRI from 4,171 healthy subjects (age: 5-100 years) were aggregated from 7 databases: IXI (n = 561), OASIS-1 (n = 316), FCP (n = 1080), ABIDE-I (n = 547), ABIDE-II (n = 571), NKI-RS (n = 411), and HCP-A (n = 685).  To minimize inter-site variability arising from differences in scanners, acquisition protocols, and preprocessing pipelines, all images underwent a unified preprocessing procedure (Fig.1a).  Non-brain tissues were removed using a deep learning-based tool (TigerBX, https://github.com/htylab/tigerbx), followed by automatic tissue segmentation into 43 subregions according to the FreeSurfer aseg atlas. Subsequent preprocessing was performed using CAT12 (https://neuro-jena.github.io/cat/), including bias correction, global intensity normalization, rigid registration to the MNI T1 template, and resampling to 1-mm isotropic resolution to generate the non-deformed T1WI, together with the gray matter and white matter probability maps (GMP and WMP). The aseg segmentation map was aligned with the corresponding T1WI using the same rigid transformation, enabling selective preservation of image intensity within regions of interest through masking of the preprocessed T1WI. For example, a deep gray matter image was generated by masking bilateral thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, amygdala, accumbens, and ventral diencephalon (Fig.1a). In addition to deep gray matter, five other masked images were constructed, corresponding to cerebral white matter, cerebral cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, and a combined hippocampus-amygdala region. These region-specific images were used independently to train the corresponding regional brain age models. To facilitate efficient training while preserving relevant structural information, masked images were spatially cropped to fixed matrix sizes: 171 × 210 × 163 for cerebral white matter and cortex, and 93 × 100 × 70 for deep gray matter nuclei.

           In this study, the SFCN architecture was implemented using two prediction strategies: soft classification and regression (Fig.1b). For soft classification, the network output consisted of 95 nodes following a softmax layer, with each node representing the probability of an age between 5 and 100 years at one-year intervals. In contrast, the regression model employed a single output node with linear activation to directly estimate chronological age. All data were divided into training (n = 3,471), validation (n = 350), and test sets (n = 350). The validation and test sets were constructed to have approximately uniform age distributions. During each training epoch, samples were drawn with equal probability across age groups to reduce potential age-related bias. Age groups with larger sample sizes were nevertheless fully utilized across successive training epochs. Models were trained iteratively for up to 250 epochs, and the network weights with the minimum validation loss were selected for testing. In total, twelve regional brain age models (6 input types × 2 prediction strategies) were trained independently and compared with the other six global brain age models (T1, GMP, and WMP × 2 prediction strategies). For each model, mean absolute error (MAE) and coefficient of determination (R2) were calculated for validation and test sets. To correct systematic bias in age prediction, a linear regression-based bias adjustment was derived from the validation data and applied to subsequent analyses. 5 All models were further evaluated in an independent clinical sample from the OASIS-3 cohort, including participants with dementia (n = 150), MCI (n = 75), and cognitively normal controls (CN; n = 150), matched for age distribution (range: 59.3–89.4 years; mean: 75.4 years). The difference between predicted brain age and chronological age, defined as the predicted age difference (PAD), was obtained to quantify deviations from normative aging associated with neurodegenerative processes.

Results

Bias-corrected MAE and R2 of validation and test sets were summarized for all global and regional brain age models in Figure 2. Overall, global brain age models based on whole-brain inputs (T1, GMP, and WMP) achieved lower MAE than regional models, consistent with the greater availability of structural information for age prediction at the whole-brain level. In contrast, models trained exclusively on the hippocampus or amygdala exhibited higher prediction errors, with MAE exceeding 6.2 years. Notably, the deep gray matter model represented an exception among regional approaches, demonstrating prediction accuracy comparable to global models despite being derived from a substantially smaller masked region. Across all input types, the SFCN models with soft classification strategy consistently yielded lower MAE and higher R2 values than their regression-based counterparts except for the combined hippocampus-amygdala input.  

Based on the superior prediction performance, SFCN models employing soft classification were used to estimate PAD in CN, MCI, and dementia groups.  Group differences in PAD were assessed using two-sample t tests. Across all models, PAD was significantly higher in the dementia group than in CN (all p < 0.05). In contrast, PAD in the MCI group was significantly elevated relative to CN only in one global model (GMP) and in five regional models, including cerebral cortex, deep gray matter, hippocampus, amygdala, and the combined hippocampus-amygdala region. Although the combined hippocampus-amygdala model showed lower age prediction accuracy compared with global brain age models (test MAE = 6.49 years vs. 3.57-3.73 years), it exhibited a pronounced separation of PAD across clinical groups. The mean PAD values derived from this model were −1.32 years for CN, 3.61 years for MCI, and 6.50 years for dementia, resulting in significant differences between all pairwise group comparisons.

Conclusion

In this study, the regional brain age model based on the lightweight SFCN has been implemented to compare with the conventional global age model. While global models based on whole-brain inputs, such as T1WI, GMP, and WMP, achieved the higher age prediction accuracy, the deep gray matter model exhibited accuracy comparable to global models despite substantially reduced spatial coverage. More importantly, regional models revealed distinct advantages in clinical sensitivity. For example, the combined hippocampus-amygdala model demonstrated enhanced ability to differentiate CN, MCI, and dementia using PAD. These findings highlight a dissociation between predictive accuracy and clinical relevance in brain age modeling and suggest that region-specific brain age estimates may provide more sensitive biomarkers for detecting early and localized neurodegenerative changes than conventional global brain age measures.
