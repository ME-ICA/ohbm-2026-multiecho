Introduction

Advancements in Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) and machine learning research have enabled researchers to explore new questions at the intersection of computer vision, neuroscience, and cognitive science. One key question is how the way the human visual system processes information is related to perceived image similarity – are pairs of images that are perceived to be more similar also more similar in their induced brain activity patterns in the visual system? While prior research relating brain activity to human similarity judgements exists, a full picture is yet to be realized.1 In this work, we investigate the relationships between human similarity judgements, the similarity judgements made by AI models (vision, caption-based language models, tag-based word frequency methods), and similarity of brain responses across early and late visual regions. Our goals are to gain a better understanding of the shared variance of human similarity judgments, AI models and the brain’s visual system representations. 

Methods

In this study, we analyzed 100 images from the BOLD5000 dataset which yielded 4950 unique image pairs.2 Examples of pairs of images can be seen in Figure 1A. For each pair of images, we have three sources of similarity: human ratings, AI models, and predicted brain activity, see Figure 1B. For human ratings, each pair of images was rated by 3-6 raters on a 0-6 similarity scale (higher = more similar), and results were averaged across raters. For AI models, we worked with 573 vision models, 64 caption-based language models, and 9 tag-based word frequency methods. Vision model similarity was calculated per image pair as the cosine similarity between embeddings form the final layer of pretrained PyTorch models such as ResNet and VGG19. For the caption-based language models, human-provided image captions were embedded using models such as BERT, averaged across captions, and compared between pairs of images using cosine similarity. Finally, for the tag-based word frequency models, human-provided image tags were compared across image pairs using word frequency methods including TF-IDF, ROUGE score, and BM25.3 For brain activity, we used voxelwise encoding models (trained using 8 individuals’ data in the Natural Scenes Dataset) to predict fMRI responses in 7 early visual regions (V1d, V1v, V2d, V2v, V3d, V3v, hV4) and 12 late visual regions (FFA1, FFA2, OFA, EBA, FBA2, PPA, RSC, OPA, OWFA, VWFA1, VWFA2, mfswords).4 Brain image similarity was assessed by correlating the encoding model’s predicted activity across voxels within a given region (or set of regions when focused on early vs late) and averaging this correlation across 8 individuals. We correlated each brain region’s similarity structure with human judgements, vision models, caption-based language models and tag-based word frequency methods. 

Results

Late visual regions are significantly more correlated with human judgements than early visual regions, and visual region’s order in the hierarchy was strongly correlated with the level of agreement with human judgements with a spearman correlation r =0.718 (p<.001).5 Some of the highest correlation areas include mid-fusiform sulcus word area with r=0.406 (p<.001), parahippocampal place area r=0.401 (p<.001), and ventral word form area 2 r=0.391(p<.001) as shown in Figure 2A. When averaging together the results of all the early visual regions versus the late visual regions, we see that there is an r=0.360 correlation (p<.001) between human judgement and late visual regions as compared to early visual r = 0.275 (p<.001). In addition, when correlating the early and late visual regions to the AI vision, caption-based language, and tag-based word frequency analysis models, we see significant differences in their behavior as shown in Figure 2B. For AI vision models, there is no significant difference in correlations between the early and late region means with r = 0.179 vs r = 0.184 (p=0.620). Similarly, with tag-based models, we see no significant difference between means of r=0.172 for the early regions and r=0.183 for the late regions (p=0.333). However, with AI language models we see a significant difference between the late and early region means with r = 0.281 vs r = 0.187 (p<.001), respectively. This suggests that not only is human similarity perception grounded in semantic representations that are found in late visual regions, but that language models uniquely capture this semantic structure compared to vision and tag-based models. 

To further investigate the contribution of semantic versus visual features to human similarity perception, we conducted partial correlation analyses as seen in Figure 2C. These analyses were designed to isolate the remaining variance in the human-brain representational similarity relationship after removing AI-based model similarity that represents semantic (language or tag models) or visual information (vision models). In late semantic regions, controlling for language model similarity reduced the brain-human correlation by 54.4%, compared to only 29.7% when controlling for vision model similarity (p<.001 for all). In contrast, early visual regions showed no significant difference between controlling for language model similarity with 48.2% reduction versus when controlling for vision model similarity 46.0% reduction (p=0.552). Of note, the early visual regions' brain-human correlation is more reduced when controlling for vision models compared to the late semantic regions brain-human correlation when controlling for vision models, which is in alignment with the assumption that the vision models contain information reflected in early vs late visual areas. Tag-based models explained significantly less variance with a 14.5%, 19.9% reduction for late and early visual regions (p<.001 for all). These findings demonstrate that the alignment between late semantic brain regions and human similarity judgments is driven primarily, but not exclusively, by shared semantic representations captured by language models, rather than visual features captured by vision models or labels from tag-based word frequency methods. 

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that human similarity perception is more grounded in semantic representations processed in the late visual cortex rather than low-level visual features in early visual regions. Interestingly, about half of the original brain-human alignment in late visual areas cannot be explained by semantic similarity, indicating that there may be more than just semantic information driving both the brain representations and human similarity judgements. Future work will explore what these features are, which could have implications for developing AI systems that better mimic human perception.
