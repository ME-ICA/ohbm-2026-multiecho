Introduction

To understand the neuroscience underlying psychopathology, we need valid measurements of brain function. Group atlases for functional connectivity allow for efficient comparisons, but they fail to account for inter-individual variability, a problem that personalized methods address.1 Personalized functional network approaches classify points in the brain as belonging to certain networks for each person. Using these methods, researchers have shown that functional networks are dominated by stable group and individual factors, with network topography being largely stable across time and cognitive states.2 One key consideration when choosing a personalized method is whether or not the method assumes that each point in the brain belongs to exactly one network. Previous work suggests that there are regions in the brain known as hubs that are critical for communication between networks, and as such, can be considered to belong to multiple networks.3 Therefore, it is advantageous to choose a personalized method that allows for points in the brain to belong to multiple networks, as we do in the current work.

Further, researchers have obtained mixed results on the predictive utility of functional connectivity based on personalized versus group approaches. Some have found larger effects for functional connectivity based on personalized approaches, whereas others have found larger effects using group approaches.4 The larger effects sometimes found using group approaches may be explained by effects of mixing in connectivity from other networks, or of noise introduced by personalized approaches. Here, we assess the predictive utility and validity of group and personalized approaches by 1) comparing the effect sizes between different ways of quantifying connectivity and clinical metrics; and 2) controlling for spatial features of brain networks when examining the association between functional connectivity and clinical metrics.

Methods

Participants: 324 teens ages 13-16 were in included in the final sample. The final sample consisted of teens who have complete depression, ruminative coping style, sensitivity to punishment, sensitivity to reward, and neuroimaging data as of June 18, 2025. Neuroimaging data were considered complete for a given participant if they have a T1-weighted structural scan, at least five minutes worth of degrees of freedom remaining following confound regression from the combined functional scans, and had a mean framewise displacement less than 0.5mm.

Assessment: The Beck Depression Inventory - II (BDI-II) was used to assess depression severity over the preceding two weeks. The 10-item Ruminative Responses Scale (RRS) is being used to assess tendency to use a ruminative coping style. The Sensitivity to Punishment and Sensitivity to Reward Questionnaire (SPSRQ) was used to assess sensitivity to punishments and rewards.

Neuroimaging: Three functional scans were concatenated after processing for use in all analyses: the Chatroom Interact task, and the two runs of the Monetary Incentive Delay task. The Chatroom Interact task uses a slice-accelerated multiband EPI sequence (multiband acceleration factor: 2. GRAPPA acceleration factor: 2) covering 64 axial slices (voxel size=2.0mm3; TR=2050ms; TE=25ms; FOV=208x208mm; Matrix=104x104; Flip Angle=76 degrees). The Monetary Incentive Delay (MID) task also uses a slice-accelerated multiband EPI sequence with identical scan parameters. A total of 390 volumes were collected for the Chatroom Interact task, and 289 for each of the runs of MID.

Magnetic resonance imaging data were processed using fMRIPrep version 23.2.0, Connectome Workbench, and R packages. T1-weighted images were corrected for intensity non-uniformity, and skull-stripped. Functional images were slice-timed corrected, resampled into their native space, and then projected into the fsLR-32k space (resampled to 10k per hemispheres for computational purposes) for surface analysis. The pre-processed resting state then underwent simultaneous nuisance regression (translation (x, y, z), rotation (yaw, pitch, roll), and global signal; derivatives, second powers, and second powers of the derivatives of translation, rotation, and global signal; outlier flagging with DVARS (dual cutoff method); and .01 Hz HPF using 12 discrete cosine transform bases). DVARS was estimated on otherwise nuisance regressed data to reduce redundancy in the covariates.

To construct individual-level network engagement maps, a Bayesian brain mapping technique was used (Figure 1).5 This method employs a hierarchical Bayesian framework with priors on spatial functional topography. The priors mitigate noise to produce reliable maps of individual vertex-level network engagement and membership. The Yeo17 atlas was used as a template to guide the prior such that it was assumed that there are 17 networks, and that they should resemble the topographical layout of the Yeo17 atlas while being allowed to overlap. We identified vertices belonging to each of the 17 networks by performing the Bayesian equivalent of a hypothesis test at every vertex based on the estimated engagement level and its posterior standard deviation.

Statistics: Effect size comparisons were done by comparing the correlations between ways of quantifying connectivity (group, personalized, and intersection) and the clinical metrics (depression, ruminative coping style, and sensitivity to punishment/reward) with Steigler's Z-test. The group approach used the canonical Yeo17 atlas; the personalized approach used the hierarchical Bayesian model results onto the Yeo17 atlas; and the intersection approach took the vertices belonging to each individual's personalized functional networks and the Yeo17 atlas. We also conducted regressions, with clinical metrics as the dependent variables. Models included connectivity and spatial features, together and alone.

Results

In the current study, we investigated a series of hypotheses pertaining to the predictive utility versus validity of personalized and group-based approaches to quantifying functional connectivity. The effect size comparison analyses showed at the α = .01 level that two functional connectivity metrics differed in the magnitude of their association with various clinical metrics when they were quantified with the group versus intersection approaches, five differed in the group versus personalized comparison, and thirteen differed in the personalized versus intersection comparison (Figure 2). These results did not survive FDR correction. On average, the magnitude of the associations between the intersection estimates and the clinical outcomes were larger than the magnitude of the associations between the group estimates and the clinical outcomes such that the average difference of their absolute values was 0.005. On average, the magnitude of the associations between the group estimates and the clinical outcomes were smaller than the magnitude of the associations between the personalized estimates and the clinical outcomes such that the average difference of their absolute values was 0.003. On average, the magnitude of the associations between the personalized estimates and the clinical outcomes were smaller than the magnitude of the associations between the intersection estimates and the clinical outcomes such that the average difference of their absolute values was 0.002.

To derive spatial covariates for the multiple regression models, we performed singular value decomposition on the engagement maps for each network, resulting in 17 separate singular value decompositions. To reduce background noise, the engagement maps were first thresholded such that the engagements for each subject that were less than two times the standard deviation of the prior mean map for that network were changed to zero. Components that explained at least 1% of the variance in their respective maps were retained for the regressions. Similarly, we performed singular value decomposition on the group-based functional connectivity estimates to reduce the dimensionality, and retained components that explained at least 1% of the variance in functional connectivity.

Using the 1% variance explained cutoff for inclusion of components from the SVDs, 10 functional connectivity components were retained, and 115 spatial components were retained.

The multiple regression analyses showed that neither functional connectivity nor spatial features of networks explained a significant portion of variance in depression (Connectivity: F(10, 313) = 0.35, p = 0.97, R2 = 0.011, R2adj = −0.021; Spatial: F(115, 208) = 0.90, p = 0.741, R2 = 0.331, R2adj = −0.038), ruminative coping style (Connectivity: F(10, 313) = 0.69, p = 0.738, R2 = 0.021, R2adj = −0.010; Spatial: F(115, 208) = 0.74, p = 0.961, R2 = 0.291, R2adj = −0.101), or sensitivity to punishment (Connectivity: F(10, 313) = 1.05, p = 0.398, R2 = 0.033, R2adj = 0.002; Spatial: F(115, 208) = 1.20, p = 0.129, R2 = 0.400, R2adj = 0.066). Spatial features of networks did explain a significant portion of variance in sensitivity to reward (F(115, 208) = 1.69, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.483, R2adj = 0.196), while functional connectivity did not (F(10, 313) = 0.96, p = 0.482, R2 = 0.030, R2adj = −0.001). Functional connectivity also did not explain additional variance in sensitivity to reward above and beyond spatial features (F(198, 208) = 0.84, p = 0.588). Using the 2% variance explained cutoff for inclusion of components from the SVDs, 5 functional connectivity components were retained, and 28 spatial components were retained. Results using these features were analogous to those explained in detail above.

Conclusion

In the current study, we proposed that a personalized approach may result in more valid estimates of functional connectivity, while both personalized and group approaches may have good predictive utility, depending on the outcome of interest. To begin to assess this idea, we compared the predictive utility of three different methods for quantifying functional connectivity, and tested if functional connectivity derived from a group atlas explains additional variance in clinical outcomes above and beyond spatial features of the networks. We found that, after controlling for multiple comparisons, there was no difference in the predictive utility of various ways of quantifying functional connectivity. Further, we found that, among the four clinical metrics tested, only the spatial features of networks explain a significant portion of variance in sensitivity to reward.

These results suggest that, with respect to the four clinical outcome metrics used in the current study, it does not matter how we quantify connectivity. It is important to note that, generally speaking, functional connectivity is more strongly associated with cognitive metrics than clinical metrics, so it will be important to conduct similar analyses in a large dataset with subjects who have cognitive measures. In such a dataset, we may gain greater clarity on the nature of what is useful about group and personalized estimates of connectivity. In the current dataset, we are not able to conclude if what is useful in the sense of predictive utility about group-based estimates of functional connectivity is their tendency to mix signals across networks, nor are we able to determine if personalized estimates have excessive noise weakening effects. Our results do suggest that future studies should also consider quantifying spatial features of brain networks, as these demonstrate the greatest predictive utility in the current study.
