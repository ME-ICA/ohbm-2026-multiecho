Introduction

Humans navigate environments where outcome contingencies are hidden according to latent states and can change without warning. To successfully adapt to such situational demands, humans need to infer these latent states, detect when transitions occur, and flexibly adjust behavior. Evidence suggests that transient arousal-related neuromodulatoryfluctuations originating from the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC-NE) system play a role in driving dynamic changes in internal brain states that support behavioral flexibility^1. Arousal is hereby thought to enhance global information processing efficiency and reduce the influence of prior expectations and biases on decision-making. Specifically, the LC responds to unexpected, surprising events by projecting noradrenergic signals^2, throughout cortical and subcortical networks involved in learning and executive control, triggering brain state changes for adaptation. However, direct fMRI evidence that the human LC shows heightened responses to latent state transitions, and which brain networks are recruited during transition detection, remains limited. Addressing this gap requires simultaneous measurement of activity in LC and other brain areas, and arousal during a task that require detecting changes in hidden environmental states.

The present work is part of a larger study combining structural and functional brain imaging, pupillometric recordings, and a latent state retrieval task. Here, we focus specifically on the functional brain imaging data acquired during the task. The purpose of this work is to examine how the brain and neuromodulatory systems (e.g., LC-NE) respond to arousing events that signal environmental change. We examined whether the brain and LC-NE system show heightened responses to feedback that signals latent state transitions compared to feedback during stable task periodsusing a retrieval task with simultaneous pupillometry-functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI).

Methods

In the MRI scanner, participants (N = 52) with no history of psychiatric disorders performed a latent state retrieval task while pupil diameter was simultaneously recorded. On each trial, they were instructed to move a colored shield from a random start position to a spot at which they predict an attack within a circular arena. Three shield colors were used, each associated with a specific attack location that participants learned through trial-by-trial feedback (indicated by a red circle). The task involved three distinct latent states that alternated spontaneously without warning throughout the session, with each state defining a different set of color-location mappings for the attacks. Participants had to infer the current latent state from recent feedback and adjust their predictions on appropriate locations accordingly. Correct predictions were rewarded monetarily. Participants operated the joystick with the hand corresponding to their dominant arm. The data from 48 of the 52 participants was included in the univariate fMRI analysis. Two participants were excluded for excessive head motion (framewise displacement > 0.8 mm in >5% of TRs) and two for not understanding the task.

MRI data were acquired on a 3 T MAGNETOM scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). A whole-brain T1-weighted MPRAGE structural images (voxel sizes: 1 mm isotropic, TE = 3.02 ms, TR = 1900.0 ms) were acquired as an anatomical scan for image registration. The fMRI acquisition parameters were tuned to enable high-resolution imaging of the LC, additional brainstem nuclei, and the orbitofrontal/dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Aneuromelanin sensitive Gradient Echo MRI sequence (voxel sizes: 0.8 × 0.8 × 1.0 mm3 anisotropic, TE = 5.97 ms, TR = 45.0 ms) was used to provide structural localization of LC. High-resolution functional scans (voxel sizes: 2 mm isotropic, TE = 30.00 ms, TR = 1800.0 ms; multiband EPI (slice acceleration factor 2)) were acquired as part of the protocol. During the latent state retrieval task, each subject had a total of 1476 measurements spaced across 6 runs of 246measurements each, resulting in 6 runs, each lasting approximately 7.38 minutes.

fMRI data preprocessing and statistical modeling were performed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12; Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, University College London, UK, 2012) and the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) software package (version 2.5.0; http://stnava.github.io/ANTs/). Preprocessing of the functional images included slice-timing correction, realignment and unwarping, and spatial smoothing with a 3 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. To improve the reliability of functional LC analyses, structural and functional images were normalized to MNI space (MNI ICBM152 nonlinear asymmetric template), using a multistage ANTs registration workflow, following Yi et al.^3.

We constructed a general linear model (GLM) with feedback onsets as a primary event regressor, parametrically modulated by type of transition (+1 for transition-related feedbacks and two subsequent feedbacks after it, -1 otherwise). Response onsets included parametric modulators for stimulus color (not reported here) and joystick activity (calculated as Euclidean distance between trial starting and ending shield positions), serving as a positive control for motor activity.To account for physiological noise, we included regressors derived from simultaneously recorded physiological signalsusing the breathing belt and pulse oximeter. When these data were unavailable, noise regressors derived from white matter, gray matter, and cerebrospinal fluid signals were used. We conducted whole-brain analysis and region-of-interest(ROI) analyses focusing specifically on the LC using small-volume correction to test whether LC activity differentiates transition feedback from regular feedback. For the LC ROI analysis, we used the LC meta-mask developed by Dahl et al.^4 (https://osf.io/sf2ky/).

Results

Whole-brain statistical maps were thresholded at p < 0.001 uncorrected at the voxel level, with cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < 0.05. Given lower signal-to-noise ratios in the brainstem, for the LC ROI, a voxel-level threshold of p < 0.005 was applied, with cluster-level FWE correction at p < 0.05.

Whole-brain analyses revealed several significant clusters showing greater BOLD response to transition feedback compared to regular feedback, overlapping with established regions of the salience network. The strongest activation was observed in the left angular gyrus (peak MNI: -33, -62, 44, T = 13.21; k = 208480 voxels, pFWE < 0.001). Additional significant clusters were found in left supplementary motor cortex (peak MNI: -3, 17, 51; T = 12.07; k = 153869 voxels; pFWE < 0.001), right cerebellum exterior (peak MNI: 29, -62, -33; T = 11.81; k = 13143 voxels; pFWE < 0.001), left anterior insula (peak MNI: -31, 22, -1; T = 11.15; k = 5189 voxels; pFWE < 0.001), right middle cingulate gyrus (peak MNI: 5, 4, 28; T = 8.17; k = 1402 voxels; pFWE < 0.001), left fusiform gyrus (peak MNI: -28, -44, -14; T = 6.55; k = 492 voxels; pFWE < 0.001). Activations were detected bilaterally in the caudate (right: peak MNI: 16, 2, 17; T = 8.45; k = 2766 voxels; pFWE < 0.001; left: peak MNI: -16, 7, 16; T = 8.37; k = 2438 voxels; pFWE < 0.001) and in middle temporal gyrus (right: peak MNI: 60, -37, -16; T = 10.34; k = 7166 voxels; pFWE < 0.001; left: peak MNI: -63, -44, -16; T = 6.99; k = 7531 voxels; pFWE < 0.001). Small-volume corrected analysis within the anatomically defined LC mask revealed no significant activation for the transition > regular feedback contrast (pFWE > 0.05).

Significant clusters showed furthermore BOLD responses during response events that scaled parametrically with joystick movement distance. The strongest activation was observed in the left precentral gyrus (peak MNI: -37, -24, 53; T = 9.50; k = 97163 voxels; pFWE < 0.001) and right precentral gyrus (peak MNI: 40, -6, 57; T = 8.99; k = 5600 voxels; pFWE < 0.001). Additional significant clusters were found in right celebral white matter (peak MNI: 31, -40, 53; T = 7.81; k = 7474 voxels; pFWE < 0.001), right middle temporal gyrus (peak MNI: 43, -63, 4; T = 6.94; k = 3936 voxels; pFWE < 0.001), and left thalamus proper (peak MNI: -17, -25, 8; T = 6.12; k = 574 voxels; pFWE < 0.001). Activations were detected bilaterally in the putamen (right: peak MNI: 26, 9, 2; T = 6.28; k = 1039 voxels; pFWE < 0.001; left: peak MNI: -26, 4, 5; T = 7.10; k = 1366 voxels; pFWE < 0.001), cerebellum exterior (right: peak MNI: 15, -63, -49; T = 5.20; k = 307 voxels; pFWE = 0.008; left: peak MNI: -27, -44, -26; T = 5.72; k = 1535 voxels; pFWE < 0.001), and parietal operculum (right: peak MNI: 39, -31, 20; T = 5.21; k = 710 voxels; pFWE < 0.001; left: peak MNI: -47, -26, 18; T = 7.73; k = 2154 voxels; pFWE < 0.001).

Conclusion

As expected, brain networks differentiate between transition and non-transition feedback. The pattern of activation during transition feedback suggests engagement of distributed networks involved in detecting, learning from, and adapting to environmental state changes.

Specifically, detection of surprising transition feedback engaged the salience network, represented by activity in the anterior insula, a region in bidirectional connectivity with the LC that initiates control signals to regulate behavior in response to salient events^5. Greater right angular gyrus activation during transition feedback suggests its contribution to updating predictions by aligning unexpected feedback with the brain's internal model of hidden latent states. Activation in the right lateral cerebellum suggests its involvement in comparing predicted and actual outcomes and updating to support optimal motor and cognitive prediction. Activation in the right middle cingulate cortex, a region involved in behavioral error analysis and feedback processing, together with nearby activations in the left supplementary motor area and bilateral caudate, points to a network for behavioral updating following unexpected feedback. The robust bilateral caudate activation is consistent with its role in behavioral learning and updating of state-action associations based on reward prediction error. Greater BOLD in both bilateral middle temporal gyrus and left fusiform gyrus suggests these regions support enhanced color-cue processing for latent state decoding and rapid mapping to latent contingencies. As expected for the positive control contrast, the distance of joystick movement parametrically modulated BOLD responses in bilateral sensorimotor regions, including the precentral gyrus, putamen, cerebellum, parietal operculum, and thalamus, confirming the successful detection of task-related motor activity.

Despite our hypothesis that phasic LC responses would differentiate state transitions, small-volume corrected analysis within the anatomically defined LC did not reveal significant BOLD signal differences between transition and regular feedback. This null outcome may be due to persistent challenges in human LC-fMRI assessment, such as technical limitations (suboptimal coregistration, LC masks failing to capture individual variability) or physiological limitations (small nucleus size, remaining noise after cleaning), which can lead to undetectable BOLD changes despite arousal-induced activity. An alternative interpretation suggests that cortical and subcortical systems can detect transitions through the integration of multiple inputs beyond phasic LC signals, or that transition detection involves distributed processing across multiple neuromodulatory systems.
