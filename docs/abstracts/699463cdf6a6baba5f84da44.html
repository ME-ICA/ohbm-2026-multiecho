Introduction

Schizotypy is a dimensional construct spanning subclinical traits in the general population to schizophrenia spectrum disorders at its extreme1. The triple network model posits that schizophrenia reflects dysregulated interactions among the salience network (SN), default mode network (DMN) and executive control network (ECN)2. Resting-state fMRI findings in schizotypy broadly align with this account, suggesting increased coupling between DMN–SN and DMN–ECN with higher trait levels3. However, fMRI-derived resting-state networks are temporally limited. EEG microstates, spontaneous quasi-stable topographies, offer a complementary, temporally resolved index of large-scale network dynamics4. Evidence linking microstate dynamics to schizotypy remains sparse. Here, we test whether schizotypal traits (SPQ-BR) correlate with resting-state microstate coverage and transition probabilities, and whether these patterns resemble those reported in schizophrenia. To benchmark neurophysiological validity, we also assess P3b amplitude in an auditory oddball task as a marker of salience processing. We hypothesise that higher SPQ total scores will be associated with greater coverage of microstate C (putative SN) and microstate F (putative DMN), reduced coverage of microstate D (putative ECN), and increased D-to-C transitioning; additionally, higher SPQ scores are expected to predict smaller P3b amplitudes.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-one adults participated (M age = 21.97, SD = 6.32; 4 male; 2 left-handed). One participant was excluded from ERP analyses due to insufficient valid trials; six were excluded from microstate analyses due to failing data-quality criteria.

Tasks

For the resting state data acquisition, participants fixated a central cross in a dimly lit room and were instructed to remain still during 5 min of continuous recording.

Afterwards, they performed a three-stimulus auditory oddball task, comprising two blocks (vocal; spectral-rotated vocal). Each block contained 336 trials: standards (75%, 350 Hz, 150 ms), targets (12.5%, 500 Hz, 150 ms), and social oddballs (12.5%, 550 ms neutral “aaa” vocalisations; spectrally rotated in the spectral block). Inter-stimulus interval was 1000 ms. Sequences were pseudorandomised (≥5 standards after each target; ≥4 standard/target trials after each oddball). Participants responded to targets via keyboard (down arrow). Block order was counterbalanced; a 66-trial practice preceded the task. Stimuli were presented in Presentation® on a 120 Hz monitor (~50 cm viewing distance).

Schizotypy traits were assessed with the SPQ-BR (32 items; five-point Likert), yielding total and three-factor scores (interpersonal, cognitive-perceptual, disorganised). SPQ total was analysed dimensionally.

EEG acquisition and analysis

EEG was recorded with a 64-channel BioSemi ActiveTwo system (10–20 montage) using CMS as the online reference. Data were sampled at 2048 Hz (anti-aliasing low-pass: 3 dB at 400 Hz). EOG was recorded from bilateral outer canthi and infraorbital right electrodes. Offsets were kept within ±40 µV. 

Resting-state data were preprocessed in EEGLAB/ERPLAB (MATLAB R2022b): downsampled to 512 Hz; filtered (1–35 Hz); continuous segments with elevated 15–35 Hz power were rejected; residual artefacts were removed using sliding-window detection (200 ms window, 50 ms step; ±400 µV). Data were average-referenced and cleaned with ICA (ICLabel; components >70% ocular or muscle removed). Data were epoched into 2 s segments. Microstates were computed in MicrostateLab using GFP-peak k-means clustering (100 restarts). Individual maps were labelled by spatial correlation with Custo et al. (2017) templates5; topographic outliers were detected via MDS (one outlier excluded). Temporal parameters were derived via backfitting; coverage indexed percentage time per class and delta transition probabilities indexed transitions relative to chance.

Oddball data were downsampled to 256 Hz; filtered (0.1–35 Hz); bad channels were removed/interpolated; average reference applied. ICA (ICLabel) components >90% ocular were removed. Artefacts were rejected using a ±400 µV sliding-window criterion and a 300 µV moving-window peak-to-peak criterion. Data were epoched −200 to 850 ms. Correct target trials required responses 150–850 ms; correct standard/oddball trials required no response. A minimum of 30 valid trials per condition was required (one exclusion). P3b was quantified at P1/CPz/Pz/POz/P2 (250–500 ms) using positive-area integration; a target–standard difference score was computed.

Statistics

Four bivariate linear regressions tested SPQ total as a predictor of microstate C/D/F coverage and D-to-C transitioning; significant effects were followed by regressions on occurrence and mean duration. Bonferroni correction controlled multiple comparisons. The SPQ total and the P3b difference were correlated using Pearson's correlation. Analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.1; rstats, car).

Results

Microstates

The shared variance—indexing the similarity of scalp topographies—between our mean maps and the Custo template maps5 was generally high (M = 91.42%, SD = 6.74%). Shared variance ranged from 79% to 98% across maps. The seven mean-sorted topographic maps (A–G) we derived are shown in Figure 1.

The total explained variance, calculated by summing the individual explained variance across all seven maps, was modest (M = 58.74%, SD = 7.35%).

SPQ Scores

Participants’ SPQ total scores conformed to a Gaussian distribution. The mean SPQ total score across the sample was 101.52 (SD = 16.09; range = 61–131). Regarding specific dimensions, mean scores for the cognitive-perceptual, interpersonal, and disorganised dimensions were 46.97, 30.65, and 23.90, respectively.

Regression Analysis

Class C coverage, class D coverage, and transitions from D to C were not significantly predicted by SPQ total scores (p > .0125). However, SPQ total scores significantly predicted microstate class F coverage, F(1, 23) = 10.01, p = .004, adjusted R² = .27.

SPQ total scores were positively related to microstate F occurrence, F(1, 23) = 5.69, p = .026, adjusted R² = .16. Although this did not meet the corrected significance threshold (p < .0125), it nonetheless represents a strong trend. No significant relationship was observed for microstate duration.

ERP Results

To test whether a biomarker observed in schizophrenia extends to our sample, a Pearson correlation was computed between the P3 amplitude difference and SPQ total scores. We found no statistically significant association between SPQ total score and P3 amplitude difference, r(28) = −0.26, p = .798.

Conclusion

We present a novel study characterising associations between EEG microstate dynamics and schizotypal traits in the general population. Higher schizotypal loading was associated with increased coverage of microstate F, consistent with greater engagement of the default mode network (DMN). Exploratory analysis suggested this effect was primarily driven by increased occurrence (i.e., more frequent activations) rather than prolonged duration, indicating a higher rate of DMN recruitment rather than increased state stability. In contrast, we did not observe significant associations between schizotypal traits and microstate C or D coverage, nor D-to-C transitioning, suggesting that—within this subclinical range—alterations in salience and executive control network dynamics, as inferred from microstates C and D, are not robustly expressed. Complementing these results, we found no relationship between schizotypal traits and the P3b (target–standard) amplitude difference, consistent with relatively intact salience-driven context updating in this sample. 

Together, the microstate and ERP results support a model in which early, subclinical schizotypal expression is preferentially linked to increased engagement of internally oriented network states. In contrast, more overt salience/executive dysregulation may emerge later and be associated with greater symptom severity. Multimodal follow-up work combining EEG microstates with resting-state fMRI would be valuable to test whether increased microstate F engagement corresponds to altered DMN coupling and/or reduced DMN–ECN anticorrelation using seed-based or network-level connectivity analyses.
