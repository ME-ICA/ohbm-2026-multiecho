Introduction

The Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) is a landmark multisite study providing longitudinal neuroimaging and clinical data across the cognitive aging and Alzheimer’s disease spectrum. ^1 While ADNI structural MRI and PET data are widely used (6000+ papers), resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) remains underutilized (~150 papers) due to substantial heterogeneity in acquisition protocols, scanner vendors, metadata completeness, and imaging session–clinical data mismatches. These challenges complicate preprocessing, quality control, and reproducible downstream analysis, often resulting in studies that rely on small, selectively curated subsets of the available data.

To address these barriers, we developed a transparent, reproducible, end-to-end pipeline for curating, preprocessing, and quality controlling ADNI rs-fMRI data across ADNI 2, GO, and 3 study phases. Our goal is to enable large-scale, reproducible use of ADNI rs-fMRI data for biomarker discovery and machine learning applications, while operating within ADNI data-use constraints.

Methods

We implemented an eight-stage rs-fMRI data curation and preprocessing workflow using a combination of open-source neuroimaging tools and custom quality-control procedures. Raw DICOM imaging and clinical data were accessed via the ADNI LONI Image and Data Archive and converted to Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) format using Clinica. ^2 Post-conversion checks identified scanner- and metadata-related inconsistencies using expert-defined heuristics targeting scan duration, repetition time, brain coverage, phase-encoding field of view, and receiver coil type.

Automated image quality assessment was performed using MRIQC, generating 16 structural and functional image quality metrics (IQMs). ^3 We applied a robust, data-driven outlier detection framework to define exclusion thresholds without relying on arbitrary cutoffs. ^4 Sessions with any outlying IQM were excluded, and BOLD images were removed if the corresponding T1-weighted image failed quality criteria.

Remaining sessions were preprocessed using containerized fMRIPrep, producing volumetric, surface-based, and grayordinates outputs. ^5 ADNI-specific preprocessing failures (e.g., missing phase-encoding metadata) were systematically identified, categorized, and addressed through targeted reprocessing strategies. Final quality control incorporated motion-based exclusion criteria and FreeSurfer Euler number–based structural quality assessment, including site-specific thresholds. All scripts, configuration files, and logs are openly available to enable full reproducibility  (https://github.com/saigerutherford/ADNI_fMRI_protocol).

Results

Starting from 1,153 participants and 2,893 imaging sessions with downloaded DICOM data, successive quality-control stages resulted in a final curated dataset of 913 participants and 2,026 rs-fMRI sessions. Major attrition sources included BIDS conversion failures, scanner-parameter violations, automated MRIQC outlier detection, preprocessing failures, and final motion and structural quality thresholds.

The pipeline successfully harmonized rs-fMRI data across more than 60 sites and multiple scanner vendors, producing BIDS-derivatives–compliant outputs including volumetric MNI-space images, cortical surface data, grayordinates files, and standardized confound regressors. All inclusion and exclusion decisions were recorded in machine-readable tables, enabling transparent auditing and sensitivity analyses. The resulting dataset represents the largest openly reproducible, quality-controlled rs-fMRI resource derived from ADNI to date.

Conclusion

We present a scalable, fully reproducible pipeline for curating and preprocessing ADNI resting-state fMRI data that addresses longstanding barriers to its widespread use. By combining standardized preprocessing with rigorous, data-driven quality control and comprehensive documentation, this workflow enables large-scale rs-fMRI analyses while preserving transparency and reproducibility. The curated dataset and accompanying codebase provide a robust foundation for future studies of functional connectivity, biomarker development, and machine learning in Alzheimer’s disease and cognitive aging.
