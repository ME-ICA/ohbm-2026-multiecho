Introduction

The Julich-Brain atlas1 provides probabilistic maps of cortical areas and subcortical nuclei derived from histological sections from a total 23 postmortem brains with ten brains per area. It is anchored to the MNI-Colin27 template via volumetric alignment, enabling comparisons across studies in a common reference space for fMRI activations, connectivity networks, gene expression, and structural analyses. A Maximum probability Map (MPM) is used to assign each volume to a single structure1. However, volume-based intersubject alignment is suboptimal for cortex2. The goal of this project is to leverage FreeSurfer’s surface-based alignment3 to create a more accurate probabilistic atlas of human neocortical cytoarchitecture. This abstract summarizes our progress to date.

Methods

We obtained 21 of the 23 Julich-Brain postmortem datasets from the EBRAINS Infrastructure (https://www.ebrains.eu/tools/human-brain-atlas). The intensity images used for the analysis  (Fig 1.A1) are the histological slices with intensity inverted and stacked into a 3D volume. The corresponding cytoarchitectonic labels (Fig. 1.A2) are also reassembled into the same volume space. Using FreeSurfer4, we reconstructed the surface between cortical white and gray matter (known as the “white” surface; yellow line in Fig 1.A3) with a contrast-insensitive method5. The white surface was then projected outward 1.5 mm into the cortex (red line, Fig. 1.A3), and labels were sampled by intersecting with this projected surface. This process was repeated for all 21 subjects (Fig 1.B). Next, each subject’s cortical surface was registered3 to the fsaverage template, a widely used surface-based reference analogous to the volume-based MNI-Colin27. The cytoarchitectonic labels were mapped into this surface space, yielding 137 labels per hemisphere. An MPM segmentation was then constructed by assigning the most frequently occurring label at each point on the surface. While not all labels are present in every subject, each label exists in at least 10 subjects allowing for a dense labeling of cortex with all 137 regions represented in fsaverage space. Finally, to test applicability to in vivo data, the atlas was reverse-mapped to a subject’s native cortical surface and projected into its volume space.

Results

Figure 1.A3 illustrates the white and projected surfaces on histological volumes, while Fig. 1.A4–A5 show cytoarchitectonic labels on the left hemisphere surface. Individual renderings are shown in Fig. 1.B for each of the 21 subjects, and the group-level dense MPM atlas across both hemispheres in Fig. 1.C. Note that different combinations and numbers of areas have been mapped in the 21 brains. Application to an in vivo T1-weighted MRI is shown in Fig. 2: FreeSurfer surfaces (2.B–C) were aligned to fsaverage, labeled using the MPM atlas, and projected back into subject volume space (2.D).

Conclusion

Our initial results demonstrate that it is feasible to reconstruct cortical surfaces from ex vivo histological images, project cytoarchitectonic labels onto the surface, and generate a probabilistic surface-based atlas in fsaverage space. The atlas can be mapped to in vivo subjects, enabling computation of label-specific metrics (surface area, volume, thickness) and integration with functional or molecular modalities (fMRI, PET, DTI). Ongoing challenges include refining surface placement, sampling labels from volume to surface, enforcing topological constraints on labels, and further validation of labeling accuracy. Importantly, surface-based rendering offers improved visualization and quality control of cytoarchitectonic label boundaries compared with volumetric approaches.
