Introduction

Across the lifespan, the human brain undergoes profound physiological change1 s that shape neural dynamics and, consequently, cognitive abilities. Understanding how aging affects the brain’s functional mechanisms is essential for characterizing typical developmental trajectories and for improving our knowledge of age-related disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, dementia, and mild cognitive impairment. However, physiological alterations alone do not fully account for cognitive decline, particularly in older adults, where inter-individual variability in cognitive performance becomes increasingly pronounced.


Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) has enabled the study of spontaneous neural fluctuations
through the Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD) signal, revealing how functional activity evolves
with age. In particular, functional connectomes estimated via Pearson’s correlation show an inverted
U-shape trajectory of the global connectivity along the lifespan, with a peak in the fourth decade of life2.
In addition, nodal strength analyses revealed a system-level reorganization along the sensory-association axis2. Recently, the effective connectomes estimated via regression Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM) have been investigated in the case of typical development, unveiling peaks around the firsts decade of life, resembling the structural changes more than functional connectomes3.

Despite extensive research, the patterns of brain interactions that are most strongly linked to aging and cognitive deterioration remain insufficiently understood. Beyond traditional correlation-based functional connectivity, directional connectivity measures offer additional insights into how information flows across brain regions4. Such directed connectivity estimation techniques can capture asymmetries in communication, highlight hierarchical disruptions, and reveal mechanistic alterations that may not be detectable through undirected connectivity alone.

Methods

1000brains cohort.5 The population-based study is a subsample of the 10-year follow-up cohort of the
Heinz Nixdorf Recall Study. Eligibility for MRI measurements (coronary arterystents, surgical implants
or prostheses in head or trunk, tattoos or permanent make-up on the head) is the only exclusion criteria.
People with dental implants were also excluded, in order to avoid artifacts in the brain images. Each
participant gave written informed consent prior to the admission in the cohort. The study protocol of
1000BRAINS was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Essen, Germany. All participants
were scanned with a 3T Siemens Tim-TRIO MR scanner (32- channel head coil, Erlangen, Germany)
located in Germany, at the Forschungszentrum J¨ulich. During resting-state image acquisition, participants
were instructed to keep their eyes closed, be relaxed, let their mind wander. Wakefulness was verified
through post-scan debriefing.
The 1000BRAINS cohort was design to investigate the typical aging process, in order to link brain
structure and function to behavioral, environmental and genetic factors. In the current study, after data
quality controls, we consider a total of 921 subjects (43% females) aged from 18.5 to 85 years (mean =
60.8 years, STD = 13.3 years). The population distribution is shown in Figure a. The BOLD timeseries
were pre-processed using the fMRIPrep pipeline (v24.1.1) with an additional step of low pass filter (0.1
Hz). Together with the imaging recordings, a battery of neuro-physiological assessment was conducted.
A total of 13 cognitive tests were performed by each participant, in order to investigate the population-
based cognitive decline and link it to the brain aging process. The tests were then labeled into five domain
specific categories (attention, executive functions, language, episodic memory and working memory) in
order to obtain one cognitive score per domain.
The structural connectivity matrices and BOLD time series were pre-processed and parcellated using
the Schaefer atlas with 100 regions of interest (ROIs), grouped into seven canonical resting-state networks
(RSNs): visual (VN), somato-motor (SMN), dorsal attention (DAN), ventral attention (VAN), limbic
(LN), fronto-parietal (FPN), and default mode (DMN). The reference atlas is shown if Figure b.


Connectivity estimates. To characterize normative trajectories of functional organization across
typical aging, we estimated both functional and effective connectivity for each participant.
Functional connectivity (FC) was defined as the Fisher’s z-transformation of the Pearson’s correlation
between all pairs of regions, resulting in a 100 by 100 matrix. As directed connectivity measures we
employed the Granger Causality (GC), and multivariate Ornstein–Uhlenbeck effective connectivity (MOU-
EC)4. Granger Causality captures whether past activity in one region predicts future activity in another,
providing a data-driven estimate of directional influence, defining by definition non-negative weights of
connection. The multivariate Ornstein–Uhlenbeck effective connectivity, in contrast, relies on a generative
model to infer causal interactions among the populations, resulting in a directed and signed connectivity
matrix.
Each connectivity estimate was represented as a network, with nodes corresponding to ROIs and
edges the connectivity weights. As first step we assessed the global connectivity tendency against age, by
averaging the connectivity values across the connectivity matrices.
Then, to identify regions most implicated in age-related changes, we computed nodal strength by
summing incoming (or outgoing) connection weights across each region’s neighborhood. For undirected
measures such as FC, incoming influence (in-strength) and outgoing influence (out-strength) will coincide
(strength). Figure c shows a schematic representation of in-strength and out-strength.

Results


At the global level, FC significantly decreased with age (Spearman’s r = -0.30, p < 0.001), as the structural connectivity (Spearman’s r = -0.37, p < 0.001). These results are consistent with previous findings2. In contrast, directed connectivity measures exhibited different age-related behaviors: MOU-EC significantly increased with age (Spearman’s r = 0.24, p < 0.001), whereas GC showed no significant correlation with age (Spearman’s r = 0.04, p = 0.88).

For both FC and EC, we further examined positive and negative sub-networks, previously referred to as excitatory and inhibitory sub-networks3. Both excitatory and inhibitory FC networks showed significant age-related decreases (excitatory FC: Spearman’s r = -0.30, p < 0.001; inhibitory FC: Spearman’s r = -0.22, p < 0.001). In contrast, the excitatory EC sub-network exhibited a positive association with age, although weaker than the one observed in the full EC network (Spearman’s r = 0.11, p < 0.001). The inhibitory EC sub-network showed no significant age-related effect (Spearman’s r = 0.03, p = 0.79).

At the nodal strength level, both SC and FC strengths (Fig. a) show a clear overall decline with age, in agreement with previous findings2. In particular, SC strength appears to accelerate its decline across all RSNs after approximately 55–60 years of age. On the other hand, FC strength shows an earlier acceleration of decline, occurring around 50–55 years. As previously reported, FC nodal strength follows the sensory–association axis, with higher strength values observed in sensory and attentional networks compared to association networks.

Directed connectomes, in contrary, reveal complementary and partially divergent patterns. Both Granger Causality (GC) and effective connectivity (EC) exhibit an overall increase in nodal in-strength across regions (Fig. b). This result differs from previous work based on regression Dynamic Causal Modeling, which reported a post-childhood decline in nodal effective connectivity in-strength3. Notably, out-strengths (Fig. c) display more heterogeneous and mixed behaviors for both directed connectomes. In particular, regions within the visual network show mixed out-strength trajectories for EC, while dorsal attention and default mode regions exhibit heterogeneous out-strength patterns for GC. Such an imbalance points to a reorganization of directional information flow, in aging, rather than a uniform strengthening or weakening of connectivity. Finally, it is important to note that significant nodal strength effects do not involve the same number of regions of interest across RSNs, indicating network-specific sensitivity to aging.

Taken together, these results suggest that aging is associated with a progressive decoupling between incoming and outgoing neural influences at the regional level. While undirected measures capture a general weakening of connectivity, directional metrics reveal selective changes in how regions integrate versus transmit information. This reconfiguration may reflect compensatory mechanisms or altered hierarchical organization in aging brain networks, offering a more nuanced view of functional reorganization beyond traditional connectivity analyses.

In addition to nodal strength analyses, we investigated age-related changes in whole-network connectivity patterns. This analysis revealed an overall decrease for GC, but a mixed increasing/decreasing patterns for EC, suggesting that these methods capture distinct—and potentially complementary—aspects of directed brain interactions. Such discrepancies may arise from differences in underlying assumptions, temporal sensitivity, and modeling frameworks between data-driven and model-based approaches. These incoherent patterns are currently under further investigation to better understand their relationship and how they can be most robust and informative for characterizing brain aging.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated age-related changes in brain connectivity across the adult lifespan by jointly analyzing structural, functional, and directional connectivity derived from resting-state fMRI in a large, population-based cohort. By integrating undirected functional connectivity with multiple estimates of directed and effective connectivity, we provide a comprehensive characterization of how large-scale brain networks reorganize with aging.

Our results confirm a robust global and regional decline in both structural and functional connectivity with age, with distinct temporal profiles: structural connectivity shows an accelerated decline in later adulthood, whereas functional connectivity begins to deteriorate earlier. At the regional level, functional connectivity follows the well-established sensory–association axis, with sensory and attentional systems exhibiting higher baseline strength but pronounced age-related reductions.

Crucially, directional connectivity measures reveal complementary patterns that are not captured by undirected approaches. While functional connectivity decreases, effective connectivity—here based on multivariate Orstein-Uhlenbeck processes—shows a significant increase with age at the global and nodal levels. Moreover, the dissociation between in-strength and out-strength trajectories highlights an age-related imbalance between incoming and outgoing neural influences. This suggests that aging alters not only the magnitude of interactions but also the directional roles that regions play within large-scale networks, potentially reflecting changes in information integration, hierarchical organization, or compensatory mechanisms. Such insights would remain inaccessible without explicitly modeling directionality.

At the same time, our connectivity pattern analyses reveal incoherent and partially non-overlapping results across different directed connectivity estimators, such as Granger Causality and MOU-EC. These discrepancies underscore the sensitivity of directional metrics to modeling assumptions, temporal constraints, and estimation frameworks. As such, they represent both a limitation and an opportunity: while they caution against over-interpretation of any single method, they also motivate deeper methodological investigations to disentangle estimator-specific effects from genuine neurobiological signals.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional nature of the data limits inferences about individual aging trajectories, and directed connectivity estimates remain sensitive to pre-processing choices and model assumptions. Additionally, not all resting-state networks exhibit the same degree of age-related effects, pointing to heterogeneous vulnerability across estimates.

Future work will focus on systematically evaluating the robustness of directional connectivity measures, refining pre-processing and modeling strategies, and possibly extending analyses to independent dataset. Ultimately, our findings highlight the added value of directional connectivity for understanding brain aging and suggest that effective connectivity may serve as a promising candidate for developing personalized biomarkers of healthy brain trajectories and early vulnerability to cognitive decline.
