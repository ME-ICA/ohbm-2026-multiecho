Introduction

Misdiagnosis between Alzheimer's Disease (AD) and Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD) carries serious clinical consequences. Recent findings reveal that nearly 70% of patients initially suspected of having FTD ultimately do not have the disease, with accurate diagnosis taking an average of 3.6 years.

  Previous machine learning approaches have achieved accuracies above 90% in AD-FTD discrimination using resting-state EEG. However, these models relied on hand-crafted features—manually engineered metrics requiring domain expertise—which fundamentally limits their generalizability and scalability. Traditional feature engineering fails to generalize across diverse datasets and clinical populations.

  Recently, EEG foundation models have emerged, trained on large-scale datasets to learn generalizable representations directly from raw signals without manual feature engineering. However, current foundation models show limited performance on AD-FTD discrimination at the sample level, raising critical questions: Can foundation models match traditional approaches without explicit feature engineering? What neural representations—spatial patterns, temporal dynamics, or spectral features—are most critical?

   We employed DIVER (Deep Integration of Vast Electrophysiological Recordings at Scale), which models spatial, temporal, and spectral information through multi-scale spectral learning. Our objective was to compare foundation model architectures on AD-FTD discrimination using raw resting-state EEG to evaluate whether domain-informed models can match traditional hand-crafted feature approaches.

Methods

Dataset and Preprocessing We utilized resting-state EEG recordings from AD and FTD patients from the publicly available OpenNeuro dataset (ds004504, version 1.0.7; https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds004504/versions/1.0.7), containing 36 AD patients, 23 FTD patients, and 29 healthy controls. EEG was preprocessed with bandpass filtering and artifact rejection. Analysis was performed at the sample level on individual EEG segments without manual feature extraction, ensuring models learned representations directly from raw signals.

Model Architectures We primarily employed DIVER as our main model for AD-FTD discrimination. To contextualize its performance, we compared it against two other EEG foundation models with distinct architectural philosophies. CBraMod represents a cross-dataset brain foundation model trained for general neurophysiological pattern recognition. LEAD employs large-scale pretraining with general-purpose transformer architectures. DIVER introduces explicit architectural components for domain-relevant structures: spatial encoding via channel-type embeddings and positional encodings to model inter-channel relationships; temporal dynamics through Rotary Position Embeddings combined with Any-Variate Attention for improved multivariate attention; and multi-scale spectral learning with FFT and STFT reconstruction heads to encode frequency information explicitly in learned representations.

Evaluation All models were evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation at the sample level. Primary metrics included F1 score and sensitivity, appropriate for potentially imbalanced clinical datasets. We compared foundation model performance against published results from hand-crafted feature approaches on the same dataset to contextualize performance gaps and inform future development directions.

Results

Foundation Model Comparison
At the sample level, all three foundation models showed modest performance on AD-FTD discrimination using 5-fold cross-validation (Figure). DIVER achieved the highest performance among foundation models, with 17.6% relative improvement in F1 score over CBraMod and 5.3% over LEAD, demonstrating consistent superiority across evaluation metrics.

Comparison with Hand-Crafted Features
For context, previous studies employing hand-crafted features on the same dataset reported substantially higher performance. Hachamnia et al. (2025) achieved >93% accuracy using wavelet-derived features with light gradient boosting machine (LGBM), evaluated via leave-one-out cross-validation at the subject level. The performance gap between sample-level foundation model predictions (F1≈0.51) and subject-level feature-engineered approaches (accuracy>93%) reflects fundamental differences in evaluation paradigms and feature learning strategies. Specifically, subject-level predictions aggregate evidence across multiple EEG segments per patient, substantially reducing noise compared to single-segment predictions. Additionally, hand-crafted approaches utilize expert-selected features (e.g., wavelet coefficients, spectral power in specific frequency bands) that have been optimized over decades of neuroscience research, while foundation models learn representations directly from raw temporal signals without domain-specific priors.

Conclusion

Foundation Models Show Promise but Require Domain-Informed Architecture This study demonstrates that foundation models can learn diagnostically relevant representations from raw EEG without manual feature engineering, addressing the generalizability and scalability limitations of hand-crafted approaches. However, architectural design critically determines success. DIVER's explicit modeling of spectral information through multi-scale spectral learning significantly outperformed general-purpose architectures (LEAD, CBraMod), revealing that domain-informed inductive biases remain essential. While foundation models underperformed traditional approaches at the sample level, they offer automatic feature discovery and cross-dataset transferability.

From Sample-Level Performance to Clinical Application The modest sample-level performance (F1≈0.51) reflects the preliminary nature of applying foundation models to this challenging task. Critical next steps include: (1) subject-level aggregation across multiple EEG segments per patient, (2) frequency band analysis to identify established or novel biomarkers, (3) architecture optimization investigating the unexpected improvement when removing Rotary Position Embeddings, (4) spatial pattern interpretation for anatomically-specific biomarkers, and (5) multimodal integration with structural MRI or CSF markers.

Implications for Addressing Diagnostic Challenges Given that nearly 70% of suspected FTD cases are misdiagnosed, with diagnosis taking 3.6 years on average, scalable objective diagnostic tools are urgently needed. This work establishes proof-of-principle that models with appropriate architectural inductive biases can automatically prioritize clinically-relevant signal modalities without explicit programming. Future analysis of which frequency bands DIVER's multi-scale spectral learning attends to will determine whether the model rediscovers established biomarkers or identifies novel patterns, potentially providing insights that overcome current diagnostic delays.
