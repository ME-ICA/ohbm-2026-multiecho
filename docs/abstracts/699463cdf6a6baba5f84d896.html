Introduction

Interoception refers to the process by which the nervous system senses, interprets and integrates internal signals arising from multiple systems, including the cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and autonomic pathways1.  It plays a crucial role in emotional processing, decision making, consciousness, and the sense of self, which are altered in psychiatric disorders2,3. Clinical neuroscience increasingly examines interoceptive processing across psychiatric disorders to understand how its disruption contributes to pathophysiology. While alterations have been reported using questionnaires and behavioural tasks, electroencephalography (EEG) and electrocardiography (ECG) may provide quantitative measures that are widely available in routine clinical settings. Their coupling enables measurement of cortical responses to heartbeats, heartbeat evoked potentials (HEP), which may serve as biomarkers of interoception1,4. HEP amplitude differs between psychiatric populations and healthy controls; it is increased in schizophrenia2 and reduced in depression4 relative to healthy subjects, indicating shared but heterogenous dysfunction3. Heart rate variability (HRV), provides a complementary interoceptive marker, indexing brain-body regulatory flexibility. Higher resting HRV reflects better emotional regulation, whereas lower HRV indicates autonomic inflexibility consistently observed across psychiatric disorders3,5. HRV also provides a crucial physiological context for HEP extraction, accounting cardiac dynamics. Linking HEP with HRV offers a bidirectional view of brain-body communication. Using a clinical EEG database, we compared HEP and HRV across psychosis, mood and neurodevelopmental disorders. Given the psychiatric phenotypic continuum, we hypothesised differences between psychosis and mood disorders, with larger effects in neurodevelopmental disorders where interoceptive disruptions likely emerge in earlier stages of development and thus may be more severe.

Methods

2.1 Population

In this retrospective study, we analysed previously acquired clinical data from the GHU Paris Psychiatry and Neurosciences data warehouse, consisting of clinical EEG recordings, coupled to ECG, recorded between 2019 and 2024. 

A total of 910 subjects were screened from this database, based on diagnosis and recording quality. Only individuals having received a unique diagnosis on one of three spectra : psychosis (N=313), mood (N=226), and neurodevelopment (N=108) were kept for analysis. This study was approved by the GHU Paris Research Ethics.

2.2 EEG and ECG recordings

EEG data was recorded using 19 scalp electrodes with gel, following the international 10–20 system, on a Micromed® set-up. Impedances were kept below 5 kΩ and sampling rate was 256 Hz. These routine clinical EEGs consist of 20-min recordings performed in a semi-reclined position, including rest sequences with eyes open and closed, followed by two stimulus conditions (not analysed here). The ECG was recorded simultaneously, using a right-shoulder to left-abdomen lead configuration.

2.3 Preprocessing 

EEG preprocessing was done using MNE-Python software. EEG data were band-pass filtered (1–100 Hz). Bad channels were identified using PyPREP RANSAC-based detection and interpolated via spherical splines, after which data were re-referenced to the common average. Independent component analysis (ICA) was performed and components labeled as “eye-blinks”, “muscle”, “line noise”,”heart” by ICLabel were rejected. retaining “brain” or “other” components. 

2.4 Heartbeat evoked potential

To extract the HEP, resting-state EEG segments were selected. Heartbeat-locked epochs were then generated from 250ms to 600ms around the R-peaks using ECG-EEG coupling. Autoreject was applied to remove epochs with excessive artifacts. Clean epochs for each subject were preprocessed as described above, and concatenated, then averaged to obtain the HEP. Its peak amplitude was computed in a fronto-central region of interest consisting of the following channels: Fp1, Fp2, Fz, F7, F3, F4, F8, C3, Cz, C4, during a predefined 300-500 ms post-R peak temporal window of interest1,2. Peak latency is the latency at which the peak amplitude occurred in the region of interest, and mean amplitude is the averaged amplitudes during the entire timeframe.

2.5 ECG-derived metrics  

The ECG channel was filtered with a high-pass of 0.5 Hz and low-pass of 40 Hz. Aside from mean, minimum and maximum heart rate, additional ECG-derived measures were extracted. HRV was computed over the entire ~20-minute recording. R-peaks were detected automatically using MNE-Python and RR intervals were calculated between successive beats, excluding intervals <300 ms or >2000 ms. From cleaned RR intervals, we derived RMSSD, reflecting short-term beat-to-beat variability and mean inter-beat intervals, the average RR interval. 

2.6 Statistics 

For the HEP analyses, ANCOVA examined three variables of interest 1) peak latency, 2) peak amplitude, and 3) mean amplitude across the three diagnostic groups (psychosis, mood, and neurodevelopmental disorders), covarying for sex, age at recording, and HRV-RMSSD. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey HSD tests, and corrected for multiple comparisons. HRV-RMSSD was analysed using ANCOVA, covarying for sex and age at recording. Normal distribution was assessed using QQ-plots.  

Results

3.1 Heartbeat Evoked Potential 

The present study was conducted on 647 subjects during resting state. There was a significant main effect of diagnosis on peak latency, F(2, 641) = 3.77, p = .024. HRV (RMSSD) was also a strong predictor of peak latency, F(1, 641) = 34.37, p < .001. Post-hoc tests found no difference between psychosis and mood disorders (p = .86), no difference between mood disorders and neurodevelopment (p = .08), but neurodevelopmental peak latency was significantly longer than psychosis’ one (mean difference of 18 ms, adjusted p-value = 0.022) (Figure 1). Neither sex, F(1, 641) = 3.02, p = .083, nor age at EEG, F(1, 641) = 0.73, p = .395, showed a significant effect on peak latency. There were no significant effects of diagnosis, age, sex, or HRV on peak amplitude and mean amplitude.

3.2 ECG-derived measures

There was no significant effect of diagnosis on RMSSD (F(2, 641) = 2.45, p = .087). There was a significant main effect of diagnosis on mean inter-beat interval (F(2, 641) = 24.46, p = 5e-11).

Conclusion

This study aimed to compare HEPs’ peak latency, peak amplitude, and mean amplitudes transdiagnostically, between psychosis, mood, and neurodevelopmental disorders to test their association with disease. This design allowed us to address the lack of diagnostic specificity in psychiatry and the continuum along which these psychiatric conditions manifest. We found that HEP peak latency was higher in neurodevelopmental disorders than in psychosis (significant after multiple correction) and mood disorders (nominal, not significant after multiple correction). We found no difference in terms of peak and mean amplitude. 

Previous studies have reported altered HEP amplitudes when comparing psychiatric populations to healthy controls; however less is known about differences between clinical groups. Hence, the absence of HEP amplitude differences between our three groups does not imply intact interoception, but rather reflects comparable levels of alteration. 

Contrary to expectations, we did not observe the polarity differences in HEP amplitudes noted in the literature, whereby resting-state HEP amplitude is decreased in major depressive disorder4, and increased in psychosis relative to controls2. Hence, one would expect a difference between these two groups. A possible explanation is the heterogeneous composition of our subject groups, which included all diagnoses falling under mood or psychosis disorders. Further stratifying our groups could clarify these effects. 

Next, peak latency comparisons yielded promising results, revealing significant differences between neurodevelopmental and psychosis groups. Longer latencies in the neurodevelopmental group could suggest altered temporal processing or integration of cardiac interoceptive signals. Alternatively, the shorter latencies in the psychosis group may reflect increased prediction-error signalling. Indeed, previous research has pointed to aberrant prediction updating in schizophrenia, likely stemming from excessive release of dopamine, impairing the ability to accurately minimize prediction error between predicted and actual signals. 

Moreover, HRV-RMSSD strongly predicted HEP latency (F=34.37, p < .001), highlighting the interaction between autonomic regulation and interoceptive cortical processing. Yet, the lack of diagnostic effects on RMSSD suggests that latency differences are not solely driven by peripheral autonomic variability, but rather reflect distinct central integrative mechanisms across psychiatric diagnoses. 

Finally, several limitations need to be acknowledged. First, most individuals were receiving psychotropic medication at the time of recording, which can bias HRV. Further analyses will therefore include medication-stratified analyses to control for psychotropic effects on cardiac activity. Second, while we corrected for HRV, HEP can be strongly influenced by blood pressure, which could not be corrected for here.  Further analysis will include photoplethysmography, to compute relative blood volume changes as a proxy of blood pressure variation. Third, the observed difference between neurodevelopmental and psychotic groups, while significant, remains modest, with substantial overlap in group distributions. Thus, HEP latency by itself cannot be considered a reliable marker at the individual level. It may however be useful in combination with other measures of interoception : for instance, concurrent examination of spectral power in alpha and theta bands in the selected resting-state segments could elucidate oscillatory correlates of interoceptive processing across groups and strengthen the multimodal characterization of interoception. 

In conclusion, despite its limitations, this study shows promise in the use of clinical EEG to reveal transdiagnostic interoceptive differences. Coupling HEP with HRV offers further insight into brain-body integration, supporting the use of these measures as complementary markers in psychiatric research.
