Introduction

The relationship between brain structural connectivity (SC) derived from diffusion tractography and functional connectivity (FC) derived from BOLD fMRI has been widely investigated and is spatially heterogeneous, yet the precise link remains unknown. Structure and function correspond closely in unimodal sensory/motor regions but diverge in transmodal association cortices1. This hierarchical decoupling implies that macroscale topology, conventionally quantified via number of streamlines (NoS), is insufficient to fully explain the integrative dynamics of higher-order systems. Indeed, a critical limitation in addressing the structure-function gap lies in the definition of SC, typically weighted by NoS. While NoS captures macroscale topology it relies heavily on tracking parameters and lacks specificity to the biological properties of the underlying tissue. In other words, current models overlook the underlying tissue microstructure (e.g., axon properties and extra-axonal processes) that creates the direct physical substrate for signal transmission. Biophysical models of diffusion, such as Standard Model Imaging (SMI)2, offer a solution by quantifying specific microstructure parameters that are physically grounded, as opposed to NoS. Complementing SC specificity requires precise FC definition. Classically estimated using Pearson correlation, FC captures direct and indirect connections, obscuring the mapping to underlying structural pathways. Normalised precision-based FC (pcFC) yields a more biologically reliable target for SC-FC comparisons by removing mediator effects. 

In this work, we aim to model pcFC as a function of microstructural properties of the associated white matter (WM) to further assess the microstructure basis of SC-FC coupling in different resting-state networks. We hypothesise that different resting-state networks exhibit coupling with distinct microstructural profiles that reflect their specific physiological and computational demands.

Methods

MRI Data and Preprocessing

Data consisted of N=66 subjects (age 22–36 years, 29 males) from the Human Connectome Project (HCP), including T1-weighted images, high-resolution DWI data (b-values = 0, 1000, 2000, 3000 s/mm2; >90 directions), and four runs of resting-state fMRI (TR=720 ms, 1200 frames/run). DWI preprocessing followed the standard HCP pipeline, including eddy current and motion correction, gradient-nonlinearity correction, registration to T1-weighted images and resampling to 1.25 mm resolution. fMRI preprocessing included distortion and motion correction, bias field correction, ICA-based denoising with motion regression, and high-pass filtering. Tractography data was obtained from a probabilistic WM atlas (MultiConn Atlas)3 available at four parcellation scales; here we focused on the highest resolution (Scale 4, 472 regions) to ensure fine-grained cortical coverage.

Microstructure-Informed Structural Connectomes

We selected five microstructural parameters derived from SMI as connectome weights: axon water fraction (f), intra-axonal diffusivity (Da), extra-axonal parallel diffusivity (De||), extra-axonal perpendicular diffusivity (De⊥) and fibre alignment (p2). For each subject, parameter values were averaged along every streamline within a bundle and then averaged across streamlines to produce five distinct microstructure-weighted connectomes. A standard streamline count (NoS) weighted connectome was also generated for comparison. All matrices were group-averaged across subjects to yield a single representative structural connectome for each parameter.

Partial Correlation Functional Connectivity (pcFC)

To isolate direct functional connections and remove indirect polysynaptic effects inherent in standard Pearson correlation, we computed partial correlation FC (pcFC). For each subject, the average BOLD signal was extracted from each of the 472 regions, demeaned, low-pass filtered (0.1 Hz), and concatenated across runs. The regularised (Tikhonov regularisation) precision matrix (inverse covariance) was calculated, and entries were normalised to obtain partial correlation coefficients between all region pairs4. These matrices were Fisher z-transformed and averaged across subjects to obtain a group-level pcFC matrix.

Node-wise Dominance and Network Analysis

To determine the primary microstructural driver of function, we performed a node-wise competitive linear regression (Figure 1). For every brain region i, we modelled its direct FC profile (pcFCi) to all other nodes using microstructural predictors: NoS, f, Da, De||, De⊥ and p2 while controlling for fibre length. The microstructural predictor yielding the largest standardised beta coefficient (|ß|), was identified as the “dominant driver” of SC-FC coupling for that region. To test for network-specific coupling, regions were mapped to the seven functional networks of the Yeo resting-state atlas5 using a coordinate-based approach. The centroid of each region was computed in MNI space and assigned the label of the corresponding voxel in the resting-state atlas (Visual, Somatomotor, Dorsal Attention, Ventral Attention, Limbic, Fronto-Parietal, Default Mode)5 + subcortical regions. We then quantified the microstructural profile of each network by calculating the proportion of nodes dominated by each parameter. To validate these distinct profiles, we performed Binomial Tests comparing the frequency of the winning parameter against a random chance level of P=1/6 (16.7%). This determined whether specific microstructural properties significantly dominated structure-function coupling within specific networks.

Results

To isolate the specific contribution of tissue microstructure to functional connectivity, we modelled pcFC using NoS and SMI microstructure parameters (Da, De||, De⊥, f, p2), while controlling for fibre length. The results highlighted distinct spatial organisations for each dominant microstructure predictor. Regions dominated by NoS appeared dispersed across the brain, lacking a clear spatial pattern. In contrast, regions driven by microstructural parameters showed spatial clustering consistent with functional anatomy, notably with diffusivity-driven (De|| and De⊥) nodes aggregating in posterior sensory regions and density-driven (f) nodes clustering in motor and control areas (Figure 2A). To characterise these patterns, we mapped brain regions to the Yeo-7 resting state networks (RSNs)5 and subcortical regions to quantify the distribution of dominant drivers. Our results highlight that NoS failed to significantly dominate any RSN (p > 0.05 in all networks). This shows that the predictive power of structural connectivity (structure-function coupling) is primarily driven by underlying microstructural tissue properties rather than standard streamline count (Figure 2B). Moreover, binomial tests on the microstructural parameters revealed significant network-specific heterogeneity, identifying three distinct profiles:

1. Axon water fraction (f): f was the significant dominant predictor in three functional networks. The Somatomotor network exhibited the strongest dependence, with f driving 52% of regions (p < 10-8). Similarly, the Ventral Attention network was significantly dominated by f (38.8%, p < 10-3), as was the Default Mode network (36.2%, p < 10-4), indicating that coupling in these networks is primarily predicted by axon density or axonal packing.

2. Extra-axonal parallel diffusivity (De||): In contrast, Visual and Dorsal Attention networks, and subcortical regions, were primarily driven by De||. In the Visual network, De|| was the dominant driver in 41.7% of regions (p < 10-5), significantly exceeding chance levels. The Dorsal Attention network showed a similar profile (42.9%, p < 10-4), while subcortical regions showed the highest dependence on De|| (50.0%, p < 10-4).

3. Fibre alignment (p2): The Limbic network exhibited a distinct profile, being the only network significantly driven by p2, which dominated 43% of regions (p < 0.005).

Finally, the Fronto-Parietal network did not exhibit a single statistically significant microstructural profile above chance levels, suggesting a more heterogeneous reliance on multiple microstructural features.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrated that structure-function coupling is spatially heterogeneous and constrained by specific WM microstructure properties, rather than macroscale topology alone. When controlling for fibre length, streamline count failed to serve as a dominant predictor for FC in any RSN, suggesting that the number of reconstructed streamlines is a less biologically relevant proxy for transmission efficacy than the microstructural composition of the tissue itself. By using the SMI framework, we reveal that pcFC is associated with distinct compartment-specific microstructural properties of the WM: the intra-axonal volume fraction, the extra-axonal diffusivity and the fibre alignment, depending on the functional demands of the network.

We identified three distinct microstructural profiles governing resting-state networks. First, the Somatomotor, Default Mode and Ventral attention networks were preferentially driven by f, which biologically represents the intra-axonal volume fraction, a proxy for axonal packing and density. The sensitivity of the motor network to this parameter aligns with the heavy myelination of the corticospinal tract, suggesting that signal transmission in these executive function circuits is strictly constrained by the sheer volume or density of the axonal substrate. Similarly, the dependence of the Default Mode and Ventral Attention networks on f suggests that the integrative capacity of these metabolically demanding hubs is limited by the density of interconnecting cabling, essential for their role in detecting salient stimuli and coordinating internal states. Second, the Visual, Dorsal Attention networks and subcortical regions were primarily constrained by De||. This parameter characterises diffusion within the extra-axonal space parallel to the fibres and is modulated by crowding of the extra-cellular space (e.g., glial density). The dominance of De|| could imply that signal precision in the subcortical, Dorsal Attention and Visual networks is not limited by the axons themselves, but by the properties of the extra-axonal environment and possibly by glial density via metabolic demands. Finally, the Limbic network exhibited a unique dependency on p2, a marker of fibre alignment. This dissociation mirrors the transition from neocortex to allocortex. Unlike the linear projection tracts of the motor system, the Limbic network is characterised by complex branching patterns and short association fibers. It is plausible that functional connectivity in these associative networks is constrained by the geometric architecture of the tract (e.g., fanning or crossing) rather than the tissue density or diffusivity.

To conclude, this study advances the understanding of the nonuniform structure-function coupling in the brain, demonstrating that the structural basis of FC is biologically specific and spatially distinct. By integrating WM microstructure with precision-based FC, we identified a microstructural hierarchy across the human cortex: action and integration networks are constrained by axonal packing, sensory and attention networks by extra-axonal diffusivity, and limbic circuits by fibre alignment. While the discussed biophysical interpretations require further validation, these findings suggest that the human brain optimises different biophysical properties of the WM scaffold, to meet the distinct computational requirements of segregation and association across different cognitive domains.
