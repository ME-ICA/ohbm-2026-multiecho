Introduction

The choroid plexus forms the blood–cerebrospinal fluid barrier and orchestrates neuroimmune signaling, making it a compelling target to investigate in the context of immunity. Prior evidence suggests choroid plexus morphological alterations may be present in autism spectrum disorder (ASD)1, but scalable investigation has been constrained by time-consuming manual labeling or the lack of accurate and generalizable automated segmentation tools. ASCHOPLEX enables automatic segmentation of choroid plexus based on T1-weighted MRI and has demonstrated high specificity for identifying choroid plexus in adults with and without multiple sclerosis2. Here, we assessed the generalizability of the ASCHOPLEX tool to ASD cohorts and implemented a probabilistic variant to quantify model confidence when “ground truth” labels obtained by manual tracing are unavailable. In a local adult dataset comprised of ASD and neurotypical control (CON) MRI brain scans, we finetuned ASCHOPLEX and tested accuracy against manual choroid plexus labels; we then evaluated cross‑dataset generalization and algorithm uncertainty in adults and children from the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) dataset3. Our aims were: (i) determine whether finetuning enables accurate choroid plexus segmentation in ASD, (ii) quantify the algorithm’s uncertainty to gauge reliability, and (iii) identify conditions (e.g., age) where generalization is limited and additional finetuning is warranted.

Methods

Datasets & Imaging:  High‑resolution T1‑weighted MEMPRAGE MRI were acquired from a local adult cohort (n=65; 36 ASD, 29 CON; ages 18–40 years) using a 3T TIM Trio with 1.0 mm isotropic voxels. Manual choroid plexus segmentations in the lateral ventricles served as ground truth. We conducted visual quality control for missing data, image artifacts, and orientation issues on the ABIDE dataset (final n = 1,802; ages 5–64 years) for evaluation of ASCHOPLEX generalization.

Model & Finetuning: ASCHOPLEX, an ensemble of five transformer and convolutional backbone-based neural networks,  was finetuned with manual segmentations from a 12‑subject subset (training = 6: 3 ASD [2 male/1 female], 3 CON [2 male/1 female]; validation = 6: 3 ASD [2 male/1 female], 3 CON [2 male/1 female]). We evaluated the finetuned model on the remaining 53 local subjects (ASD=30; CON=23). The deterministic pipeline produced binary masks by majority vote across ensemble members. To evaluate generalization, we applied the finetuned ASCHOPLEX to the ABIDE dataset.

Probabilistic Variant & Uncertainty: We enabled Monte Carlo (MC) dropout (p = 0.1) during finetuning and inference in each ensemble model to approximate Bayesian behavior without architectural overhaul5. For each subject, we generated voxel‑wise probability maps and computed four uncertainty metrics: (i) prediction standard deviation, (ii) total predictive entropy, (iii) aleatoric entropy (expected per‑sample entropy), and (iv) epistemic uncertainty (mutual information). We ran 100 iterations per local subject (20 MC passes × 5 models). For ABIDE, we ran 100 iterations on a representative 86‑subject subset and 25 iterations (5 MC passes × 5 models) across the full cohort for computational feasibility. Metric values were aggregated within the final choroid plexus masks.

Accuracy & Bias Analyses: Accuracy was quantified via Dice similarity coefficients between automated and manual labels. For a baseline comparison to another automated method, we generated choroid plexus labels using FreeSurfer’s (v6.0) automated segmentation. To test performance bias, we fit a linear mixed‑effects model on Dice coefficients with fixed factors of segmentation method, sex, and diagnosis, and random intercepts per subject. This was followed by FDR‑corrected post‑hoc t‑tests. For uncertainty, we modeled fixed effects of diagnosis and cohort (local adults vs. ABIDE adults vs. ABIDE children) with random intercepts for brain imaging site and conducted FDR-corrected post‑hoc t-tests.

Results

Accuracy Gains from Finetuning. Finetuning markedly improved segmentation versus FreeSurfer and non‑finetuned ASCHOPLEX. In the local test set (n=53), both finetuned deterministic and probabilistic pipelines achieved Dice ≈ 0.78, outperforming FreeSurfer (≈ 0.28) and the non‑finetuned model (≈ 0.42). Mixed‑effects models showed a strong main effect of method (finetuned deterministic: b=0.52, t=13.02, p<.001; finetuned probabilistic: b=0.52, t=13.06, p<.001), and post‑hoc comparisons confirmed that finetuned ASCHOPLEX performed better than FreeSurfer (T(53)=39.0, p<.001; T(53)=40.6, p<.001) and non‑finetuned ASCHOPLEX (T(53)=9.93, p<0.001; T(53)=9.54, p<.001). Deterministic vs. probabilistic finetuned model performance did not significantly differ (T(53)=1.16, p=0.25).

Bias Removal with Finetuning. Without finetuning, ASCHOPLEX showed better performance on CON participants (higher Dice in CON vs. ASD: T(23,30)=4.04, p<0.001). Finetuning removed the diagnostic bias for both deterministic and probabilistic pipelines (ASD vs. CON: n.s.; male vs. female: n.s.).

Uncertainty Profiles Indicate Generalization Limits. Uncertainty was minimal in the local adult dataset, near the MC‑dropout “floor,” consistent with in‑distribution inputs far from decision boundaries. In ABIDE, uncertainty increased significantly (standard deviation and total entropy: ABIDE adults > local, ABIDE children > ABIDE adults, all p<0.01 to p<0.001). Decomposition revealed that the rise was driven by epistemic, not aleatoric, uncertainty, implicating model unfamiliarity (age and acquisition diversity) rather than data noise per se (e.g., epistemic: ABIDE adults/local: T(53,529)=−2.70, p<0.05; ABIDE children/ABIDE adults: T(529,1273)=−7.90, p<0.001). These findings align with known age effects on ventricular anatomy that diminish CP–CSF contrast in children, resulting in poorer CP segmentation without finetuning on children.

Conclusion

ASCHOPLEX, when finetuned on a small adult subset of ASD and CON MRI scans, achieves near–human‑level CP segmentation accuracy and removes performance biases, supporting its utilization for large‑scale ASD studies. Extending the ensemble to a probabilistic implementation enriched with MC dropout preserves accuracy while yielding robust voxel‑wise confidence maps, which are of great significance for assessing the reliability in cohorts when manual labels are unavailable (e.g., ABIDE). Elevated epistemic uncertainty in ABIDE children underscores limited age generalization and the need for age‑diverse finetuning and/or training datasets, plus standardized manual CP segmentation protocols to support future model development. Together, finetuning and uncertainty quantification offer a practical path to trustworthy CP segmentation across cohorts.
