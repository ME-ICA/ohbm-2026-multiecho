Introduction

The brain's ability to process salient stimuli while perceiving and modulating the body’s interoceptive signals is essential for survival. Converging evidence from decades of research suggests that the insular cortex is a key brain region involved in both of these functions in health and disease1-4. However, its complex anatomy and deep localization hidden behind the other cortices have limited our understanding of its precise functional architecture with causal methods. Our current study was designed to address this important gap of knowledge by combining intracranial direct recordings with direct electrical stimulation of different insular subregions as well as peripheral nerve stimulations guided by a newly developed fine-grained microstructural atlas of the human insula. 

Methods

We applied direct cortical stimulations across 342 insular sites in 50 neurosurgical patients. Insular sites were localized with the iELVis protocol using fused and co-registered pre‑implant T1 MRI and post‑implant CT scans. Anatomical locations were determined by fusing each patient’s native space with Julich microstructural atlas5, 6. Each contact was additionally reviewed by two anatomists (JP, JQ) using native anatomical information and projected onto an MNI‑based group template for visualization. This workflow allowed us to determine the precise localization of sites whose direct electrical stimulation caused distinct subjective sensations. To ensure validity, we included only trials of electrical stimulation with i) clear dose–response with increasing current, ii) reproducible effects across trials, iii), no reports during sham stimulation, and iv) no after-discharges evoked by electrical stimulations. In addition, insular iEEG recordings were obtained in 35 patients (511 sites) during a salient target‑detection task (Gradual continuous performance7) in which subjects were instructed to respond differently to frequently appearing stimuli versus infrequent target stimuli.   Moreover, we obtained data from peripheral nerve stimulations of the mandibular (V3), median, and tibial nerves in an independent Italian cohort of 37 patients (223 sites). Lastly, we analyzed intra-insular (53 patients, 509 sites) and cortico-insular (56 patients, 2585 sites) as well as hypothalamo-insular (1patient, 19 inusla/ 6 hypothalamic sites) effective connectivity using a procedure known as cerebro-cerebral evoked potentials (CCEPs) during which  50 repeated single electrical pulses were delivered between adjacent  insular or cortical sites while recording evoked responses in all other available recording sites using our newly developed method8. We included data if we had coverage in a given brain parcel in at least five subjects. We measured outflow (efferent) and inflow (afferent) evoked responses. This bidirectional protocol provided direct measures of directional connectivity among stimulated and recorded sites within insular subregions and between these and different neocortical sites.

Results

All subjective reports could be grouped into five clear categories: somatosensory (i.e., tingling, numbness, or pressure sensations, 67 pairs, 16 patients), pain and/ or temperature (i.e., noxious or heat sensations occurring either separately or together, 56 pairs, 15 patients), chemical perception (i.e., taste and olfaction, 8 pairs, 6 patients), visceral (i.e., internal bodily sensations of nausea, dizziness, wave-like energy in the body core, throat or head, 34 pairs, 5 patients), and emotional (mostly anxiety and nervousness, 11 pairs, 5 patients). Patient with anxiety responses had higher Beck-Depression and Anxiety Inventory scores and showed significantly increased heart rate during stimulation.  Anatomical localization of responses revealed a four-folded insular organization closely aligned with the underlying microstructure. Pain and temperature responses localized predominantly to the granular dorsal posterior insula (Region I: 44/56 pairs)(Fig.1A) and exhibited a clear somatotopy (leg far posterior, then arm, followed by the oral cavity most anterior); this parcel was the most responsive overall (73% response rate) (Fig. 1B). Peripheral stimulation of the mandibular branch of the trigeminal nerve, the median nerve, and the tibial nerve elicited significant early responses in the same insular region, recapitulating the same somatotopic pattern. Moving ventrally along the posterior insula, most responses within the dysgranular and agranular ventral posterior insula (Region II) were associated with somatosensory sensations across multiple body parts without a clear somatotopic pattern, suggesting a larger segment of the body being coded by these sites. Visceral and emotional sensations were confined to Region III in the anterior middle insula (24 of 34 pairs) (Fig.1C), whereas the insular anterior pole  Region IV was “silent” to direct stimulation (40 of 45 pairs) (Fig.1D), but became significantly active during a salience target-detection task (53/83 electrodes), whenever the salient target appeared. During “click” trials, only the dorsal posterior insula was activated, precisely overlapping with the hand region identified by direct cortical stimulation.

 Next, we examined the causal effective connectivity of each insular region using the CCEP approach. The dorsal posterior insular Region I (pain hub) showed strong, bidirectional connectivity with primary and secondary somatosensory cortices. The anterior middle insular Region III had bilateral connections with the hypothalamus, with significantly stronger connections to rather than from the hypothalamus. In contrast, the insular anterior pole Region IV (salience hub) showed strong bidirectional connectivity with the prefrontal and anterior cingulate as well as lateral temporal cortices. Intra-insular CCEPs indicated weak direct coupling between Region I and IV (Fig.2 B-G). However, Region I  connected bidirectionally with Region II (integrative site), while connectivity to Region III (visceral site) was more directional, stronger from Region I to Region III than the reverse, especially in the form of low-frequency bands (possibly feedback projections). Region IV exhibited pronounced unidirectional connectivity in the high-frequency band (possibly feedforward projections) to both Regions II and III, indicating a top-down, hierarchical influence from the anterior pole onto more integrative posterior and emotional/visceral insula domains.

Conclusion

The results of our comprehensive approach provide causal evidence that the functional architecture of the insular cortex can be refined into four distinct regions, each of which with idiosyncratic microanatomical architecture. Dorsal posterior insula contains a primary nociceptive/thermosensory cortical Region I (with clear somatotopic organization), and a ventral posterior second integrative Region II. Anterior mid insular Region III is involved in processing visceral information with strong output to the hypothalamus. Anterior pole Region IV, which is connected with higher association cortices, was strongly engaged during salience detection and yet remained silent to direct electrical stimulation. This region was the source of strong “top-down” connections to Region II and III, offering a physiological and anatomical means by which higher association cortices can interact with interoceptive insular sites, for monitoring as well as regulating bodily responses when the organisms faces important salient challenges in the environment.      Our causal map of the human insula, along with its connectivity profiles, offers a new framework for studying the role of this important cortical region in chronic pain and neuropsychiatric conditions and offers new targets for insular neuromodulation and biomarker development.
