Introduction

Individuals born preterm (PT) or with complex congenital heart disease (CHD) often present a similar pattern of motor and cognitive difficulties across their neurodevelopmental trajectories.1 Neuroimaging studies in both populations have also documented a similar brain dysmaturation syndrome, commonly characterized by white matter (WM) alterations that persist into childhood and adolescence.2 Nonetheless, the underlying metabolite profile of these WM alterations remains to be investigated. Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (1H-MRSI) is a non-invasive neuroimaging technique that quantifies brain metabolite concentration, which could offer complementary information to the structural WM alterations. Metabolites commonly evaluated include N-acetylaspartate (NAA), choline (Cho), and creatine (Cr), which inform about neuronal integrity, membrane turnover, and energy metabolism, respectively. Prior research reported a reduced NAA/Cho ratio in the frontal WM of neonates born PT or with CHD, suggestive of delayed metabolic maturation.3,4 However, WM metabolic profiles beyond the neonatal period remain insufficiently explored. 

Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to compare absolute metabolite concentrations in the centrum semiovale of cerebral WM among youth born PT or with CHD, as compared to healthy controls, using multi-voxel 1H-MRSI. A secondary aim was to explore whether individual and perinatal clinical risk factors were associated with WM metabolite profiles. We hypothesized that youth born PT or with CHD would present reduced NAA and elevated Cho when compared to controls, and that these metabolite alterations would be associated with group-specific risk factors.

Methods

Youth born PT (<33 weeks’ gestational age), youth born with CHD (>36 weeks’ gestational age) who underwent open-heart surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass within the first two years of life, were enrolled at 16-27 years of age. Exclusion criteria for both clinical groups included congenital infection, genetic or chromosomal abnormalities, multi-organ dysmorphic syndromes, moderate to severe neonatal brain injury or malformation, contraindications to MRI (e.g., pacemaker), and inability to communicate in English or French fluently. We also recruited age- and sex-matched controls (CTL) participants with no history of neurological or developmental conditions from the community. 

Participants underwent a brain MRI at the Montreal Children’s Hospital using a Philips 3T Achieva X system with a 32-channel head coil. A high-resolution three-dimensional T1-weighted (T1w) anatomical image was acquired for each participant (1 mm isotropic voxel; echo time (TE)=3.7 ms, repetition time (TR)=8.1ms, inversion time=1010ms, shot interval=3000ms, flip angle=8). 1H-MRSI data were acquired using a two-dimensional Point RESolved Spectroscopy (2D-PRESS) sequence. The acquisition plane was positioned within the cerebral WM at the level of the centrum semiovale (Figure 1). Acquisition parameters of 1H-MRSI were TE/TR=144/2000ms, spectral bandwidth=2000Hz, number of frequency sampling points=1024, and voxel size=9.58×9.50×15mm³. 

1H-MRSI data were processed using Tarquin software to quantify absolute metabolite concentrations within the chemical shift range of 4.0-0.2ppm, using the unsuppressed water signal as an internal reference. Individual metabolite spectra were generated for Cr, phosphocreatine (PCr), glycerophosphorylcholine (GPC), phosphocholine (PCh), NAA, and N-acetylaspartylglutamate (NAAG). To improve the accuracy of estimation, metabolite concentrations were summed as follows: total NAA (tNAA=NAA+NAAG), total choline (tCho=GPC+PCh), and total creatine (tCr=PCr+Cr). Absolute concentrations were corrected for tissue volume fractions estimated from T1w images using FAST, FSL, adjusted for T1 and T2 relaxation times based on prior literature, and expressed in millimolar (mM). Most representative WM voxels were included if they met the following criteria: high spectral quality (FWHM<0.1ppm; SNR>5), the highest WM volume fraction, and location within the centrum semiovale. One voxel was selected per hemisphere, yielding two voxels per participant, and the final metabolite concentrations consisted of the average of the two voxels. 

Individual characteristics were collected and included age at MRI, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed using the Hollingshead Four-Factor Index questionnaire. The presence of brain abnormalities on conventional MRI was assessed by an experienced neuroradiologist blinded to participants’ medical history. White matter volume (WMV) was estimated from acquired T1w images using the SIENAX toolbox. A collection of other perinatal risk factors was tailored to each clinical population and was extracted from medical records. 

Descriptive analyses were conducted to characterize the sample. Group differences in continuous demographic variables were examined using Kruskal–Wallis tests, and in categorical variables (e.g., sex) using Chi-square tests. Group comparisons in metabolite concentrations were first performed in a simple linear model with group, sex and SES as covariates and then conducted separately for sexes between and within groups using ANCOVA, controlling for SES, with Tukey’s post-hoc tests when appropriate. Multiple comparisons were then corrected using the Benjamin-Hochberg’s method, with statistical significance set at corrected p<0.05. Within each clinical group, relationships with metabolite concentration were evaluated with Pearson correlation analysis for continuous/binary variables and with Spearman correlation analysis for categorical variables. 

Results

Completed data from 52 PT, 48 CHD, and 50 CTL participants were analyzed. No significant group differences in age at MRI, sex, BMI, or brain abnormality were observed. However, SES was significantly lower in both PT and CHD groups when compared to CTL (PT: 43.75 ± 11.78; CHD: 40.75 ± 12.00; CTL: 53.25 ± 10.40; p<0.001). PT participants were born at a mean gestational age of 27.90 weeks (SD = 2.15) with a mean birth weight of 971.33 grams (SD = 217.39). Among the 48 participants with CHD, 41 had a two-ventricle cardiac physiology (including dextro-transposition of the great arteries (n = 17), tetralogy of Fallot (n = 13), ventricular septal defect (n = 4), double outlet right ventricle (n = 3), total anomalous pulmonary venous connection (n = 2), and truncus arteriosus type I (n = 2)). Seven participants had a single-ventricle physiology (pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum (n = 4), double inlet left ventricle (n = 1), Ebstein’s pulmonary atresia (n = 1), and tricuspid atresia (n = 1)). 

No significant group differences were found for tNAA concentrations (p>0.05). In contrast, significant group effects were observed for tCho (p<0.05) and tCr concentrations (p<0.01). Post-hoc analyses demonstrated that individuals born PT exhibited significantly higher tCho concentrations than those with CHD (p<0.01) and significantly higher tCr concentrations than both CHD (p<0.01) and CTL (p<0.01). Notably, the sex-by-group interaction term was significant across all three metabolites (tNAA: p<0.01; tCho: p<0.05; tCr: p<0.01). 

Given the consistence presence of a significant sex-by-group interaction, subsequent group comparison analyses were conducted separately for males and females and then between sexes within each group, controlling for SES and correcting for multiple comparisons. In females, significant group differences were observed in tNAA concentrations only (p<0.05). Post-hoc analyses indicated that PT females had significantly lower tNAA concentrations than CTL (p<0.05; Figure 2(a)). No significant group differences were observed in tCho or tCr concentrations among females (all p>0.05; Figure 2(b) & 2(c)). In contrast, significant group differences were identified in males in tCho (p<0.05) and tCr (p<0.05) but not in tNAA. Post-hoc testing revealed that males born PT had significantly higher tCho (p<0.01; Figure 2(b)) and tCr (p<0.05; Figure 2(c)) than males with CHD and significantly higher tCr (p<0.05; Figure 2(c)) than CTL males. 

Sex comparisons within groups revealed statistically differences in the PT group only, where females presented with significantly lower concentration than males for all three metabolites: tNAA (p<0.001; Figure 2(a)), tCho (p<0.001; Figure 2(b)), and tCr (p<0.001; Figure 2(c)). No significant sex differences in metabolite concentrations were found within the CHD and CTL groups. 

Associations between clinical variables and metabolite concentrations were examined separately in the two clinical groups. In the PT group, no significant associations were observed between metabolite concentrations and any clinical or individual variables, including gestational age, birth weight, pregnancy type, length of hospital stay after birth, WMV or MRI abnormalities (all p>0.05). In the CHD group, metabolite concentrations revealed no significant associations with any CHD-related clinical variables, including age at first cardiac surgery, number of open-heart surgeries, cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time, WMV or MRI abnormalities (all p>0.05). 

Conclusion

This study revealed that youth born PT exhibit sex-specific WM metabolite profiles that are distinct from those in youth born with CHD and CTL peers. Indeed, PT females presented with significantly lower tNAA concentrations compared to CTL peers. As NAA is primarily located in neurons, a reduction implies a disruption in neuronal integrity. Previous literature reported reduced NAA in individuals born PT within the central WM when assessed during infancy and childhood. Previous studies reported lower axonal density in youth born PT using diffusion MRI; however, these observations have not yet been assessed in relation to brain metabolite concentration. Our current findings are suggesting that early life decreased axonal integrity persists into young adulthood and may be more pronounced in PT females. 

Conversely, PT males exhibited higher tCr levels than CTL and CHD males, and higher tCho than CHD males. As tCho and tCr are markers of membrane turnover and of metabolic activity and are predominantly concentrated in glial cells, their elevation may reflect a glial-specific enrichment as potential consequences of perinatal neuroinflammation. Indeed, PT neonates are predisposed to neuroinflammatory cascades within WM because of their frequent experience of intermittent hypoxia. This inflammation may delay astroglial maturation and progress into astrocytic scarring, which was observed in an increased myo-inositol concentration in school-age PT children. While only assessing tCho and tCr, our results could suggest that this astrocytic scarring may endure into adolescence and young adulthood. Fetuses and neonates with CHD also experience chronic hypoxia due to the cumulative burden of their cardiac defects, yet metabolite alterations were not observed in this group. This difference could be explained in part by the group-specific WM vulnerability topography. Indeed, PT neonates frequently present with injuries in the central periventricular WM, including the centrum semiovale, while neonates with CHD most often present with injuries in the anterior or posterior WM. This distinct pattern of injuries likely leads to long-term WM that is spatially different. This hypothesis is coherent with results from previous diffusion studies, which reported lower axonal architecture complexity in tracts crossing the central WM in youth born PT than those born with CHD. 

Within-group comparisons highlighted the presence of sexual dimorphism in WM metabolism among the PT participants. PT males had higher concentrations across all measured metabolites compared to PT females. Integrating these observations with between-group findings suggested distinct pathological phenotypes where PT females showed reduced neuronal integrity and PT males presented with persistent astrogliosis. While previous research identified PT females with higher NAA, Cho, and Cr than males during the neonatal period, suggesting male sex as a risk factor of neurodevelopment, our results revealed a reversal trajectory in young adulthood. This shift may result from the interplay between sex-specific neuroplasticity, pubertal hormonal effects on brain development, and other in/extrinsic factors that were not captured in the present study. Future studies should investigate the associations between epigenetic factors, social determinants, and WM metabolic maturation. 

In summary, by directly comparing WM metabolism in PT and CHD survivors, this study provided novel insights into the underlying mechanisms of brain maturation features in populations at risk for atypical neurodevelopment. The observed sex-specific metabolic alterations may reflect disparities in early-life experiences and sex-dependent WM susceptibility in PT and CHD groups, emphasizing the need for more sex-specific studies of neurodevelopment in clinical populations. Future work looking at other metabolites with longitudinal study designs would improve our knowledge of WM metabolic maturation in clinical populations.
