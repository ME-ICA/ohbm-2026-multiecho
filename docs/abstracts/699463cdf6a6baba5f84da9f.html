Introduction

Skull stripping (SS), or brain extraction, defines the brain mask used in downstream analyses, including tissue segmentation, spatial normalization, and quantitative summaries.1 Traditional tools include the Brain Extraction Tool (BET) in FSL, 3dSkullStrip in AFNI, and the Hybrid Watershed Algorithm (HWA) in FreeSurfer.2-4 These approaches rely on intensity information and morphological assumptions, and performance can vary with anatomy, tissue contrast, and intensity nonuniformity that obscures boundaries. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)-based architectures learn boundaries from expert-drawn reference masks; however, predictions can remain coupled to how the reference boundary is defined.5 In this study, we compare four CNN baselines across human, macaque, and piglet MRI datasets, and quantify sensitivity to clipping and leakage in manual boundary definitions. This design contrasts model-specific sensitivity to boundary definition within each species and differences across species.

Methods

We analyzed MRI from three publicly available datasets: human (n=125), macaque (n=100), and piglet (n=32), each with expert-drawn gold standard masks (GSMs). Images were resampled to 1.0 mm³, cropped to a species-specific field of view, and aligned to a species-specific template; the base pipeline applied no additional intensity normalization. To model variability in manual boundary definition, we generated GSM derived training masks by iterative, isotropic 1 voxel erosion or dilation at the brain boundary (up to five iterations) to induce clipping or leakage, and trained four CNN baselines (VoxResNet, U-Net, 3D U-Net and V-Net) using GSMs and, separately, the boundary-modified masks. Predictions were evaluated against GSMs using Geometric Similarity (SI) for overall agreement, Overlap Fraction (OF) for brain coverage, and Extra Fraction (EF) for non-brain inclusion, and metrics were compared across species, models, and training-mask conditions. To localize failure patterns, we generated voxel-wise false positive (FP) and false negative (FN) error-frequency maps by averaging subject-level FP and FN volumes across subjects, and visualized these maps using mean-intensity projections along three orthogonal axes.

Results

Using GSMs, performance showed a consistent gradient across species: human scans yielded high SI and OF with low EF across architectures, whereas macaque and piglet scans showed lower SI, reduced OF, and higher EF. In the non-human datasets, VoxResNet and V-Net showed larger boundary deviations in macaques, and V-Net showed more frequent over inclusion in piglets. Boundary modified masks produced species and model dependent sensitivity to manual boundary definition. In humans, SI and OF remained high and EF changes were small across clipping- and leakage oriented masks. In macaques and piglets, clipping reduced OF and leakage increased EF, with larger shifts for 3D U-Net and V-Net and smaller shifts for U-Net and VoxResNet (Fig. 1). Voxel-wise FP and FN error-frequency maps localized these effects. Mean intensity projections showed stable FP/FN concentration near midline and basal regions in humans, broader heterogeneity including occipital and ventral frontal regions in macaques, and recurrent errors at anterior olfactory and posterior hindbrain boundaries in piglets (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

SS performance and boundary sensitivity differed by species and architecture. Across species, clipping primarily reduced OF by increasing FN, whereas leakage primarily increased EF by increasing FP. Together, these findings show that boundary definition in training masks systematically shifts model behavior and should be considered when building and comparing cross-species SS pipelines.
