Introduction

With a growing and aging global population, the incidence of age-related disorders such as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is steadily increasing. MCI is characterized by cognitive or memory impairments beyond normal age-related decline and is considered a prodromal stage of dementia, for which no disease-modifying treatment is currently available. An open-label trial with the antidepressant vortioxetine reported beneficial effects on MCI symptoms,1 and vortioxetine has previously been associated with functional connectivity (FC) changes in major depression.2 We therefore investigated whether vortioxetine modulates FC in MCI and whether such effects are dose-dependent.

Methods

In a randomized, double-blind study, 47 patients with MCI received either 10 mg or 20 mg vortioxetine or placebo daily for twelve weeks. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging was acquired at baseline, four weeks, and twelve weeks, using parameters aligned with Human Connectome Project recommendations. Participants also completed a neuropsychological test battery at each time point.

Preprocessing included thermal noise reduction (NORDIC), slice-wise motion and physiological noise correction (SLOMOCO, PESTICA), wavelet despiking, and distortion correction (topup). T1-weighted scans were processed with FreeSurfer and parcellated using the Desikan–Killiany–Tourville (DKT) and Destrieux atlases. Regional time series were extracted as first eigenvariates, and partially correlated against each other, adjusting for white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals as well as bandpass components to limit the FC estimations to 0.01 to 0.10 Hz. We extracted the time series in individual space to avoid delineation errors due to atrophy. The resulting FC matrices were Fisher-z-transformed for further analysis.

Group-level analysis was performed using Network-Based R-statistics, a mixed effects modeling extension of the Network-Based Statistics approach.3 Model comparison using likelihood ratio testing resulted in treatment groups being modeled as continuous dose (0, 10, or 20 mg vortioxetine) and sessions as factor (baseline, four weeks, twelve weeks). The following model was run for the DKT and Destrieux atlases with 1000 random permutations each:

FC ~ session * dose + age + sex (1 | subject)

Agreement between atlases was assessed using concordance correlation. Multiple comparisons were Bonferroni adjusted.

Results

Of the 47 participants included in the analysis (median age 72.9 ± 10.5 years, 13 female). Complete data was available from 34 participants. Neuropsychological testing indicated symptom improvements across all groups.

We did not observe any significant modulatory influence of vortioxetine on FC but an extensive network of mostly reduced FC four weeks after treatment initiation compared to baseline across all groups (ps ≤ 0.003). A similar effect was observed after twelve weeks of treatment (ps ≤ 0.012) without a significant difference to week four. There was also a significant, mostly negative correlation between age and FC (ps < 0.003). The significant networks for the DKT atlas are depicted in Figure 1. The concordance of the significant networks between the atlases was high (rs ≥ 0.97).

The bilateral transverse temporal gyri (Heschl’s gyrus) showed the strongest reduction in FC after four and twelve weeks and with increasing age. FC of the precentral gyrus was markedly reduced after four and twelve weeks of treatment. The insula and posterior cingulate showed strong FC reductions after four, and the caudal anterior as well as the isthmus cingulate cortex after twelve weeks. FC of the cerebellum strongly increased after four and twelve weeks of treatment and with age. Moreover, we observed particular increases in FC over treatment for the cuneus and fusiform gyrus.

Conclusion

Widespread FC reductions in MCI are often interpreted as disease-related changes, but compensatory hyperconnectivity has also been described.4 It has been shown that this hyperconnectivity can be reduced after successful treatment, which is indicated in the current study by symptomatic improvements across groups.5 Moreover, FC reductions four weeks into treatment without further significant changes at twelve weeks together with symptom improvements contradict disease-related changes in FC.

FC adaptations in Heschl’s gyrus (part of the auditory cortex) and the precentral gyrus (part of the somato-motor cortex) may have occurred to compensate for deficits in sensory and motor processing. The default mode network, which includes the anterior and posterior segments of the cingulate cortex, is known to be largely impacted in MCI. Reduced FC of these brain regions potentially constitutes a reversal of previous compensatory hyperconnectivity.

Compensatory hyperconnectivity related to cognitive impairments has also been reported in the cerebellum. Since we observed increased cerebellar FC with age and over the twelve weeks of treatment, its temporal trajectory may differ from that of cerebral hyperconnectivity. Furthermore, as the functional organization of the cerebellum is now recognized as being similar to that of the cerebrum, cerebellar hyperconnectivity may constitute a more efficient compensatory mechanism that takes effect at a later stage and may be promoted by successful therapy.
