Introduction

The transition to adolescence is marked by dramatic increases in adrenal and gonadal hormones and a neurodevelopmental shift from sensorimotor to higher-order associative systems. Despite the widespread anatomical distribution of sex hormone receptors across actively developing cortical regions (e.g., orbitofrontal, parietal cortices) and in subcortical structures (e.g., striatum, amygdala), there is a paucity of human neuroimaging research on pubertal neuroendocrine dynamics. Lifespan brain charts provide population-level insight into age-related change and a starting point for investigating the influences of pubertal hormone influences on adolescent development.1 To this end multivariate, predictive modelling is a powerful tool for identifying brain-phenotype associations from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data. Deep neural networks (DNNs) and explainable artificial intelligence (xAI) approaches have been used to identify brain biomarkers of physical health, inspiring a powerful framework for understanding population-level brain-hormone associations. Leveraging data from the landmark Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD Study), we used DNNs and xAI to identify hormone-sensitive gray matter regions and, then, used population-level approaches from epidemiology to investigate how confounding variables influence predictions. 

Methods

This study used data from 3963 female (Ntrain = 2536, Nvalidation = 634, Ntest = 793) and  5137 male (Ntrain = 3287, Nvalidation = 822, Ntest = 1028) youth ages 9-10 years from the ABCD Study. Preprocessed T1-weighted MRI data from the 5.1 annual release included FreeSurfer estimates of surface area and thickness for 68 cortical regions of interest from the Desikan-Killiany atlas, in addition to estimates of gray matter volume for 13 subcortical regions. Salivary hormone estimates include levels of dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), testosterone (T), and estradiol (E2, only in female youth) from saliva samples, analyzed by Salimetrics. First, DNNs and integrated gradients (implemented in PyTorch v2.6.0) were used to identify hormone-sensitive brain regions and individual-level hormone predictions. Specifically, we trained two fully connected, feed-forward, 4-level DNNs to predict hormone levels from gray matter morphology, one in female and one in male youth. Training and validation data were used for hyperparameter (i.e., nodes per level, batch size, dropout rate, learning rate) tuning and model training, then integrated gradients were used to calculate feature importance for an xAI approach.2 Trained DNNs were applied to the held-out test sample to obtain individual-level hormone predictions from gray matter metrics. Second, we used a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to identify neuroendocrine confounders and mixed-effects models to assess how confounders affected predictions. Specifically, theory-driven causal relationships between variables related to both brain development and hormone levels were modeled in a DAG from which we used dagitty (dagitty.net) to identify a minimally sufficient set of variables that adjust for all identified biasing relationships.3 We, then, regressed individual-level prediction errors (i.e., predicted – actual hormone levels) from the test sample on each of these bias-adjusting variables, as well as MRI- and hormone-related precision variables, using mixed effects models with a random effect of data collection site. Bias-adjusting variables included caregiver education, household income, maternal alcohol and tobacco intake, youth age, waist-to-height ratio, birth weight, physical activity, sleep quality, nutrition, and race/ethnicity. Precision variables included elapsed time between waking and saliva sample collection, elapsed time between sample collection and sample freezing, youth caffeine intake and physical activity before sample collection, and duration of sample collection. Significant influences on DNN hormone prediction errors were assessed at α < 0.05.

Results

In both sexes, cortical surface area and subcortical volume were predictive of individual differences in hormone levels, while cortical thickness was not. In both sexes, volumes of the cerebellum and brainstem were relevant to individual differences in levels of each hormone.

In both sexes, DHEA-predictive regions included the thalamus, lateral occipital cortex, rostral middle frontal gyri, and right parietal lobe. In female youth, regions relevant to individual differences in DHEA levels also included the bilateral postcentral gyri, ventral diencephalon, right putamen, and right fusiform gyrus (Figure 2A); in male youth, bilateral lateral orbitofrontal gyri, left putamen, hippocampus, and caudate (Figure 2B). Post-hoc regressions found that individual levels of DHEA were underestimated in female youth exhibiting earlier pubertal timing (B = -3.35, 95% CI: [-6.01, -0.69]) and slower saliva collection (B=-28.78, 95% CI: [-43.5, -14.1]); and in male youth who were older (B = -0.019, 95% CI: [-0.028, -0.010]) and with greater waist-to-height ratios (B=-1.30. 95% CI: [-2.45, -0.16]).

In both sexes, T-predictive regions included the left superior parietal, superior frontal, lateral occipital gyri, as well as the left thalamus. In female youth, inferior temporal and parietal gyri, left rostral middle frontal gyrus, right precuneus, left putamen, and left insula were also relevant to T levels (Figure 2C). In male youth, left precuneus, right rostral middle frontal gyrus, bilateral caudate, and left hippocampus were additionally relevant (Figure 2D). Individual T levels were overestimated in female youth with slower saliva collection (B=22.3, 95% CI: [9.81, 34.8]) and underestimated in older male youth (B = -0.018, 95% CI: [-0.028, -0.008]). 

In female youth, E2-predictive regions included bilateral superior frontal gyri, precuneus, lateral occipital lobe, left parietal lobe, ventral diencephalon, bilateral thalamus, left putamen and right caudate, (Figure 2E). As with DHEA, individual E2 levels were underestimated in female youth exhibiting earlier puberty timing (B=-0.767, 95% CI: [-1.45, -0.86]) and slower saliva collection (B=-7.64, 95% CI: [-11.4, -3.88]).

Conclusion

Here, we used xAI and population health approaches to identify hormone-sensitive brain regions in female and male youth ages 9-10 years and investigate how individual differences in confounding variables impact hormone predictions. Interestingly, and in contrast to prior findings, no brain regions exhibited hormone-relevant differences in cortical thickness, only in cortical surface area and subcortical volume. Hormone-sensitive gray matter was largely similar between DHEA, T, and E2 and between sexes, but with a few between-sex differences. This similarity may be due to co-expression of hormone receptors across brain regions, promiscuity in hormone signaling across receptors, and collinear hormone levels due to co-occurring increases in DHEA, T, and E2 with age and pubertal development. Predicted hormone levels were consistently lower in female youth with more advanced pubertal development and in older male youth, suggesting that brain-hormone relationships may be changing even in earlier pubertal/adolescent development. This builds on our prior work that found increasing diversity in neurodevelopmental trajectories from pre- to mid-puberty.4 Together, these findings point to increased complexity and individual variability in neuroendocrine development as youth move through puberty.

Consistently hormone-relevant gray matter regions included those known to co-express estrogen and androgen receptors (e.g., cerebellum, brainstem, thalamus, hippocampus, frontal cortex), as well as precuneus, occipital cortex, and superior parietal lobule. Female, but not male, youth exhibited DHEA- and T-relevant differences in postcentral gyri surface area, while male youth exhibited DHEA and T-relevant differences in lateral orbitofrontal gyrus surface area and caudate volume. Notably absent was hormone relevance in the amygdala, which exhibits high androgen receptor expression. Prior literature suggests that amygdala nuclei show patterns of endocrine-mediated development during adolescence that are differentiated, in part, by individual androgen receptor sensitivity.5 Thus, it is possible that these differences precluded the present work from identifying hormone relevance in amygdala volumes, as it did not consider individual nuclei or androgen sensitivity. Future work should build on the hormone relevance found here, and the identified role of pubertal development and age in predictive accuracy, to assess individual trajectories of hormone-mediated gray matter development throughout puberty.
