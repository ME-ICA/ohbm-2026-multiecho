Introduction

A central challenge in cognitive neuroscience is understanding how the brain constructs coherent representations of the world during continuous multisensory experience. Standard laboratory paradigms isolate tightly controlled features but often sacrifice ecological validity, obscuring how perception, language, memory, attention, and emotion interact in real time. Naturalistic paradigms address this gap, yet few datasets combine rich sensory variation with detailed annotation and diverse participant groups. The 101 Dalmatians Dataset [1] advances this direction by providing continuous fMRI recordings during a feature-length narrative presented in audiovisual, auditory-only, or visual-only formats. The inclusion of individuals with congenital blindness or deafness offers a rare opportunity to examine how cortical systems develop and reorganize when major sensory channels are absent from birth. The movie is annotated across a deep hierarchy of features, spanning motion energy, spectro-temporal acoustics, high-level visual and auditory embeddings, semantic scene labels, and subtitle-aligned linguistic embeddings. By pairing naturalistic stimulation with dense computational annotation and systematic manipulation of sensory access, the dataset provides a flexible model system for studying modality-specific pathways, modality-independent representations, and cross-modal plasticity under conditions that approximate real-world cognition.

Methods

Fifty participants underwent 3T fMRI while viewing or listening to an edited version of 101 Dalmatians. Typically sighted and hearing individuals were assigned to audiovisual, auditory-only, or visual-only conditions. Congenitally blind participants experienced the auditory-only version, and congenitally deaf participants viewed the visual-only version, enabling controlled tests of sensory accessibility and cortical reorganization (Figure 1a).

Stimulus annotations span multiple representational levels: motion-energy filters and VGG-19 embeddings for visual features; spectro-temporal power and VGGish embeddings for auditory features; scene-level categorical labels describing characters, objects, and events; and subtitle-aligned linguistic embeddings capturing high-level semantic structure (Figure 1b-c).

All imaging data follow BIDS conventions and include extensive quality-control metrics. The dataset is optimized for voxelwise encoding and decoding, representational similarity analyses, intersubject synchronization, and multivariate analyses of cortical dynamics. Manipulating sensory input across groups enables precise mapping of how low-level sensory signals propagate into higher-order association networks during narrative comprehension. The dataset, fully described in [1], provides transparent documentation of acquisition, preprocessing, and annotation to ensure rigorous and reproducible research.

Results

Quality assessments (Figure 2) confirmed robust and reliable fMRI signals across all participants and sensory conditions, with consistent motion profiles, temporal SNR, and global signal characteristics. Whole-brain intersubject correlation (ISC) [2] quantified stimulus-driven neural coupling within each group.

The audiovisual group showed robust ISC across temporal, parietal, and prefrontal association cortices, reflecting shared high-level processing of narrative content. Sensory cortices exhibited expected modality-specific synchronization: visual regions synchronized during audiovisual and visual-only conditions, whereas auditory regions synchronized during audiovisual and auditory-only conditions.

Sensory-deprived groups demonstrated reliable, modality-appropriate synchronization. Blind participants showed strong ISC in auditory and associative cortices, with reproducible engagement of occipital regions—consistent with cross-modal recruitment of “visual” cortex. Deaf participants showed synchronized activity in visual and parietal cortices during the visual-only movie, indicating intact high-level integration in the absence of auditory input.

All data, annotations, and analysis code are publicly available in the associated repository [3], ensuring transparency, reproducibility, and immediate reusability. Together, these results demonstrate that the dataset provides a validated and versatile platform for studying sensory pathways, modality-independent representations, and the geometry and dynamics of naturalistic brain function, with relevance for cognitive, developmental, and clinical neuroscience.

Conclusion

The 101 Dalmatians Dataset offers a large-scale, deeply annotated, and openly accessible resource for investigating the organizing principles of human cortical function under naturalistic conditions. Its combination of continuous narrative stimulation, hierarchical multimodal annotation, and controlled manipulation of sensory access enables detailed tests of how sensory signals and higher-order representations interact across cognitive domains.

The inclusion of congenitally blind and deaf participants provides a rare opportunity to study cross-modal recruitment and developmental plasticity in a naturalistic context, complementing and extending existing naturalistic fMRI datasets. The dataset is BIDS-compliant, extensively documented, and readily compatible with modern computational approaches, including voxelwise modeling, ISC, RSA, and dynamic network analyses.

By aligning ecological richness with methodological rigor and open accessibility, the dataset offers a strong foundation for advancing theories of multisensory integration, semantic representation, and naturalistic cognition. Its scale, diversity, and representational depth position it as a valuable resource for future large-scale, reproducible neuroscience.
