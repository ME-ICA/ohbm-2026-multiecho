Introduction

Neural representations are increasingly understood to be distributed across multiple cortical regions, rather than localized to single, discrete areas. Retinotopic encoding provides a well-characterized example of this shift: once thought limited to early visual areas, retinotopic information has now been reported across large portions of parietal and frontal cortex1,2,3,4. This distributed architecture raises the question of how the spatial distribution of functional specialization is organized across the cortical sheet, and how it changes gradually at the macroscale beyond the boundaries of classical sensory areas.Recent advances—including large-scale, high-quality retinotopy datasets such as those from the Human Connectome Project1,2, together with analytic frameworks that explicitly model spatial structure on the cortical surface5—now make it possible to ask how distributed patterns of functional specialization are shaped by geometry and interactions between cortical systems, rather than being described post hoc in terms of predefined areas or networks. Here, we ask whether the large-scale layout of retinotopic specialization can be explained by spatial influences embedded in cortical geometry itself, and explore which cortical regions contribute to shaping its distribution, including potential interactions between sensory and transmodal systems5. Finally, while retinotopy provides a well-grounded model system, the same spatial analysis framework could in principle be applied to any cortical statistical map, offering a general approach to uncovering spatial influences that shape the organization of distributed representations.

Methods

We analyzed population receptive field (pRF) derived retinotopic selectivity maps from two independent, openly available fMRI datasets. The primary dataset consisted of high-resolution 7T Human Connectome Project (HCP) retinotopy data1 (181 participants), acquired using standard moving-aperture visual stimuli and projected onto a common fsLR32 cortical surface. An independent NYU retinotopy dataset2 (44 participants) was used for validation. Retinotopic selectivity was quantified at each cortical vertex as the fraction of signal variance explained by the pRF model, yielding whole-cortex statistical maps of functional specialization3. All analyses were performed on previously published, openly available datasets collected with informed consent and approved by the relevant institutional review boards.

To characterize the spatial organization of retinotopic specialization, we introduced Spatial Component Decomposition (SCD), an data-driven machine learning framework grounded in sparse dictionary learning. SCD models a cortical statistical map as the positive recombination of a small number of spatially localized influences constrained by cortical geometry. Each spatial component is anchored to a cortical vertex and defined by three parameters: a source location, a spatial extent over which its influence decays, and a magnitude. Components are implemented as threshold-linear functions of geodesic distance along the cortical surface, ensuring spatial locality while respecting cortical folding. Both facilitative and suppressive influences are identified, but the reconstructed maps are constrained to remain non-negative, reflecting modulation of a positive specialization signal. Importantly, suppressive influences reflect spatial modulation of specialization strength rather than negative activation or task-induced deactivation.

Geodesic distances were computed once on the template cortical surface to capture intrinsic spatial relationships along the cortical sheet, rather than Euclidean distance through volume space. This allowed spatial influences to propagate along the surface in a biologically interpretable manner.

Formally, SCD constitutes a sparse dictionary learning problem in a non-convex parameter space, as component locations, extents, and amplitudes must be jointly optimized. As no closed-form solution exists, we employed a greedy heuristic inspired by matching pursuit. Components were added iteratively by selecting cortical source locations that maximally increased explained variance in the training data, while preserving previously selected sources. After each addition, component parameters were locally refined to improve model fit. Model complexity was controlled using early stopping based on performance on held-out participants.

To assess robustness, Spatial Component Decomposition was trained on the HCP dataset and evaluated using cross-validation across held-out participants, retaining only components that generalized beyond the training set. These components were then tested on an independent NYU retinotopy dataset, with spatial locations fixed and only component amplitudes refit. Performance was measured as the proportion of vertex-wise variance explained across individuals and benchmarked against ceiling estimates derived from group-average maps.

Notably, this approach yields a compact representation of whole-cortex statistical maps, as a small number of interpretable spatial parameters. While applied here to retinotopic selectivity, Spatial Component Decomposition is designed to operate on arbitrary cortical statistical maps defined on the surface.

Results

Despite the complex, fine-grained spatial structure of retinotopic selectivity, its large-scale organization was captured by a compact set of spatial influences: eight sources per hemisphere explained over 92% of the explainable variance. Notably, the decomposition converged on the same number of components in the left and right hemispheres, indicating a strong degree of hemispheric symmetry in the underlying spatial organization.

The identified components exhibited a highly constrained structure. In each hemisphere, only two facilitative (positive) sources were required to account for the overall spread of retinotopic specialization, while six suppressive (negative) sources systematically reshaped this distribution across the cortical sheet. This balance between a small number of driving influences and multiple constraining influences provided an accurate reconstruction of the full retinotopic selectivity map, despite its apparent complexity at the vertex level.

The dominant spatial influence identified by the decomposition was anchored in V1. This single facilitative source accounted for the majority of the spatial spread of retinotopic selectivity across posterior cortex, extending up to ~60 mm along the cortical surface, far beyond the anatomical boundaries of early visual areas. Remarkably, the overall retinotopic organization of the occipital cortex could be explained by this single positive influence, with finer spatial structure emerging through the action of competing suppressive sources rather than additional driving inputs.

Beyond V1, only one additional facilitative source was consistently identified, located in the intraparietal sulcus in area LIP, at the distal end of the dorsal visual stream. The presence of this parietal source indicates that retinotopic specialization is not solely driven by early visual cortex but is also reinforced at higher-order stages of visuospatial processing. In contrast, the decomposition did not capture positive sources within frontal retinotopic regions. This suggests that retinotopic influences in frontal cortex are weaker, more spatially distributed, or more variable across individuals, and may require additional data or greater methodological sensitivity to be reliably identified.

Suppressive spatial influences were more numerous and more widely distributed than facilitative ones. These negative sources arose from both competing primary sensory regions and transmodal hubs associated with the default mode network (DMN). Notably, suppressive influences were also identified within primary visual cortex itself, with focal sources located at the occipital pole. Rather than eliminating retinotopic selectivity, these components locally attenuated its expression relative to that predicted by distance from V1 alone. When projected into a low-dimensional functional connectivity embedding, suppressive sources systematically occupied peripheral regions of this manifold, rather than the cortical core.

Several suppressive sources were anchored in somatosensory regions and in eccentric visual zones that were not stimulated in the experimental paradigm, indicating competition from representational systems unrelated to the presented visual input. These sources aligned with the peripheral ends of a canonical macroscale functional gradient spanning sensory and transmodal systems. The spatial extent of suppressive influences ranged from ~15 to 50 mm, allowing them to exert long-range constraints on retinotopic organization well beyond their cortical origin. Importantly, these negative sources did not correspond to an absence of retinotopy, but to systematic reductions in specialization strength relative to geometric expectations.

The spatial configuration of facilitative and suppressive sources was highly consistent across hemispheres and generalized to an independent dataset, indicating that the identified spatial influences reflect reproducible features of cortical organization.

Conclusion

Together, these findings suggest that the large-scale organization of retinotopic specialization is governed by a small number of spatially structured influences embedded in cortical geometry. A dominant facilitative influence originating in primary visual cortex provides a global scaffold for visual specialization, extending far beyond the classical boundaries of early visual areas. This broad influence is not expressed uniformly across cortex, but is progressively refined through the action of multiple suppressive influences that locally constrain and reshape the spatial embedding of retinotopic information.

Importantly, these suppressive influences arise from both competing primary sensory systems and transmodal hubs associated with the default mode network, indicating that retinotopic organization might be shaped by interactions across functionally heterogeneous systems representing both sensory and abstract information. Even within primary visual cortex, focal suppressive influences at the occipital pole reveal internal spatial structure that cannot be explained by feedforward sensory drive alone. Together, these results suggest that retinotopic specialization reflects an emergent equilibrium between a small number of driving inputs and widespread spatial competition across cortical systems.

More broadly, our findings support a view of cortical organization in which functional specialization emerges through spatial competition for cortical territory, constrained by the geometry of the cortical sheet.

By explicitly modeling both facilitative and suppressive spatial influences, Spatial Component Decomposition provides a compact and interpretable framework for studying how distributed representations are embedded in cortex. While demonstrated here using retinotopic selectivity, the approach is agnostic to representational content and can be applied to arbitrary cortical statistical maps, including those derived from sensory, motor, or higher-order cognitive domains. As such, SCD offers a general strategy for uncovering the spatial principles that shape large-scale brain organization, bridging gradient-based, areal, and network-level accounts within a unified geometric framework.
