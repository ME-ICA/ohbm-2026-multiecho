Introduction

Subjective tinnitus is a phantom auditory percept heard with the absence of any external sound source, typically occurring after damage to the peripheral auditory system  and is closely associated with hearing loss1. The exact pathophysiology of tinnitus remains elusive, notably the mechanisms behind the generation of the phantom sound and the chronicization of the condition. There is broad agreement that the tinnitus percept arises from the central auditory system rather than the inner ear. After hearing loss, neural hyperactivity along the auditory pathway and a maladaptive tonotopic reorganization of the auditory cortex would lead to chronic tinnitus. Recent computational models suggest that tinnitus emergence results from the combination of increased neural noise from the cochlear nuclei (bottom-up) and altered auditory priors (top-down)2.

Human case-control studies with scalp EEG have reported abnormal brain patterns associated with tinnitus, namely increased δ, α and β power in frontal areas along with increased γ power and decreased α power in auditory areas3. However, it is challenging to disentangle if these abnormalities are directly related to the tinnitus generation rather than to comorbidities or secondary effects.

In this study, intracranial EEG signals were recorded from an epileptic patient with chronic tinnitus while manipulating tinnitus intensity using two behavioral paradigms: Residual Inhibition (RI)4 and Low-Intensity-Noise Tinnitus Suppression (LINTS)5. This setup enabled precise evaluation of neural activity through invasive recordings and accurate characterization of tinnitus-specific processes, with the participant serving as their own control. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents only the second published attempt to perform invasive investigations of tinnitus mechanisms in humans4, with the first revealing a wide “tinnitus network” extending far beyond circumscribed auditory areas.

Methods

The participant was a middle-aged male with chronic tinnitus (right-lateralized, whistle-like) undergoing intracranial monitoring in the right cerebral hemisphere using depth electrodes (sEEG) in the context of a presurgical evaluation of a medically intractable epilepsy. Subsequent audiometric evaluation revealed a moderate hearing loss in his right ear. The patient provided written informed consent (INSERM project: C19-55; ethical approval: CPP Paris VI).

The experimental procedure included two paradigms using free-field acoustic stimulation to elicit subjective tinnitus suppression.

Residual Inhibition (RI): A loud 30-second white-noise masking sound was presented, causing short-term tinnitus reduction. Subjective tinnitus suppression was then rated by the participant every 10-s after sound offset until it receded. In total, 40 trials led to 240 ratings. 

Low-Intensity-Noise Tinnitus Suppression (LINTS): The LINTS sound consisted in a tinnitus-matched 15-s noise (centered around 11kHz ± 1 octave) played at a threshold slightly above detection level (15 dB SL) causing tinnitus suppression during sound presentation. Three different conditions involving either the LINTS sound, sham sound (noise centered around 1kHz ± 1 octave, played at the same volume), or silence were used, leading to 60 trials in total. Subjective tinnitus suppression ratings were obtained after each sound offset. Silent trials were excluded from EEG analyses.

Electrode locations were determined from the pre- and post-implantation MRIs and CT scan using the Epiloc toolbox and signal processing was performed with MNE-Python. sEEG signals were re-referenced to a common average. Then, only the 30 gray-matter contacts were retained for further analysis, covering the hippocampus (Hp), the amygdala (Amyg), the orbitofrontal (OFC) and ento-/peri-rhinal cortices (dEC/PRC), the middle and superior temporal (MTG, STG), supramarginal (SMG) and angular gyri (AG), and the temporal pole (TP). 

For the RI paradigm, continuous signals were segmented into 200 10-s epochs corresponding to the period preceding a rating; for the LINTS paradigm, continuous signals were segmented into 40 15-s epochs corresponding to sound presentations (LINTS or sham). Multitaper spectral analysis (frequency range: 0.1–200Hz, frequency smoothing bandwidth: 2 Hz) was performed. Neural power spectra were parameterized into periodic/oscillatory and aperiodic components using the specparam toolbox. Aperiodic-corrected spectral power was extracted for each canonical frequency band (δ 0.5–4 Hz, θ 4–7 Hz, low-α 7–10 Hz, high-α 10–13 Hz, low-β 13–20 Hz, high-β 20–30 Hz, γ 30–70 Hz) and full spectral power was extracted for high-frequency activity (HFA: 70–150 Hz). 

For each frequency band, linear mixed models examined the relationship between spectral power and tinnitus suppression (with trials as random factor), with post-hoc tests assessing channel-specific effects in presence of significant channel-by-power interaction. Results were considered significant for p-values under 0.05.

Results

Both paradigms managed to elicit tinnitus suppression. RI produced on average an initial 58% tinnitus suppression (min: 3%; max: 89%) and a recovery time of 60s (min: 0s; max: 150s). As expected during the LINTS paradigm, no amount of suppression was reported during silence, and significantly more suppression was reported during the LINTS sound than during the sham sound (63% vs. 39%, p=0.0002, independent t-test). 

Spectral parameterization revealed that sEEG power spectra were mainly dominated by δ, low-α and β oscillations, as indicated by the presence of spectral peaks within those bands in most trials and channels.

During the RI task, tinnitus suppression was significantly associated with (1) a widespread increase of low-α power (STG, dEC, SMG, AG, Amyg) and a decrease in TP; (2) an increase of either low- or high-β power in MTG, PRG, SMG, Amyg; (3) an increase of γ power in MTG; (4) a widespread increase of HFA (MTG, dEC, TP, AG, SMG, Hp). 

During the LINTS task, tinnitus suppression was significantly associated with (1) an increase of θ power in MTG, AG and a decrease in STG; (2) a widespread decrease of α power (OFC, STG, MTG, SMG); (3) an increase of β power in AG, dEC; (4) a widespread increase of HFA (MTG, dEC, TP, SMG, AG, Hp). 

Conclusion

Both tasks elicited mostly convergent behavioral and electrophysiological effects. They succeeded in consistently inducing subjective tinnitus suppression as attested by the self-evaluated ratings. In both tasks, tinnitus suppression was associated with widespread activation of temporo-parietal and hippocampal areas as revealed by HFA. β activity was also found to positively correlate with tinnitus suppression in temporo-parietal regions. β rhythms are thought to reflect the maintenance of sensory predictions: β modulations might therefore reflect the shift of auditory priors caused by the RI noise or the LINTS sound. However, divergent effects on low-α oscillations were observed between tasks, possibly due to differences in task structure and time-windows rather than to tinnitus-specific processes. Overall, these results indicate that tinnitus suppression by RI or LINTS is mediated by mostly common neural processes despite the use of different strategies.

These results constitute the first electrophysiological investigation of the LINTS effect. They also extend and partly reproduce the reported findings in the only other published study using invasive recordings with a tinnitus patient4. The association between decreased low-frequency oscillations (δ/θ/low-α) and tinnitus suppression was not conclusively reproduced, possibly due to different analysis methods and electrode coverage. However, we confirm the involvement in the perception of tinnitus of a large network of cortical and subcortical regions, extending beyond auditory cortices.
