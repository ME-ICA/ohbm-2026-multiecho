Introduction

Patients undergoing neurosurgical resection frequently suffer from cognitive impairments after surgery, the underlying mechanisms of which are poorly understood. Given that the network of white matter connections (structural connectivity) is essential for supporting brain function, information about the impact of resection on this network is likely needed to understand impairment in specific cognitive functions. The quantification of this impact requires reliable estimates of longitudinal structural connectivity changes.

It has recently been pointed out that quantification of longitudinal structural connectivity is biased by variations in tractography reconstruction, manifesting as spurious connectivity changes in the entire brain, including unaffected regions.1 To address these biases, a robust longitudinal quantitative tractography pipeline was developed, showing promising results for epilepsy surgery patients.1

In this work, we adapt this robust pipeline to study the impact of resection on patients with a glioma, a malignant infiltrative brain tumor, for whom cognitive impairments cannot be adequately explained by cortical location or clinical variables alone.2,3 We use longitudinal structural connectivity changes quantified by the robust pipeline to successfully find network correlates of various postsurgical cognitive impairments in this patient group. Furthermore, we demonstrate that conventional cross-sectional analysis fails to do so, likely due to low test-retest reliability of longitudinal connectivity estimates.

Methods

Retrospective data of 87 patients with unilateral glioma who underwent resection surgery were used. Patients underwent diffusion-weighted MRI (DWI) before (T0) and three months after (T3) surgery. Patients completed neuropsychological screening at T3, testing verbal fluency, working memory (digit span backwards), information processing speed (symbol digit coding), reaction time, cognitive flexibility (task switching), and inhibition (Stroop test). Outcomes were corrected for effects of age, education and sex, and standardized to Z-scores. Patients were classified as impaired on a task when Z ≤ -1.5.

DWI images were pre-processed using MRtrix3, after which Orientation Distribution Functions (ODFs) for white matter, grey matter and cerebrospinal fluid were acquired with Single-Shell 3-Tissue Constrained Spherical Deconvolution (SS3T-CSD). Parcellation was performed using the convolutional neural network-based SLANT segmentation model.

Figure 1 illustrates key differences between the cross-sectional and robust pipelines. In the cross-sectional pipeline (Figure 1A), tractograms of ten million streamlines were generated using white matter ODFs in each session separately, applying Anatomically Constrained Tractography (ACT) with tissue segmentations derived from ODFs to allow tracking in glioma-infiltrated white matter.4 Tractograms were weighted with Spherical-deconvolution Informed Filtering of Tractograms (SIFT2).

In the robust pipeline (Figure 1B), patient-specific templates were created by combining T0 and T3 images with rigid transformations from Freesurfer’s robust unbiased template construction and non-linear refinement with symmetric image normalization (SyN) in ANTs. Tissue ODFs from both sessions were averaged in each patient’s template, excluding T3 ODFs in resection cavities from averaging to preserve T0 ODFs in resected structures. Ten million streamlines were generated with ACT in each patient’s template and weighted on average white matter ODFs with SIFT2. T0 connectivity was calculated using SIFT2 initialized with these weights on the template tractogram and T0 ODFs. T3 connectivity was calculated in the same way using T3 ODFs, additionally forcing streamlines intersecting the resection cavity to zero weight.

Parcels were assigned to Yeo’s 7 functional networks by overlapping the 7 network atlas with a SLANT segmentation of the MNI152 template. Connectivity differences from pre- to post surgery within and between each of the 7 networks were calculated using estimates from both pipelines separately, resulting in two sets of 28 network-wise connectivity difference variables per patient. Random forest classifiers were trained on these variables to predict impairment in each cognitive task. Performance was assessed using Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC AUC), cross-validating in 100 data splits. Statistical significance was assessed with 1000 iterations of permutation testing, correcting p-values for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Shapley additive explanations were calculated to determine predictor importance. Finally, as we hypothesized that the robust pipeline would outperform the cross-sectional pipeline by avoiding spurious connectivity changes in unaffected regions, we assessed T0-T3 test-retest reliability by calculating intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) for all connectivity values between parcels of the full SLANT parcellation in patients’ contralesional hemispheres.

Results

Random forest models trained on network connectivity changes estimated by the robust longitudinal pipeline predicted postsurgical cognitive impairment in the following cognitive functions (Figure 2A): verbal fluency (AUC = 0.712, p = 0.015), cognitive flexibility (AUC = 0.716, p = 0.023), and inhibition (AUC = 0.661, p = 0.034). Models trained on cross-sectionally estimated connectivity changes did not predict impairment in these cases (AUC < 0.65, p > 0.15). Working memory, information processing speed, or reaction time could not be predicted from either set of metrics (AUC < 0.62, p > 0.05 in all cases).

Inspection of Shapley values revealed function-specific disconnection patterns. Most important predictors were: high connectivity loss between the Default-Mode Network (DMN) and the Salience Network (SN) for verbal fluency impairment, high connectivity loss within the Fronto-Parietal Network (FPN) for cognitive flexibility impairment, and high connectivity loss within the Dorsal Attention Network (DAN) for inhibition impairment.

Contralesional reliability differed markedly between methods (Figure 2B): average ICC was 0.536 ± 0.233 (mean, std) in the cross-sectional pipeline and 0.964 ± 0.085 in the robust pipeline. Proportions of values in the “poor” to “moderate” range (ICC < 0.75) in the cross-sectional and robust pipelines were 80.0% and 2.5% respectively. Importantly, proportions of values in the “excellent” range (ICC ≥ 0.9) were 2.9% and 92.4% respectively.

Conclusion

We robustly quantified longitudinal structural connectivity changes in individual glioma patients, revealing network-level predictors of postsurgical cognitive impairment in multiple cognitive domains. This represents an improvement over earlier studies, which predicted either only a general cognitive impairment score3 or impairment on a single task.5  An important factor in the failure of cross-sectional analysis is likely its low test-retest reliability, scoring at most “moderate” in the majority of values, while the robust pipeline generates estimates almost exclusively in the “excellent” range, avoiding the previously noted problem of spurious connectivity changes.1

We uncovered three distinct correlates for specific cognitive impairments, which can provide insights into the mechanisms behind impairment in these functions beyond the glioma patient population. Firstly, we found that loss of intra-FPN connectivity is predictive of cognitive flexibility impairment, reflecting the well-established importance of the FPN for executive function. Secondly, for verbal fluency, where patients are asked to list words, we found that connectivity loss between the DMN and SN was predictive of impairment, possibly indicating a disruption of the SN’s role in recruiting the DMN for the mind-searching aspect of verbal fluency. Finally, for inhibition, we found that intra-DAN loss of connectivity was predictive of impairment, which might relate to a deficit in focussing attention on the correct stimulus during the Stroop test.

Our findings demonstrate the value of robust longitudinal quantitative tractography in identifying disruptions underlying cognitive impairment in neurosurgical cohorts. Furthermore, if the contribution of direct and downstream effects of resection on the structural network are disentangled,1 our analyses could be extended to pre-operatively predict the impact of a virtual resection plan on the identified network connections. Subsequently, cognitive risks could be assessed pre-operatively, allowing surgeons to make more informed decisions in the trade-off between maximizing surgical benefit and minimizing postsurgical cognitive impairments.
