Introduction

Due to the inherently high-dimensionality of task-based functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI), down-sampling voxels is critical for whole-brain decoding. The degree of down-sampling is typically determined through a parameter optimized via cross-validation (CV) and traditionally selected using the one standard error (1SE) rule. However, the 1SE rule does not account for the inherent dependency in fMRI data structure within subjects and sessions, which can result in biased parameter selections. We propose an alternative rule, the Fold-wise Comparison Approach (FCA), which compares CV-folds across different parameter settings and selects the most down-sampled parameter that does not perform significantly worse than the best-performing one. We compared the FCA with the 1SE rule across three studies. In the first conceptual simulation, we evaluated the general suitability of both approaches for parameter selection based on performance outcomes. Our results showed that the 1SE rule was influenced by outliers, whereas the FCA remained robust against them. In the second study, we simulated simplified voxel grids and constructed decoders across different decoding techniques. The results revealed that the 1SE rule was again affected by outliers, leading to the systematic false exclusion of relevant brain signals, while the FCA showed greater resistance to outliers. In the third study, we conducted an fMRI experiment and trained brain decoders for different experimental conditions. Our results indicated that the FCA decoders generally included more voxels and demonstrated performance that was significantly better or at least comparable to those selected using the 1SE rule. In conclusion, our findings suggest that the FCA offers a more reliable method for selecting down-sampling parameters in fMRI decoders, exhibiting greater resistance to outliers without compromising performance.

Methods

Study 1 serves as a proof of concept. We simulated decoder performance for varying down-sampling parameter values and found that performance reaches its peak at the highest parameter values. This emulates the performance of a brain decoder, which improves as a function of the number of voxels.

In Study 2, we compared the FCA method to the outcomes of the uncorrected parameter selection through CV and the 1SE rule. We emulated fMRI data by creating simplified voxel grids where some voxels were correlated with a task-related dependent variable Y, while others were random. We used two decoding approaches to create decoders that predict Y based on the voxel grid. To determine the optimal parameter that regulates decoder parsimony (i.e., the number of voxels to be included in the decoder), we conducted a CV, and then selected the parameter based on the best rule, the 1SE rule, and FCA, respectively. 

Our studies 1 and 2 utilize simulated fMRI data, which provides a controlled environment for systematically evaluating performance differences. However, testing our method on actual fMRI data is crucial for several reasons. Firstly, real fMRI data presents a level of complexity and unpredictability not found in simulations. This includes inherent noise, variability in brain activity, and artifacts that arise from physiological and technical sources. By applying our method to real data, we can better understand how it performs under these realistic conditions, providing insights into its robustness and reliability. Secondly, real-world data encompasses a broader range of scenarios and subject variability, which simulations might not fully capture. Lastly, real fMRI data allows for the identification of practical challenges and limitations that might not be apparent in simulations. This can lead to improvements and refinements in the methodology. In Study 3, we tested our method using real fMRI data from a task specifically designed to activate the classic mentalizing-related brain network. This task is sufficiently complex, incorporating multiple experimental conditions, which allows us to thoroughly evaluate the method's effectiveness in capturing dynamic and nuanced brain activity associated with mentalizing processes.

Results

In Study 1, parameter selection based on the best average performance strongly depends on the optimum parameter. High values of the optimum parameter led to a high variance in parameter selection. Outliers had almost no effect on the results of the uncorrected CV. 

The 1SE correction resulted in lower parameter selections than the uncorrected CV, leading to more parsimonious decoders. However, the parameter selection was influenced by outliers. When there were no outliers, the optimum parameter had a strong effect on the parameter selection. Furthermore, the variance was significantly increased when the optimum parameter is high. 

The FCA remained unaffected across various simulation settings, indicating its resilience to outliers and stability even with slowly converging decoders. The results suggest that the FCA yields the most stable and reasonably parsimonious parameter selections. 

In Study 2, the simulation procedure was repeated 1,000 times, and the results are presented in Figure 1. Generally, an uncorrected CV tends to include more non-signal voxels in the decoder than both correction techniques. This often leads to the detection of more signal voxels as well, since larger decoders naturally encompass more signal voxels. When there were no outliers, the 1SE rule and our proposed FCA produced similar results. However, in the presence of outliers, the detection performance of the 1SE rule significantly decreased, while the FCA remained closer to the detection rate of the uncorrected CV. Notably, while both the 1SE rule and FCA often rule out most non-signals and have comparable specificity, the 1SE rule tends to rule out more signals than the FCA. This suggests that the 1SE rule often produces more parsimonious decoders at the cost of systematically excluding signals, which the FCA can retain, showing the efficacy of fold-wise comparison over the 1SE rule.

In Study 3, we conducted paired t-tests to compare the predictive performance of the correction technique within each random split. Across all tasks and decoding techniques, there is a strong tendency for the decoder corrected by FCA with p = 0.2 to perform significantly better than when corrected with the 1SE rule. When no correction was applied, decoder performance was often higher than that of decoders using the FCA. However, this came at the cost of larger decoders that included more voxels. The FCA favours more liberal parameter selections than the 1SE rule, identifying a parameter setting that balances the trade-off between parsimony and predictive performance. This aligns with our previous simulations, which demonstrated that the 1SE rule tends to be excessively conservative in the presence of outliers, even when it adversely affects decoding performance. Thus, our results suggest that the FCA provides a more sensitive approach to determining parsimony levels for decoders compared to the 1SE rule. 

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current research introduces the FCA as a robust method for selecting parsimonious parameters in whole-brain decoding, addressing the limitations of traditional approaches like the 1SE rule. By demonstrating the FCA’s resilience to outliers and its ability to retain task-relevant voxels, the study provides empirical evidence of its effectiveness across multiple contexts. Overall, our studies offer a significant advancement in the methodology of brain decoding, improving both reliability and clarity in parameter selection.
