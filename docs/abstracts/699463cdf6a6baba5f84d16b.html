Introduction

Modern medicine heavily relies on individual health scores that summarize complex physiological states into one informative value. Examples are BMI, which gives insights into a person's adiposity, or blood pressure that informs on cardiovascular risk. These metrics are actionable scores that facilitate informed assessment, risk stratification, and treatment plans in clinical workflows. Initial efforts have been made to extend these indices to brain health, such as brain age models1 and polyneuro scores,2 which can be especially informative for the field of (neuro-)psychiatry where neurobiological mechanisms play a key role. Nevertheless, these methods, and by extension most existing approaches in the clinical field, focus on predefined clinical endpoints, predicting specific diagnoses or classifying a specific diagnosis versus controls. Such diagnosis-focused approaches contradict the growing consensus that mental disorders represent a continuum of deviations from typical health, rather than distinct categories, and fail to capture the heterogeneity and comorbidity inherent to psychiatric disorders. In this study, we aim to shift the attention from predicting the presence of a specific disorder (diagnosis-centered) to estimating the probability of an individual being healthy (health-centered). To this end, we estimate a Biological Brain Health Index (B-BHI), a probabilistic index, derived by applying supervised classification to multivariate deviation patterns obtained from normative brain functioning. The B-BHI estimates the probability of healthy brain functioning, thus enabling us to potentially inform multiple levels of the clinical process. A decreasing B-BHI could indicate emerging pathologies, even before observable symptoms are present (early diagnosis), whereas an increasing probability after treatment could suggest an effective treatment response.

Methods

To estimate the B-BHI, for each imaging-derived phenotype (IDP), a normative model fj(·) is first trained exclusively on healthy reference data to learn expected values as a function of covariates such as age, sex, and site. The normative models are estimated using Hierarchical Bayesian Regression (HBR) with a Sinh-Arcsinh (SHASH) likelihood and a cubic B-spline basis function with 5 knots.3  HBR enables removing the site-effect from estimated deviations, providing the possibility to combine multiple datasets collected using different recording devices.4 These normative models are then used to obtain deviation (z-)scores for each individual in the left-out test set, including typical individuals and clinical patients, irrespective of diagnosis, quantifying how much an individual's IDPs deviate from the norm of the population, taking into account the estimated population variability.5 For a participant with covariates c ∈ Rq and observed j-th IDP values xj, deviation is expressed as a normative z-score: 

zj = (xj - fj(c)) / (σj(c)), 

where σj(c) denotes the square root of the sum of epistemic (i.e., parametric) and aleatoric (i.e., population) uncertainty. Putting all these z-scores across p IDPs together yields a confound-adjusted deviation vector z ∈ Rp, summarizing multivariate departures from the normative distribution. 

We propose to feed the resulting z-scores to a classifier to estimate the B-BHI, i.e., the posterior probability of an individual being healthy:

p̂ = P(Y = 1| z ), 

where Y = 1 and Y = 0 respectively denote labels for a healthy individual and a patient. To this end, a supervised classifier g(·) is trained to map z-scores to the B-BHI. To benchmark this idea, we used two multi-site resting-state neuroimaging datasets: i) an MEG dataset that is composed of Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN), Boys Town National Research Hospital (BTH), the Open MEG Archive (OMEGA), the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH), and the Mother of Unification Studies (MOUS) and includes 1,931 individuals of which 1,734 are typical and 197 have a clinical diagnosis; ii) an EEG dataset that is pooled from the Healthy Brain Network dataset (HBN), the Multimodal Resource for Studying Information Processing in the Developing Brain (MIPDB), the Two Decade Brainclinics Research Archive for Insights in Neurophysiology (TDBrain) dataset, and an EEG dataset collected as part of the Dortmund Vital Study (DVS), comprising 2,906 individuals; 1,000 healthy and 1,906 patients. Detailed demographic information of the data can be found in Figure 1a. Both datasets are processed using the same pipeline implemented in the MEGaNorm package3, including resampling to 1,000 Hz, 1-45 Hz bandpass filtering, average referencing, artifact removal using ICA, segmentation, computation of the Power Spectral Density (PSD), and parameterization of the PSD into oscillatory and non-oscillatory components. Then, the relative θ, α, β, and γ power spectral densities are extracted for each individual, averaged across all sensors. 

To estimate the B-BHI mapping, we then evaluate two classifiers, Random Forest (RF) and Logistic Regression (LR). We use a repeated stratified nested K-fold cross-validation procedure for each of the two classifiers to obtain unbiased estimates of predictive performance. The outer K-fold loop stratifies participants into balanced healthy and patient subsets for performance evaluation, while an inner K-fold loop performs hyperparameter tuning. This process is repeated 5 times with different fold partitions to mitigate variance due to sampling fluctuations and to obtain stable generalization estimates. Predictive performance is assessed using AUC on the held-out test folds. We then used the best classifier for estimating B-BHI in the test set. Figure 1b shows a schematic visualization of the methodology. 

Results

Mean AUC across 5 folds is highest for the RF classifiers, for both EEG (M = 0.66, SD = 0.02) and MEG (M = 0.69, SD = 0.04), compared to LR (EEG 0.59, 0.02; MEG: 0.63, 0.02). Therefore, RF is used for subsequent analyses. ROC curves, along with their 95% confidence intervals, are plotted in Figure 2a. The AUC values and corresponding ROC curves show above-chance performance, indicating that the RF classifiers can, based on multivariate deviation scores, discriminate between healthy and clinical individuals, irrespective of specific clinical diagnoses. Then, kernel density distributions of predicted probabilities of health for healthy vs. all clinical individuals are plotted in Figure 2b. While healthy individuals overall have higher predicted probabilities, the distributions are broad and overlapping, supporting the idea that these groups are not easily separable, but rather on a continuum. A difference between EEG and MEG can be noted, possibly due to the difference in included clinical populations. That is, the MEG data mainly consists of Parkinson's disease patients, a neurological disorder, and therefore, differences in deviation scores between healthy and clinical individuals may be more pronounced than in the EEG dataset, which primarily includes non-neurological disorders such as Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder and Anxiety Disorder.

Conclusion

With this study, we take a step towards deriving a brain health index from physiological brain recordings. The proposed B-BHI quantifies the probability that an individual’s brain profile is consistent with the healthy population. The methodological choices underlying the B-BHI follow from both conceptual and statistical considerations. By constructing all features as normative z-scores, the input space is explicitly corrected for covariates such as age, sex, and site, allowing the classifier to focus on biologically meaningful deviations rather than demographic or acquisition-related variance. Moreover, the transdiagnostic formulation of the learning problem reflects the hypothesis that many psychiatric and neurological disorders share common deviation signatures when viewed through the lens of normative modeling. Pooling diverse patient groups into a single non-healthy class encourages the classifier to learn boundaries that generalize across disorders, thus framing B-BHI as a broad index of brain health rather than a disease-specific biomarker. To capture the complex, multivariate structure of these deviations, we employ a non-linear classifier such as RF, which can model interactions and non-additive effects that would be inaccessible to linear methods. Although individual trees produce piecewise decision surfaces, the ensemble’s aggregated output yields a smooth probability landscape across z-space, supporting the interpretation of B-BHI as a continuous measure of proximity to the healthy distribution. Also, by expressing an individual's brain health as a probability, B-BHI adopts a scale that directly expresses how likely an individual’s multivariate deviation profile is to originate from the normative population. This probabilistic formulation facilitates interpretation, supports thresholding for different clinical or research contexts, and enables future extensions to longitudinal tracking of brain health trajectories. 

As a first step toward deriving B-BHI, the proposed approach is subject to several limitations. First, the dependence on data-driven model selection implies that the optimal classifier is not uniquely identifiable, but instead varies across resampled training sets. This reflects a fundamental trade-off between unbiased performance estimation and the definition of a single, stable decision function, thereby limiting immediate clinical deployment. Second, the overall generalization performance of the classifier was only moderate and could possibly be improved by adding more detailed features. Lastly, the use of classifier outputs as probabilistic health indices depends critically on probability calibration. However, reliable calibration is not guaranteed in complex, high-dimensional learning problems, particularly under class imbalance. Even when calibration strategies are applied, probabilistic estimates may remain sensitive to sampling variability, limiting their interpretability as absolute measures of brain health.

Besides the limitations related to supervised methods, there are other aspects to consider further. We only used functional oscillatory features averaged across all sensors, limiting the level of detail of information. Future work could focus on lobe-wise or source-localized features, non-oscillatory components, as well as functional and structural features derived from other neuroimaging modalities. Adding more distinct features might improve classifier performance and lead to more reliable estimates. 

Overall, B-BHI establishes a conceptual and methodological foundation for a shift from diagnosis-centered frameworks toward probabilistic, transdiagnostic representations of brain health. By embedding normative population references into a unified probabilistic index, this work opens avenues for future brain health models that can support early risk stratification, longitudinal monitoring, and personalized clinical decision-making across a wide range of neurological and psychiatric conditions.
