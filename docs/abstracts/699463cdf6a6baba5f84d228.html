Introduction

In daily life, our brains constantly encode a vast array of experiences into not only visual but also tactile memories. The capacity to control these memories, i.e., to enhance (remind) desirable memories and to suppress (forget) unwanted memories, is an impactful cognitive function, allowing us to regulate the accessibility of our thoughts, serving a critical role for emotional stability and mental health.1 In vision, this process involves active, top-down regulation where executive control networks in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) modulate activity in memory-related structures such as the amygdala and hippocampus.1 To deconstruct this process, the Think-NoThink (TNT) paradigm has been widely used. This framework employs a direct contrast between two distinct cognitive tasks to reveal the neural network of volitional memory modulation: actively retrieving (Think) and actively suppressing (No-Think) a memory trace.2 While this has been extensively applied to visual and semantic modalities, the network of tactile memory control still remains unexplored.2

Here, we employ a combined behavioral-fMRI approach to study tactile memory control using the TNT paradigm, focusing on tactile memories derived from stimulation on the distal extremities (hand, foot). A defining feature of tactile processing is the distinct somatotopic organization in the primary and secondary somatosensory cortices (SI, SII) and the insula, where sensory inputs are mapped to spatially segregated regions corresponding to specific body parts.3,4 We hypothesise that the regulation of tactile memories requires targeted modulation of body-part representations, and successful retrieval/suppression of the memories relies on distinct pathways guided by the associated body-part representations (hand vs. foot). Characterising these segregated pathways would provide a mechanistic basis for therapeutic interventions to ‘remap’ maladaptive somatic memories, paving new avenues for treating somatoform disorders.

Methods

N=18 healthy younger adults (7 males, mean age 24 years) were recruited. All participants were right handed, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no limb-related disorders, and satisfied the inclusion criteria for 3T MRI. All participants took part in the experiment voluntarily and provided written informed consent. They were informed of personal data protection and pseudoanonymity, and the right to withdraw their consent and terminate the experiment without any cost. The participants were compensated for their participation.

The TNT paradigm consists of three phases: training, TNT and recall. We acquired fMRI data during the TNT and recall phases. The participants were first presented with a short demonstration of each phase outside the scanner to familiarise with the task. The stimulus materials used in the demonstration were excluded from the formal experiment. A 6 cm line was drawn using a water-soluble pen on the participants’ four body parts (i.e., L and R hand, L and R foot) to ensure consistency of the stimulation. Three textures (i.e., a soft brush (smooth), a plastic strip (neutral), and a piece of sandpaper (rough)) were used, and applied occluded from view. Before the demonstration, the 3 textures were presented to the participants until they could distinguish them successfully.

The training phase comprised 6 blocks with 6 trials per block (36 trials in total), equally divided into 3 conditions: Think, NoThink and Baseline (12 trials each). In each trial, one of the body parts was stimulated with one of the stimulators for 8 s at 3 cm/s velocity, while a neutral picture of a room (e.g., living room, bedroom) was presented. The pairing of the body part, the stimulus type and the picture were pseudorandomised for each participant. Participants were asked to remember the picture with the stimulated body part, and the type of stimulation. After each block, participants were presented with each picture from the former block for 2 s, followed by two questions asked sequentially (each 9 s): which body part was stimulated, how did the stimulation feel. A feedback (‘right’ or ‘wrong’) was shown to the participants for 0.5 s, trials with wrong responses were repeated once (i.e., 8 s stimulation with the picture being presented). If the accuracy of the stimulated body part was ≥ ⅔ for one block, the training continued to the next one, otherwise the block was repeated (maximum 3 repetitions). If the accuracy of any block failed to reach the requirement, the experiment was terminated.

During the TNT phase, the pictures for the Think and NoThink conditions were presented over 2 runs, with 6 repetitions per picture in each run. The pictures for the Baseline condition were omitted to model natural forgetting. The pictures were presented with a blue or yellow frame, defining the Think or NoThink condition, respectively, with the color-condition pairing counterbalanced across participants. The Think condition required participants to recall the feeling of the associated stimulation, i.e., body part and stimulus type, while the NoThink condition required them to only focus on the details of the pictures but not to think about the past experience. Each picture was presented for 2 s. The inter-stimulus interval (ISI) was pseudorandomly varied between 1.4 s and 2.6 s (in 0.4 s increments) 2.

During the recall phase, the pictures were shown for 3 s each. Following each picture, participants answered the same two questions as in the training phase (3s per question). No feedback was provided, each picture was presented once.

In addition, fMRI data were collected using a blocked-design paradigm while the 4 body parts were stimulated using the three textures, to localise the hand and foot area in SI and SII.

Results

We first investigated the facilitatory effect (FE) and suppression-induced forgetting (SIF) by comparing the percentage of correctly recalled stimulation pairs in each condition and each body part (see Fig. 2A,B)2. Homogeneity of variances was first tested with Levene’s test, using 𝛼=0.05. Although the data violated the assumption of equal variance, a three-way ANOVA was still performed with the factors condition (Think, Baseline, NoThink), body part (Hand, Foot) and texture (Rough, Neutral, Smooth) as the sample size is equal5. There is a significant main effect of condition (F(2)=2.05, p=0.01), but no significant main effect of body part (F(1)=0.07, p=0.57) and texture (F(2)=0.89, p=0.13). The main effect of condition is due to significantly higher percentage of correctly recalled pairs in the Think condition compared to the NoThink condition (%Think: 0.45±0.50, %NoThink: 0.31±0.46, t(630)=-3.02, p=0.008). There is also a significant interaction between body part and texture (F(2)=13.70, p<0.001), and between condition and texture (F(4)=2.41, p=0.05), but not between body part and condition.

All fMRI data were first skull-stripped using the FSL Brain Extraction Tool. The skull-stripped images went through the preprocessing pipeline in Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM25, Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, UCL, London, UK, running on MATLAB R2023a), including motion and slice-timing correction, field-map correction, coregistration to T1 images, normalization to MNI152 space (2 mm), and spatial smoothing (4 mm FWHM). Fixed-effects models on the 1st level were calculated for each subject using the general linear model (GLM). The analyses were performed on the TNT and the recall phases, and the hand-foot localisers. Group-level statistical inference was performed using the summary statistic approach (random effects) on the contrast of parameter estimates generated from the 1st level, with the threshold at p<0.001 (uncorrected). 

First, we inspected the tactile memory enhancement network (see Fig. 2D). In the TNT phase, we found activation in dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), medial frontal gyrus (MFG), supplementary motor area (SMA),  supramarginal gyrus (SMG), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), anterior insula (AI), inferior parietal lobule (IPL), superior parietal lobule (SPL) and middle occipital gyrus (MOG). In the recall phase, activations were found in the DLPFC, superior frontal gyrus (SFG), MFG, medial temporal lobe (MTL), middle temporal gyrus (MTG), superior temporal gyrus (STG), PCC, posterior insula (PI), SMG, SPL, IPL, primary motor cortex (MI) and SI. For the tactile memory suppression network (see Fig. 2E), in both TNT and recall phases, activations were observed in DLPFC, IFG, SFG, MTG, STG, PCC, insula, SMG, IPL, SMA, M1 and S1. Interestingly, activations were localized more in the anterior insula (AI) in the TNT phase, and more in the posterior insula (PI) in the recall phase.

We then inspected the body-part specific tactile memory enhancement and suppression networks, focusing the hand and foot areas (see Fig.2F,G). For hand tactile memory enhancement, DLPFC, SFG, IFG, AI, SMG and IPL were activated in the TNT phase, and STG, MTG, AI, SMG and M1 were activated in the recall phase. For the foot area, MTG, ITG, and middle occipital gyrus (MOG) were activated in the TNT phase, and STG, MTG, AI, SMG and M1 were activated in the recall phase. For tactile memory suppression of the hand, activations were found in MFG, SFG, STG, PI, SMG, PCC in the TNT phase, and in DLPFC, SFG, MFG, MTG, STG,  PI, SMG, IPL and MI in the recall phase. For the foot area, DLPFC, SFG, STG, PI, SMG and PCC were activated in the TNT phase, and DLPFC, SFG, MFG, MTG, STG, IPL, PI, SMG and MI in the recall phase.

Conclusion

We here transferred for the first time the TNT Paradigm, which has in the past been used to investigate the memory control network of visual and semantic memories, to the domain of touch. We investigated the neural networks underlying volitional tactile memory modulation, specifically for experiences at hand and foot. Our analyses reveal distinct yet overlapping recruitments of the general memory control mechanisms and sensorimotor-specific regions.

The tactile memory enhancement network reinforces encoding and retrieval primarily via DLPFC, which supports focusing attention and organising tactile information. DLPFC is notably active during hand but not foot enhancement encoding, suggesting that the hand relies more on classical executive control, while the foot employs a more perceptual/associative strategy involving MTG, ITG, and MOG. MTL and PCC are integral to the broader memory process, but not related to body-part specific enhancement. STG and MTG are crucial in recall phase for both body parts, underscoring the temporal lobe's role in memory reconstruction.

The tactile-specific network nodes highlight the somatotopic representational difference. For both body parts, SMG and MI are activated in the recall phase, linking retrieval to re-experiencing the tactile sensation and associated motor imagery. Hand memory encoding engaged the fronto-parietal network (IPL, IFG) for high-precision somatosensory attention and manipulation. Foot memory encoding, however, activated ITG and MOG, suggesting a greater reliance on visual imagery and object recognition, likely due to limited manual exploration of the foot.

Interestingly, the AI is activated for both body parts, highlighting its role in the enhancement network irrespective of specific topography. It may mobilise bodily attention and may be involved in highlighting experiences as salient, thus initiating or sustaining the cognitive effort required for successful encoding or retrieval.

The tactile memory suppression network employs a top-down inhibitory mechanism to prevent memory retrieval. DLPFC is again observed to be activated for foot suppression but only in the recall phase for hand suppression. This suggests foot memories require continuous executive control to prevent intrusion, whereas hand memories rely more initially on MFG and SFG. PCC is consistently active in TNT phase for both body parts, signifying that suppression is an active, internally-directed control mechanism managed by the default mode network (DMN).

The tactile-specific signatures in memory suppression are the sensorimotor areas actively inhibited by the frontal control regions. SMG, STG and M1 are widely involved for both body parts, indicating the suppression effort universally targets the spatial, associative and motor components of the tactile memory. IPL is suppressed for both body parts only in recall phase, indicating a late stage inhibition of spatial integration.

The insula’s role in memory suppression is primarily carried by the PI. This suggests the PI is constantly identifying and targeting the physical, sensory elements of the memory trace for inhibition.

In conclusion, volitional tactile memory modulation relies on general executive control to modulate sensory-motor components. However, hand memory modulation is characterised by a strong initial reliance on fronto-parietal attention, whereas foot memory modulation shows a broader recruitment of top-down inhibitory control and more involvement of perceptual/associative areas during encoding. Critically, the insula provides a clear functional dissociation: anterior area is recruited for the salience and control motivation for enhancement, while the PI monitors and targets the sensory trace for suppression. Delineating these specific neural pathways offers a mechanical foundation for clinical procedures designed to restructure dysfunctional bodily memories, potentially identifying novel approaches for the intervention of somatoform disorders.
