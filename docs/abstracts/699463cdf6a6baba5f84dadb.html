Introduction

How the human brain coordinates with others during complex social interactions remains a central question in social neuroscience. While previous research has demonstrated increased inter-brain synchrony during cooperative tasks, it remains unclear whether neural coupling follows a hierarchical organization based on coordination complexity. Using a novel 4-person EEG hyperscanning paradigm, we investigated whether inter-brain synchrony systematically scales with social coordination demands.

Methods

We recorded EEG from 19 groups of 4 participants (N=76) engaged in five musical coordination tasks with varying social demands: (1) random playing, (2) synchronized playing, (3) turn-taking, (4) imitation, and (5) cooperative performance. Inter-brain phase locking value (PLV) was computed across four frequency bands (delta: 1-4 Hz, theta: 4-8 Hz, alpha: 8-14 Hz, beta: 14-30 Hz) for all pairwise combinations. Statistical analysis included repeated-measures ANOVA, linear trend analysis, and pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction.

Results

Contrary to a simple hierarchical model, we found condition-specific rather than linear patterns of inter-brain synchrony. Delta-band synchrony was significantly stronger during the synchrony condition compared to all other conditions (all p<0.05, Cohen's d = 0.64-0.87), with the highest PLV observed in this condition (M=0.3392, peak=0.3718). Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant condition effects for delta-band synchrony (F(4,90)=7.537, p<0.001). Effect sizes were large for delta (Cohen's f=0.579) and medium for theta (f=0.311) bands. Although beta-band showed a statistically significant linear trend in regression analysis (p=0.003), the effect was small (R²=0.091) and mean PLV differences across conditions were negligible (<0.001), suggesting limited physiological relevance.

Conclusion

Our findings challenge the notion of a simple hierarchical organization of inter-brain synchrony. Instead, they reveal distinct neural signatures for different coordination modes, with delta-band synchrony particularly enhanced during rhythmic synchronization. These results suggest that different social coordination tasks engage specialized neural mechanisms rather than recruiting a single continuum of inter-brain coupling. The robust delta-band enhancement during synchronized playing may reflect the unique demands of maintaining precise temporal alignment in multi-person interaction.
