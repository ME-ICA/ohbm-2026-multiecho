Introduction

Brain activity is composed of both rhythmic (periodic) and arrhythmic (aperiodic) signal components 1,2. These ubiquitous components are observed across spatial and temporal scales. Rhythmic signal components appear as peaks in the frequency domain that sit above the arrhythmic background 2,3 and have been the subject of much research over the past 100 years 4. They are theorized to relate to the synchrony of populations of neurons 5, correlate with perceptual and cognitive processes, can differentiate individuals from one another,  and are altered by neurological and psychiatric diseases. More recently, scientists have also begun to explore how arrhythmic brain activity—a component once believed to reflect neural noise—relates to perception, cognition, and how it is altered in healthy aging and disease. 

Despite interest in understanding the behavioural and mechanistic consequences of arrhythmic brain activity, the methodology for quantifying rhythmic from arrhythmic activity remains in its infancy. While previous approaches quantified brain rhythms using relative power, this method muddies the relationship between rhythmic and arrhythmic components. More recent work has focused on computational models to describe neural power spectra, such as specparam2. Differences in how researchers quantify these two neural signal components may challenge the robustness, reproducibility, and interpretability of findings in the field.

Here, we sought to characterize how choice in the method for quantifying rhythmic and arrhythmic neural signal components impacts their robustness. Specifically, we assessed how well different methods recovered simulated relationship between rhythmic alpha power and the arrhythmic signal component.

Methods

To explore the influence of methodology on the quantification of rhythmic and arrhythmic brain activity, we simulated 16,000 neural time series across five experiments with known ground truth relationships between different spectral parameters of interest.  Simulated neural time series were synthesized using the NeuroDSP toolbox, lasted 30 seconds, and had a sampling rate of 500 Hz. We then asked how well three different methods for quantifying rhythmic brain activity could recover these simulated relationships. These three methods were denoted i) modelled power, ii) linear-detrended power and iii) log-detrended power (see Methods for details). 

Rhythmic brain activity within the alpha band (8-12 Hz) was estimated as either i) modelled power, or detrended power in ii) log-log space and iii) linear space (see Figure 1). We defined modelled power as the height of the Gaussian peak fit by the specparam algorithm within the alpha band. For detrended spectral power, we defined rhythmic activity as the average residual variance in the observed power spectrum after subtracting the modelled 1/f arrhythmic component. This was done by subtracting the 1/f arrhythmic signal component in either linear or log-log space. 

Specparam models the neural power spectra as the independent contributions of rhythmic and arrhythmic activity (i.e., the 1/f slope and Gaussian peaks) 2,3. The algorithm iteratively searches for Gaussian rhythmic peaks that sit above the power law (1/f𝒳), and parametrizes their centre frequency, amplitude, and bandwidth. In addition, the arrhythmic signal component is characterized by two parameters: the offset and slope (i.e., exponent; 𝒳). The rhythmic Gaussian peaks and arrhythmic signal component are summed together in the frequency domain to generate the broadband signal observed in neurophysiological recordings 2,3.

In our first experiment, we evaluated the accuracy of each method in independently recovering the arrhythmic exponent and rhythmic alpha power when there was no ground truth correlation between arrhythmic and rhythmic signal components (i.e., ⍴ = 0). We hypothesized that detrending approaches, as they depend on first modelling the neural power spectrum to accurately remove the contribution of arrhythmic activity, would introduce spurious correlations between the two signal components due to errors in model fit. We ran three sets of simulations varying i) alpha centre frequency, ii) alpha amplitude, and iii) arrhythmic exponent. 

In a second experiment, we tested whether the method for quantifying rhythmic brain activity impacts the recovery of a monotonic relationship between arrhythmic exponent and rhythmic alpha activity. We simulated 100 neural time series for 10 different effect sizes representing the relationship between alpha amplitude and arrhythmic exponent, starting from ⍴= 0.0 up to ⍴= 0.9 in steps of 0.1. Alpha amplitude ranged from 0.1 to 1.1 a.u., and arrhythmic exponent ranged from 0.5 to 1.5 Hz-1. Beta centre frequency, amplitude, and bandwidth were held constant at 19 Hz, 0.4 a.u. and 5Hz, while alpha centre frequency and bandwidth were held constant at 10 Hz and 2 Hz, respectively. We tested whether the recovered correlation between spectral parameters matched the simulated ground truth for the three methodologies of computing rhythmic alpha power—i.e., modelled power, log- and linear- detrending. 

In the last experiment, we tested how the choice in methodology for quantifying rhythmic and arrhythmic activity impacts the interpretations drawn from an empirical dataset. Here, we use age-related alterations in resting-state changes in brain activity as a case example. To do this, we relied on the CamCAN dataset (606 participants) to validate our simulated results. Each participant completed an eye-closed MEG recording using a 306-channel VectorView MEG system (MEGIN, Helsinki, Finland) that lasted approximately 8 minutes, sampled at 1 kHz with a 0.03-330 Hz bandpass filter.

Results

In experiment 1, we evaluated how well the three methods were able to recover a ground truth null relationship of alpha power and arrhythmic exponent for various simulated values of arrhythmic exponents, holding alpha amplitude and aperiodic component constant. For modelled power, the observed relationship between alpha power and exponent was generally close to the ground truth correlation of 0, with a mean of ⍴ = -0.03. Spearman correlation values were less than |0.15| for most simulated centre frequencies, except 7 and 8 Hz, where we observed weak spurious relationships between alpha power and exponents (⍴ = -0.18, CI [-0.27, -0.09]; -0.15 (CI [-0.24, -0.06], respectively) (Figure 2a left panel). For both log- and linear- detrended power, we observed systematic spurious negative correlations between rhythmic and arrhythmic components (Spearman correlation values below -0.15). Log-detrended power performed the poorest of the three methods, with an average negative correlation between rhythmic alpha power and arrhythmic exponent of ⍴ =-0.35 (Figure 2a, middle panel). The average estimated relationship between rhythmic alpha power and the arrhythmic exponent was ⍴ = -0.17 for linear-detrended (Figure 2a, left and right panels).

Next, we evaluated the three methods’ performance on simulations where we manipulated the values of the arrhythmic exponent. Here, we similarly observed that modelled power was the closest to ground truth with an average correlation between rhythmic alpha power and arrhythmic of ⍴ = 0.03. In comparison, we observed a modest relationship for linear-detrended power (⍴ =-0.12) and an average relationship of ⍴ =-0.39 for log-detrended power (Figure 2b). A similar pattern of parameter recovery was obtained when varying alpha amplitude. Taken together, these analyses suggest that modelled power is the most robust method for quantifying rhythmic alpha power independently of arrhythmic brain activity.

Last, we examined the relationship between resting-state alpha power estimated using the three methods and the arrhythmic exponent. Based on previous literature linking both features of brain activity to cortical inhibition, we hypothesized that the two measures would be positively correlated. 

We estimated the linear monotonic relationship between resting-state arrhythmic exponents and alpha power using Spearman correlations for each of 148 cortical parcels of the Destrieux atlas. We observed a rostro-caudal gradient, with frontal brain regions showing a positive linear relationship and posterior areas having a negative relationship between log-detrended alpha power and arrhythmic exponent (Figure 5a, left panel). In contrast, the relationship between modelled alpha power and arrhythmic exponent was positive across the entire cortex, except within nine occipital parcels. These findings emphasize the discrepancy in the interpretation of findings based on two different methods for quantifying rhythmic and arrhythmic brain activity.  

Conclusion

The analysis of brain rhythms is central to the field of neuroscience: researchers seek to establish links between neural oscillations, behaviour, and disease 4. However, methods for quantifying rhythmic power vary considerably and make differing assumptions. We demonstrate that analytical choices when quantifying the amplitude of brain rhythms can lead to spurious correlations between arrhythmic and rhythmic spectral parameters and confound interpretations of findings. By relying on simulations of neural time series and empirical data, we demonstrated that detrending approaches lead to misleading correlations between the arrhythmic exponent and alpha power. In contrast, modelled power provides the most robust approach for quantifying the amplitude of brain rhythms. In empirical data, we found that the relationship between alpha-power and arrhythmic exponent flipped depending on the methodology used to quantify brain rhythms, emphasizing the importance of methodological choice in the interpretations of results. Our findings will inform future research on the neural mechanisms of these processes, encouraging more robust, reproducible, and interpretable results.
