Introduction

Adolescent nicotine use has declined in recent years, but remains a significant public health challenge1. In 2024, 2.25 million middle and high school students reported using nicotine in the past year, primarily electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes)2. Adolescent nicotine use is associated with negative health outcomes1, therefore, studying the factors associated with adolescent substance use is a critical research area. To address this, we analyzed data that were collected as part of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, a multisite longitudinal study that tracks adolescents from ages 9-10 to adulthood. The goal of the current research was to determine if functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) measures obtained at ages 9-10 predicted later nicotine use during teenage years. Blood oxygen-level dependent (BOLD) signal temporal variance in 14 brain regions and within-network functional connectivity in 4 large-scale brain networks, selected based on their association with nicotine use in previous literature3-5, were chosen as a priori fMRI predictors of later nicotine or e-cigarette use.

Methods

Tabulated data from ABCD release 6.0 were analyzed for this project, including participants with complete data for the specified predictors and outcome variables (N = 8341). The sample included n = 4327 male and n = 4014 female participants, with an average age of 9.95 ± 0.62 years when entering the study. Temporal variance in the following bilateral regions was selected as predictors: whole insula (Desikan atlas), insula subdivisions including the long insular gyrus and central sulcus of the insula, short insular gyri, anterior segment of the circular sulcus of the insula, inferior segment of the circular sulcus of the insula, superior segment of the circular sulcus of the insula (Destrieux atlas), and nucleus accumbens area (subcortical atlas). Additionally, average within-network resting-state functional connectivity (Pearson correlation) was selected in the following networks: default mode, salience, cingulo-opercular, and fronto-parietal, defined by the Gordon parcellation. Several outcome variables, measured 5 years after the fMRI scans were collected, were included in these analyses: nicotine use days since last study visit (1 year ago), e-cigarette use days since last study visit, and e-cigarette use days in the past 90 days. Hurdle regression models were built using the pscl package in R version 4.4.1. The hurdle model is a two-part regression model that consists of 1) a binomial model with logit link to estimate the probability of any nicotine use (i.e., use vs. no use) based on baseline fMRI measures and (2) a truncated negative binomial count model that predicts the amount of nicotine used among participants who reported using nicotine. Separate hurdle regression models were computed for each predictor and outcome variable, with the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied within analysis type (e.g., within all insula models). The hurdle regressions included age, sex, average fMRI motion, and household income as covariates. The analysis plan and methods were pre-registered prior to analysis: https://osf.io/htc9p/overview. 

Results

Greater average temporal variance in the left long insular gyrus and central sulcus of the insula at ages 9-10 was associated with increased likelihood of e-cigarette use (last 90 days) at ages 14-15, Estimate = 6.12 ± 1.97, p = .022 (FDR-corrected). In individuals who used e-cigarettes, average temporal variance in the left long insular gyrus and central sulcus of the insula was not associated with the amount of use, Estimate = -8.25 ± 6.36, p = .192. No other significant effects were detected in the other models following FDR-correction. Notably, however, FDR-uncorrected results suggested that within-network connectivity in the default mode network at ages 9-10 was associated with an increased likelihood of using nicotine (Estimate = 1.57 ± 0.68, p = .020) and e-cigarettes (Estimate = 1.55 ± 0.69, p = .025) in the year since their past ABCD visit, in addition to using e-cigarettes in past 90 days (Estimate = 1.74 ± 0.79, p = .028) from the ABCD visit. Of individuals who reported nicotine/e-cigarette use, within-network connectivity of the default mode network did not predict the amount of use (all p-values > .05). 

Conclusion

The preliminary results suggest that greater temporal variability of the BOLD signal within the left long insular gyrus and central sulcus of the insula during childhood is associated with an increased likelihood of using nicotine and e-cigarettes during teenage years; however, this fMRI measure was not associated with the amount of nicotine or e-cigarette use. There were trends suggesting that default mode network connectivity was associated with increased likelihood of nicotine use. Together, these results are a step towards understanding how fMRI measures are associated with adolescent substance use, which will inform our understanding of the factors associated with the emergence of addiction in adolescents.
