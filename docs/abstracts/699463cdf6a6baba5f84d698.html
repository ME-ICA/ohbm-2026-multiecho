Introduction

The cerebral cortex has been extensively studied in adults with psychotic disorders, but less is known about cortical morphology in adolescents with early-onset psychosis (EOP), i.e., psychotic disorders diagnosed before 19 years of age.1 EOP encompasses a broad clinical spectrum, including early-onset schizophrenia (EOS; persistent psychotic symptoms), affective psychosis (AFP; psychosis during mood episodes, typically mood-congruent), and other non-affective psychotic disorders (OTP; non-affective, variable presentation). The low incidence of EOP (~0.6%) poses recruitment challenges, thereby limiting statistical power and robustness. To address these limitations, the EOP Working Group was established within the Enhancing NeuroImaging Genetics Through Meta-Analysis (ENIGMA) consortium to enable collaboration among research groups acquiring magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data and performing clinical phenotyping of adolescents with EOP. Here, we report the first large-scale analysis of cortical morphology in EOP.

Methods

The dataset comprised 387 individuals with EOP (49.4% female) and 338 healthy controls (CTR; 54.4% female) recruited at nine research sites worldwide. Among patients, 57.6% had EOS (n=223), 26.9% had AFP (n=104), and 15.5% had OTP (n=60). T1-weighted brain images were processed with the recon-all pipeline of FreeSurfer (v7.1.0 or above) to extract mean cortical thickness, surface area, cortical volume, and local gyrification index (LGI) for the 68 regions of the Desikan-Killiany atlas (34 per hemisphere). Intracranial volume (ICV) was computed with the Sequence Adaptive Multimodal SEGmentation (SAMSEG) toolbox in FreeSurfer. We performed harmonized quality control, including visual inspection of T1-weighted images and surface reconstructions, and harmonization of scanner-related variation using ComBat.

To test for case-control differences, we used regression models adjusted for age, age2, and sex, with diagnostic group (CTR/EOP) as the variable of interest. This model was applied to each cortical metric and region in each hemisphere, as well as to global metrics. To assess cortical alterations in diagnostic subgroups, we used similar models in which we replaced the diagnostic group term with diagnostic subgroup (EOS/AFP/OTP), with CTR as the reference. Associations between cortical metrics and antipsychotic medication use (yes/no) and chlorpromazine-equivalent dose, psychotic symptoms (Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale; PANSS), antidepressant use (yes/no), duration of illness, and age of onset were assessed in separate regression models among patients only, adjusted for age, age2, and sex.

We further tested for interactions of diagnostic group with sex and age in separate regression models including interaction terms: 1) age-by-group, with age, sex, and group as predictors, and 2) sex-by-group, with age, age2, sex, and group as predictors. We performed complementary sex-stratified analyses by fitting regression models adjusted for age and age2, with diagnostic group (CTR/EOP) as the variable of interest separately for males and females.

Finally, we compared case-control differences in EOP with those of adults with schizophrenia (SCZ; n=4,474), adults with bipolar disorders (BD; n=1,837), and youth with BD (n=411), using Cohen's d effect sizes drawn from two prior ENIGMA studies.2,3 For each comparison group, we computed Pearson correlations (across cortical regions) with case-control effect sizes for EOP, EOS, AFP, and OTP, with p-values calculated using hemisphere-preserving spin permutations (10,000 rotations). Pairwise differences in correlations were evaluated using Steiger's test, and we compared the ratio of mean regional effect sizes in EOP to those of each comparison group.

Results

We found a widespread pattern of lower thickness, area, volume, and LGI in EOP compared to CTR. Bilateral surface area, volume, and LGI were lower across most cortical regions, with overall larger effect sizes than those for thickness. Global thickness was lower in EOP compared to CTR across both hemispheres (left/right: d=-0.36/-0.31). Similarly, global area (left/right: d=-0.42/-0.41), volume (left/right: d=-0.58/-0.56), and LGI (left/right: d=-0.39/-0.52) were lower in EOP (Figure 1a). Effect sizes for global cortical metrics were similar across subgroups for thickness, whereas case-control alterations in EOS were greater for area, volume, and LGI (Figure 1b). In contrast, only a few regions showed significantly lower area in AFP (5 regions) and OTP (3 regions).

We found smaller area (d=-0.43; pFDR=0.034) and volume (d=-0.50; pFDR=0.003) in the right rostral anterior cingulate (rACC) in antidepressant users compared to non-users. For PANSS symptoms, only the association between negative symptoms and left lingual volume was statistically significant (partial r=-0.21; pFDR=0.014). There were no significant associations between cortical metrics and antipsychotic medication use or dose, duration of illness, or age of onset.

There were no significant interactions between diagnostic group and age or sex for any cortical metric. However, sex-stratified analyses showed lower regional thickness only in female patients compared to female CTR. More regions showed smaller area in male than female patients relative to same-sex CTR, while volume and LGI alterations were similar across sexes. All global cortical metrics differed from those of CTR across both male and female patients.

Regional Cohen's d effect sizes for EOP correlated with those of adult SCZ for thickness (r=0.62; pspin=2.0×10-4) and area (r=0.49; pspin=2.0×10-4). EOP thickness deficits correlated more strongly with those of adult BD (r=0.61; pspin=2.0×10-4) than youth BD (r=0.35; pspin=1.6×10-2). Surface area alterations in EOP did not correlate significantly with either BD group. In subgroup analyses, thickness alterations in EOS and AFP showed strong correlations with those of adult SCZ and adult/youth BD, whereas correlations for thickness alterations in OTP were weaker.

Cohen's d effect sizes for cortical thickness were significantly smaller in EOP, EOS, AFP, and OTP compared to adult SCZ (pFDR<1.6×10-16), but greater compared to youth BD (pFDR<9.6×10-5). Effect sizes for cortical thickness in EOP and AFP did not differ significantly from those of adult BD, but were smaller in EOS (pFDR=2.1×10-3) and OTP (pFDR=6.3×10-3) relative to adult BD. Post hoc analyses showed that average effect sizes for cortical thickness in EOP were approximately 0.53 times those of adult SCZ and 2.11 times those of youth BD. For area, effect sizes were overall greater in EOP, EOS, AFP, and OTP compared to adult SCZ (pFDR<7.6×10-4), adult BD (pFDR<1.0×10-2), and youth BD (pFDR<5.6×10-3). Effect sizes for area in EOP were 1.51 times those of adult SCZ, 4.62 times those of adult BD, and 6.11 times those of youth BD.

Conclusion

We observed widespread lower cortical thickness, surface area, cortical volume, and LGI in EOP relative to CTR. EOS showed more widespread and greater differences in surface area, cortical volume, and LGI compared to AFP and OTP, whereas global thickness differences were similar across subgroups. Patterns of cortical thickness alterations were more similar between EOS and AFP (r=0.78) than between EOS and OTP (r=0.53). As cortical thickness deficits may reflect both static and progressive processes, subgroup differences may become more pronounced later in the illness course. The findings of greater overall surface area and LGI alterations in EOS are consistent with more substantial neurodevelopmental disruption, particularly of areal expansion and gyrification, in EOS than in AFP or OTP.

Compared to prior ENIGMA studies,2,3 we found that surface area alterations in EOP correlated significantly with those of adult SCZ, but not BD, with the strongest correlations seen for EOP (r=0.49) and EOS (r=0.50). Notably, surface area effect sizes were overall greater in EOP than in adult SCZ and adult/youth BD. While cortical thinning may be, at least partly, progressive, surface area and LGI are thought to stabilize in early adolescence and more closely reflect earlier developmental processes. These findings align with the ENIGMA-EOP study on intracranial and subcortical volumes in a partially overlapping sample by Gurholt et al. (2022), where the largest case-control effect size was reported for ICV.4 Together, the findings suggest that EOP may be subject to a greater neurodevelopmental burden than adult-onset SCZ.
