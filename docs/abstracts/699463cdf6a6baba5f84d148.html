Introduction

Affective experiences, arising from both external stimuli and internally generated states, fundamentally shape human behavior and well-being. When dysregulated, they manifest as core features of psychiatric disorders, including depression's anhedonia and negative affect, and anxiety's pervasive apprehension. Despite its centrality, the field lacks objective markers of affective states, relying primarily on self-report. Recent efforts have developed multivariate brain markers to measure subjective affect, showing potential to supplement self-report and reveal neural representations1,2. However, most markers target acute responses to simplified stimuli, which capture only a fraction of the complexity and temporal extent of affect. Given that phasic and sustained affect recruit partially distinct neural dynamics3, this challenges stimulus-locked markers' capacity to index prolonged affective states particularly relevant for clinical translation, as psychiatric disorders are characterized by persistent, dysregulated affect. Additionally, whether markers trained on externally evoked affect generalize to internally generated states—central to pathological phenomena like rumination—remains unestablished, which is crucial for evaluating the clinical applicability of neural markers.

Beyond the challenge of modeling sustained affect, the functional distinction between positive and negative affect remains incompletely understood. While dimensional accounts place them at opposing ends of a valence axis, recent evidence suggests a purely one‑dimensional valence framework may obscure relevant neural substrates. Moreover, contemporary markers often lack specificity, tracking nonspecific arousal rather than valence-specific processes4. These gaps motivate the development of neural markers that decode positive and negative affect with high sensitivity and specificity and reveal comprehensive neural representations.

Methods

We conducted naturalistic fMRI experiments to develop neural signatures (Studies 1-2), then tested them using multiple independent datasets (Fig. 1a). In Study 1 (n = 36, 17 females, mean ± SD age = 21.03 ± 2.22 years), participants viewed ~60s affective film clips (9 positive, 10 neutral, 9 negative) during fMRI scanning and rated affective intensity immediately after each clip on a 9-point scale (1 = no feelings to 9 = very high arousal). Stimuli were presented in three runs, with three or four clips from each category per run. Study 2 (n = 63 independent subjects) employed the same paradigm with different videos and presentation timings. fMRI data in Studies 1-2 were collected on a 3.0-Tesla Discovery MR750 system (General Electric Medical System, Milwaukee, WI, USA) and preprocessed using FMRIPREP 21.0.0 (RRID: SCR_016216).

In Study 1, we averaged blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal time-series within each region based on the Schaefer parcellation (400 cortical regions), the Brainnetome atlas (36 subcortical regions), and the cerebellum atlas (32 regions). We then extracted whole-brain functional connectivity and applied partial least squares regression (PLS-R) to derive separate signatures predicting positive and negative affect intensity: the positive affective experience signature (PAES) and negative affective experience signature (NAES) (Fig. 1b). We validated signature sensitivity and specificity in Study 1 using 10-fold cross-validation and in Study 2 using forced-choice tests.

We further evaluated generalizability and specificity using two publicly available datasets from another lab. Using Study 3 (n = 41), we tested whether PAES and NAES trained on externally driven affect generalize to internally generated affective experiences by examining classification accuracy between internally generated states (sad memory recall and rumination) and a control condition (distraction). Study 4 (n = 28) was used to assess specificity by examining wakefulness versus sleep discrimination.

We next evaluated clinical utility using resting-state fMRI in emotion-related disorders across five independent clinical datasets (Studies 5-9). In depression studies, we tested whether PAES and NAES differentiated patients from healthy controls and tracked symptom severity across multiple scales (Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD), Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (MFQ)), hypothesizing that signature responses would reflect depression's characteristic pattern of dysregulated affect. In anxiety studies, we examined disorder-specific patterns, hypothesizing that only NAES would classify patients.

Finally, we conducted comparative analyses to characterize shared versus valence-preferential neural architectures. We (1) performed network-level virtual lesion analyses by systematically nullifying model weights corresponding to connections within (e.g., Default Mode Network (DMN)-DMN) or between (e.g., Visual Network (VN)-DMN) canonical networks and assessing the impact on predictive performance using permutation testing; (2) conducted region-level local sufficiency analyses by testing whether each brain region's connectivity profile alone could predict affective ratings (Study 1) and discriminate affective conditions (Study 2). 

Results

The developed PAES and NAES sensitively predicted their targeted affect intensity in discovery and validation datasets (Fig. 2). In Study 1, PAES predicted positive experience intensity with overall prediction-outcome correlation r = 0.67 (P < 10-4), averaged root mean squared error (RMSE) = 2.36, and explained variance score (EVS) = 0.45. NAES predicted negative affective experience with overall prediction-outcome correlation r = 0.67 (P < 10-4), RMSE = 2.61, and EVS = 0.45. Of note, when calculating these indices, clips of opposing valence were set to zero. In Study 2, both signatures successfully classified their targeted emotions with high accuracy (all accuracies ≥ 97%, P < 10-16). PAES and NAES also exhibited high specificity: in Study 1, PAES did not predict negative experience intensity (r = -0.11 ± 0.06 SE), and NAES did not predict positive experience intensity (r = -0.19 ± 0.06 SE); in Study 2, PAES showed higher expression for neutral than negative (81% accuracy), and NAES showed higher expression for neutral than positive (84% accuracy), supporting valence-sensitive organization.

Moreover, two signatures generalized from externally evoked to internally generated affect states. In Study 3, forced-choice tests revealed that the PAES showed stronger responses during distraction and accurately classified distraction from the two negative conditions (distraction vs. sad memory: 76 ± 6.7% SE, P = 1.50 × 10-3; distraction vs. rumination: 76 ± 6.7% SE, P = 1.50 × 10-3). Conversely, NAES distinguished sad-memory and rumination from distraction with above-chance accuracy (sad memory vs. distraction: 78 ± 6.5% SE, P = 4.31 × 10-4; rumination vs. distraction: 68 ± 7.3% SE, P = 0.028). When applied to predict subjective positive affect ratings, prediction-outcome correlations were r = 0.36 (P = 8.10 × 10-4) for PAES and r = -0.28 (P = 0.011) for NAES, indicating that both signatures index internally generated affective states. In Study 4, neither signature accurately discriminated wakefulness from sleep (PAES: 46 ± 9.4% SE, NAES: 54 ± 9.4% SE, all P > 0.05), supporting that both signatures primarily reflect affective mental processes rather than nonspecific wakefulness.

More importantly, in four resting-state fMRI studies of depression (n = 342), PAES showed stronger responses in HCs, whereas NAES showed stronger responses in patients (all accuracies ≥ 64%, all P ≤ 0.022), consistent with depression's clinical profile of diminished positive affect and pervasive negative affect. Additionally, both signatures predicted symptom severity: In Study 5, PAES and NAES predicted BDI scores across all subjects (PAES: r = -0.29, P = 0.019; NAES: r = 0.37, P = 0.002), as well as within individual patients. Similar patterns emerged for HAMD and MFQ scores in Studies 6 and 8. Notably, in anxiety disorders (n = 154), only NAES successfully discriminated patients from HCs (all accuracies ≥ 65%, all P ≤ 0.04), whereas PAES did not (all accuracies ≤ 61%, all P ≥ 0.13), demonstrating disorder-specific clinical utility consistent with anxiety's predominant negative affect profile.

Finally, comparative analyses revealed both shared and valence-preferential neural architectures. Network-level virtual lesion analyses showed that both signatures relied on the VN and DMN connectivity. However, PAES was selectively sensitive to DMN-subcortical connectivity, reflecting reward-related valuation processes. In contrast, NAES was sensitive to DMN-frontoparietal and limbic-frontoparietal connectivity, suggesting a role in emotion regulation. Region-level analyses further identified a double dissociation: ventromedial putamen connectivity specifically predicted positive affect, whereas dorsomedial and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex connectivity specifically predicted negative affect. Common regions, including the middle cingulate cortex, anterior insula, and DMN nodes, predicted both valences, indicating partially overlapping yet distinct neural substrates.

Conclusion

We established comprehensive, accurate, and specific functional connectivity-based neural signatures for positive and negative affective experiences that generalize from externally evoked to internally generated affective experiences. These signatures demonstrated strong clinical utility in emotion-related disorders, successfully differentiating depression and anxiety with disorder-specific patterns. Finally, we delineated the neural architecture underlying affective valence, revealing valence-general connectivity patterns alongside region-specific contributions from subcortical and prefrontal circuits.
