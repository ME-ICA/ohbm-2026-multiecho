Introduction

Epilepsy is the fifth most common neurological cause of disability. It is estimated that approximately 1 in 26 people will develop epilepsy in their life at some point 1,2. Focal cortical dysplasia type II (FCD II) is a malformation of cortical development that contributes substantially to drug-resistant focal epilepsy. Blurring of junctions between gray and white matter and abnormal cortical thickening, or the transmantle sign, are among the MRI-based biomarkers for detection and classification of FCD II subtypes 3. In patients with focal DRE, surgical resection of epileptogenic zones (EZ) is an effective treatment for reducing the occurrence and severity of seizures. Still, 30-50% of operated cases do not achieve seizure freedom. While localizing epileptogenic zones on structural MRI is central to the reliability of presurgical evaluation and better treatment outcomes, a substantial ratio of patients with focal drug-resistant epilepsies presents without a clearly identifiable lesion due to the occurrence of subtle lesions and varying MRI field strengths 4. Despite increasing interest in hemispheric specialization and large-scale network alterations in epilepsy 5, interhemispheric asymmetry in FCD II remains largely unquantified. Using surface-based morphometry (SBM), in this work we investigated whether structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)–derived cortical asymmetries capture abnormalities beyond lesion boundaries and whether these asymmetry signatures are different within FCD II subtypes and in patients with MRI suspicion of FCD II from healthy controls (HC). 

Methods

Using an open presurgery MRI dataset 6, we analyzed T1-weighted Magnetization Prepared Rapid Gradient Echo (MPRAGE) images acquired on a 3-T Siemens scanner from 84 healthy individuals (43 females and 41 males; mean age at scan 33.42 ± 11.9 years) and 79 patients with suspicion of FCD II (32 females, 47 males; mean age at scan 30.24 ± 12.4 years) who were older than 15 years at the time of scan. We designed a three-stage workflow for preprocessing and implementing SBM using FreeSurfer (v7.4.1) 7(figure 1.A), visual inspection of atlas-based cortical parcellations (figure 1.B), and lesion projection (figure 1.C). We extracted five structural cortical measures of intensity, thickness, volume, curvature, and surface area from 34 regions of interest (ROIs) and computed unsigned asymmetry indices beyond the lesion location (figure 1.B). For statistical inference, we applied beta regression (R betareg package) 8 as a function of group and confounding variables (sex, age at the time of scan) across three models, FCD II vs HC (model 1), within FCD subtypes IIa, IIb, and non-operated patients (model 2), and between FCD IIa vs IIb subtypes (model 3) as shown in figure 1.B. Results were corrected for multiple comparisons with Storey’s false discovery rate (FDR) correction (qvalue in R) 9  at alpha = 0.05 (figure 1.C).

Results

After excluding scans with technical or reconstruction errors, 77 HC and 75 participants with FCD II remained for analysis. As seen in figure 2.A, individuals with suspected FCD II showed significantly reduced cortical intensity asymmetry across parietal, temporal, and occipital regions compared to healthy volunteers (Z = -3.236 to -4.921, q < 0.001). For the same cohorts, increased asymmetry was observed for cortical volume in the lateral orbitofrontal cortex (figure 2.C, Z = +3.342, q < 0.001) and for surface area in the inferior temporal cortex in FCD II compared to healthy participants (figure 2.E, Z = +3.544, q < 0.001), pointing to broad extra-lesional alterations. No significant asymmetry differences emerged when comparing all FCD subgroups including non-operated participants. In the subtype comparison, histopathologically confirmed FCD IIb cases exhibited greater intensity asymmetry in caudal anterior cingulate and superior temporal cortices compared with IIa (figure 2.A, Z = +3.129 to +3.432, q < 0.001). Conversely, precentral surface-area and volume asymmetries were smaller in FCD IIb relative to FCD IIa (figures 2.C and 2.E, Z = -3.229 to -3.235, q < 0.001). Across all models, thickness and curvature results in figure 2.B and figure 2.D showed no significant asymmetry differences after FDR correction. The combined pattern suggests subtype-specific involvement of both limbic and sensorimotor networks.

Conclusion

Structural MRI–derived interhemispheric asymmetry captures cortical alterations in FCD II that extend beyond the focal lesion. Group-level differences in intensity, volume, and surface-area asymmetry patterns were observed between FCD II and healthy controls, and subtype comparisons revealed distinct asymmetry profiles between histopathologically confirmed FCD IIa and IIb. These findings, based on surface-based morphometric analyses, support the presence of distributed, network-level cortical involvement in FCD II. Because extra-lesional abnormalities may contribute to incomplete seizure control after resection, interhemispheric asymmetry-based measures may offer complementary information for evaluating cortical regions that warrant closer clinical or surgical consideration.
