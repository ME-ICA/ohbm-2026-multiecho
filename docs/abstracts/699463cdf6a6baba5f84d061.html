Introduction

Advances in neonatal medical practices have improved survival rates of children with congenital heart disease (CHD) or born very preterm (VPT), which has been accompanied by an increased recognition of their frequent neurodevelopmental difficulties. Growing evidence suggests that those difficulties may be explained by an underlying brain dysmaturation syndrome that is similarly present in both populations1 and may disproportionately affects certain brain regions. As such, the most active period of hippocampal development is in the third trimester of gestation and the early postnatal period,2 coinciding with the open-heart surgery for CHD and preterm delivery, both of which often involve common complications such as hypoxia-ischemia and systemic inflammation. These early-life adverse events affect hippocampal structure and function development, with animal studies evidence suggesting a differential vulnerability level across the hippocampal subfields. Recent work in preterm-born school-aged children reported smaller volumes in their subiculum, dentate gyrus (DG) and total right hippocampus compared to full-term children. Similarly, subfield reductions have been reported in the right cornu ammonis (CA) 1, CA4, subiculum and DG of adolescents with single ventricle CHD, and across all hippocampal subfields in young adults with complex CHD compared to healthy peers. Hippocampal volume reductions in both clinical populations have been further associated with higher-order cognitive deficits. No study to date has directly compared hippocampal development between these two groups to better understand their shared and distinct neurodevelopmental profiles. Therefore, we segmented 3D T1-weighted (T1w) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to compare subfield volumes among adolescents and young adults with CHD or born VPT, alongside age and sex-matched healthy peers. We also aimed to explore whether volumetric differences are related to executive functioning.

Methods

This study included three groups of participants: (1) Individuals born at term (>36 weeks) with moderate or severe CHD who underwent open-heart surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass before the age of 2 years, (2) individuals born VPT (gestational age (GA) ≤ 33 weeks) without CHD, and (3) healthy term-born controls (CTL) with no history of neurological or developmental conditions. Additional exclusion criteria for the clinical groups included history of congenital infection, genetic or chromosomal abnormalities, multi-organ dysmorphic conditions, history of moderate to severe neonatal brain injury or malformation, severe developmental impairment, contraindications for MRI, and inability to communicate in English or French. Participants underwent a brain MRI on a 3 Tesla MRI system (Achieva X, Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) at the Montreal Children’s Hospital using a 32-channel head coil. High-resolution (1mm isotropic voxel) 3D T1w images were acquired and visually checked for quality assessment. T1w images of sufficient quality underwent the following pre-processing steps: bias field correction, followed by brain extraction using “BEaST” to remove all non-brain tissue. Hippocampal segmentation was performed with Hippunfold v1.3.2,3 a validated automatic segmentation pipeline, via the CBRAIN platform. Automatic segmentation of the hippocampus yielded volumes for 7 bilateral subfields: subiculum, CA 1–4, DG and Stratum Radiatum, Lacunosum, and Moleculare (SRLM). Each segmentation was visually assessed and rated using a 5-point scale to quantify the amount of manual correction to be performed.  Manual correction was performed when necessary using ITK-SNAP Toolbox version 4.0.1. Total brain volume (TBV) estimates were extracted using Sienax version 8.1.0. Participants’ executive functioning was assessed using the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function – Adult Version (BRIEF-A), which is a 75-item self-reported questionnaire that includes 9 subscales (Inhibit, Shift, Emotional Control, Self-Monitor, Initiate, Working Memory, Plan/Organize, Task Monitor, and Organization of Materials), which can then be merged to obtain the Behavioral Regulation Index (BRI) and Metacognition Index (MI) summary scores or the overall Global Executive Composite (GEC) scores. Descriptive statistics were computed to characterize the sample. For group comparisons on participant characteristics, one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test was used for continuous variables with post hoc Tukey's tests and Chi-square test with Bonferroni post hoc testing of χ2 residuals for categorical variables. Hippocampal volumes were compared between the three groups using ANCOVA, with the following covariates: socio-economic status (SES) - due to the presence of group differences, TBV - to determine if volume differences were specific to the hippocampus or if they were consequence of a different brain size, and a group by sex interaction term - given that sex was significantly associated with hippocampal volumes. Multiple comparison corrections were performed using the Bonferroni adjustment. The significance threshold was set at q < 0.05. To compare the executive function differences between the groups, the proportion of participants with clinically significant difficulty was determined for each BRIEF-A scale and group using the cut-off of 65.  Group comparisons were then performed using Chi-square test with Bonferroni post hoc testing of χ2 residuals for categorical variables. Lastly, to determine the relationship between hippocampal volumes and executive functions within each group, multiple linear regression models were computed between each hippocampal subfield volume and BRIEF-A scores on the scales previously identified as significantly different at the group level, while controlling for age and sex. Considering the exploratory nature of the structure-function analyses, multiple comparisons correction was not applied.

Results

A total of 51 participants with CHD, 56 individuals born VPT, and 54 CTL with good quality T1w images acquired at a mean age of 20 years and successful segmentations were included in the study. There were no significant group differences in age at MRI, sex distribution, or body-mass index. However, SES differed significantly between groups (H(2) = 18.11, p < 0.001), with CTL participants having a higher SES (M = 50.24, SD = 10.2) than both CHD (M = 40.59, SD = 12.6) and VPT participants (M = 43.33, SD = 11.4). Among CHD participants, 76% had two ventricle physiology and 24% had a single ventricle physiology. VPT participants were born at a mean GA of 27.98 weeks and a mean birthweight of 971.13 grams (n=48). 

TBV did not significantly differ between groups (H(2) = 2.70, p = 0.26), even though the raw means volumes followed the expected gradient of CHD < VPT < CTL. By contrast, after controlling for SES, TBV and the group by sex interaction, group-level differences were found across all hippocampal regions (left subiculum: q<0.001; all other regions: q<0.0001). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that CHD participants had smaller volume in all regions than CTL (left subiculum: p=0.03, all other p<0.0001) and than VPT (right subiculum: p=0.02; all other regions: p<0.01), except the left subiculum, which wasn’t significantly smaller than VPT participants (p=0.05). VPT participants had smaller volumes than CTL participants in the left CA2/CA3 (p=0.04) and left CA4/DG (p<0.05). The group by sex interaction was statistically significant for all regions, except the bilateral subiculum, which was therefore omitted from further post-hoc tests. Among females, CHD participants had smaller volumes in all regions tested than females CTL (all p<0.0001) and than females born VPT (all p<0.001). Similarly, females born VPT had smaller volumes than females CTL in all regions (all p<0.05). No significant group differences were found for males in any region. 

Two CHD participants and one CTL participant did not complete the BRIEF-A questionnaires and were thus excluded from the structure-function analyses, resulting sample of 159 participants (49 CHD, 56 VPT and 53 CTL). In the CHD group, clinically significant scores (≥65) were most prevalent in Working Memory, Organization of Materials and GEC, with percentages across all scales ranging from 14% to 33%. In the VPT group, the highest rates of clinically significant scores were found on Working Memory, Initiate and MI, with overall percentages ranging from 7% to 34%. The CTL participants showed the lowest rates of clinically significant scores, with percentages across scales ranging between 0% to 13%. Significant group differences in proportion of clinically meaningful difficulties were found on the Inhibit (χ2(2)=12.82, p<0.01), Self-Monitor (χ2(2)=8.59, p=0.01), Working Memory (χ2(2)=8.90, p=0.01), Organization of Materials (χ2(2)=14.73, p<0.001), MI (χ2(2)=6.04, p<0.05) and GEC (χ2(2)=7.05, p=0.03) scales. Pairwise comparisons revealed that CHD participants were more likely than CTL to have scores in the clinical range on Inhibit (χ2(2)=12.82, p<0.01), Organization of Materials (χ2(2)=14.73, p<0.01) and GEC (χ2(2)=7.05, p<0.05). Additionally, significantly more VPT participants had scores in the clinical range compared to CTL participants on the Working Memory scale (χ2(2)=8.90, p=0.03). The pairwise comparisons on the Self-Monitor and MI scales were not significant. There were no significant differences in the proportion of clinically meaningful difficulties between the VPT and CHD groups. While no significant association remained in the CHD group after controlling for age and sex, bilateral CA2/CA3 volumes were positively associated with Organization of Materials scores (left: p=0.04; right: p=0.02) in the VPT group. In the CTL group, left CA2/CA3 volumes were positively associated with Self-Monitor scores (p=0.04) and left SRLM volumes were positively associated with GEC scores (p<0.05). 

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that CHD and VPT birth have different effects on hippocampal development. Indeed, compared to healthy peers, adolescents and young adults with CHD presented with widespread hippocampal alterations, whereas individuals born VPT present with region-specific hippocampal volume reduction in the left CA2/CA3 and left CA4/DG. Our findings are in line with the previous literature which support that CHD individuals present with overall brain volume reductions, with an increased regional vulnerability in the hippocampus.4 In contrast, the regional differences observed in individuals born VPT may reflect interruption of the typical development by the premature exposure to the extra-uterine environment and postnatal insults during the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) stay. Indeed, the rate of development of the CA2/CA3 increases during the third trimester of gestation when compared to the other subfields.2 Alternatively, the DG is the last subfield to mature and shows its adult-like cytoarchitectural appearance only after 34 weeks of gestation. Its preprogrammed maturation may have been interrupted by postnatal exposure to risk factors such as painful procedures, ventilation and sedation.5

Participants with CHD had smaller hippocampal volumes in almost all regions compared to VPT-born participants, which could be a result of their exposure to distinct presentations of perinatal hypoxia. Individuals with CHD are exposed to chronic hypoxia from the third trimester until their corrective open-heart surgery, which has been repeatedly shown to alter global brain development in animal models and human studies. Alternatively, neonates born VPT are exposed to intermittent hypoxia resulting from their lung immaturity. Intermittent hypoxia studies have yielded mixed effects on the developing animal brain, depending on the severity of the hypoxia model used, with some even suggesting a protective effect of moderate intermittent hypoxia. It is plausible that the different presentations of perinatal hypoxia in those groups underlies the distinct hippocampal alterations found in young adulthood. 

Interestingly, we also found that females in both clinical groups have significantly smaller hippocampi in all regions when compared to healthy females. These group differences were absent in the male participants in all hippocampal regions, suggesting a sex-specific vulnerability of the hippocampus. Many studies have described sex differences in the healthy hippocampal formation, mainly evidenced by bigger subfield volumes in males compared to females until late adulthood. However, in the models of atypical hippocampal development, most animal models have exclusively studied males and very limited research on human sex differences in neonatal or pubertal periods is available. 

Finally, both clinical groups showed a higher prevalence of clinically significant executive function deficits, especially with functions required for inhibitory control, awareness of one’s behavior, holding of information in mind while completing other tasks and management of physical materials. However, those executive difficulties were not associated with hippocampal volume alterations. Although the hippocampus is known to play an important role in memory, social behavior and flexible cognition, the observed deficits pertained to functions typically governed by the frontal regions of the brain.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that perinatal complications have differential effects on hippocampal development that persist into young adulthood. Females across both clinical groups were more likely to have smaller hippocampal volumes compared to controls than males. Future longitudinal prospective studies should examine the risk factors that pertain to the NICU experience specific to CHD and VPT populations to better understand the key contributors to their distinct alterations in hippocampal development.
