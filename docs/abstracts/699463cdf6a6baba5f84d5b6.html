Introduction

Childhood and adolescence are critical periods for cognitive refinement and heightened vulnerability to neuropsychological disorders. During this phase, cortical maturation follows a spatiotemporal axis in which sensory regions mature earlier than association regions (sensory-association (SA) axis)1. Although most research focused primarily on the cerebral cortex, the cortex does not mature in isolation but is tightly coupled with the thalamus from early development onwards. Recently, the maturation of global thalamocortical white-matter connections was shown to parallel the cortical SA axis2, yet the thalamus is not a uniform structure but composed of structurally and functionally distinct nuclei that differentiate early in development. These nuclei form nucleus-specific thalamocortical networks contributing differentially to sensory perception and cognition and are variably affected in psychopathology. However, how nucleus-to-cortex structural connections develop in humans and how they relate to functional connectivity remains unclear. Here, we investigate how nucleus-specific thalamocortical structural and functional connectivity profiles mature from childhood to early adulthood to provide insights into normative brain maturation.

Methods

Diffusion-weighted MRI (b=1500/3000 s/mm2; 1.5 mm isotropic) and resting-state functional MRI data (rs-fMRI; 2 mm isotropic) were retrieved from the Human Connectome Project Development dataset3 (N = 604, ages 8-21 years, Fig. 1A). After segmenting 10 thalamic nuclei using HIPS-THOMAS4 (Fig. 1B), structural connections between nuclei and 180 ipsilateral cortical Glasser parcels were mapped using FSL’s probabilistic tractography. To avoid including false-positive connections and to reduce cross-nucleus confounding, connections were defined by selecting the top 10% of cortical targets by streamline counts, followed by manual refinement based on anatomical priors, yielding volumetric tract masks for each nucleus. As a microstructural measure of connectivity, fractional anisotropy (FA) was estimated from diffusion tensor fits and averaged within each connection. The age effects of FA measures were modeled using generalized additive models (GAMs) to capture both linear and nonlinear relations. We included sex and motion as confounds. To compare maturational profiles, we derived the normalized first derivative of each GAM fit (rate of change), and calculated the root mean square of Euclidean distance between pairs of nuclei connections. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied, to identify nuclei with shared versus distinct trajectories. PC1 was interpreted in relation to thalamic attributes (first-/higher-order nuclei, core/matrix ratio5, cortical targets) quantified by T-test and Spearman correlations. Functional connectivity (Pearson correlation, Fisher-z transformed) between each nucleus and its structurally defined cortical targets was computed from rs-fMRI and using a framework analogous to the structural analyses. FA and FC age effects were correlated (Spearman) with significance assessed using spin tests to account for spatial autocorrelation.

Results

Using probabilistic tractography, we generated group-level maps of nucleus-specific thalamocortical connections, which served as masks for extracting tract-averaged FA. Network visualizations indicate that nuclei connections target the cortical SA axis for some nuclei in a focal and for others in a distributed fashion (Fig. 1C). Across development, tract FA exhibited nucleus-specific trajectories in shape and slope, increasing with age for all nuclei except AV, a limbic thalamic nucleus involved in memory circuits (Fig. 2A). PCA of trajectory derivatives identified a PC1 gradient from trajectories with early increases followed by plateauing (LGN, MGN, VPL, Pul) to approximately linear increases (VLP, CM, VL, VA, MD) (Fig. 2B). This gradient correlated with core-matrix cell composition (ρ = 0.83, p = 0.005) and the average SA position of cortical target regions (ρ = 0.83, p = 0.004) but did not align with the first- versus higher-order nuclei classification (t = -1.66, p = 0.14)  (Fig. 2C). Finally, we found an overall decrease of thalamocortical functional connectivity with age, yielding a negative relationship between FA and FC age effects (ρ = -0.302, pspin = 0.002) (Fig. 2D). The nucleus-specific FC trajectories showed heterogeneous shapes and a PCA ordering that was not aligned with the FA-based gradient (Fig. 2E-F).

Conclusion

Extending research that treated the thalamus as an unitary structure, this work refines our understanding of the maturational timeline of nucleus-specific thalamocortical connections. Across childhood and adolescence, nuclei showed distinct structural trajectories that clustered into plateauing versus continuing maturation, aligned with cell composition that vary in cortical projection patterns and the SA position of cortical target, both thalamic features related to cortical connections. Contrary the pattern was unrelated to the classic first order and higher order categories that categorize the nuclei based on driver input. Our findings underline the strong link between thalamic nuclei and the cortex in development. Parallel to structural connectivity strengthening, we found a decoupling of functional connectivity between nuclei and cortex with age that could be driven by a strengthening of intracortical functional network connectivity.  Together, these nuclei-resolved developmental profiles offer a crucial framework of normative brain maturation and its relevance for cognitive development from a thalamocentric perspective. Further, it provides candidate targets for investigating altered development in conditions such as schizophrenia, where thalamocortical connectivity is implicated.
