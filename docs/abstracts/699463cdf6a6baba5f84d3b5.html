Introduction

Resting-state brain activity is a prime example of self-organized neural dynamics. Spatiotemporal patterns spontaneously form resting-state networks (RSNs), seen with fMRI BOLD imaging. Unlike task-evoked activity, RSNs show substantial temporal reconfiguration and exhibit highly irregular dynamics that often resemble noise. Yet, a few brief instances of strong signal (anti-)correlations, i.e. co-activation events (CAEs), suffice to reconstruct their canonical seed-based correlation maps.1 The implicit collapse of dimensions occurring transiently during such moments represents an evident manifestation of self-organization.

Non-linear dynamics provides a natural language for formulating such coordination dynamics in terms of instabilities, timescales separation, and dimensionality reduction. Macroscopic brain patterns are then considered to be encoded in a compressed form by stationary attractors or modes. The natural habitat of these modes is a low-dimensional state space or manifold, and the time evolution of their probability density dynamics is captured by the Fokker-Planck equation. However, evidence about how the resting-state manifold is organized to give rise to this range of configurations remains elusive.

Using the framework that we developed, here we identified two antagonistic states of dynamics at rest: a low coherence state (LCS) marked by widespread incoherence, and a high coherence state (HCS) exhibiting overall coordinated activity.2 We looked into their spatiotemporal profiles and uncovered their structure on the manifold. The geometries of the RSNs’ potential functions (linked to the information-theoretic surprisal) were further examined to understand the composition of the states. The attractive flow on the manifold for both LCS and HCS was extracted, and finally, state transitions were analyzed by assessing non-stationarity and the role of CAEs in shaping them.

Methods

Empirical fMRI data (repetition time (TR)=0.72s) from the Human Connectome Project were used to examine resting-state BOLD activity from unrelated participants (P=200). The analysis was replicated on the day-2 dataset and on a high-resolution subset (TR=0.6s) of the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset, consisting of elderly controls (CN, P=18), mildly cognitively impaired (MCI, P=23), and Alzheimer’s disease patients (AD, P=7).

Signals were first band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 0.18 Hz and Hilbert-transformed to obtain the analytic signal. For each participant, we extracted the instantaneous functional connectivity (FC) from the pairwise phase coherence between brain areas. The resulting N×N×T matrix, with N regions and T samples, was used to extract three metrics: 1. mean coherence of length T, used as input to a two-state hidden Markov model (HMM), i.e. the LCS and HCS; 2. temporal correlation of FC matrices, providing a brain fluidity measure, quantifying the richness of the functional repertoire; 3. aggregate of state-specific spectral radii of FC matrices evaluated the states’ effective dimensionality.

The states’ temporal organization was quantified via mean dwell times and fractional occupancy. Their manifold structure was first characterized globally by principal components (PCs) analysis. Spatial loadings were mapped to seven RSNs using a mixed effects model. States’ data were formed by concatenating the state-specific segments. The standard deviations of their temporal correlations quantified the richness of co-activation patterns for each state. Stationary probability distribution functions were empirically approximated by removing spatial (CAEs) and temporal (fast events) outliers, concatenating state-specific segments, and applying kernel density estimation to PC1 scores.

This routine was next applied separately to each RSN. Latent dynamics of networks and states were inferred with Bayesian hierarchical modeling (Figure E). Under the working hypothesis that gradient dynamics associate the densities’ modes to attractors, the stationary solution of the one-dimensional Fokker-Planck equation describes the underlying potential functions of the states (Figure B).3 The normal form of the quartic potential function (Thom’s unfoldings) admits only two free parameters —criticality (u) and bias (v) — that sufficiently explained the networks’ dynamics (Figure D). Additionally, the characteristic timescale of each network was provided via u, governing relaxation near putative pullback attractors that underlie the densities’ modes. Power analysis on the ADNI dataset allowed us to compare the networks’ u and v between CN and MCI groups only for either state.

The generalized potential has the same functional form as the information-theoretic surprisal. The stationary densities can enter into the free-energy formulation as approximate internal models, which contrast with the true posteriors when interacting with the environment (Figure C). The densities as a result admitted a model selection procedure, performed using the Bayesian/Akaike information criterion, to compare the quartic polynomial against lower- and higher-order polynomials.

The presence of attractors was evaluated by fitting a two-dimensional recurrent-states linear dynamical system with two states to HCS and LCS data for each network. Dynamic velocity quantified state-space speed in the PC1-PC2 plane. The timescale ratio of these two dimensions was derived from the velocity field.

A separate two-state HMM on the PC1 scores of the HCS networks identified two HCS modes, yielding a 3×3 transition matrix between the LCS and these modes. The transitions involving CAEs were counted and expressed probabilistically. Finally, simulation-based inference drawn on the transition matrix was applied to a simple periodic model with three solutions governed by a single free parameter.

Results

Resting-state data were partitioned into two dynamical states based on overall coherence: the LCS and the HCS. The HCS evolved in a significantly lower-dimensional space (p < 10⁻⁴) and was transient, whereas the LCS was temporally stable. Yet, time spent in the HCS strongly correlated with fluidity (rₚ = 0.77, p < 10⁻⁴), a metric associated with cognitive flexibility. Despite its short-livedness, the HCS was deduced to generate a richer variety of coactivation patterns. In the resting-state manifold, this appeared as bimodality along PC1, contrasting with the unimodal LCS distribution. Regional PC1 weights indicated that this difference was primarily driven by peripheral networks—visual (VIS), somatomotor (SMN)— and dorsal attention (DAN), and secondarily by the salient ventral attention (SVA) and core networks: executive control (CON), default mode (DMN), and limbic (LIM).

Network organization was characterized by extracting stationary density functions for each network and state, making up the observables (Figure A). The normal form of the quartic potential function provided the best fit, with two parameters, u and v (Figure D). At the state level, the LCS was sharply monostable (u ≫ 0), whereas the HCS ranged from flat monostability (u ∼ 0) to bistability (u < 0) (Figure G-left). At the network level, the LCS also showed strong monostability (u ≫ 0), decreasing mildly along the primary functional gradient. In the HCS, this gradient was mirrored and amplified, predominantly in the negative (bistable) range. However, uSVA and uCON were closer to zero, uDMN was positive and near zero and uLIM was well above zero (Figure G-right top). Pairwise HCS timescale ratios revealed that the DMN had the slowest dynamics—more than an order of magnitude slower than peripheral networks and DAN, and moderately slower than SVA and other core networks. The v parameter followed the functional gradient during HCS and was largest for VIS (Figure G-right bottom). Its generally positive values indicated a preference for low-activity solutions under bistability or left-tilted landscape under flat monostability, i.e. co-deactivations.

Analysis of the ADNI dataset showed that in the LCS, neither parameter differentiated CN from MCI except for uSMN (p < 10⁻²). In the HCS, however, the u parameter for peripheral networks and SVA significantly distinguished groups (p < 10⁻²), with uHCS showing uniformly positive effect sizes larger than uLCS (except SMN). The vHCS parameter did not separate CN and MCI, but correspondingly showed negative effect sizes. The uHCS values for CN reproduced the RSN-specific patterns described above.

State-space flow on the PC1–PC2 plane revealed the attractor manifolds across RSNs. The LCS displayed a single attractor manifold consistent with uLCS, whereas the HCS exhibited two manifolds that progressively converged along the functional gradient, as predicted by uHCS. Flow analysis in 20 participants revealed a slower manifold along PC2.

Given the HCS bimodality and the presence of the LCS, three states existed. Only 5% of all transitions corresponded to jumps between HCS modes, qualifying switches via the LCS as the mechanism for transitioning between the HCS modes. The overall transitions were asymmetric, showing a preference for mode changes, namely co-activations typically followed co-deactivations and vice versa. This preference diminished along the functional gradient. Sensitivity analysis using the transition matrices tuned a tri-stable periodic model near a bifurcation threshold at which only one solution would persist, indicating metastable dynamics.

Approximately half of the few HCS-mode jumps occurred together with CAEs that had been initially removed. These CAEs promoted longer, exploratory HCS trajectories—along PC1 during jumps and along PC2 on the slower manifolds, during mode-locked trajectories that, interestingly, exhibited less PC1 variance than the LCS trajectories.

Conclusion

The “labile brain” series posited that brain dynamics traverse from stable incoherence through instability to entrainment.4 We show this progression in spontaneous hemodynamics via a dynamically changing resting-state attractor manifold: a unimodal regime (LCS) that hosts incoherent activity, a bimodal regime with two attractors that enable high-coherence dynamics (HCS), and the associated reorganization of RSN dynamics.2

Transitioning from LCS to HCS reduced effective dimensionality and reorganized RSN dynamics along the functional gradient, producing temporal hierarchies with slower timescales in the core networks—notably the DMN—and in the SVA and CON, which we found close to criticality (u ~ 0). This configuration identifies DMN as the principal slow mode that can impose its timescale on peripheral networks and the DAN, thereby realizing a hierarchical self-organization of HCS. CON and SVA’s proximity to instability aligns with their roles in controlled and rapid attentional shifts, respectively, while DAN and sensory networks act as stabilizing, stimulus-locked modes.

Methodologically, in contrast to numerous studies focusing on fine-grained coactivation patterns to explain resting-state organization, our coherence analysis implicitly assembled patterns corresponding to similar subspaces within a low-dimensional manifold.2 The division of spontaneous BOLD signal into LCS and HCS has been noted previously, but our approach employed a temporally fine-grained method to extract these states based on instantaneous relative phases. Other phase-based studies used alternative clustering schemes that did not characterize the resting-state manifold, yet they have successfully identified distinct brain states for various applications. Projecting onto PC1 alone robustly separated LCS and HCS, and stationary densities derived from the Fokker–Planck solution provided principled estimates of the underlying attractor geometries.

This dynamical description admits an information-theoretic reading linked to the free-energy principle. In this framing, the separation observed across networks corresponds to sensory and internal states. The parameter u shaped internal-state geometries determining the type of information sampled from internal states under the principle of maximum entropy: in HCS sensory states were bimodal while internal states approached the uniform distribution, which maximizes entropy for discretized information; in LCS both sensory and internal states were unimodal Gaussian, consistent with continuous information encoding. This dichotomy parallels the rhythmic versus continuous (vigilance) sensory modes described by Schroeder and Lakatos (2009),5 and maps naturally onto an active-inference framing in which stationary densities act as marginalized priors.

HCS comprised brief, synchronized trajectories reminiscent of entrainment, with striking sensory networks’ co-(de)activation. Because HCS-mode jumps were rare, trajectories remained mode-locked and exhibited lower variability than monostable LCS trajectories—mirroring the well-known reduction of cortical variability during task-driven activity. We argue that HCS is task-like, potentially active-inference driven, where perception resolves prediction errors from mismatches between internal and external states; whereas LCS reflects spontaneous thought. Intriguingly, the structured HCS dynamics were substantially more informative than LCS for discriminating CN from MCI, primarily via the criticality parameter u, highlighting biomarker potential. Direct comparisons with task-fMRI will be crucial to validate the task-like interpretation of HCS and to further interpret the functional geometries of networks’ potential functions.
