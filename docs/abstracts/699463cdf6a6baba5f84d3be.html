Introduction

Neurodevelopmental trajectories play a critical role in shaping psychiatric outcomes and treatment response. Individuals with more pronounced neurodevelopmental burden exhibit greater clinical psychiatric severity, including increased treatment resistance and more severe symptomatology. These distinct profiles challenge the traditional "one-size-fits-all" approach in psychiatry¹, highlighting the need for objective biomarkers to characterize neurodevelopmental load and guide personalized interventions.

Electroencephalography (EEG) offers a promising avenue, combining low cost, accessibility, and direct neural oscillatory assessment. Resting-state spectral features, particularly alpha peak frequency (APF) and theta/beta ratio (TBR), consistently link to neurodevelopmental processes. APF reflects the dominant alpha band frequency (8-13 Hz), associated with cognitive maturation and cortical integrity²,³. Slower APF has been documented in autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, and intellectual disability³. Elevated TBR, reflecting increased slow-wave activity, marks neurodevelopmental alterations and executive dysfunction⁴.

Few studies have systematically examined how neurodevelopmental comorbidities influence EEG profiles in psychiatric populations. EEG biomarker research must account for substantial inter-individual variability and the continuum between normal and pathological states. Normative modeling approaches quantify individual-level deviations from expected age- and sex-adjusted trajectories, enabling outlier detection without requiring homogeneous diagnostic subgroups¹,⁵.

The present study leveraged a large clinical EEG dataset from individuals with psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders to: (1) establish normative models of APF and TBR based on psychiatric populations with neurodevelopmental disorders, (2) identify outliers among individuals with mixed neurodevelopmental-psychiatric profiles, and (3) characterize deviation patterns across diagnostic subgroups. 

Methods

We screened all individuals who received a diagnosis of neurodevelopmental and psychotic disorders (based on ICD-10 diagnostic codes) and who also underwent EEG recordings between 2019 and 2024, extracted from the GHU Paris Psychiatry and Neurosciences data warehouse. From an initial dataset of 13,803 individuals, 8,050 EEG recordings were successfully retrieved. We then kept only subjects with neurodevelopmental disorders or unique psychiatric diagnoses and no comorbidity. A final sample of 859 subjects was included. This comprised 565 subjects with primary diagnoses of anxiety disorders, mood disorders, or psychotic disorders, and 294 subjects with diagnoses outside these three categories (including mixed neurodevelopmental-psychiatric profiles and only neurodevelopmental profiles). The following clinical information was extracted for each patient: age at EEG acquisition, sex, psychiatric and neurodevelopmental diagnoses.

EEG data were recorded using 19 scalp electrodes with gel following the international 10-20 system, on a Micromed® setup. Impedances were maintained below 5 kΩ with a sampling rate of 256 Hz. Routine clinical EEGs had a mean duration of 20 minutes, performed at rest in a half-seated position. Protocols included rest sequences with eyes closed and eyes open, followed by stimulation procedures (hyperventilation and intermittent photic stimulation), all annotated.

Preprocessing was performed in MNE-Python. Signals were band-pass filtered (1–100 Hz), bad channels were detected with the pyprep RANSAC algorithm and interpolated, and data were re-referenced to the common average. Artifact components were identified using ICA with ICLabel, applying strict thresholds for brain-related vs. artifact components, followed by a second 1–45 Hz filter. Only the ~7-minute resting eyes-closed segment was retained. Data were epoched into 2-s segments, cleaned using Autoreject, and participants with excessive channel interpolation (≥3 channels or 2 occipital channels) were excluded. Power spectral density was computed across five frequency bands (delta: 1-4 Hz, theta: 4-8 Hz, alpha: 8-13 Hz, beta: 13-30 Hz, gamma: 30-45 Hz). Peak alpha frequency (APF) was computed as the frequency with maximum power within the alpha band (8-13 Hz) averaged across posterior electrodes (P3, P4, Pz, O1, O2). Theta/beta ratio (TBR) was computed as the ratio of mean power in the theta band (4-8 Hz) to mean power in the beta band (13-30 Hz) across central electrodes (C3, Cz, C4). 

Deviation from normative patterns was quantified using a normative modelling approach implemented via PCNtoolkit with Bayesian Linear Regression (Python) and GAMLss⁵. The dataset was divided into training and test sets based on diagnostic profiles. The training set comprised 565 subjects with primary diagnoses of anxiety disorders, mood disorders, or psychotic disorders, which underwent a stratified 80/20 split based on age an sex to preserve demographic distribution, resulting in 452 subjects for model training. Two distinct test sets were created: test set 1 (N=113, transferred from the initial 565 subjects) served as a comparative baseline for model validation, while test set 2 (N=294, subjects with diagnoses outside the three training categories, including mixed neurodevelopmental-psychiatric profiles) constituted our primary population of interest. 

The normative model was trained with age and sex as covariates, generating expected APF and TBR values and individual-level Z-scores, with outliers defined as |Z|>1.9. For each subject, we extracted observed, predicted, 5-95% credible intervals, and deviation scores values for APF and TBR. We then compared patterns of deviations across diagnostic subgroups and evaluated whether mixed profiles (test set 2) deviated more strongly than psychiatric-only individuals (test set 1). Group comparisons across diagnostic categories were performed using both test sets.

Results

At the population level, diagnostic subgroup distribution was as follows: psychotic/anxiety/mood disorders: 66.47%, mixed profiles (Psychotic/Anxiety/Mood AND neurodevelopmental disorders): 21.07%, neurodevelopmental disorders: 12.34%. The sample had a balanced demographic structure, with an age mean of 34.19 ± 17.07 years (range: 11–95), and 55.1% males / 44.9% females. These descriptive characteristics were used to ensure balanced stratification during model training and to contextualize subgroup comparisons in deviation analyses. Both PCNtoolkit and GAMLss normative modelling approaches yielded consistent Z-scores for alpha peak frequency (APF) deviations, demonstrating convergent validity across methods. Given this consistency, subsequent analyses focused on Z-scores derived from the GAMLss framework.

The psychiatric-trained normative model yielded stable predictions of APF and TBR across age and sex. In test set 1 (psychiatric-only validation set, N=113), we identified 5 APF outliers (4.4%), whereas test set 2 (mixed neurodevelopmental-psychiatric profiles, N=294) showed 27 APF outliers (9.2%). Mean Z-scores were comparable between the two groups (test set 1: −0.01 ± 1.03; test set 2: 0.02 ± 1.06).

A Student’s t-test confirmed the absence of significant differences in deviation magnitude between the two groups (t = –0.233, p = 0.8159), indicating that individuals with mixed neurodevelopmental-psychiatric diagnoses do not exhibit greater APF deviation on average than psychiatric-only participants. Chi-square analysis further confirmed that outlier proportions did not differ significantly between test sets (χ² = 1.94, p = 0.164), with observed outlier rates of 4.4% versus 9.2% not significantly different from expected proportions under independence.

We extended the analysis to the TBR. Using the same threshold criterion (|Z| > 1.9), we identified 16 outliers for APF and 11 outliers for the TBR in the mixed test set. The TBR did not differ significantly between test set 1 and test set 2 (t = 0.342, p = 0.7327). This suggests that neurodevelopmental comorbidity does not necessarily amplify deviations in theta/beta ratio either.

Outliers were distributed across all diagnostic categories rather than concentrated in a single subgroup, suggesting heterogeneous patterns of neurophysiological deviation. Among individuals with neurodevelopmental-only profiles, deviations from normative APF and TBR patterns occurred across multiple diagnostic categories including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), genetic syndromes, intellectual disability, and ADHD-related developmental disorders. Both low and high APF and TBR outliers were observed within these populations, indicating bidirectional deviations from expected neurophysiological patterns, with some individuals showing abnormally lower APF (Z < -1.9) and others exhibiting higher APF (Z > +1.9).

Conclusion

This study leveraged a large clinical EEG dataset to systematically quantify deviations in APF and TBR using normative modeling in individuals with psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders. By training normative models on a psychiatric-only cohort (N=452), we established age- and sex-adjusted reference trajectories enabling individual-level deviation detection in mixed neurodevelopmental-psychiatric profiles (N=294).

Our analyses revealed comparable deviation magnitudes between psychiatric-only and mixed-profile individuals (t = -0.233, p = 0.816), with no significant difference in outlier proportions (χ² = 1.94, p = 0.164). Despite this statistical equivalence, distinct patterns emerged in the distribution and direction of outliers. Deviations occurred across multiple neurodevelopmental conditions including autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, ADHD, and genetic syndromes, with both abnormally low and high APF and TBR detected, supporting bidirectional alterations in neurophysiological maturation. These findings align with prior literature on heterogeneous neurodevelopmental effects on resting-state oscillatory activity, while extending observations through a normative framework accounting for demographic variability.

The comparable deviation magnitudes and outlier rates suggest that neurodevelopmental comorbidities do not necessarily amplify APF and TBR abnormality magnitude but may reflect qualitatively different neurophysiological signatures warranting individual-level investigation. Several limitations must be considered: broad diagnostic categorization may obscure condition-specific patterns, and treatment effects (computed as olanzapine, fluoxetine, and diazepam equivalents) were not integrated into current analyses. Future work will incorporate granular diagnostic information and pharmacological covariates to refine deviation specificity.

Overall, normative modeling provides a powerful framework for identifying individual neurophysiological deviations in clinical populations, offering insights into neural mechanisms underlying psychiatric-neurodevelopmental comorbidities and enabling personalized assessments in clinical neuroscience.
