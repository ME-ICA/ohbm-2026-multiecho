Introduction

Depressive rumination refers to automatic and recurrent dwelling on negative thoughts in the context of affective, obsessive-compulsive, or generalized anxiety disorders1. It is a transdiagnostic psychopathological process and a viable treatment target. Existing neuroimaging studies have revealed a complex brain network underlying depressive rumination, involving the default mode network (DMN), the fronto-parietal cognitive network (FPCN), the dorsal attention network (DAN), and the ventral attention network (VAN)2. While these correlational studies provide valuable insights into the neural underpinnings of depressive rumination, the observed alterations or associations may be spurious, epiphenomenal, or reflect compensatory processes. Lesion network mapping can complement correlational findings by clarifying the direction of causality3. This distinction is particularly critical for therapeutic targeting, as modulating a compensatory process could worsen symptoms rather than alleviate them. Here, we first assessed the ruminative tendency of a group of patients with glioma, then characterized its relationship to the functional connectivity of the glioma locations. We hypothesized that the functional coupling between the lesion locations and other brain regions would be associated with depressive rumination.

Methods

A group of patients with glioma (N = 59, 27 females, mean age = 44.57 years) was recruited from Huashan Hospital, Shanghai, China. We evaluated participants' tendency to ruminate using the 22-item ruminative response scale (RRS). Tumor regions were manually delineated slice-by-slice by the co-author Z. F., a practicing neurosurgeon, and then spatially normalized to the MNI standard space. Traced lesions were used as individual seeds in a resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) connectivity analysis using data obtained from 1574 patients with major depressive disorder (MDD)(the DIRECT consortium phase II)4. All rs-fMRI data were preprocessed using DPABISurf 5. Functional connectivity (FC) was determined by calculating Pearson’s correlation between the averaged time series of the tumor mask and every vertex of the brain surface. The resulting FC maps were then averaged across all the DIRECT-II patients to generate a single normative FC map for each tumor mask. A general linear model (GLM) was constructed to examine the correlation between patients' ruminative tendency and normative FC across all vertices, generating a rumination circuit. Next, this rumination circuit was subjected to statistical testing. During this procedure, 1000 sham rumination circuits were generated using random coordinates. To this end, a z-score map was computed by subtracting the mean of the 1,000 null samples from the observed t map and dividing the result by the standard deviation across the 1000 null samples. Finally, a |z| ≥ 2 threshold was applied to get the corrected rumination circuit, following a previous work (Figure 1).

Results

We found a depressive rumination circuit that demonstrated a unique topographic pattern (Figure 2a). More importantly, 3 clusters were revealed after statistical tests were applied. The temporal parietal junction (TPJ), ventral lateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), and the ventral temporal cortex (VTC) in the right hemisphere showed significant positive associations, indicating that the disruption of these three regions’ activities might lead to higher ruminative tendency (Figure 2b).

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that brain lesion locations associated with depressive rumination are connected to a specific brain circuit, with significant peaks in the right TPJ, VLPFC, and VTC. These results indicate a rumination-antagonistic circuit alongside the well-documented rumination-support brain circuit in the literature. The identified rumination circuit peaks might help identify novel pharmacological and neuromodulatory treatment targets.
