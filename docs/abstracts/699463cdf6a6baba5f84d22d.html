Introduction

Childhood and preadolescence constitute a critical window for neurodevelopment, marked by extensive reorganization of brain structure at both regional and large-scale network levels. During this period, distributed cortical and subcortical systems become increasingly differentiated, supporting the maturation of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral functions. These processes are reflected in changes in morphological similarity, i.e., coordinated structural variation across brain regions, making network-level reorganization a core aspect of brain maturation. Importantly, maladaptive behaviors emerging during this sensitive developmental phase may influence ongoing neurodevelopmental processes.

Sleep disturbances (SD) are highly prevalent during childhood and adolescence and are associated with reduced quality of life as well as cognitive and emotional difficulties. Prior studies have linked SD to structural brain alterations, including differences in regional volume and cortical thickness. However, most works have focused on region-wise measures, providing limited insight into how SD relates to the reorganization of structural brain networks. In addition, reliance on group-average analyses may obscure substantial inter-individual variability. Thus, it remains unclear whether SD is associated with network-level alterations that are common across individuals or highly variable, and whether such alterations normalize with age or instead reflect delayed or altered maturation.

Here, we address these gaps by examining how SD relates to individual differences in structural brain network organization. By comparing each individual’s pattern of structural covariance to age-matched normative patterns, we identify subtle network differences at the individual level and assess whether similar patterns recur across individuals with SD. We then examine how these SD-related network patterns change over time.

Methods

We analyzed T1-weighted structural MRI and behavioral data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (Release 5.1), comprising 10,009 participants aged 9–12 years who completed two waves of assessment (baseline and two-year follow-up). Sleep disturbance (SD) status was defined independently at each wave using the Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC). Participants exceeding the validated clinical cutoff (total score >39) [1] were classified as having sleep disturbance (SD), while those scoring below the cutoff were classified as good sleepers. These two groups formed the basis for all subsequent analyses. T1-weighted images underwent standard preprocessing using the ABCD pipeline. Following preprocessing, images were parcellated using the Schaefer cortical atlas and the Melbourne subcortical atlas, yielding a total of 234 parcels. Parcel-wise gray matter volumes (GMVs) were extracted using the CAT12 toolbox.

For each wave, parcel-wise GMVs were regressed out for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and total intracranial volume (TIV) using linear regression. Using the resulting residuals, we constructed reference structural covariance networks (SCNs) by computing pairwise Pearson correlations between all parcels in 80% of good sleepers only. Importantly, individuals used to construct the reference SCNs were not included in any subsequent perturbation analyses. 

To characterize individual-level deviations from normative network organization, we applied a network perturbation framework. Specifically, each participant, who was not part of the reference set, was iteratively added to the reference SCN, and a new covariance network was recomputed (the “perturbed SCN”). The difference between the perturbed SCN and the original reference SCN was calculated for each edge, yielding subtle, edge-specific deviations that quantify how an individual departs from the normative structural covariance pattern, rather than inducing changes in the overall reference network itself. This perturbation procedure was applied to all individuals with SD symptoms and to the holdout 20% of good sleepers, ensuring that deviation estimates were derived exclusively from participants independent of the reference set.

At baseline, deviation prevalence was quantified across individuals with SD, and a data-driven top-20 SD-associated network motif was identified, defined as the 20 connections most frequently exhibiting significant deviations. This step ensured that subsequent analyses focused on network features that consistently recur across individuals rather than effects driven by single subjects. To assess robustness, we performed an edge-level permutation analysis in which edge identities were randomly shuffled while preserving the overall deviation distribution. Deviation prevalence was recomputed for each permutation to generate a null distribution against which the observed motif was statistically evaluated. 

The baseline SD-associated motif was held fixed across waves. Signed deviation scores (Z) and absolute deviation magnitudes (|Z|) for motif edges were extracted for each participant and wave and examined longitudinally in the SD and good-sleeper groups. Longitudinal effects were modeled using a stacked, cross-classified mixed-effects framework, with edges nested within subjects. Fixed effects included group (SD vs. good sleepers), wave, and their interaction, as well as covariates (age, sex, BMI). Random intercepts for subject and edge accounted for repeated measures and edge-specific variability. An overview of the analytic workflow is shown in Figure 1.


Results

Network perturbation analysis revealed significant individual-level deviations in structural covariance networks. Individuals exhibited statistically significant edge-wise deviations relative to age-matched normative networks, indicating that their structural covariance patterns systematically differed from reference expectations. An example of an individual differential structural covariance network (IDSCN) is shown in Figure 2A.

At baseline, we identified a data-driven top-20 SD-associated network motif whose deviation prevalence exceeded chance levels, demonstrating that these edges did not arise from random deviation patterns. The spatial distribution and full list of the 20 edges are shown in Figure 2B. The most frequently deviating edges showed negative deviation scores, indicating reduced structural covariance relative to normative expectations, and primarily involved subcortical connections centered on the nucleus accumbens shell. Deviation prevalence for individual edges was moderate, indicating that while a reproducible SD-associated network pattern was present, substantial inter-individual heterogeneity remained within the SD group. 

We next examined longitudinal changes in this SD-associated motif across two waves and in comparison with good sleepers. When tracking signed deviation scores (Z) over time, we observed a significant group-by-wave interaction, indicating that the direction of network reorganization differed between groups. As illustrated in Figure 2C, good sleepers showed a significantly steeper decline in structural covariance over two years (β = 0.011, p < .001), resulting in lower covariance at follow-up. In contrast, youth with SD exhibited a markedly flatter trajectory, consistent with reduced or delayed network reorganization. Analyses of absolute deviation magnitude (|Z|) revealed no significant group differences over time, indicating that developmental effects reflected differences in directional network reorganization rather than overall deviation strength.

Conclusion

Together, these findings indicate that sleep disturbances in preadolescence are associated with reproducible yet subtle alterations in the organization of structural brain networks at the individual level. SD was linked to a shared network pattern characterized by consistent deviations in specific connections, alongside substantial inter-individual heterogeneity in how this pattern is expressed. This underscores the importance of individual-level approaches for capturing meaningful neurodevelopmental differences that may be obscured in group-average analyses.

Notably, the most prominent deviations involved subcortical connections centered on the nucleus accumbens shell, a region implicated in sleep–wake regulation, arousal, and the integration of motivational and affective processes. The convergence of our network-level findings with prior evidence [2-4] suggests that circuits centered on the nucleus accumbens may be particularly sensitive to sleep disruption during development, with potential implications for emotional and behavioral maturation.

Importantly, longitudinal analyses indicate that SD is associated with altered trajectories of network reorganization, rather than increased overall deviation magnitude, consistent with delayed or less dynamic maturation of specific structural network features. Such alterations may have downstream consequences for cognitive and behavioral development over time. While these findings are based on normative deviation patterns and should be considered preliminary, they motivate future work integrating clinical, behavioral, and multimodal neuroimaging measures to clarify the mechanisms linking sleep disturbances to neurodevelopmental outcomes.
