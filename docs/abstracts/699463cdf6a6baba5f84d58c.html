Introduction

The claustrum is a thin, irregular, sheet-like neuronal structure located beneath the insular cortex.1 The claustrum is the most densely connected brain structure by size — it sends and receives connections to and from almost all regions of the cortex.2 This widespread connectivity suggests that the claustrum is critically involved in various widely distributed cognitive functions, including attention and executive control.3 Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) reliably demonstrates the claustrum's cortical and subcortical connections across populations and protocols.4

The current study aims to investigate the development of claustrum connectivity by examining claustrum microstructure and connectivity at various developmental stages and linking this connectivity with cognitive performance in neonates, children, and young adults. Our outcomes included (1) claustral microstructure, quantified using fractional anisotropy of mean diffusion, and (2) claustrum–cortex connectivity, assessed through connection probability and density. Microstructural and connectivity measures were first characterized within each cohort and then compared across cohorts using canonical ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. Finally, (3) we studied the associations between cognitive performance and claustrum connectivity within each cohort. 

Methods

We conducted a study involving 73 neonates (scanned at 40 gestational weeks) from the Developing Human Connectome Project (HCP), 77 children aged 6–9 years from the HCP Development, and 81 healthy adults aged 22–28 years from the HCP Young Adults. Diffusion-weighted images were preprocessed using optimized pipelines tailored for these datasets, which included correction for motion, susceptibility-induced distortions, and eddy currents, as well as the generation of a synthetic undistorted b0 image. These preprocessing steps are specifically designed to account for the significant variations in brain tissue properties among adults, children, and neonates. The delineation of the bilateral claustra was achieved through a deep learning-based automated segmentation approach and transfer learning from adult scans5. Fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) maps were calculated using the DTIFIT tool implemented in FSL to assess the microstructure of the claustrum. Structural connectivity between the claustrum and primary, associative, and cingulate cortices was evaluated using probabilistic tractography in the FSL toolbox, and connection probability (CP) was computed. To reduce site-related variability, microstructural (FA, MD) and connectivity (CP) measures were harmonized across acquisition sites using NeuroCombat (version 0.2.12), preserving biological variability associated with age and sex. The following statistical analyses were performed in Python: (1) OLS regression models were performed to compare microstructural and connectivity metrics between cohorts, controlling for age and sex. (2) We used principal component analysis (PCA) to identify patterns of claustrum connectivity across cortical regions and relate them to cognitive performance. Cognitive scores, encompassing fluid cognitive performance, attention, and executive functioning, were used in correlation analysis. A Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed (SciPy, version 1.11.4) to assess the relations between individual scores on the first (PC1) and second (PC2) principal components and cognitive performance.

Results

We observed that FA values increased from the neonatal to the adult group, while MD values decreased (p < .05) (Figure 1A-B).  For most cortical targets—including the prefrontal, parietal, and temporal associative cortices, as well as the primary motor, sensory, and auditory cortices—CP values increased from neonates to children and then decreased from children to adults (p < 0.05), although adults generally maintained higher CP than neonates, suggesting an inverted-U developmental trajectory (children > adults > neonates; p < .05) (Figure1 C). In contrast, the occipital associative cortex and primary visual cortex exhibited a distinct developmental pattern, showing a continuous increase in CP across cohorts (neonates < children < adults). Connections to the contralateral hemisphere exhibited a broadly similar developmental trajectory to ipsilateral connections, with increasing CP from neonates to children and then slightly decreasing in adults (children > adults > neonates; p < .05) (Figure 1D). Although CP between the claustrum and all primary and associative cortical regions was found to be higher ipsilaterally than contralaterally, CP for the cingulate cortices tended to be stronger for contralateral connections. 

Claustrum connectivity showed a relationship with cognitive performance in the adult stage. Significant relationships were found between contralateral CP PC2, which explains 14.8% of contralateral claustrum-cortical CP (Figure 2A) and indicates an anteroposterior gradient (Figure 2B) and executive function (r = -0.26, pFDR = 0.04) (Figure 2C). However, contralateral CP PC2 and fluid cognition (r = -0.22, p = 0.04, pFDR = 0.09) reached nominal significance but did not survive FDR correction in the adult cohort (Figure 2C).

Conclusion

The results suggest that the claustrum’s microstructure becomes more organized, and its connectivity to most brain regions strengthens from newborns to adulthood. Tractography analyses revealed extensive ipsilateral connectivity of the claustrum with cortical targets, while also identifying less dense yet widespread contralateral connectivity. Furthermore, claustrum connectivity patterns (particularly the anteroposterior gradient) achieve functional integration with executive networks and fluid cognition only in adulthood. The observed variations in the structural connectivity of the claustrum may reflect its adaptive capacity to modify connections based on the individual's developmental stage.
