Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common cause of dementia, progresses from cognitively normal (CN) through mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia, yet substantial clinical and neuropathological heterogeneity complicates early diagnosis and targeted intervention1. Multimodal neuroimaging offers a comprehensive view of brain changes in AD, as different imaging modalities are sensitive to both overlapping and distinct neurobiological processes. Yet, many multimodal predictive models remain black boxes, constraining biologically informed early detection.

To address this gap, we apply SuperBigFLICA (SBF)2, a semi-supervised data fusion framework that jointly decomposes multimodal images while predicting outcomes. It identifies linked latent spatial components that capture shared covariance across modalities while maximizing predictions, increasing sensitivity to gradual disease progression and individual variability.

We demonstrate this approach by predicting cognitive decline (clinical dementia rating–sum of boxes [CDR-SOB]), a continuous measure closely aligned with clinical diagnosis.

Methods

Data were obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI-3)4. One visit per participant with complete Aβ‑positron emission tomography (PET), tau‑PET, 3T T1‑weighted MRI, and CDR‑SOB was selected. The cohort spanned CN, MCI, and dementia; amyloid status was not used as an inclusion criterion, allowing the sample to represent broad cognitive aging rather than the biomarker‑defined AD continuum.

We applied SBF2 to gray matter density, cortical thickness, pial surface area, amyloid PET, and tau PET (Fig. 1A). The model (70/15/15 split) was trained to predict CDR-SOB and derive 50 latent multi-modal components most predictive of CDR-SOB. Subject loadings were then tested with Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression to predict diagnosis (i.e., CN, MCI, dementia) and Apolipoprotein E (APOE4) carrier status, with performance compared against demographic, single-modality, and naïve fusion baselines.

Results

The ADNI-3 sample included 274 participants split into training (n = 192), validation (n = 41), and test (n = 41) sets. The mean age was 70.8 ± 6.9 years, 56.2% were female, and most were White (92.7%) and non-Hispanic (96%); 40.5% were APOE4 carriers. Clinically, 63.9% were CN, 21.9% had MCI, and 14.2% dementia. 

SBF modestly predicted cognitive decline (r = 0.21) and outperformed all baselines for APOE4 prediction (AUC = 0.83 [0.69–0.94]) (Fig. 1B), driven primarily by Component 28, reflecting uni-modal amyloid alterations in sensory areas along a sensory–association axis of organization5 (Figure 1C), whose loadings were positively linked to CDR-SOB. SBF also achieved the highest diagnostic performance (macro AUC = 0.80 [0.60–0.92]). Component 28 also distinguished early dementia from CN and MCI (Fig. 2A-B), whereas CN vs. MCI classification was driven by Component 7 (Fig. 2C), a multimodal Amyloid-Tau-Neurodegeneration (A–T–N)5 pattern with loadings negatively associated with CDR-SOB (Fig. 2D), reflecting a CN phenotype that weakens with impairment. Component 7 combined low posterior amyloid, elevated temporal tau, and coordinated pial surface area changes across association and sensory regions.

Component 7 loadings positively correlated with CSF Aβ42 and negatively with CSF pTau181, while Component 28 showed the opposite CSF associations, consistent with their respective associations with brain amyloid and tau burden. Together, these results show that SBF captures biologically meaningful multimodal patterns aligned with CSF biomarkers while improving prediction of clinical diagnosis and genetic risk.

Conclusion

Applying SBF to the A–T–N framework demonstrates that semi-supervised multimodal fusion captures continuous cognitive variation while identifying interpretable brain patterns linked to diagnosis and genetic risk, enabling biologically grounded disease staging.
