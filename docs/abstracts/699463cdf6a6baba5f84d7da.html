Introduction

Stress is an unavoidable aspect of modern life that can profoundly impact well-being, and emotion regulation serves as a promising protective factor against the detrimental effects of stress ¹. Acute psychosocial stress disrupts cognition and amplifies negative affect, and engages salience and control-related brain systems, yet individuals differ markedly in the magnitude and consequences of this response 2. Understanding factors that can shape stress reactivity is critical because stress-related impairments in attention and executive control often emerge precisely when effective regulation is most needed. Emotion regulation (ER) strategies such as cognitive reappraisal reduce negative affect by reframing the meaning of emotional stimuli and recruiting prefrontal control mechanisms 3. However, most neuroimaging studies examine ER and stress in isolation or focus on how stress impairs ER, leaving open whether ER deployed before facing stressful situations can buffer subsequent stress processing and preserve goal-directed performance.

Here, we report an fMRI study testing the effect of ER on stress by combining a standard reappraisal task with the Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST) 4. This design allowed us to test whether regulating emotions can influence later stress at behavioral and neural levels, distinguishing effects on subjective appraisal from effects on cognitive performance during stress exposure. We hypothesized that, relative to the Maintain condition, engaging in Reappraisal before stress would reduce subsequent stress and attenuate performance decreases under acute stress. At a neural level, we also hypothesized that Reappraisal would dampen stress-related recruitment of salience and sensory–attentional systems (e.g., anterior insula/ACC, and occipito-parietal regions). We further expected that any protective effects of ER would be most evident under higher cognitive demands.

Methods

So far, 15 participants (8 F; 23.4±4.8 years) took part in the study and completed the ER and MIST tasks while undergoing fMRI (a sample of 60 participants is anticipated). Whole-brain functional and anatomical images were acquired on a 3T Magnetom Prisma MRI scanner (Siemens, Germany) using a 32-channel head coil. Functional images were collected with a T2*-weighted gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence (voxel size 2.5×2.5×2.5 mm³; TR=1500 ms; flip angle=70°; field of view=200×200 mm²; matrix=64×64).

The experimental design manipulated ER instruction within subjects (Reappraisal vs Maintain) and assessed its impact on stress. ER strategy was implemented as Reappraisal, operationalized as actively reinterpreting the meaning of negative images to make them seem less threatening, less negative, and reduce their emotional intensity. In contrast, during Maintain, participants were instructed to attend to the image without trying to change their emotional response. On each ER trial, an instruction cue (“Reappraise” or “Maintain”) was followed by a negative emotional image during which participants implemented the instructed strategy, and then emotional state ratings were assessed using a continuous visual analogue scale from -200 to +200 (“How do you feel?” from ‘very negative’ to ‘very positive’), providing a trial-by-trial index of regulation success. Negative pictures were taken from the International Affective Picture System and the Nencki Affective Picture System, and counterbalanced for arousal across conditions. Stress was induced using an adapted version of the MIST. On each trial, a time-pressured mental arithmetic problem was presented together with response options (0–9), followed by performance feedback. Arithmetic problems were generated at three levels of difficulty based on calculation complexity (low: 2–3 single-digit operands with +/−; moderate: up to 4 operands, two-digit numbers, allowing multiplication; high: 4 operands, two-digit numbers, including multiplication and division). In the Stress condition, time pressure adaptively changed to each participant’s performance to enforce a high failure rate (25–40% correct), whereas trials in the No-Stress (control) condition used matched arithmetic content without time restriction. Subjective stress ratings were obtained at baseline and after each stress manipulation using a continuous visual analogue scale from -200 to +200 (“How stressed do you feel?” from ‘not at all’ to ‘very much’) (see Fig.1).

Behavioral outcomes (emotional state ratings, stress ratings, and MIST accuracy) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for participants. Fixed effects tested manipulation checks for ER (Reappraisal vs Maintain) and stress (Stress vs No-stress), and ER effects on stress, operationalized as stress ratings and MIST accuracy following Reappraisal vs Maintain, including models evaluating moderation by MIST difficulty. fMRI data were preprocessed and analyzed in SPM25 (FIL, University College London, UK). Preprocessing included slice-timing correction to account for interleaved acquisition, realignment to the mean functional image to correct for head motion, spatial normalization to MNI space using the SPM EPI template, and spatial smoothing with an 8-mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) isotropic Gaussian kernel. For first-level analyses, subject-specific GLMs modelled the ER picture-viewing periods with separate regressors for Reappraisal and Maintain (Reappraisal vs Maintain), and the subsequent stress phase with separate regressors for Stress and No-Stress trials (Stress vs No-Stress), further split by preceding ER instruction (Stress-after-Reappraisal vs Stress-after-Maintain). At the group level, one-sample t-tests on individual contrast images assessed ER-related neural responses during picture viewing, Stress-related neural responses relative to No-Stress, and differences between stress following Reappraisal versus Maintain.

Results

The ER manipulation was effective, as Reappraisal shifted affect ratings in a more positive fashion relative to Maintain (t(1367.14) = 24.00, p < .001). Subjective stress ratings were higher in the Stress condition than in the No-Stress (t(26.56) = 8.49, p < .001), confirming the effectiveness of the manipulation. As task difficulty increased, MIST task accuracy decreased (t(1288.32) = -2.35, p = .019). Additionally, increased subjective stress ratings were linked to lower overall accuracy (t(71.65) = -3.24, p = .002), and this relationship was particularly pronounced for high-difficulty trials (stress rating-by-difficulty: p < .001). On a subjective level (based on stress ratings), Reappraisal did not significantly change subjective perceived stress in subsequent stress conditions, nor in the Stress (t(7.09) = 0.41, p = .70), nor in the No-Stress condition (t(7.79) = −1.51, p = .17). However, on an objective level (based on task performance) regulating emotions significantly influenced arithmetic task accuracy. Within Stress trials, Reappraisal was associated with higher accuracy than Maintain (t(25990) = 5.64, p < .001), with a difficulty-dependent pattern in which Reappraisal improved performance at moderate and high levels (reappraisal-by-moderate level: t(26190) = 13.43, p < .001; reappraisal-by-high level: t(26190) = 7.29, p < .001) (see Fig.2a).

At a neural level, whole-brain analyses used a cluster-forming threshold of p < .001 uncorrected (voxel-wise), and clusters were considered significant at cluster-level FWE-corrected p < .05. During the regulation phase, REAPPRAISAL > MAINTAIN engaged the superior parietal lobule and lateral/medial prefrontal cortex. Across participants, the contrast STRESS > NO STRESS elicited strong activation in the bilateral anterior insulae and inferior frontal gyri, dorsomedial prefrontal/anterior cingulate cortex, and medial temporal regions (hippocampus/parahippocampal gyrus). Critically, stress following Maintain of negative pictures was associated with greater activation than stress following Reappraisal (STRESS-after-MAINTAIN > STRESS-after-REAPPRAISAL) in left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex/anterior insula and bilateral occipito-parietal regions, whereas the reverse contrast yielded no suprathreshold clusters (see Fig.2b).

Conclusion

These preliminary findings indicate that engaging in cognitive emotion regulation before stress exposure supports goal-directed performance under acute psychosocial stress. Reappraisal selectively improved accuracy at moderate and high task difficulty, despite comparable levels of self-reported stress, suggesting that its benefits are not driven by reduced subjective stress but by more efficient task engagement. At the neural level, stress recruited salience and attentional-control networks, with stronger activation following Maintain than Reappraisal. Together, these results suggest that pre-stress emotion regulation acts as a proactive mechanism that buffers the impact of stress on performance and neural processing, particularly under high cognitive demands.
