Introduction

Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD) is a common psychiatric condition marked by excessive fear in social situations, negative emotionality, and reduced social pleasure. It typically emerges in childhood or early adolescence, impairing long-term psychosocial functioning. While its etiology involves genetic, neurobiological, cognitive, and environmental interactions, traditional research has focused on abnormalities in brain structure and function within regions like the anterior cingulate cortex, prefrontal areas, amygdala, and others, assessed via metrics such as cortical morphology, ALFF, and ReHo.

Recently, the microbiota–gut–brain axis has highlighted the gut microbiome’s role in anxiety disorders, influencing brain function through neuroendocrine, immune, and neural pathways. Although microbial alterations have been identified in depression and generalized anxiety, SAD-specific gut microbiome profiles and their relationships with brain abnormalities remain understudied.

This study integrates multimodal neuroimaging and metagenomics in adolescents with SAD. After confirming clinical abnormalities in social anxiety and related symptoms, we hypothesize that SAD adolescents exhibit: (1) distinct gut microbial composition and functional pathways; (2) characteristic structural and functional brain alterations in emotion- and cognition-related regions; and (3) specific associations between gut microbial features and neuroimaging metrics. We further explore the diagnostic potential of these microbial and neuroimaging markers. By simultaneously examining gut and brain profiles in SAD, this work provides an integrative perspective on its pathophysiology and supports future interventions targeting the gut–brain axis.

Methods

Participants
 This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Shanghai Mental Health Centre and local communities. Forty-eight participants (31 with social anxiety disorder [SAD]; 17 healthy controls [HC]), aged 8.98–17.64 years, were enrolled. HCs were recruited via advertisements. Diagnostic accuracy was ensured through independent clinician-administered MINI-Kid interviews (child and parent versions) followed by confirmation by a senior psychiatrist per DSM-5 criteria. Exclusion criteria for both groups included left-handedness, organic brain lesions, severe somatic illness, or high suicidal risk. SAD participants with other mental disorders were excluded. All were treatment-naïve and medication-free for two weeks prior to MRI. Symptoms were assessed using the Children's Social Anxiety Scale, Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders, Self-Esteem Scale, and Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Mental Health Centre (2018-46); written informed consent was obtained.

Fecal Sample Collection and Sequencing
 Fresh fecal samples were collected after a 12-hour fast, transported on ice, and stored at -80°C. DNA was extracted from 200 mg aliquots using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified via PCR (95°C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 98°C for 20 s, 58°C for 15 s, 72°C for 40 s; 72°C for 5 min) with barcoded primers 341F/806R. Reactions contained KFX HiFi Master Mix, primers, template DNA, and water. Negative controls were included. Amplicons were gel-purified, quantified, pooled, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform.
 Bioinformatics analysis was performed in QIIME2 (v2022.2). Primers were trimmed with cutadapt. DADA2 was used for quality filtering, denoising, merging, and chimera removal to generate an Amplicon Sequence Variant (ASV) table. Taxonomy was assigned using SILVA v138. Downstream analyses included intergroup comparisons (Wilcoxon rank-sum test), biomarker screening (LEfSe, LDA≥2), and functional prediction (PICRUSt2).

MRI Analysis
Images were acquired on a 3.0T Siemens Trio Tim scanner. High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical and 8-minute resting-state BOLD fMRI data were collected. Processing was performed using the validated multimodal pipeline, PhiPipe,1 to derive structural and functional metrics.

Statistics
 Datasets with >20% missing data were excluded. Missing continuous variables were imputed using random forest. Group comparisons employed two-tailed t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests (continuous variables) and chi-square or Fisher's exact tests (categorical variables). P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics and Clinical Profiles

The study included 48 participants (31 SAD, 17 HC). Groups did not differ in gender or BMI, but the SAD group was significantly older (14.6±2.0 vs. 12.3±2.0 years, p<0.001); age was controlled in analyses. As expected, the SAD group scored significantly higher on all clinical scales (all p<0.001 unless noted): Social Anxiety Scale for Children (SASC) total, Fear of Negative Evaluation (FNE), Social Avoidance and Distress (SAD subscale), Children's Depression Rating Scale (DSRS-C), total FNE Scale, and total Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED). SCARED subscales for social phobia, school phobia, somatic/panic (p=0.003), and generalized anxiety were also elevated. Only separation anxiety showed no difference (p=0.065), confirming the SAD cohort's marked symptom severity (Figure 1).

Gut Microbiome Alterations

LEfSe analysis revealed differentially abundant taxa (LDA>2). The SAD group showed enrichment of Roseburia, Parasutterella, and Clostridium sensu stricto 1. The HC group was enriched in Desulfovibrio, *Erysipelotrichaceae UCG-003*, and Saccharimonadales (Figs 2A,B). Functional prediction revealed enriched pathways in SAD included photosynthetic antenna proteins and inositol phosphate metabolism, while HC pathways included the Vibrio cholerae cycle and retinol metabolism (Fig 2C).

Brain Structural and Functional Differences

After age-adjustment, SAD showed significant neuroimaging differences. Structural alterations included: volume changes in left caudal anterior cingulate, left caudal middle frontal, and right entorhinal cortex (Fig 2D); cortical thickness differences in right rostral anterior cingulate, right posterior cingulate, and left paracentral cortex (Fig 2E); and surface area changes in left caudal middle frontal and right precentral regions (Fig 2F). Functional differences included: ALFF in right pars opercularis/triangularis (Fig 2G); ReHo in bilateral parahippocampal gyri, left middle temporal/fusiform, and right inferior parietal cortex (Fig 2H); and fALFF in bilateral pars opercularis (Fig 2I).

Microbiota-Brain Associations

Partial correlation analysis (age-adjusted) revealed significant associations between gut microbial features and brain measures (Fig 2J). Structural (thickness, area, volume) and functional (ALFF, fALFF, ReHo) indices correlated with specific taxa. For example, cortical thickness of right rostral anterior cingulate and left paracentral cortex, volume of left caudal middle frontal cortex, and functional metrics like ALFF in right pars opercularis showed moderate-to-strong microbiota correlations.

Diagnostic Potential of Biomarkers

ROC analysis evaluated diagnostic accuracy (age-adjusted). For gut microbiota, Saccharimonadales had highest AUC (0.871), followed by Parasutterella (0.848) and Ruminococcus_gauvreauii_group (0.841) (Fig 2K). For MRI biomarkers, left entorhinal cortex volume showed highest discrimination (AUC=0.850), followed by left posterior cingulate thickness (0.846) and left entorhinal area (0.843). Other notable biomarkers included left caudal middle frontal volume (0.835), right inferior parietal ReHo (0.831), and left pars opercularis fALFF (0.841) (Fig 2L).

Conclusion

In summary, the present study provides empirical evidence for a pronounced dysregulation of the microbiota–gut–brain axis in adolescents with social anxiety disorder (SAD). We not only characterized SAD-specific gut microbial profiles and multimodal brain abnormality patterns, but, more importantly, were the first to reveal an intertwined association network between gut microbiota features and brain structural and functional alterations in an adolescent population, and to validate their potential value as candidate biomarkers. These findings advance the pathophysiological understanding of SAD toward a more systemic and integrative framework. Although the causal mechanisms and therapeutic implications remain to be further elucidated, the current work lays an important theoretical foundation for the future development of novel diagnostic strategies and intervention approaches targeting the microbiota–gut–brain axis in SAD.
