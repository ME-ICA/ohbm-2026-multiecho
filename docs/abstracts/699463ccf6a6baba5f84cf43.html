Introduction

Collective decision making in human society is based on a principle of a majority rule but also accompanies consensus-making among individuals with different interests and ideas. Making a collective decision is often more desirable than non-decision even if it results in requiring some individuals to compromise. This motivates humans to make concessions, and also requires coordinating conflicting interests to make a majority decision. Our functional MRI study required participants make a majority decision in a small group and penalized them when a collective decision was failed to make and examined the neural mechanism underlying consensus-making.

Methods

Inside MRI, participants were required to decide on one out of two alternative candidates, A and B, by a majority rule with three others outside the MRI, i.e., one with the same interest as her (e.g., supporting candidate A) and two with conflicting interests (e.g., supporting candidate B). Whereas participants gain most from a decision on a preferred outcome (e.g., A), they gain nothing from a drawn one, and gain just in between from a decision on a less preferred outcome (e.g., B). Participants were compensated along with the points they gained during experiments. Based on the results of behavioral experiments, we manipulated the responses of three others into two opposite strategies. In high (low) concession strategy, participants faced others with high (low) probability of concessions, and thus were more (less) likely to make a majority decision by making concessions.  Additionally, the task had a preliminary ballot before voting on a majority decision. This served as the opportunity for “communication” before voting to let participants know whether others were willing to concede or not, beforehand. Participants could change which alternative to support between a preliminary ballot and a voting on a majority decision (e.g., from A to B or from B to A).  We focused on the brain activity during majority rule voting.

 24 participants took part in the experiment, including 10 women. The average age was 19.5 ± 2.2 years. MRI scan was performed using a SIMENS Prisma (3T) scanner with a Minnesota Multiband EPI sequence covering the whole brain. The number of slices was 51, with a slice thickness of 3 mm. The voxel size for each slice was 64×64, with a resolution of 3 mm. The volume TR was 1 second. There are two types of sessions: HIGH cooperative and LOW cooperative. Half of the participants began with the HIGH cooperative session followed by the LOW cooperative session, while the other half started in the reverse order.

Results

We defined the concession voting when participants voted on B (A) in the present round when B (A) won a majority support. We have twenty-nine round results to judge on participants’ concession voting. Figure 1 shows a distribution of proportion of concession voting among participants from low to high numbers. Figure 2 shows the distribution of concession voting with high concession strategy from low to high numbers with the distribution of numbers of concessionvoting with low concession strategy. Number of concession voting with partners with high concession strategy is correlated with a difference between numbers of concession voting between high and low concession strategis (r = 0.75, p>0.05). This means that participants responded to high concession possibility of others when making more concessions. Thus, we divided participants into two groups: concession group (CG) and non-concession group (NCG) with higher (0.41<) and lower (<0.35) concession with high concession strategy, respectively.

Significant activation was found in the left insula [−30 14 −6], right Insula [34 24 8], right BA08 [6 20 34], and left BA32 [-10 24 26]. No differential activation was found between CG and NCG. (Fig.3)

The proportion of concession voting with high concession strategy is negatively correlated with activation in the right Insula, the right BA8, and the left BA32 only among NCG.

DCM and PEB analyses were performed between the four significantly activated regions, and significant connectivity was found at almost all possible linkages between the four sites.

Conclusion

Observing activation in BA 8 and the bilateral Insula, the concessive voting decision might be implicated with both cognitive control and emotion. Correlation results also implies that the presence and absence of willingness to concede to others was represented in the brain activation.
