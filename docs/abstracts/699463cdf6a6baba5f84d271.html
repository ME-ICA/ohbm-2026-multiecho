Introduction

Sulci are the grooves formed by cortical gyrification, a developmental process that begins in mid-gestation and continues to evolve after birth. Sulcal morphometry provides a fine-grained characterization of cortical folding, and sulcal features have been shown to be genetically independent from other cortical metrics, such as cortical thickness and surface area, with higher heritability1. Among sulcal features, sulcal width, which reflects the degree of sulcal opening, has been identified as the most heritable metric2.

The central sulcus is one of the most robust and reliable sulci2 and represents a prominent anatomical landmark of the brain, delineating the primary motor and somatosensory cortices and playing a critical role in motor function. Previous studies have reported age-related sulcal widening, stabilizing around 30-40 years before resuming3. However, despite growing interest in sulcal morphology as a sensitive marker of cortical organization, no study has yet established a normative reference describing how central sulcus shape is expected to vary as a function of age.

Normative modelling provides a principled framework to characterize age-related variation in brain structure at the population level while accounting for inter-individual variability4. Importantly, this approach enables subject-level statistical inference by quantifying individual deviations from the expected normative pattern through deviation scores, thereby facilitating the identification of atypical brain features. 

Here, we establish a normative reference describing age-related trajectories of central sulcus width across adulthood in a large population-based cohort. As a relevant application, we examine deviations from this normative reference in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), a motor neuron disease known to affect the primary motor cortex.

Methods

Participants and image acquisition

To build the normative reference, we used data from the UK Biobank (UKBB; https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/) cohort, a large population-based study including neuroimaging, demographic, and behavioural measures. Full details of the imaging protocol are reported elsewhere (https://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/ukbiobank/protocol/); briefly, it included isotropic T1-weighted MPRAGE images (1 mm³ voxel size) with bias-field correction applied during acquisition.

To test the model on an independent dataset, we used data from the Canadian ALS Neuroimaging Consortium (CALSNIC; https://calsnic.org), a multi-centre longitudinal cohort including healthy controls and patients with ALS. Full details of the imaging protocol are reported elsewhere5; briefly, T1-weighted anatomical scans (1 mm³ voxel size) were acquired at each site using harmonized protocols on 3T scanners. 

Image processing

T1-weighted MRI data from the UKBB and CALSNIC datasets were processed using the ENIGMA-Sulci pipeline (https://enigma.ini.usc.edu/protocols/imaging-protocols/), which combines FreeSurfer (v6 for UKBB and v7.4.1 for CALSNIC) for cortical reconstruction and segmentation with BrainVISA (v5.1.2) for sulcal identification and labelling. The extracted widths of the left and right central sulci were used as response variables in the subsequent normative modelling analyses.

Normative modelling: 

To generate normative models of the central sulcus, we employed the open-source Python package PCNtoolkit (v1.1.1). Bayesian Linear Regression implemented in PCNtoolkit was used to predict central sulcus width from age, while adjusting for sex and site as fixed effects. Participants with missing values for sulcal width or any covariates (age, sex, or site) were excluded. The final UKBB sample included N = 55,459 individuals (age 44-85 years), which was split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets, stratified by sex and site to ensure balanced distributions. A B-spline basis expansion of age was used to capture non-linear effects, implemented as a cubic spline with three evenly spaced knots. Non-Gaussianity was modelled using a likelihood warping approach. Additionally, we ran separate models for males (N = 26,086) and females (N = 29,373) to assess sex-specific normative trajectories.

After building and evaluating the model on UKBB, it was applied to the independent CALSNIC dataset. For CALSNIC, we removed participants with missing values and those outside the age range of the UKBB sample. Only sites with at least 45 individuals were retained, resulting in a total of three sites. The remaining participants were split into two subsets: 20% (only healthy controls, stratified by sex and site; N = 47) were used for adaptation to estimate site-related effects while keeping the UKBB normative model parameters fixed, and 80% were used for testing (N = 132 ALS and N = 55 healthy controls). 

Statistical analyses: 

For each subject (n) of the test set of UKBB and CALSNIC datasets, deviation scores (z) were calculated for left and right central sulcus width (d) as:

znd = ynd - ŷnd /  √(σd2 + (σ*2)d)

where ynd is the observed sulcus width, ŷnd is the predicted mean from the normative model, σd2 is the estimated residual (noise) variance, and (σ*2)d represents the variance due to model uncertainty.

Model performance was evaluated in the test set using explained variance, mean squared log-loss (MSLL) and root mean squared error (RMSE), as computed by PCNtoolkit. 

To compare deviation scores between healthy controls and ALS in the CALSNIC dataset, two-sample t-tests were performed on z-scores.

Results

The normative trajectories for left and right central sulcus width, estimated across the full sample (N = 55.459), are shown in Figure 1A. Model performance in the test set indicated good predictive accuracy, and building the model separately for males and females revealed a qualitatively similar age-related pattern in both sexes (see Figure 1A).

As expected, z-scores in the UKBB test sample were close to a standard normal distribution for both the left (mean = 0.02, standard deviation, SD = 1.00) and right (mean = 0.02, SD = 0.99) central sulcus, indicating good calibration of the normative model. 

In the independent CALSNIC dataset, the model was first adapted, yielding approximately normal z-scores in healthy controls (left: mean = 0.019, SD = 1.05; right: mean = 0.020, SD = 1.04), which suggest good calibration. Compared with healthy controls, Z-scores from the CALSNIC testing set were significantly higher in ALS patients for both hemispheres (left: t = 3.86, p < 0.001; right: t = 4.16, p < 0.001), as illustrated by the rightward shift of the distributions in Figure 1B.

Conclusion

In line with previous work3, we observed that central sulcus width increases with age, partly reflecting age-related cortical atrophy. Here, we establish for the first time a normative reference, extending prior structural normative modelling efforts, which have mainly focused on cortical thickness, surface area, and subcortical volumes4. Notably, normative models enable inference at the individual level, quantifying deviations from the norm and capturing patient-specific heterogeneity, thus providing a concrete step toward precision medicine.

The application to an independent ALS cohort demonstrates that the model captures clinically meaningful deviations, highlighting its sensitivity to disease-related motor cortex pathology. Future work should extend the normative model beyond adulthood to include childhood and adolescence, capturing the full trajectory of sulcal development across the lifespan.  Furthermore, extending the model beyond the central sulcus and including other sulcal features, such as depth, length, and surface area, could provide a more comprehensive reference for studying deviations in brain structure associated with neurological and psychiatric disorders.
