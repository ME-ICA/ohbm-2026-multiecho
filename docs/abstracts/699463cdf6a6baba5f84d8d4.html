Introduction

Inter-manual transfer of a learned motor sequence is a well-documented but incompletely understood phenomenon. Previous work has shown that functional connectivity within the default mode, dorsal attention, and motor networks predicts motor sequence learning and transfer to the untrained hand.1,2 However, the temporal dynamics of these processes, and the specific neural mechanisms that determine successful transfer, remain unclear. Similar mechanisms may also contribute to recovery of function in a paretic limb following stroke, where improvements in motor performance could arise from transfer-like processes occurring during recovery. The present study investigated functional brain connectivity using fMRI during a motor sequence learning task to determine when, and how, inter-manual transfer emerges during learning. We further examined whether these neural dynamics are recapitulated in stroke patients to assess whether similar processes support post-stroke motor recovery.

Methods

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (TR = 1.2 s) was acquired before, during, and after a motor sequence learning task in 23 healthy participants (15 females, mean age: 28 years). Participants completed testing on trained and untrained sequences using the left and right hand before and after learning a sequence with the left hand. Transfer was quantified as the change in response time on the untrained hand relative to the trained hand. Using a sliding-window approach (window length: 30 TR, step: 1 TR), functional connectivity within and between the dorsal attention network (DAN), motor network (MOT), and default mode network (DMN) was assessed throughout learning to identify network-level and node-level connectivity patterns associated with transfer. Hierarchical clustering of node-level network characteristics was used to identify subsets of dynamic patterns, isolating groups of nodes whose activity was associated with transfer.3 Correlations between node characteristics and transfer score were used to isolate at what point during learning neural connectivity predicted behavioural transfer. In a second cohort of 16 patients (6 females, mean age: 72.9 years) with ischemic stroke restricted to the primary motor cortex and contralesional hand paresis, resting-state fMRI and nine-hole peg test performance were collected at three recovery stages: acute (< 10 days), early sub-acute (3 weeks), and late sub-acute (3 months).4 The same approach as above was used to identify dynamic network-level connectivity metrics. Using the network patterns identified in healthy participants, we quantified how often each patient expressed similar transfer-like neural states and examined associations between state occurrence and paretic hand dexterity.

Results

In healthy participants, mean betweenness (r = 0.65, p < 0.001), mean participation (r = -0.73, p < 0.001), and mean modularity (r = 0.79, p < 0.001) within the DAN-MOT-DMN networks correlated with later transfer during early and mid-stages of sequence learning at overlapping time points. These findings indicated that greater network segregation was associated with improved transfer, while preserved inter-network connectivity suggested a role for specific hub nodes in supporting transfer processes. Clustering of node-level dynamics identified nine stable clusters of nodes. One cluster showed a significant correlation between participation coefficient and transfer (r = 0.66, p = 0.001) at the time points of interest. The identities of these nodes and their relative participation coefficients were used to define a model of transfer-related neural dynamics. In stroke patients, resting-state fMRI revealed variable engagement of this model across individuals and time points. Importantly, the frequency of which patients expressed this transfer-like neural state correlated positively with paretic hand dexterity at the acute stage of recovery (r = 0.75, p = 0.001).

Conclusion

Inter-manual transfer during motor learning is supported by early and mid-stage reorganization of functional connectivity across dorsal attention, motor, and default mode networks, characterized by increased network segregation alongside selective hub-node engagement. These transfer-related neural dynamics were also observed in stroke patients and were associated with better paretic hand dexterity in the acute phase of recovery. Together, these findings suggest that inter-manual transfer and post-stroke motor recovery may rely on shared network-level mechanisms, with early learning-related connectivity patterns providing a neural framework for functional recovery.
