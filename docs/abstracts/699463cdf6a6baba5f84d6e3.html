Introduction

White matter (WM) architecture is essential for efficient neural communication and supports higher-order cognition. WM microstructure, assessed by diffusion MRI (dMRI), has consistently been shown to predict performance in complex cognitive functions — such as inhibitory control (IC), attention, working memory — during development. Longitudinal studies also indicate age- and tract-specific trajectories of WM microstructure as measured by Fractional Anisotropy (FA) in pathways such as the arcuate fasciculus, inferior longitudinal fasciculus, and cingulum, reflecting the protracted and heterogeneous maturation of WM during these developmental periods.

WM exhibits developmental but also training-related plasticity. A number of working memory training studies have been shown to induce WM changes, whereas far fewer studies have examined the impact of IC-specific training. Existing evidence shows that IC training can increase FA in frontotemporal and frontostriatal pathways, typically paralleling task-specific behavioral improvements, though findings in youth remain mixed. Mindfulness-based interventions, which engage broader self-regulatory mechanisms, have also been shown to improve IC and to change WM microstructure in fronto-cingulate networks in adults, with preliminary age-specific effects. Hence, if prior work suggests that IC training and mindfulness may promote WM plasticity, their comparative effects during childhood and adolescence-periods marked by heightened plasticity and rapid WM maturation-remain largely unknown. 

In this context, the present study aimed at directly compare two different training targeting IC using dMRI and behavioral measures to determine whether they elicit shared or modality-specific changes in WM microstructure and EF performance, with a focus on IC.

Methods

METHODS

Participants were enrolled in the APEX project, a multi-site longitudinal study of executive function training in healthy children and adolescents. Ethical approval was obtained from two IRBs, and informed consent and assent were collected for all participants. A total of 201 right-handed or ambidextrous participants (screened for full-term birth, typical vision, and absence of neurological/psychiatric history or MRI abnormalities) were recruited from schools. The final sample comprised 110 children (49 males; M = 9.84 ± 0.53 years) from Caen and 91 adolescents (36 males; M = 16.71 ± 0.53 years) from Paris, randomly assigned to Inhibitory Control (IC), Active Control (AC), or Mindfulness Meditation (MM) training groups.

MRI Acquisition and Processing
MRI was acquired on 3T scanners at Cyceron (Caen) and CIREN (Paris) including T1 and diffusion MRI. Diffusion-weighted images were collected using a single-shell SE-EPI sequence (2 mm isotropic resolution, b = 1500 s/mm², 30 diffusion directions). In children, one b=0 s/mm² image was acquired (TE = 75 ms; TR = 9 s), whereas in adolescents three b=0 s/mm² images were acquired (TE = 86 ms; TR = 11 s; flip angle = 90°). All adolescent scans additionally included a reverse phase-encoding acquisition (6 directions and one b=0 s/mm²) to enable susceptibility-induced distortion correction. 

Preprocessing (FSL 6.0.7.10) included susceptibility correction (topup), eddy-current and motion correction with outlier replacement, and slice-level motion modeling tailored for high-movement pediatric data. Diffusion tensors were then fitted to derive FA, MD, RD, and AD maps. Longitudinal TBSS was applied using midpoint registration and nonlinear alignment to the FMRIB58_FA template; skeletonization used an FA threshold of 0.3.

Voxelwise analyses were performed with randomise using 5,000 permutations and TFCE (P < 0.05, corrected). Significant TFCE clusters served as masks for extracting mean pre/post values from skeletonized metrics to determine the direction of microstructural change. Anatomical localization used the JHU WM atlas.

Cognitive and Behavioral Assessment
Eight executive tasks were assessed: Color–Word Stroop, Emotional Stroop, Simon, Stop-Signal Task (SST), Dot task, N-back, Trail Making Test, and ANT. Outcome measures included SSRT (SST), delay-discounting k2, and span scores. Reaction times were cleaned using a 2.5-MAD threshold within participant, session, and condition.

Training Procedure
Over five weeks, participants completed home-based computerized individualized tablet sessions (~15 min/session). The IC group trained adaptively on Stroop and SST variants targeting interference control and response inhibition. The AC group completed difficulty-adjusted general knowledge quizzes. The MM group practiced guided meditation (body scan, breathing) based on the French “Calme et attentif comme une grenouille” method; sessions matched the other groups in duration/frequency but were non-adaptive. All participants kept training logbooks, and at least 15 sessions were required for inclusion.

Behavioral Statistical Analysis
Only participants with ≥15 completed sessions and valid pre/post behavioral and MRI data were analyzed. For each task, linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) assessed training effects with Time (Pre, Post) and Condition (IC, MM, AC) as fixed factors, plus age and sex covariates, and a random intercept for participant. Significant Time × Condition effects were followed by Bonferroni-corrected pairwise contrasts using estimated marginal means (emmeans). Statistical analyses were conducted in R (4.3.1) using lmer; Type III ANOVAs were computed via afex. 

Results

RESULTS 

Demographic characteristics 

The final sample included 82 adolescents (mean age = 16.7 ± 0.53 years; 51 females) and 91 children (mean age = 9.8 ± 0.52 years; 52 females), evenly distributed across conditions (adolescents: AC = 26, IC = 29, MM = 27; children: AC = 30, IC = 32, MM = 29).

Behavioral results

In children, all tasks showed overall pre–post improvement, reflecting both training and test-retest effects. Training-specific effects, assessed by Time × Condition interaction, were detected only for the ANT task (χ²(2) = 7.96, p = .019): the IC group showed the strongest pre–post gains (estimate = 0.044, p < .0001), followed by the Mindfulness Meditation (MM) group (estimate = 0.023, p = .005), whereas the Active Control (AC) group showed no significant change (p = .15). No training-specific effects were found for the remaining tasks (all ps > .10).

In adolescents, training-specific effects were detected for two tasks. For the ANT, a significant Time × Condition interaction was found (χ²(2) = 10.80, p = .0045), with significant improvement in IC (estimate = 0.044, p < .001) and MM (estimate = 0.023, p = .005), but not AC (estimate = 0.011, p= 0.15 ). For the Emotional Stroop Task, there was a significant interaction (χ²(2) = 7.10, p = .029) associated with improvement in the MM group only (estimate = 0.081, p = .002). 

Together, these findings indicate that IC training robustly enhanced attentional control across age groups, while MM training additionally improved emotional interference processing during adolescence.

White-matter microstructure

Voxel-wise longitudinal TBSS analyses revealed no training-specific WM difference in children. In adolescents, however, significant training-related differences were detected in IC and MM. There was a higher RD reduction in MM compared to IC across major fronto-limbic and fronto-parietal tracts, including ATR, cingulum, forceps minor, IFOF, SLF, and UF, with largest clusters in forceps minor, left cingulum, and left IFOF. Conversely, there was increased FA in MM compared to CC in the cingulum and forceps minor. These findings indicate training-specific WM plasticity in adolescence, with broader microstructural changes following mindfulness training.

Conclusion

Taken together, these results show that training-related white-matter plasticity during development is both age- and modality-dependent. While inhibitory control training produced consistent behavioral gains across childhood and adolescence, mindfulness meditation elicited broader behavioral and microstructural changes specifically during adolescence, affecting fronto-limbic and fronto-parietal pathways implicated in attentional and emotional regulation. These findings suggest that adolescence may represent a sensitive window during which mindfulness interventions can more strongly engage large-scale self-regulatory networks at both behavioral and neurobiological levels. More broadly, our results highlight the importance of considering developmental stage and training content when assessing experience-dependent white-matter plasticity, and support the use of longitudinal diffusion MRI to disentangle shared versus intervention-specific mechanisms underlying executive function training.
