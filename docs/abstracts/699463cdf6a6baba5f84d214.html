Introduction

Microgravity disrupts vestibular, visual, and somatosensory balance, requiring new weighting strategies. As human space missions extend, understanding how space conditions alter brain function is essential. Cosmonauts face combined stressors that challenge neural integrity and performance. While somatic effects are well mapped, CNS consequences are only beginning to be defined.¹ Microgravity also alters intracranial physiology through fluid shifts and loss of hydrostatic gradients. MRI studies show CSF displacement, ventricular enlargement, and gray-matter redistribution in frontal, temporal, and sensorimotor regions, suggesting adaptive plasticity.² Functional MRI reports reduced connectivity in posterior cingulate, angular gyrus, and thalamus after flight, with increases in sensorimotor networks.³ A complementary foot-stimulation fMRI study in the same 11 ISS cosmonauts showed task-specific post-flight changes in an extended sensorimotor network, including increased coupling of right posterior supramarginal gyrus and reduced connectivity in vestibular nuclei, inferior parietal cortex, cerebellum, and visual regions.⁴ These patterns reflect network-level recalibration to a novel sensory environment. Parietal regions, particularly the inferior and superior parietal lobules, integrate multisensory cues for orientation and body schema and likely support this recalibration.¹ Cosmic radiation may further strain hippocampal and prefrontal circuits.¹ Cosmonauts also show subtle spaceflight-related cognitive changes across multiple domains after flight.⁵ We therefore asked whether connectivity relates to performance and whether post-flight parietal changes are uniform or individualized. Using ALE meta-analyses and Jülich atlas–derived right IPL and SPL seeds, we tested whether connectivity shifts track task-specific performance. We expected both group effects and performance-dependent variability, with individualized adaptations dominating.

Methods

Cosmonauts: Thirteen right-handed male Russian cosmonauts (median age 45.7) completed both ESA/Roscosmos Brain-DTI (MRI) and Pilot-T (cognition) studies. All flew long-duration ISS missions (median 173 days, range 157–199). Pre- and post-flight data were collected under ethical approval from Antwerp University Hospital, ESA, IBMP, the Committee of Biomedicine Ethics, and the Human Research Multilateral Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained. Post-flight MRI and testing occurred 7–14 days after landing to ensure comparable recovery.

Cognitive Assessments: Cosmonauts completed the Pilot-T battery before and after flight, probing spatial and executive domains:
 (1) SWM: dots appeared briefly in 5×5–7×7 grids; cosmonauts reproduced original locations (RT, errors).
 (2) ECE: subjects indicated each revolution of a point moving on a circular trajectory, either visible or occluded; accuracy indexed predictive timing.
 (3) VP: keypress to blue rectangle; RT and accuracy measured visual alertness.
 (4) NR: two digits appeared; subjects indicated odd/even sums (RT, errors).

Behavioral Indices: Post–Pre differences (RT, errors, accuracy) were z-scored within task levels and averaged to generate ΔPerformance (SWM, VP, NR) or ΔAccuracy (ECE). Higher values reflected better post-flight performance. Covariates were entered as subject-level regressors in connectivity models. Identical preprocessing across sessions prevented artificial asymmetry.

Psychological Covariates: State Self-Esteem Questionnaire subscales were repeatedly sampled. Paired t-tests and Reliable Change Index values (|RCI| > 1.96) identified mission-related changes. Only Anxiety (group increase) and Attention (individual declines) showed consistent effects and were included as nuisance covariates.

MRI Acquisition: Subjects lay supine with eyes closed. Resting-state fMRI was collected on a 3T GE MR750 with a 16-channel head-neck coil. Functional: T2*-EPI, TR 2000 ms, TE 30 ms, flip 77°, voxel 3 mm³, FOV 192×192×126 mm, 42 slices, 300 volumes. Structural: 3D T1 FSPGR, TR 7.9 ms, TE 3.06 ms, flip 12°, voxel 1 mm³.

Preprocessing: Preprocessing used SPM12/CAT12/CONN: realignment; slice-timing correction; coregistration to T1; normalization via CAT12 geodesic shooting; 6-mm smoothing. ART flagged motion outliers (>0.5 mm displacement or >3 SD global signal change).

Denoising: CONN’s standard pipeline included aCompCor (5 WM + 5 CSF components), 6 motion parameters plus derivatives, ART outliers, linear trends, and temporal filtering (0.008–0.09 Hz). Effective post-denoising degrees of freedom were ~170–181.

Seed Definition: Task-specific meta-analyses (GingerALE) identified peaks matching SWM, ECE, VP, and NR domains. Searches targeted ~10 papers per task; peaks (MNI) were extracted, cross-checked, and entered into random-effects ALE (5000 permutations, cluster-level FWE p < 0.05, voxelwise p < 0.01). Only two peaks converged across tasks: right IPL (+44, −38, +46, SWM) and right SPL (+40, −58, +44, ECE) (see Figure 1). Seeds were generated in R and scaled to ALE-cluster voxel counts. Cytoarchitectonic validation used size-matched Jülich ROIs (PFm/PGa; 7A/7PC). IPL and SPL were modeled jointly in CONN via a Between-Sources [1 1] contrast to capture shared parietal contributions; complementary analyses used Jülich ROIs.

Functional Connectivity: For each subject/session, seed-to-voxel maps were computed as Fisher-z correlations. Second-level GLMs tested Post > Pre and performance-dependent effects while controlling for Anxiety and Attention. Example model: [AllSubjects, ΔPerformance, Anxiety, Attention] with contrast [0 1 0 0]. Thresholding used voxelwise p < 0.005 and cluster-FDR q < 0.05. Maps were inspected to ensure anatomical validity.

Statistical Rigor: Permutation testing (10,000 permutations, cluster-FWE p < 0.05 at voxelwise p < 0.001) validated parametric results and ensured stable connectivity-behavior associations.

Results

Behavioral changes: Across the four cognitive domains (SWM, ECE, VP, NR), cosmonauts showed heterogeneous Post–Pre trajectories rather than uniform improvement. Composite ΔPerformance and ΔAccuracy indices (z-scored, sign-reversed for RT and errors) revealed both gains and declines, consistent with individualized adaptation. These inter-individual differences were used as covariates in resting-state analyses.

Main group effect: No uniform Post > Pre connectivity change emerged when all subjects were modeled together. Connectivity shifts varied as a function of task performance, indicating that parietal plasticity was performance linked rather than global. The combined contrast across tasks also showed no group effect unless behavioral covariates were included, demonstrating that resting-state plasticity depended on individual differences in cognitive adaptation. This held for both ALE-derived IPL/SPL seeds and corresponding Jülich ROIs.

SWM: Better SWM performance predicted stronger post-flight coupling of the right IPL/SPL seed with pre- and postcentral cortices, middle frontal gyrus, and occipital regions including fusiform and occipital pole (voxelwise p < 0.005, cluster-FDR q < 0.05) (see figure 2). These regions form a fronto-parietal–occipital circuit supporting visuospatial updating and executive–motor coordination. Jülich ROIs (PFm/PGa; 7A/7PC) reproduced this pattern, including increased left inferior frontal and precentral coupling (−48, +10, +22), highlighting a robust IPL/SPL–IFG pathway for adaptive control. Nonparametric permutation testing (10,000 permutations; cluster-FWE p < 0.05, voxelwise p < 0.001) retained a single right pre/postcentral SPL cluster (+40, −32, +70; p-FWE = 0.02), confirming that the most reliable SWM-related reorganization centered on a sensorimotor–parietal node. No Jülich clusters survived permutation correction, reflecting limited power and conservative thresholds.

ECE: ALE analysis revealed a bidirectional pattern: increased IPL/SPL coupling with fronto-motor and occipital cortices and decreased coupling with left cerebellar (VIIb/VIII, Crus I–II) and temporal regions (STG/MTG) (see figure 2). Jülich ROIs reproduced these trends, showing enhanced occipital fusiform/lingual connectivity and reduced cerebello-temporal coupling, including bilateral VIIb/VIII clusters and left STG/planum polaire. Permutation testing retained only the left superior lateral occipital cortex (sLOC) / angular gyrus cluster (p-FWE = 0.03). Jülich analyses supported occipital (fusiform/lingual) and cerebellar (−44, −44, −52; −24, −70, −50) effects. Together, results identify sLOC/AG as a robust locus for predictive visuomotor recalibration after spaceflight.

VP & NR: VP showed a local occipital pole increase under parametric testing, consistent with subtle sensory recalibration; no effects survived permutation testing (see figure 2). NR revealed no significant connectivity change under the prespecified model, suggesting minimal impact on symbolic processing.

Cross-task convergence: Across domains, right IPL/SPL connectivity changes tracked behavioral improvement but reflected opposite orientations: fronto-parietal–occipital enhancement for SWM versus cerebello-temporal suppression for ECE. These complementary patterns indicate task-specific network reweighting supporting either visuospatial updating or predictive efficiency. Permutation validation confirmed the reliability of this bidirectional parietal organization.

Conclusion

Post-spaceflight changes in resting-state parietal connectivity were not uniform across cosmonauts but varied systematically with task-specific behavioral outcomes. Apparent group-level increases, such as AG–frontal pole coupling, disappeared once performance was modeled, indicating that mean effects largely reflected shared components of recovery rather than true uniform plasticity. Including ΔPerformance and ΔAccuracy revealed distinct patterns across cognitive domains, demonstrating that neural reorganization depended on individual adaptation rather than exposure alone.

Two complementary reorganization profiles emerged. For SWM, better post-flight performance was associated with strengthened coupling between right IPL/SPL and pre/postcentral, middle frontal, fusiform, and occipital regions. This fronto-parietal–occipital integration aligns with visuospatial updating demands and mirrors effects reported in analog models such as head-down bed rest, suggesting efficient re-engagement of spatial control networks upon return to 1G. For ECE, performance predicted a bidirectional pattern: increased IPL/SPL coupling with fronto-motor and occipital cortices and decreased coupling with cerebello-temporal regions. This profile resembles the transition from error-based to feed-forward control in expert visuomotor learning, reflecting more efficient predictive processing during gravitational re-adaptation.

VP and NR showed limited or no parietal reorganization. VP effects were confined to local occipital increases, consistent with low-level sensory recalibration. NR showed no connectivity change, implying that symbolic processing relies on stable intraparietal mechanisms that do not require large-scale network adjustment. Thus, post-flight plasticity selectively engages networks supporting spatial updating and prediction but not simpler perceptual or symbolic operations.

Convergence across ALE-derived seeds and cytoarchitectonic Jülich ROIs confirmed that effects were not artifacts of seed choice. Both approaches identified performance-linked strengthening of fronto-parietal pathways for SWM and complementary cortical-enhancing and cerebello-temporal-reducing patterns for ECE. Neither approach produced a group-level Post > Pre effect independent of behavior, reinforcing the central role of individualized adaptation. Divergences between ALE and Jülich patterns primarily reflected seed granularity rather than conceptual differences, with both supporting a shared visuospatial and predictive control network.

Permutation-based validation identified the most robust and reproducible clusters: a right SPL/postcentral node for SWM and a left sLOC/AG cluster for ECE. These stable loci anchor broader but more variable task-dependent patterns, consistent with heterogeneous recovery trajectories across cosmonauts.

Overall, the findings support a model in which spatial working memory and predictive visuomotor tasks drive targeted reweighting of parietal, frontal, occipital, and cerebellar systems, whereas perceptual and symbolic tasks rely on more stable circuits. Individual behavioral adaptation—not group-mean exposure—best explains the direction and magnitude of connectivity changes. These results highlight individualized neuroplasticity as a key feature of gravitational re-adaptation and identify IPL/SPL-centered networks as promising targets for monitoring, modeling, and countermeasure development in future long-duration missions.
