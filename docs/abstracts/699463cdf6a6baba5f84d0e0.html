Introduction

Neuroscience approaches to the study of emotions have flourished since the advent of neuroimaging techniques, with focus shifting from a one-to-one mapping between categorical emotions and brain regions, to network-based accounts of emotion behavior. Yet, despite extensive work adopting discrete or dimensional theoretical approaches, relatively few studies have examined emotions though a componential lens.
Methodological limitations such as low statistical power, heterogeneous paradigms and blurred distinctions between emotional experience, perception and expression have made it difficult to interpret previous findings ^1. Moreover, the ecological validity of passive, indirect, third-person emotion induction via pictures, faces, words have been increasingly questioned ^2.
Recent studies highlight the importance of distributed functional networks, reporting overlaps in activation patterns across emotion categories ^3, suggesting that emotion networks are not dissociable from cognitive and perceptual systems ^4. These findings align with multi-componential theories, which propose that emotions arise from recursive interactions between cognitive, motivational, physiological, motor and subjective feeling components. In this view, appraisal theorists argue that the cognitive component, the contextual appraisal of stimuli, modulates the others to drive changes in emotion episodes ^5.
In this project, we investigate emotional brain systems with fMRI using a dynamic, naturalistic, first-person video game paradigm to manipulate anticipation and outcome of reward and loss. This approach aims to link theoretically defined emotion components to brain networks during immersive emotional episodes.

Methods

We developed a dynamic first-person video game to systematically manipulate uncertainty and expectation appraisals (Fig 1A). Participants aimed to maximize points by collecting energy batteries (reward) while eliminating robot enemies that removed points (loss). Visual cues were used to manipulate anticipation of reward or loss, followed by either confirmed or disconfirmed (no item) outcomes (Fig 1B). Subjective feeling ratings and Appraisal checks were obtained for each condition.
Participants (N = 41) performed the task while undergoing fMRI (3T Siemens, GE-EPI, TE=31ms, 54 axial slices, TR=720ms, MB factor=4). Brain data was preprocessed using fMRIPrep 20.2.2 with fieldmap distortion correction, coregistration, normalization, followed by 6mm FWHM smoothing. A general linear model (GLM) was used to examine main effects of anticipation (positive, negative and uncertainty) and outcome conditions (positive/negative confirmed/disconfirmed). Motion parameters and white-matter and CSF signals were included as nuisance variables. Behavioral data were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVAs and paired t-tests.

Results

Behavioral analyses revealed a significant Condition × Emotion interaction (p < .001), indicating that emotional ratings depended on both anticipated and revealed outcomes. Post-hoc tests demonstrated robust differentiation between trial types across emotion categories, with valence-congruent anticipatory affect (Fig. 2A) and outcome-dependent emotional responses (Fig. 2B).
Whole-brain contrast analysis showed overlapping striatal, thalamic and midbrain activation during both loss and reward outcome, with anterior insula activation specific to loss outcome contrast (Fig 2C). Expectation violations elicited stronger activation in parahippocampal and lingual gyri for both positive and negative disconfirmed outcomes, while bilateral insula activation was observed selectively for positive disconfirmation (Fig. 2C–D).

Conclusion

By combining immersive, first-person task design with component-specific manipulations, this study provides an ecologically valid framework for mapping emotional components onto distributed networks. Findings show that anticipation and outcome stages of emotion episode recruit partially overlapping but dissociable brain networks, consistent with multi-component theory of emotions.
