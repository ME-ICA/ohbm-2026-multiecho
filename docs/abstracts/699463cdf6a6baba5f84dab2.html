Introduction

Rapid advances in cognitive neuroscience have positioned functional connectomics as central to understanding brain organization, identifying psychiatric biomarkers, and guiding precision neuromodulation. Yet, a “neuropsychometrics crisis” now hinders progress: brain-behavior studies often lack reliability, validity, standardized designs, or cross-site consistency—undermining replicability and translation. In response, a “reliability-first” mega-framework has emerged, prioritizing psychometric transparency as the foundation for cumulative knowledge. This demands a new knowledge infrastructure that integrates high-reliability evidence at scale, informs rigorous experimental design, and tracks evolving consensus.

While Large Language Models (LLMs) offer powerful tools for organizing scientific knowledge, general-purpose LLMs fall short in connectomics due to insufficient grasp of methodological nuance, statistical constraints, and domain-specific ontologies. Their heterogeneous, outdated training data rarely reflect the field’s current emphasis on neuropsychometrics, risking misleading outputs in high-stakes contexts like clinical inference or multimodal integration.

To bridge this gap, we propose a specialized AI engine rooted in functional connectomics methodology and organized around neuropsychometrics. It formally encodes the triadic reliability–validity structure—test-retest stability, measurement precision, and cross-site replicability—as the scaffold for evidence retrieval, organization, and inference. Built on this principle, we developed deepCoRRseek, a localized, domain-specific knowledge base that aligns modern AI capabilities with the rigor of connectomics, advancing the field toward a neuropsychometric-driven science.

Methods

To build deepCoRRseek, a reliability-centric knowledge infrastructure for human brain mapping, we employ a four-stage methodology: core-literature anchoring, citation-network expansion, structured knowledge extraction, and LLM-enhanced retrieval and generation.

Stage 1: Core-Literature Anchoring.

We anchor the system on three foundational papers: (1) “An open science resource for establishing reliability and reproducibility in functional connectomics” [1]; (2) “Harnessing reliability for neuroscience research” [2]; and (3) “Test-retest reliabilities of resting-state fMRI measurements in human brain functional connectomics” [3]. These works established frameworks for evaluating test–retest reliability, harmonizing multi-site data, and optimizing reliability in clinical translation—serving as theoretical and empirical bedrock.

Stage 2: Citation-Network Expansion.

Using Web of Science and Semantic Scholar APIs, we perform a two-tier citation crawl: first collecting all direct citations to the anchor papers (n = 1,215), then identifying secondary references cited the first-degree corpus (n = 34,293). After deduplication, we obtain a local corpus of 24,833 articles (2014–2024), covering functional connectomics, reliability methodologies, multi-site fMRI protocols, and clinical biomarker validation

Stage 3: Structured Knowledge Extraction.

We parse full-text PDFs using GROBID for structure recognition and SciSpacy with custom fine-tuned NER models. From each article, we extract five structured categories:

(1) Reliability metrics: ICC, Cronbach’s α, Dice coefficient, confidence intervals;

(2) Imaging parameters: TR/TE, scan duration, motion correction, denoising pipelines (e.g., ICA-AROMA, CompCor), software versions;

(3) Parcellation schemes: Yeo-7, Schaefer-400, Power-264, HCP-MMP;

(4) Sample characteristics: age, sex, DSM-5 criteria, sample size, recruitment sites;

(5) Key conclusions and limitations, especially regarding generalizability and replicability.

These entities and relationships are formalized into a domain-specific ontology and stored as a queryable knowledge graph.

Stage 4: LLM-Enhanced Retrieval and Generation.

We implement a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework based on the open-source DeepSeek-32B LLM[4]. Starting from pre-trained weights, we apply two-stage fine-tuning:

(1) Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) using 1,200 expert-curated “question–evidence–answer” triples, constraining outputs to reliability-focused findings with explicit literature citations;

(2) Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) to improve evidence tracing under ambiguity and promote calibrated uncertainty expressions (e.g., “This finding is from a single-site study, N=30; cross-site replication is limited”).

The retriever uses a hybrid embedding strategy combining Sentence-BERT (dense) and BM25 (sparse), with ranking biased toward high-reliability studies (e.g., ICC > 0.7 or multi-site validation).

Deployed on a secure local server, deepCoRRseek ensures data provenance, inference transparency, and scientific rigor. By fusing methodological precision with AI reasoning, it empowers researchers to design reproducible experiments, assess biomarker robustness, and generate hypotheses grounded in neuropsychometrically validated evidence—advancing brain-wide association studies (BWAS) into a reliability-driven science.

Results

The deepCoRRseek knowledge engine is now publicly accessible at http://www.deepmri.cc/deepcorrseek, marking the debut of the first localized, interactive knowledge platform explicitly dedicated to reliability in functional connectomics. It supports core research workflows—including experimental design consultation, methodological validation, and literature synthesis assistance—by delivering evidence-based, context-aware responses grounded in high-quality neuroimaging literature.

In terms of knowledge coverage, the system incorporates a rigorously curated corpus of 24,833 peer-reviewed articles, of which 87.9% (n = 21,832) explicitly address reliability assessment in functional connectomics. These studies span multiple imaging modalities: resting-state fMRI (76.1%, n = 16,606), task-based fMRI (15.7%, n = 3,425), multimodal integration (6.3%, n = 1,375), and methodological innovations (1.9%, n = 426). Core reliability concepts—such as “test–retest reliability” and “cross-site consistency”—occur with high frequency (>150 mentions per 1,000 articles), confirming the corpus’s strong thematic alignment with reproducibility principles.

deepCoRRseek systematically integrates 17 authoritative brain atlas templates, and organizes them into the following three categories:

(1) Resting-state–derived parcellations: Yeo-7, Schaefer series (100–1000 parcels), and Power-264;

(2) Structure–function hybrid atlases: HCP-MMP1.0, Brainnetome-246, Desikan–Killiany, and AAL (90/116 regions);

(3) Disorder-specific templates: including the ABCD pediatric atlas, ENIGMA schizophrenia network, and ADNI-AD atlas.

Notably, the Schaeferatlas is employed in 78.2% of relevant studies, establishing it as the current de facto standard in the field.

With respect to preprocessing pipeline standardization, deepCoRRseek identifies 309 distinct combinations of processing steps. A clear generational transition is evident: traditional sequential pipelines (e.g., SPM12 + DPABI + bandpass filtering) now represent only 19.7% of usage, whereas fully automated, best-practice frameworks (e.g., fMRIPrep + ICA-AROMA + CompCor) account for 28.1%. Significantly, privacy-preserving federated learning–enabled pipelines (e.g., COINSTAC-based federated preprocessing) have achieved a 3.8% adoption rate, facilitating secure, multi-institutional collaboration without raw data sharing.

To assess content fidelity and domain alignment, we conducted representative query evaluations. In response to “What are the top ten scientific questions in human brain mapping?”, deepCoRRseek avoided speculative or generic forecasting. Instead, it synthesized consensus-driven insights from Zuo et al. (2019) and its citation network, articulating reliability-centered frontiers such as:

“How can we establish reliable functional connectivity biomarkers across the lifespan?”

“How should measurement error be modeled in large-scale multi-site studies?”

“How do individual differences and transient state fluctuations affect test–retest reliability?”

Each proposition was explicitly anchored to source literature (e.g., Nature Human Behaviour 2019; NeuroImage 2021), reflecting strict adherence to the “reliability-first” mega-paradigm.

When queried about “key privacy and security challenges in human brain mapping,” deepCoRRseek—though primarily focused on methodological reliability—drew upon secondary citations from data-sharing initiatives (e.g., COBRE, ABIDE) to identify critical issues such as ‘the difficulty of de-identifying high-dimensional neuroimaging data’ and ‘re-identification risks in cross-institutional data aggregation’. It further referenced emerging safeguards, including INCF data standards and nascent federated learning approaches, while transparently noting that ‘this topic remains underrepresented in the current reliability-focused literature’. This response exemplifies calibrated uncertainty communication and robust epistemic boundary awareness—hallmarks of a trustworthy scientific AI system.

Conclusion

The development of deepCoRRseek represents an initial realization of a novel knowledge infrastructure for neuroimaging—one explicitly grounded in methodological reliability as its foundational principle. By systematically integrating high-impact literature into functional connectomics and fusing large language models (LLMs) with structured, domain-specific knowledge graphs, deepCoRRseek delivers professional, traceable, and context-sensitive knowledge services tailored to the demands of rigorous brain science. Built upon three seminal publications and their expansive citation network, the system’s local corpus comprises 24,833 peer-reviewed articles. This corpus not only encompasses core themes such as test–retest reliability and cross-site consistency but—through formal ontology modeling—enables fine-grained semantic relationships among imaging acquisition parameters, brain atlas templates, cohort characteristics, and statistical reliability metrics. This architecture facilitates deep semantic retrieval that substantially transcends conventional keyword-based search.

deepCoRRseek is now publicly deployed and freely accessible to the global research community. Preliminary user feedback highlights its practical utility in literature synthesis, experimental design optimization, and the interpretation of reliability metrics, offering early validation of the “reliability-driven” knowledge engine paradigm. Nonetheless, a rigorous, systematic evaluation of its scientific impact remains essential. In forthcoming work, we will implement a domain-expert blind assessment framework: cognitive neuroscience researchers will independently evaluate system-generated responses to representative queries—such as multi-site protocol selection or biomarker stability judgment—along dimensions of factual accuracy, methodological rigor, and evidential transparency, with performance directly benchmarked against general-purpose LLMs.

In the long term, deepCoRRseek will broaden its scope to incorporate reliability evidence from structural connectomics, longitudinal developmental trajectories, and clinical intervention responses. We also aim to integrate the system with mainstream neuroimaging analysis pipelines (e.g., fMRIPrep, Nilearn), thereby enabling a closed-loop “from knowledge to code” workflow that bridges evidence synthesis and computational implementation. More fundamentally, we advocate a paradigm shift: in brain science, AI systems should not prioritize linguistic fluency alone but must be constrained by methodological consensus, functioning as enablers—not sources of noise—in the pursuit of reproducibility. deepCoRRseek embodies this vision: a transparent, locally deployable, and rigor-first knowledge engine that offers a sustainable, technically grounded pathway toward mitigating neuroscience’s reproducibility crisis.
