Introduction

Traumatic stress exposure commonly triggers chronic pain, but its development remains poorly understood. We sought to identify distinct pain trajectories after trauma and examine brain connectivity differences between groups during the peritraumatic period. We leveraged the Advancing Understanding of Recovery After Trauma (AURORA) Study,1 which collected questionnaires and neuroimaging data from trauma survivors for six months post-trauma. We examined phase-gradient directionality (PGD), a metric that quantifies the predominant direction of wave propagation within a specific brain region,2 measured two weeks after the traumatic event. We hypothesized that individuals with more severe pain symptoms would exhibit altered PGD in hubs of the default mode (DMN), salience (SLN), and sensorimotor (SMN) networks previously associated with chronic pain.3 

Methods

We performed hierarchical clustering using the participant’s age (from emergency department intake), number of painful body sites (measured by the Regional Pain Scale) and pain intensity (measured by PROMIS Pain Interference - Short Form 4a) collected two weeks following the traumatic event. Resting-state fMRI data acquired at the two weeks post-trauma visit were preprocessed using fMRIPrep, denoised using the CONN Toolbox, and parcellated using the 400-parcel Schaefer atlas (7 networks, 2mm resolution). PGD was calculated from extracted timeseries data, focusing on selected parcel subsets associated with pain processing, and spatial phase gradients were estimated across neighboring parcels. Group differences in PGD were analyzed using general linear models with race/ethnicity, imaging site, and framewise displacement as covariates. Sex differences were investigated by sex-stratifying our sample.

Results

Four distinct groups characterized by differing spatial distributions of pain emerged from hierarchical clustering: minimal pain (n=39), localized high-intensity pain (n=123), diffuse high pain (n=80), and older-adult moderate to high pain group (n=91). The minimal pain group served as a reference for all analyses. There was a difference in the distribution of participant sex amongst the four groups (p=0.001), so subsequent analyses were stratified by sex. Among females, all three groups with moderate or high pain had significantly higher PGD bilaterally in the postcentral gyrus (older adult group: β=0.012, p=0.002; localized high-intensity group: β=0.009, p=0.006; diffuse high pain group: β=0.008, p=0.023). In males, the moderate pain older adult group had significantly lower PGD in the right (β=-0.013, p=0.003) and left insula (β=-0.011, p=0.020), higher PGD bilaterally in the cingulate (β=0.034, p=0.018), precuneus (β=0.011, p=0.005) and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC; β=0.012, p=0.007). 

Conclusion

Altered spatiotemporal patterns in core SLN (insula), SMN (postcentral gyrus), and DMN (precuneus, PCC) regions may reflect altered pain processing following trauma in individuals with moderate pain symptoms. These findings also suggest that pain mechanisms may vary according to age and sex, underscoring the need for future studies to elucidate these relationships. The promise of new spatiotemporal modeling techniques may advance our understanding of the neurobiological basis of chronic pain.
