Introduction

Family history of alcohol and other substance use-related problems (FH+) and individual alcohol and substance use have both been individually linked to neurodevelopmental alterations, particularly within the frontal cortex (Gonçalves et al., 2024; Miller et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2021). However, potential synergistic effects of both FH+ and early substance initiation (before age 15) on frontal cortical development, and whether synergy varies based on the type of substance, remain to be explored. Here, we extend our previous work showing that FH+ is associated with accelerated frontal cortical thinning at ages 9-13, and explore if this neurodevelopmental pattern continues to age 15, with more pronounced frontal cortical thinning among those with alcohol and/or cannabis use initiation. Our aim was to test the associations of family history with potential additive and/or synergistic effects of substance use initiation among adolescents from ages 9-17. 

Methods

We used data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study (ABCD, Release 6.0, total sample size 11,878). Our primary exposure was FH+, defined as having ≥1 biological parent(s) and/or ≥2 biological grandparents with a history of alcohol/substance use problems (FH+, n=2,571, FH- [no parents or grandparents with history of alcohol/substance use related problems], n= 6,299). Individuals with only one grandparent with alcohol/substance use problems (n= 1,401) were excluded from the models. Our secondary exposure was substance use initiation, measured by self-report and/or positive hair toxicology. We defined lifetime substance use initiation as reporting having a full nicotine tobacco product, full drink of alcohol, 1+ puﬀ of cannabis or any cannabis product, or any illicit substance use (e.g., stimulants, opioids, inhalants, and others), or confirmed positive hair toxicology results (n=2,522). We also generated a 3-level substance initiation variable (i.e., no use, early, or late onset) representing no substance use (n= 6,348), early initiation of substance use (before age 15, n=1,516), and late initiation of substance use (after age 15, n=1,006). Age of initiation was calculated based on the first year the participant reported use as defined above or the first year of a positive hair sample from ages 9-17. Our primary outcome was frontal cortical thickness trajectories from baseline to 6-year follow-up (4 time points, ages 9-17). Linear mixed-effects models were fit to estimate associations between FH and cortical thickness of 11 frontal regions bilaterally from the Desikan-Killiany atlas. Our final sample comprised 8,870 participants after excluding those with only one grandparent with a positive family history of alcohol/substance use. (n= 1,401), did not pass MRI quality control, and/or reported using alcohol only for religious purposes.

We ran a series of linear mixed-effects models. We started by running models with only age, modeling age-related frontal thickness change as a linear and quadratic function. Linear age models demonstrated better model fit parameters; therefore, we modeled age as a linear effect in all subsequent models, similar to what we have done in prior work (Gonçalves et al., 2024). We then ran a 4-way interaction model with overall frontal thickness (22 ROIs), age, FH, and substance use initiation, followed by a model examining whether family history and any substance initiation (binary variable) was related to cortical development. Our main models focused on testing the relationships of FH, brain development, and the 3-level substance initiation variable by implementing a 4-way interaction term (FH x age x brain location x time of substance initiation). The models included sex at birth, pubertal development status (time-varying), MRI software as fixed effects, and MRI device, family relatedness, and participant ID as random effects. The second step of the analysis included performing an estimation of the slopes for each group and pairwise comparisons (e.g., FH [+/-] and substance initiation [no use, early or late substance onset]), adjusting for multiple comparisons using the Tukey method. 

Our sensitivity analyses included a) examining substance specificity, investigating the potential impact of alcohol only and cannabis only on frontal cortical trajectories after controlling for any nicotine as fixed effects, and excluding those with other substance use; b) fitting the same main model in temporal, parietal and occipital regions to determine if our primary findings were specific to frontal cortices.

Results

In our main models, we observed significant 3-way interactions between FH, substance initiation and frontal cortical development, including models with any substance use (p value = 0.017, with more rapid thinning among those with a FH+ and substance use with a slope of -0.152 compared than those FH- and no substance use, -0.141) and the 3-level substance initiation variable (p value = 0.022). Pairwise comparisons identified significant differences in frontal cortical trajectories between those with a FH- | no use vs FH- | early initiation (estimated difference of the rate of change per year = 0.021, p value < 0.001) and FH+ | no use vs FH- | early initiation (0.019, p value < .001), suggesting a more rapid frontal cortical thinning among adolescents with early substance use regardless of FH status. 

The next step was to observe how the age of onset of specific substances, such as alcohol use (455 participants with early alcohol initiation, 672 with late alcohol initiation), and cannabis use (741 and 742, respectively), would relate to FH and frontal development. The model showed significant interactions between FH, age of alcohol initiation, and frontal cortical development (p = 0.034), with pairwise comparisons showing differences among those with FH- and no alcohol use vs. FH+ and late alcohol initiation (difference of the rate of change per year = 0.013, p = 0.001). Results also exhibited interactions between FH, age of cannabis initiation and frontal cortical development (p = 0.015); as displayed in Figure 1, pairwise comparisons showed that the FH+ with early cannabis initiation had significant differences when compared to participants with FH+ with no cannabis use (p=0.012), FH- and no cannabis (p = 0.006) and FH- and early cannabis initiation (p = 0.050). Therefore, those with a FH+ and early cannabis initiation showed a steeper acceleration of frontal cortical thickness than those without cannabis use or FH-, indicating that a biological vulnerability may intensify the effects of early cannabis initiation on brain development. 

Finally, when examining the substance age initiation (i.e., any substance exposure) models with other brain regions (i.e., temporal, parietal, and occipital), we did not observe the same interactions of FH, age, and substance use initiation. 

Conclusion

Relationships among family history and substance use initiation are complex; we found that the combination of FH+ and early substance use initiation was associated with a greater rate of frontal cortical thinning, above and beyond the additive effects of these risk factors. Our findings show links between early substance initiation and more accelerated frontal cortical thinning, combined effects of FH+ and late alcohol use, and potential synergistic effects of FH+ and cannabis use. Early adolescence is a sensitive period for neurodevelopment, and the present work suggests that any substance use before age 15 could have a long-term impact on brain developmental trajectories that span across adolescence (i.e., late childhood to mid-to-late adolescence). Our second finding on FH+ and late alcohol use suggests that familial risk may impact brain developmental trajectories compared to those with neither exposure (FH- and no alcohol use). Our results also indicate that adolescents at familial risk for substance use problems may be more likely to exhibit altered frontal cortical development following early cannabis use exposure. This latter finding is in line with research showing that cannabis use among those with a history of psychiatric disorders increases the risk for adverse mental health outcomes. Therefore, future research should investigate how the interplay between familial risk and substance use contributes to behavioral and clinical-related outcomes. 

Some limitations should be noted. We did not address more granular facets of substance use behavior (e.g., frequency, quantity), as well as mode of consumption and types of cannabis products. We also did not assess for family history of other psychiatric disorders and did not include adolescent psychopathology levels in the models.
