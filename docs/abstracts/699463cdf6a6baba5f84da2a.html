Introduction

Studying how the language network varies across individuals is essential for understanding differences in linguistic abilities and susceptibility to language-related disorders. While group-averaged neuroimaging studies portray a relatively stable large-scale language network, mounting evidence indicates that this uniformity masks meaningful heterogeneity in functional connectivity, with individuals expressing distinct but reproducible connectivity patterns [1,2]. Resting-state functional connectivity subtyping has emerged as a powerful approach to capture this variability in a structured way. Identifying connectivity subtypes could reveal distinct organizational configurations that underlie inter-individual differences in language function and provide a framework for linking connectivity to behavior and clinical outcomes. Moreover, it is unclear to what extent biological sex, previously shown to influence aspects of language organization and connectivity, contributes to the expression of these subtypes. We hypothesize that resting-state functional connectivity of the language network can be decomposed into reproducible subtypes that capture meaningful inter-individual variability. Furthermore, we expect that the expression of some subtypes may differ between men and women, reflecting sex-related differences in language network organization.

The objective of this study was to identify resting-state functional connectivity subtypes of the language network using the SENtence Supramodal Areas AtlaS (SENSAAS) in a large sample of healthy young adults. Then, we quantified each participant’s expression of these subtypes and examined whether subtype expression differed between men and women.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were obtained from 520 young adults (243 men, 277 women, ages 22-35) from the Human Connectome Project. Data were processed using the updated HCP-Young Adult (2025) preprocessing pipelines, including gradient- and distortion-corrected minimal preprocessing, surface-based alignment, spin-echo–based bias-field correction, and ICA-based denoising with improved spatial ICA and temporal ICA to reduce motion and physiological noise. Preprocessed grayordinate (CIFTI) time series were used for all analyses. ROI-to-ROI functional connectomes were computed using the SENSAAS language atlas[3], yielding 64×64 Fisher Z transformed connectivity matrices for each participant. Functional connectivity subtypes were derived from vectorized ROI-to-ROI matrices using a hierarchical cluster analysis, which clusters individuals based on shared patterns of connectivity while controlling for noise and enforcing minimum subtype stability. For each participant and each subtype, a subtype expression weight was computed as the correlation between the participant’s individual connectivity profile and the subtype connectivity template, providing a continuous measure of how strongly each individual expressed each subtype. Associations between biological sex and subtype expression were assessed using non-parametric two-sample rank-based tests (Mann–Whitney U), which test for differences in the distribution of subtype expression weights between men and women. P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate (FDR).

Results

Results: Five connectivity subtypes of the language network were identified, each capturing distinct patterns of within- and between-language-network coupling. Qualitative inspection of the subtype connectivity maps revealed distinct organizational hubs across the dominant subtypes: subtype 1 showed strong connectivity between bilateral dorsal frontal and parietal language-related regions, subtype 2 exhibited pronounced left-lateralized frontal-temporal connectivity, subtype 3 was characterized by a diffuse but stronger bilateral temporo-parietal connectivity, subtype 4 was showed reduced overall connectivity particularly in the frontal and parietal regions and, subtype 5 exhibited stronger connectivity between the left and right temporal regions. 

Comparisons of subtype expression weights between men and women revealed significant sex-related differences for two subtypes after FDR correction (Subtype 1: p = .003; Subtype 2: p = .015). For both subtypes, women showed higher subtype expression weights than men, indicating that these connectivity patterns were more strongly expressed in women. The spatial organization of these subtypes further revealed subtle differences in intra- and inter-hemispheric connectivity patterns associated with sex.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that the resting-state functional connectivity patterns of the language network can be decomposed into reproducible connectivity subtypes.  Differences in subtype expression between men and women suggest that biological sex may shape specific functional configurations of the language system. Furthermore, by identifying connectivity subtypes that likely support different aspects of language processing, this framework provides a principled approach for linking intrinsic network organization to individual differences in language function and for guiding future studies examining behavioral, developmental, and clinical variability in language abilities.
