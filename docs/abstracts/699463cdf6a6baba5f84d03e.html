Introduction

Three-dimensional reconstruction of serial histological sections enables high‑resolution neuroanatomical analysis. Accurate alignment of slices is essential because each section may undergo non‑uniform deformations during cutting, mounting, or staining. This task is particularly challenging when no 3D reference, such as MRI, is available.  

Image‑based registration methods are commonly used 1 but can be sensitive to intensity variations and inter‑slice inconsistencies, especially when anatomical structures are smaller than the slice thickness. Contour‑based registration instead emphasizes geometry and topology, providing a stable basis for alignment and facilitating subsequent surface reconstruction, which is crucial for functional and structural cortex mapping 2.

Registration is typically conducted in successive rigid, affine, and non‑linear stages. A methodological difficulty arises from the proper handling of geometric transformations: Euclidean operations such as linear averaging are not well defined on transformation manifolds. To ensure invertibility and consistent averaging, we adopt a Lie‑group formulation for rigid, affine, and stationary velocity field (SVF)‑based non‑linear transformations 3. 

In this work, we investigate contour‑based registration strategies for aligning serial histological slices without 3D reference. Variants of the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm are employed, and transformations are parameterized in their Lie‑group form. We compare simple sequential registration schemes, which are prone to cumulative drift, with multi‑slice and alternating optimizations designed to enhance global consistency.

Methods

Registration algorithms

Registration is performed using variants of the ICP algorithm applied successively in rigid, affine, and non‑linear stages. The rigid and affine components follow the classical ICP formulation 4.

The non‑linear stage is cast as an energy minimization within the SVF framework to guarantee diffeomorphic transformations 5. We optimize a set of velocity vectors, one per point of the moving contour. These vectors are convolved with a Gaussian spatial kernel to generate a smooth velocity field, which is then integrated to produce the diffeomorphic transformation evaluated at each point. The objective function comprises a symmetric Chamfer‑distance data term and a Laplacian‑like regularization term promoting spatial smoothness of the SVF. 

In all stages, correspondences are estimated bi‑directionally, using nearest neighbors from reference to moving contours and vice versa.

Serial registration strategies

Registration across the stack of slices was organized along two independent design axes: propagation scheme and multi‑neighbor formulation.

  • Propagation scheme:

    • Sequential: Slices are registered in order, where all preceding slices have already been transformed, the current slice is being optimized, and subsequent slices remain in their original positions. 

    • Alternating: Slice updates are performed in alternating passes. During the first pass, a subset of slices (e.g., even‑indexed) are moved while the others (anchors) are held fixed; the roles are then reversed in the subsequent pass. 

  • Multi‑neighbor formulation:

    • Independent then average: The current slice is registered independently to each of its neighbors, yielding multiple pairwise transformations. These transformations are subsequently combined through averaging performed on the Lie algebra and then Lie exponentiated (log-Euclidean average) to obtain a that still lies within the same transformation group.

    • Simultaneous: The current slice is simultaneously registered to all its neighbors by jointly aggregating correspondences and estimating a single transformation. A single registration per slice is necessary but each involves a higher computational load.

Naïve approaches that use sequential propagation with a single neighbor are vulnerable to drift (the “banana effect” 1). Drift can be mitigated by employing symmetric neighbor selection (before and after the current slice) and/or using fixed anchors. Registration of the full stack may be iterated to improve consistency.

Optimization in the non‑linear stage was implemented efficiently using automatic differentiation tools (JAX).

Results

Material

We used a postnatal day 32 (P32) ferret brain surface reconstructed with manual editing from anatomical MRI. One (left) hemisphere was isolated and resliced along the antero‑posterior axis to obtain a ground truth stack of 100 slices.

To create a synthetically deformed stack, we applied randomly generated global and local deformations with magnitudes encompassing those typically encountered during sectioning, mounting, and staining.

Competing methods:

We tried methods using various combinations of propagation schemes and multi-neighbor formulation for the alignment of the synthetically deformed stack of slices:

  - A: Sequential, 1 neighbor (before)

  - B: Sequential, Independent then average, 2 neighbors (before and after)

  - C: Alternating, Independent then average, 2 neighbors (before and after)

  - D: Sequential, Simultaneous, 2 neighbors (before and after)

  - E: Alternating, Simultaneous, 2 neighbors (before and after)

A single iteration of full‑stack registration was performed for the rigid and affine stages, while the non‑linear stage was tested with one and five iterations. For sequential propagation we started from the middle slice outward.


Evaluation metrics:

The quality of alignment of the above-mentioned methods was quantified relative to the ground‑truth using both point‑wise and surface‑based measures:
  - Point‑to‑point error: Mean Euclidean distance between corresponding points in the registered and ground‑truth slices.
  - Surface‑based shape metrics: Distance between distributions of geometric features derived from the reconstructed surfaces, including normals (N), mean curvature (H) and Gaussian curvature (K). Each feature distribution was discretized into histograms and the Jensen-Shannon distance to the ground truth distributions were computed. For surface normals, a spherical histogram representation was used to properly account for orientation.

Aligned brain slices

Results are shown in Fig. 1.

As expected, the naive approach (A) performed worst overall, showing strong drift and clear misalignment. All other methods produced quite similar results. Visually, registration improved from uncorrected to rigid, affine, and non‑linear stages: surfaces became progressively smoother and more anatomically realistic (except for method A). The point‑to‑point error decreased up to the non‑linear stage but tended to plateau or slightly increase after five iterations. For surface‑based metrics, the distributions of normals and Gaussian curvature moved much closer to the ground truth from rigid to deformable with 5 iterations, while mean curvature changed little. The final deformable with 5 iterations reconstructions appeared smooth and brain‑like, though some low‑frequency distortions remained.

Conclusion

We investigated contour‑based registration strategies for aligning histological slice series without a 3D reference, employing a Lie‑group framework for rigid, affine, and non‑linear transformations. Methods incorporating multiple slice neighbors effectively reduced drift and yielded consistent reconstructions. Additional non‑linear iterations improved overall visual appearance, although some low‑frequency distortions persisted.

Overall, approaches B - E achieved accurate, geometrically consistent alignment suitable for reference‑free 3D reconstruction. Our method has successfully worked for the alignment of histological data from cerebral slices, and is a promising approach to the alignment of cerebellar slices with more complex geometric shapes.
