Introduction

To support stable perception, cortical circuits must encode information about many stimuli using neurons with limited dynamic range, despite fluctuations in overall input drive and activity in surrounding populations. Divisive normalization has been proposed as a canonical solution to this problem: a neuron’s response is scaled by the pooled activity of its neighbors, implementing contextual gain control and competition among stimuli.1 However, directly testing normalization in humans with fMRI is challenging because the fundamental spatial unit of fMRI—the voxel—reflects the pooled activity of multiple, diversely tuned neuronal populations, obscuring the sub-voxel interactions that implement normalization.¹,²

Frequency-tagged fMRI (ft-fMRI) can address this sub-voxel limitation by exploiting the temporal structure of the BOLD signal to dissociate simultaneous neural processes within the same voxel.³ By driving distinct stimulus components at separable temporal frequencies, we can independently track the activity of differentially tuned neuronal populations in the frequency domain, even when they spatially overlap. Nonlinear interactions between these populations generate responses at intermodulation (IM) frequencies that do not arise when the populations respond independently. The presence and magnitude of IM components in the BOLD frequency spectrum therefore provide a non-invasive readout of interaction strength between co-localized neural subpopulations.

The normalization model predicts that suppressive interactions between differentially tuned neuronal populations within voxels should yield stronger nonlinear IM components in the frequency-tagged fMRI time series. We tested this prediction in a multi-session 7T ft-fMRI experiment with two simultaneously presented visual checkerboard stimuli that drove distinct temporal frequencies. We then quantified IM responses and examined how these nonlinear components varied across voxels with different tuning preferences.

Methods

Participants

Seven right-handed participants (3 female; age 20–31 years, M = 26.25, SD = 3.49) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision took part after providing informed consent.

Stimuli & Task

Participants viewed pairs of oscillating and static checkerboard wedges in opposite visual-field quadrants (Fig. 1a). Wedges spanned 1.5°–10° eccentricity. Oscillating wedges were sine-squared modulated at F1 = 0.125 and F2 = 0.2 Hz. Participants covertly attended to the oscillating wedges while maintaining central fixation and detected brief (500 ms) color changes in a distributed-attention task. Online eye-tracking confirmed central fixation. Each participant completed sixteen 219-s runs across four sessions (four runs per session). 

MRI Acquisition

MRI data were collected on a Siemens 7T scanner across four study visits. A T1-weighted MP2RAGE scan was acquired in the first visit for registration purposes and to enable cortical surface projection. Functional data were acquired each visit using a multiband T2* weighted echo-planar imaging sequence covering an oblique slab centered on the ventral occipital cortex (TR 250 ms, TE 20 ms, flip angle 30°, FOV 208 mm, 2.5-mm isotropic voxels, 16 slices, RL phase encoding, MB=4). Each run comprised 880 volumes (220 s).

MRI Data Processing

To preprocess each fMRI dataset, we used slice-timing correction, followed by single-shot interpolation to T1w space, surface resampling to 32k vertex fsLR surfaces, and nuisance regression of estimated head motion parameters (and their first and second order derivatives) and physiological noise (CSF/WM) timeseries. We truncated each fMRI run between 59 and 219 seconds to account for transient hemodynamic effects associated with the onset of continuous oscillatory stimulation. Since our stimuli had periods of 8s (0.125 Hz) and 5s (0.2 Hz), we were able to obtain 20 and 32 full cycles, respectively, at our frequencies of interest from the resulting pre-processed and noise-corrected 160s time course.

Time-Series Modelling

To model our frequencies of interest in the fMRI data, we fitted voxel-wise BOLD time series with a general linear model (GLM) (Fig. 1b). The model included sine and cosine regressors associated with each frequency of interest. An F-test was performed on the combined fit to the sine and cosine regressors at each frequency of interest to test for a frequency-specific response. We repeated this procedure across 500 sub-samples of the data using randomized half-splits, where voxels exhibiting a significant model fit (p < 0.05) across at least 80% of subsamples were classified as suprathreshold stimulus-synchronized responses. Voxels exhibiting suprathreshold responses to only one of the two frequencies of interest were classified as singular (F1 only or F2 only) voxels, while voxels exhibiting suprathreshold responses to both frequencies were classified as multiplexing voxels (Fig. 1c).

Population Receptive Field (pRF) Mapping

We performed a pRF mapping experiment4 to assess visual-field preferences of suprathreshold voxels. For each participant, we combined the Gaussian population receptive field models for suprathreshold voxels. The summation of these Gaussians resulted in a maximum value for each location in the visual field. The resulting visual field coverage thus represents the maximum envelope of sensitivity across the visual field for singular and multiplexing voxels (Fig. 1d).

Non-linear Response Analysis

To examine frequency-specific non-linear responses, we computed the Fourier SNR (fSNR) at our intermodulation (IM) frequency of interest for each participant and experimental session. This was determined as the difference between the Fourier amplitude at the IM frequency (ΑIM) and the mean amplitude at the four neighbouring resolved frequencies (2 on each side;  μFn), divided by the standard deviation of neighbouring frequencies (σFn).5

Results

We began by constructing a classic receptive field (RF) model by using Gaussian receptive fields to represent the spatial tuning of sub-voxel neuronal populations. Simulating the responses of different subpopulations to our frequency-tagged paradigm and convolving the neural response time course with a gamma hemodynamic responses function (HRF) yielded responses confined to the stimulus frequencies F1 and F2, with no power at the intermodulation (IM) frequency (Fig. 2a). We then contrasted the classic RF model against the divisive normalization model,2 in which Gaussian receptive fields had suppressive surrounds to represent competing neuronal subpopulations within fMRI voxels (Fig. 2b). Simulations  of this  dynamic normalization model predicted  that mutual suppression between neuronal subpopulations synchronized to distinct stimulus frequencies would generate  a non-linear intermodulation (IM) frequency component at the difference frequency  (IM = F2 - F1) in voxel-wise time series. 

To test these model predictions in our human data, we computed the Fourier signal-to-noise ratio (fSNR) at the IM frequency in voxels showing significant responses to both stimulus frequencies (“multiplexing” voxels) and in voxels responding selectively to only one frequency  (“singularF1” and “singularF2” voxels)  in our ft-fMRI dataset (Fig. 2c,d). We found that IM fSNR was significantly greater than zero across participants, experimental sessions, and voxel groups (tmultiplexing = 7.38; pmultiplexing = 6.12x10-8; tsingularF1 = 8.62 ; psingularF1 = 3.11x10-9; tsingularF2 = 7.77 ; psingularF2 = 2.37x10-8 ),  consistent with the normalization model and inconsistent with a purely linear model. 

We next tested a more specific prediction of our dynamic normalization model: Multiplexing voxels, which contain a mixture of neural subpopulations tuned to either of the two competing frequency-tagged stimuli, should exhibit stronger suppressive temporal interactions than singular voxels. Our model predicts that this should result in a stronger IM component in multiplexing voxels. Confirming this prediction, we observed significantly stronger IM fSNR in multiplexing than in singularF1 (t = 2.94; p = 0.003) and singularF2 (t = 1.73; p = 0.047) voxels (Fig. 2d).

Conclusion

In this experiment, we used ft-fMRI to isolate cortical BOLD responses synchronized to two concurrent stimulus-driven frequencies and to probe sub-voxel interactions between the underlying neuronal populations. Across participants and sessions, we observed robust IM components in the BOLD frequency spectrum, and their presence and magnitude matched key predictions of a dynamic normalization model while being incompatible with a purely linear receptive field model. These findings support divisive normalization as a sub-voxel computation that shapes aggregate voxel responses in the human visual cortex. 

More broadly, our ft-fMRI paradigm introduces a novel marker of suppressive interactions at a sub-voxel scale. IM components constitute a distinct spectral signature that arises from the interaction of stimulus-driven frequency responses in the brain, providing an objective readout of nonlinear dynamics whose frequency can be controlled by the choice of tagging frequencies.³  This framework opens the door to using ft-fMRI to adjudicate between different models of sub-voxel computations that give rise to the characteristic nonlinear structure in the fMRI time series. 

Prior work has shown a non-linear relationship between population level voxel responses and visual stimulation. Notably, population receptive field (pRF) mapping experiments show that pRFs exhibit subadditive responses to simultaneously presented stimuli relative to the individual responses of stimuli presented in isolation.4 However,  the mechanisms underlying this response compression have remained unclear. Our ft-fMRI results nominate divisive normalization as a parsimonious computational account of these non-linearities: compression of the pRF response can be understood as arising from suppressive interactions between subpopulations within a given pRF. Future pRF mapping studies can integrate  ft-fMRI and IM based analyses to more directly link sub-voxel normalization dynamics to measured population responses.

Together, these findings further support the normalization model as a canonical cortical computation, establish IM-based ft-fMRI as a novel tool for quantifying the suppressive interactions which support normalization at a sub-voxel scale, and provide a general framework for testing computational models of fine-grained neuronal dynamics in humans.
