Introduction

Differential susceptibility to environmental contexts constitutes a fundamental biological trait that drives individual differences in cognition and mental health(Argentieri et al., 2025). Although environmental sensitivity has been extensively investigated at the behavioral and phenotypic levels, whether it is instantiated as a distinct dimension of brain functions remains unknown. Here, we investigate whether environmental sensitivity can be dissociated as a specific axis of individualized brain dynamics with cross-scale mechanistic relevance.

Spontaneous regional brain activity offers a privileged substrate for addressing this question. Our previous work has demonstrated that regional spontaneous dynamics constitute robust, trait-like representations of individual variability that generalizably predict behavior, establishing a reliable foundation for studying individual differences(Tian et al., 2025). Crucially, such regional characterization is particularly well suited for cross-scale analyses, as brain regions serve as a natural interface linking macroscale functional organization with underlying biological mechanisms across microscopic scales(da Silva Castanheira et al., 2025; Shafiei et al., 2020).

Building on this framework, we hypothesize that environmental sensitivity manifests as a dissociable dimension of brain dynamics, defined as the component of trait-like regional dynamics that remains independent of heritable constraints. We term this environmentally contingent variance the Environmental Sensitivity Index (ESI). Leveraging multivariate analysis, we identify a robust spatial covariation between ESI and transcriptomic profiles, which delineates two distinct transcriptional programs: one characterized by high plasticity and heightened sensitivity to environmental modulation, and the other associated with early maturation and structural consolidation.

Methods

Multidimensional Characterization of Individualized Spontaneous Brain Dynamics

The current study capitalizes on the time-series feature representation of brain-wide regional spontaneous brain dynamics established in our previous work (Fig.1A; Tian et al., 2025). Briefly, resting-state fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP S1200 release) were preprocessed. Here, we focused on 200 cortical regions defined by the Schaefer atlas. For each region, a comprehensive library of 44 high Intra-class Correlation Coefficient (ICC) time-series features was extracted to capture the multifaceted temporal patterns of brain regional dynamics, spanning linearity, non-linearity, non-stationarity, and stochasticity. This validated 200 by 44 feature matrix, which serves as a highly reliable and robust representation of individual brain function, constitutes the basis for the current analysis; detailed computational protocols are provided in Tian et al. (2025).

Operationalizing the Environmental Sensitivity Index (ESI) 

We defined ESI by mathematically dissociating two distinct sources of individualized functional brain representation using the HCP twin pedigree structure. For every region-feature combination extracted as described above, we calculated two metrics: (1) the Intra-class Correlation Coefficient (ICC) using test-retest scans to quantify trait-like stability at the level of the brain functional phenotype; and (2) narrow-sense heritability (h2) using the variance component model in SOLAR-Eclipse to leverage the twin pedigree structure for quantifying the genetic contribution to individual differences. Aligning with the standard additive genetic framework (Phenotype = Genetics + Environment; Fig.1B), we posited that reliable interindividual variance unexplained by genetics represents a persistent capacity for environmental embedding. Accordingly, ESI was defined as the residual stability: ESI = ICC – h2. Resulting in a 200 by 44 matrix, this calculation yields a specific metric identifying highly individualized features of spontaneous brain activity that remain biologically open to environmental modulation.

 Imaging-Transcriptomics Validation of ESI

To investigate the cross-scale biological basis of ESI, we employed Partial Least Squares Correlation (PLSC) to identify latent modes of spatial covariance between the brain-wide ESI map and gene expression profiles from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA). Spatial autocorrelation was corrected using spin permutation tests. This analysis revealed a statistically significant latent mode (covariance explained=0.79, Pspin< 0.05; Fig. 1C). Based on the contribution of ESI weights, we stratified this axis into two distinct poles: an ESI-positive component (High Sensitivity) and an ESI-negative component (Low Sensitivity). The top-ranking genes associated with each pole were then functionally characterized using: (1) Developmental trajectories (BrainSpan) to pinpoint temporal windows of expression; (2) Cognitive decoding (Neurosynth) to delineate the functional topography of high versus low sensitivity regions; and (3) Gene Ontology analysis to decode the biological and molecular functions underlying high or low ESI gene sets.

Results

To characterize the biological basis of Environmental Sensitivity, we employed Partial Least Squares Correlation (PLSC) to link the brain-wide ESI topography with transcriptomic profiles from the Allen Human Brain Atlas. This analysis identified a single, statistically significant latent axis of spatial covariance.

A Unified Dynamic Dimension (Fig. 2A). First, we examined the contribution of individual dynamic features to this latent axis. Notably, the loading profile revealed global consistency: the ESI spatial maps derived from all 44 RSRD features contributed unidirectionally (negative loadings) to the latent mode. This indicates that ESI represents a robust, coherent dimension of cortical organization, where the spatial patterns of environmental sensitivity uniformly map to the high sensitivity pole (ESI+).

Developmental Dissociation: Late Plasticity vs. Early Structure (Fig. 2B). We next investigated whether the transcriptomic profile of ESI temporally segregates into the window of environmental interaction, distinct from the early developmental periods of genetic structural formation. To test this, we stratified the PLS-derived gene list into positive (ESI+) and negative (ESI-) poles and performed developmental enrichment analysis using the BrainSpan. This analysis revealed a pronounced temporal dissociation. High ESI (Plasticity Pole) genes exhibited a distinct "postnatal shift": they were significantly up-regulated during adolescence and young adulthood, coinciding with critical periods for environmental interaction, while being down-regulated throughout prenatal stages. In contrast, Low ESI genes displayed the opposite trajectory: they were extensively up-regulated during prenatal development (peaking in the mid-prenatal period) to support early brain morphogenesis, but were significantly down-regulated during adolescence and adulthood. These findings support the biological validity of ESI from a neurodevelopmental perspective, linking high environmental sensitivity to prolonged postnatal developmental windows associated with plasticity, while associating low sensitivity with the early prenatal genetic programming of brain architecture.

Mechanistic and Functional Divergence (Fig. 2C-D). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis provided mechanistic insight into this dichotomy. The ESI+ pole was enriched for energy metabolism (e.g., Aerobic Respiration, Mitochondrial Organization) and ion transport, suggesting a high metabolic demand associated with maintaining a plastic, environment-sensitive state. In contrast, the ESI- pole was enriched for structural assembly (e.g., Synapse Organization, Cell Junction Assembly), reflecting the genetic establishment of stable neural circuits. Furthermore, neurosynth-based cognitive decoding of ESI+ versus ESI- regions corroborated this functional divergence: ESI+ regions were spatially associated with affective reactivity (mood, anxiety, stress), while ESI- regions supported stable internal processing (memory, retrieval, recognition). Collectively, this cross-scale convergence suggests a fundamental organizational trade-off: ESI tracks the transition from genetically canalized structural architecture optimized for reliable internal computation, to a metabolically demanding adaptive capacity essential for dynamic environmental interaction.

Conclusion

In this study, we establish a conceptual framework that isolates environmental sensitivity as a distinct and intrinsic axis of brain organization. Moving beyond the traditional approach of mapping external behavioral measures onto neural features, we operationalize the Environmental Sensitivity Index (ESI) by partitioning stable variance in regional brain dynamics into heritable and environmentally contingent components. This formulation defines ESI as a trait-like yet biologically open dimension of brain function, indexing the capacity for neural adaptation that remains independent of genetic constraints.

Crucially, the derivation of ESI is enabled by the RSRD framework, which provides a stable, individualized, and multidimensional representation of spontaneous brain activity(Tian et al., 2025). By capturing the reliable patterns of intrinsic dynamics, RSRD allows the principled separation of genetic and environmental contributions within intrinsic brain function. Leveraging this framework, we demonstrate that ESI is not a scattered artifact but a coherent organizational dimension, consistently expressed across diverse linear, non-linear, and stochastic features of brain dynamics.

Linking ESI to transcriptomic architecture reveals a diametric biological dissociation. High environmental sensitivity aligns with a plasticity-oriented state characterized by delayed postnatal gene expression and elevated bioenergetic demands, including metabolism and ion transport—features required for sustained plasticity and affective reactivity. In contrast, low environmental sensitivity is anchored in prenatal gene expression and structural assembly pathways, supporting stable internal computation through early genetic canalization. Together, these findings delineate a fundamental organizational trade-off between the metabolic cost of plasticity and the efficiency of structural stability.

Broadly, our work establishes ESI as a biologically grounded axis of individual difference while highlighting the practical value of RSRD as a generalizable framework for extracting meaningful phenotypes from intrinsic brain activity. This integrated approach provides a foundation for future investigations into gene–environment interactions, offering a roadmap for understanding how environmental contexts engage intrinsic plasticity gradients to shape heterogeneity in cognition and mental health.
