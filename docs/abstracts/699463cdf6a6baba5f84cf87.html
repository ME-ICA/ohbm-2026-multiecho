Introduction

Adolescence is a period of heightened sensitivity to social feedback and is often when experimentation with substance use begins. Evidence has shown that peer and familial environments play a critical role in shaping early substance use1. Accordingly, understanding how youth learn from social information may help identify the pathways through which social exposure translates into substance use curiosity and ultimately early initiation. Reinforcement learning (RL) models provide a powerful framework for characterizing how individuals learn from positive and negative feedback over time. However, relatively few studies have applied these models to understand the social feedback learning mechanisms involved in early substance use. Dopaminergic signaling within frontostriatal circuits is thought to encode prediction errors2, with increased neuronal firing rates when outcomes are better than expected and decreased firing when outcomes are worse than expected3. By signaling outcomes based on their predictability, prediction errors support learning and guide behavior. The present study used a social RL fMRI task in a sample of youth to test how behavioral and neural indices of social learning relate to substance use curiosity and social exposure to substance use. We hypothesized that adolescent’s exposure to household and peer substance use would be positively associated with their use curiosity, and that this association would be strongest among individuals with heightened sensitivity to social reinforcement and greater neural responsivity to positive social prediction errors.

Methods

Participants (N=261; 7.4-14.6 years old; 54.7% self-reported female at birth) were recruited in 2nd through 8th grade in the southeast United States. After consent/assent and demographic assessment, youth completed a 1.5-hour MRI session that included a social reinforcement learning task (Finding Friendliness Task). A subsample of older participants (n = 73; 10.9–14.6 years old) additionally reported their curiosity about using various substances (e-cigarettes, cannabis, alcohol), as well as their lifetime use of the substances, their household exposure to substance use, and the frequency of substance use among their five closest friends.

In the Finding Friendliness Task, participants learned via trial and error which adolescent faces were the “friendliest,” defined as those with a higher probability of delivering positive (smile) versus negative (scowl) feedback. During the learning phase of the task, pairs of neutral adolescent faces, with fixed probabilistic feedback contingencies, are repeatedly presented, and participants select one face per trial. In a subsequent testing phase, face pairs are recombined and participants again chose the face they learned to be the friendliest, but this time they do not receive feedback. Positive reinforcement learning performance was indexed as approach accuracy for the face that most reliably delivered positive social feedback, and negative reinforcement learning was indexed as avoidance of the face that most reliably delivered negative feedback. Learning-phase accuracy was operationalized as the proportion of optimal choices during the learning phase. 

Behavioral data from the learning phase were fit to multiple Q-learning models using the VBA toolbox in MATLAB4. The best-fitting model was selected based on model evidence (posterior model probability and exceedance probability). The selected asymmetric Q-learning model included separate learning rate parameters for positive (α₊) and negative (α₋) social feedback and an inverse temperature parameter (β) indexing choice stochasticity. Trial-wise expected values and prediction errors (actual outcome – expected value) were calculated for each participant. Forward simulations using best-fitting parameters were used to validate model selection. 

fMRI data were preprocessed using standard pipelines (fMRIPrep and AFNI). For the learning run, participant-level GLMs modeled choice, feedback, and no-response events. Trial-by-trial prediction error was entered as a parametric modulator at feedback onset, allowing identification of brain regions where activity scaled with prediction error magnitude and valence. Timepoints with framewise displacement exceeding 0.9mm3 were censored, and participants with >25% censored timepoints were excluded from imaging analyses. Whole-brain prediction error–tracking maps were estimated (pcluster-corrected<0.05), and average prediction error–related activity was extracted from a priori bilateral striatum and anterior insula regions defined via the Brainnetome atlas.

Among older youth, we examined correlations between substance use curiosity, household and friend group substance use, social learning performance, and ROI-based prediction error–related activity. We then tested whether social learning behavior and neural prediction error signals moderated associations between social exposure (household and friend use) and substance use curiosity using regression-based moderation models (PROCESS, Model 1), probing conditional effects at low, mean, and high levels of the moderators.

Results

As prediction error increased (outcomes increasingly better than expected), activity increased in the bilateral striatum (including the putamen and nucleus accumbens), ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and paracentral gyrus, as well as bilateral clusters in the fusiform face area and occipital lobe. As prediction error values decreased (outcomes increasingly worse than expected), activity increased in bilateral anterior insula, supplementary motor area, temporal pole, right superior temporal gyrus, and an epithalamic cluster encompassing the habenula.

Individuals who showed weaker (i.e., less negative) anterior insula coupling with prediction-error signals exhibited poorer learning-phase performance (r[236]=-0.2, p=0.010), suggesting that attenuated sensitivity to outcomes that are worse than expected may impair optimal feedback learning. Prediction error–related striatal activity was not significantly associated with learning-phase or testing-phase performance.

In the older subsample, 27.4% reported at least some curiosity about using e-cigarettes, cannabis, or alcohol, and 16.4% reported having tried one of these substances. Most participants (71.2%) reported at least some household substance use, and 12.3% reported substance use among their close friends. Consistent with social influence models, household e-cigarette (r[71]=0.6, p<0.001) and cannabis use (r[71]=0.3, p=0.025), as well as friend cannabis use (ρ[70]=0.3, p=0.017), were positively associated with curiosity for the respective substances. Lower prediction error–related striatal activity was significantly associated with higher substance use curiosity (r[70]=-0.3, p=0.010) and with a greater number of substance-using individuals in the household (r[70]=-0.4, p=0.001). Lower prediction error–related anterior insula activity was also associated with greater household substance use (r[70]=-0.3, p=0.033), but not with substance use curiosity. Social learning behavior (learning-phase optimal choices and testing-phase performance) was generally not related to substance use exposure or curiosity.

Moderation analyses revealed that the effect of friend group substance use on substance use curiosity was significantly stronger among adolescents with better social learning performance (higher proportion of optimal choices during learning, F[1,68]=4.6, p=0.035 and higher approach accuracy during testing, F[1,66]=6.6, p=0.012). Similarly, higher striatal prediction error responsivity amplified the association between friend substance use and curiosity such that peer use strongly predicted curiosity among youth with elevated striatal tracking but not among those with lower tracking (F[1,67]=12.1, p<0.001). In contrast, household substance use showed a main effect on curiosity that was attenuated among youth with especially strong positive social learning performance (F[1,66]=4.8, p=0.032), possibly suggesting resilience to familial influences among this subgroup. Moderation patterns were robust to heteroskedasticity corrections and adjustment for age.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that social reinforcement learning mechanisms, quantified using computational modeling and prediction error–related brain activity, are meaningfully linked to early substance use propensity in adolescence. We identify two possible neurocognitive pathways to risk. First, blunted social reward learning-signals, characterized by reduced striatal prediction error responsivity, is associated with higher substance use curiosity and greater household substance use exposure, even in the absence of overt differences in social learning behavior. This profile may reflect diminished reward sensitivity that increases the appeal of especially potent reinforcers, such as nicotine, alcohol, or cannabis, or it may index an inherited or early environmental phenotype shaping reinforcement circuitry prior to heavy substance use. Second, a socially sensitive pathway among adolescents with stronger positive social learning performance and heightened striatal prediction error tracking. For these youth, exposure to substance-using friends may translate into elevated substance use curiosity. In contrast, youth with poorer social learning appear less influenced by peer use, highlighting how the same social environment may confer different levels of risk depending on underlying learning mechanisms. More broadly, this work illustrates the value of integrating social RL paradigms, computational modeling, and neuroimaging with real-world exposure measures to identify phenotypes associated with a propensity for early substance use.
