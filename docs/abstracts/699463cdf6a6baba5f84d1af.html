Introduction

Disruptive behavior disorders affect over 113 million youth globally and are characterized by symptoms of irritability/anger, aggression, and/or noncompliance.^1 Disruptive behavior is also a transdiagnostic dimension that often co-occurs across a range of psychiatric disorders. On a neural systems level, alterations in large-scale networks supporting the cognitive control of emotion—including frontoparietal, default mode, and salience networks—are implicated in childhood disruptive behavior disorders. However, the majority of studies examining links between the connectome, or brain-wide connectivity, and disruptive behavior severity in children are cross-sectional and/or focus on a single imaging modality, particularly functional connectivity. Thus, it is unclear whether baseline patterns of the functional and structural connectome predict later changes in disruptive behavior severity. While sex differences in network connectivity have been observed developmentally, it is also largely unknown whether males and females show distinct connectomic markers of disruptive behavior. Addressing these gaps is crucial for elucidating developmental neural circuit mechanisms underlying childhood disruptive behavior disorders and identifying robust brain biomarkers for targeted interventions. This study investigates whether multimodal imaging markers—leveraging functional and structural connectomes—predict cross-sectional and prospective changes in disruptive behavior in youth. We also examine whether sex-specific predictive networks might index distinct, divergent mechanisms underlying disruptive behavior trajectories for males versus females.

Methods

This study included a transdiagnostic sample of 3,630 youth from the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study with high-quality multimodal imaging data across two timepoints.^2, 3 Children were aged 9-10 years at baseline and aged 11-12 years at the 2-year follow-up (n=1,830 males; n=1,800 females). Functional connectomes were derived from resting-state fMRI with eyes open. Structural connectomes were derived from quantitative anisotropy (QA), which measures white matter integrity. The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) Externalizing Behavior Problems subscale was used as a continuous measure of disruptive behavior, consistent with prior ABCD studies.^4 Connectome-based Predictive Modeling (CPM)—a machine learning, data-driven approach with 10-fold cross-validation—identified networks predicting disruptive behavior.^5 Permutation testing with 1,000 iterations estimated significance. Separate models were conducted for functional and structural connectomes. First, cross-sectional brain-behavior associations were tested in the total sample and then separately for males and females. Second, for prospective prediction, we investigated whether baseline connectomes predicted changes in disruptive behavior (i.e., baseline to 2-year follow-up) using a CBCL raw change score. All models accounted for age, cognition/IQ, and motion. Prospective models additionally accounted for baseline disruptive behavior levels. Models conducted in the total sample included sex as a covariate. FDR-correction was applied to permutation p-values across models within each timepoint and modality. As a supplemental analysis to understand the overlap and specificity of predictive connectivity patterns for models reaching significance, we compared CPM-selected edge masks at the subnetwork level. Binary masks were each aggregated into a 10×10 matrix counting the number of selected edges between subnetworks. We quantified: (1) the number of shared subnetwork connections; (2) the number of unique subnetwork connections present in only one mask; and (3) the statistical significance of the overlap and uniqueness scores using permutation tests (1,000 iterations).

Results

Cross-sectionally, functional (ρ = 0.07, RMSE = 5.8, pFDR = 0.009) and structural (ρ = 0.05, RMSE = 5.6, pFDR = 0.006) connectivity predicted disruptive behavior; positive and negative edges contributed comparably (Figure 1Ai and Aiii). Sex-stratified analyses revealed significant predictions only in males, with functional connectivity driven by negative edges (decreased connectivity) (ρ = 0.07, RMSE = 6.4, pFDR = 0.016) and structural connectivity driven by positive edges (increased connectivity) (ρ = 0.01, RMSE = 6.2, pFDR = 0.019) (Figure 1Aii and 1Aiv). Neither modality was significant in females (all pFDR > 0.1). Prospectively, only the male subgroup showed significant prediction of symptom change (Figure 1B). Increased functional connectivity at baseline predicted increases in symptom severity (ρ = 0.07, RMSE = 4.9, pFDR = 0.033) (Figure 1Bii). In contrast, decreased structural connectivity at baseline predicted increases in symptom severity (ρ = 0.06, RMSE = 4.8, pFDR = 0.006) (Figure 1Biii). No prospective associations reached significance in females (all pFDR > 0.2). 

Subnetwork-level analyses revealed both shared and distinct predictive patterns across modalities and timepoints. Functional and structural connectivity showed significant shared engagement of large-scale networks including frontoparietal-frontoparietal connections (p = 0.01), while differing in unique patterns: functional models uniquely involved default mode and visual networks, whereas structural models uniquely involved cerebellar-sensorimotor connections (p ≤ 0.009). Cross-sectional models involved significantly more frontoparietal and other cognitive control networks (including medial frontal) compared to prospective models, which primarily involved default mode and salience networks (p ≤ 0.007), suggesting distinct developmental mechanisms underlying concurrent versus prospective disruptive behavior prediction.

Conclusion

Both functional and structural connectomes provide distinct and complementary insights into disruptive behavior severity in childhood. First, functional and structural connectivity predicted disruptive behavior problems in youth, both cross-sectionally and prospectively. Second, in males, functional and structural connectivity showed opposite patterns: decreased functional but increased structural connectivity predicted greater baseline symptoms, while increased functional but decreased structural connectivity predicted worsening symptoms. Third, distinct patterns of network connectivity predicted symptoms cross-sectionally, with a distributed pattern across cognitive control systems, and prospectively with a focal pattern primarily spanning default mode and subcortical networks. Shared patterns involving frontoparietal hub nodes were common to both cross-sectional and prospective models. 

These findings suggest that multimodal prediction may offer a more nuanced understanding of neural circuit-level markers of disruptive behavior problems in youth. Cross-sectional and prospective circuit markers involve distinct predictive networks for delineating current symptoms versus trajectories of symptom change. Male-specific neural markers suggest divergent neurodevelopmental pathways for disruptive behavior in boys versus girls. Taken together, a multimodal, network-level framework holds promise for refining clinical phenotyping and guiding circuit-based targeted treatments for childhood disruptive behavior disorders.
