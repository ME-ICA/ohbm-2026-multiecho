Introduction

Concepts of brain reserve (BR) —differences in brain morphology given a similar degree of pathology—and cognitive reserve (CR) —variability in cognitive performance despite comparable structural losses—1 have helped to conceptualize and study healthy and pathological aging.  However, despite the global research effort devoted to this area, we still lack a clear understanding of the factors that confer greater resilience to certain individuals, understood as the capacity to maintain brain functionality in the face of age-related challenges. 

Several factors related with educational and language experiences have been reported to influence CR, including general intelligence, educational attainment 1 and multilingualism. Bilingualism or multilingualism is not dichotomous and should be considered in regard of several Experience Based Factors including proficiency, frequency and variety of use or age of acquisition of second language 2. Taking into consideration the need to study multilingualism in combination with other CR factors 3, we propose here to extend the term EBFs to other education and language experience that contribute to CR, for instance educational attainment and general intelligence, in particular verbal IQ. Mentioned factors have been proposed to contribute to morphological differences in the adult brain 4 (i.e. they would be BR factors as well as CR factors).

There are different tools that allow an estimation of age based on brain morphology (i.e. predicted age). Subtracting chronological age from predicted age gives a value “brain age gap” (BAG) that correspond conceptually to BR. These tools use machine learning so they are not fully explanatory of the features conditioning an older or younger brain age.

Our aim in the present study is to describe how age, education, general intelligence and multilingualism influence changes in brain structure and cognitive performance as we age.

Methods

Participants.

Two hundred forty-four healthy, right-handed adults aged 55 years and older participated voluntarily. The sample comprised 143 women (mean age = 68 years, IQR = 65–71) and 101 men (mean age = 69 years, IQR = 66–73), with comparable age distributions across sexes. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of psychiatric, neurological, or learning disorders. Written informed consent was obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Euskadi and by the Ethics and Scientific Committee of the Basque Center on Cognition, Brain and Language (BCBL), where data collection was conducted.

Procedure.

Participants underwent structural MRI scanning and completed a comprehensive cognitive–behavioral assessment.

Questionnaires and Multilingualism Assessment.

For each known language, age of acquisition (AoA), current use, and proficiency were recorded. These measures were combined into an AoA–Proficiency composite score for each language and averaged across languages, weighted by the number of languages spoken (up to three), yielding a Multilingual Index (MLI). Educational attainment was quantified as a discrete numerical variable, approximating total years of formal education.

Cognitive Assessment.

General intelligence was assessed using the Kaufman Brief Intelligence Test (K-BIT), providing Verbal and Non-Verbal IQ scores. Executive functions were evaluated using Semantic Fluency, the Attentional Network Test, and the Stroop task. Semantic Fluency performance was quantified as the total number of correct animal names produced in 60 seconds. Reaction times and accuracy were analyzed to derive conflict-related measures for the attentional and inhibitory control tasks.

MRI Data Acquisition.

Structural MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Magnetom scanner using a T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (176 sagittal slices; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³; TR = 2530 ms; TE = 2.97 ms; flip angle = 7°; matrix = 256 × 256). Up to three T1-weighted scans were obtained per participant due to the multisession design.

Brain Age Gap (BAG) Estimation.

Brain age predictions were computed using the pyment package, which implements a 3D convolutional neural network trained on 53,542 structural MRI scans from adults aged 45–80 years. BAG was calculated as predicted brain age minus chronological age, with positive values indicating older-appearing brains and negative values indicating younger-appearing brains.

Statistical Analysis.

A conceptual framework linking observable variables (e.g., age, education, multilingualism) with latent constructs (BR and CR) was developed to guide hypothesis testing (Figure 1). Statistical analyses were conducted in JASP and included correlational and multivariate analyses informed by the proposed structural model.

Results

Executive function performance showed the expected age-related decline, whereas linguistic measures remained largely stable across the older adult sample (Figure 2A). Brain age gap values exhibited substantial interindividual variability: some participants showed predicted brain ages younger than their chronological age (negative BAG), whereas others exhibited older predicted ages (positive BAG) (Figure 2B).

The statistical model revealed a clear double dissociation between cognitive domains. Age was significantly associated with executive function performance but not with linguistic measures, supporting domain-specific vulnerability and preservation patterns in aging. BAG showed significant associations with experiential variables, consistent with its interpretation as a morphological correlate of brain reserve. Crucially, higher levels of multilingual experience (MLI) and education-related measures were positively associated with cognitive reserve indicators, highlighting their joint contribution to cognitive resilience in later life.

Conclusion

The present findings indicate that age-related decline in older adulthood is primarily expressed in executive functions, whereas linguistic performance remains relatively preserved, in line with cognitive reserve accounts positing domain-specific resilience of language. Variability in BAG captures meaningful interindividual differences in structural brain aging among individuals of similar chronological age, providing a quantitative marker of brain reserve.

The observed associations between BAG and education- and language-related EBFs, together with the links between multilingual experience and cognitive performance, support the view that education, verbal intelligence, and multilingualism jointly contribute to both brain and cognitive reserve in late life. By integrating a brain-age framework with a graded, multidimensional characterization of multilingualism, this work extends existing BR/CR models and underscores the importance of considering combined experiential profiles—rather than single proxy measures—when accounting for resilience in brain and cognitive aging.
