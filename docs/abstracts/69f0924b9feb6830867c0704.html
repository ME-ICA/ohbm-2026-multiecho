Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is marked by the accumulation and spread of tau pathology, which is measured in vivo using tau-PET imaging. Clinically, tau burden is typically summarized using regional Standardized Uptake Value Ratios (SUVRs), which average tracer uptake within predefined anatomical regions. While regional SUVRs are robust, this spatial averaging obscures fine-grained heterogeneity, limiting our understanding of disease spread and evaluating focal treatments. Furthermore, the high cost, lengthy protocols, and need for intravenous tracer administration restrict the widespread clinical use of tau-PET. These challenges highlight the need for computational methods that can infer high-resolution tau distributions from simple regional measurements without requiring a full voxel-wise PET scan.

Reconstructing voxel-level tau distributions from regional summaries is an ill-posed problem because spatial detail is not explicitly encoded in the regional input. Although prior deep learning models have shown partial success (e.g., CNNs achieving ∼ 80% specificity in tau-positivity classification or MRI-only models showing modest correlation in asymmetry (R2 ∼ 0.28–0.42)),^[1,2] existing methods tend to oversmooth focal pathology or regress the reconstruction toward the population-average uptake. This fails to produce highly individualized and spatially accurate voxel-level reconstructions.

Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) offer a promising solution. These coordinate-based neural networks encode images as continuous functions of spatial coordinates, providing a framework that is resolution-agnostic and memory-efficient. INRs are capable of recovering fine-scale spatial structure from sparse data. However, existing INR-based PET studies have not focused on reconstructing voxel-level tau distributions directly from regional SUVRs, nor have they integrated subject-specific brain morphology to ensure that the preserved variation is biologically meaningful.

Methods

Dataset and preprocessing:

Multimodal neuroimaging data from 61 participants enrolled in the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) were analyzed. Each subject had AV1451 tau-PET imaging and high-resolution T1-weighted structural MRI acquired using standardized ADNI protocols. Cortical and subcortical anatomical segmentation was performed on T1-weighted MRI using FreeSurfer (v7.4) with the Desikan–Killiany atlas, parcellating the brain into 86 cortical and subcortical regions of interest (ROIs) for regional feature extraction.

Tau-PET preprocessing was conducted using a customized PETSurfer-based pipeline integrating FSL, FreeSurfer, and ANTs. Multi-frame PET scans were motion-corrected by realigning all frames to the initial frame using FSL’s MCFLIRT and then summed to generate a single static PET volume. The summed PET image was rigidly coregistered to the subject-specific T1-weighted MRI within the FreeSurfer framework. Voxel intensities were normalized to standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) using eroded cerebellar gray matter as the reference region, consistent with ADNI preprocessing standards.

Regional SUVR values were computed by resampling FreeSurfer-derived cortical and subcortical ROIs into PET space to ensure accurate anatomical correspondence across modalities. Mean SUVR was extracted for each ROI and used as a regional summary measure. In parallel, regional cortical and subcortical atrophy measures were obtained from FreeSurfer volumetric outputs and z-scored relative to a healthy, reference cohort to capture subject-specific neurodegeneration. All images and derived features were nonlinearly registered to MNI space using ANTs, yielding a common voxel-wise coordinate system.

Model architecture and training:

An Implicit Neural Representation (INR) model was developed to reconstruct voxel-level tau-PET distributions from regional summary measures. For each voxel in MNI space, the input feature vector consisted of spatial coordinates (X,Y,Z), the regional SUVR corresponding to its anatomical ROI, and the z-scored regional atrophy value derived from structural MRI. The prediction target was the voxel-level tau SUVR, enabling learning of a continuous mapping from sparse regional descriptors and spatial context to high-resolution molecular imaging estimates (Figure 1).

The INR architecture comprised a fully connected feedforward network with 16 hidden layers, each containing 512 neurons. Gaussian activation functions, ø(x)=e-x^2, were used to promote smooth continuous representations while preserving localized tracer uptake variations. A single global INR model was trained across all subjects, with voxel-wise input–output pairs pooled (concatenated) and randomly partitioned into training (80%), validation (10%), and test (10%) sets using a fixed random seed.

Model optimization employed the AdamW optimizer (learning rate 10^-4, weight decay 10^-5), with cosine annealing and early stopping (patience = 20 validation evaluations) to mitigate overfitting. Performance was evaluated exclusively on the held-out test set using mean squared error (MSE) as the primary loss, along with root mean squared error (RMSE) and Pearson correlation coefficient (R) to quantify voxel-wise agreement between predicted and ground-truth tau SUVR.

Results

We evaluate pooled and subject-level reconstruction accuracy, examine the contribution of MRI-derived atrophy, assess regional anatomical fidelity, and conclude with an ablation study to quantify feature importance. Together, these results demonstrate the accuracy, robustness, and biological plausibility of the proposed framework.

Pooled and individual-level prediction performance:

Pooled analysis evaluates model performance across all subjects and all brain voxels simultaneously, rather than within individual participants. Voxel-wise predicted SUVR values from the entire test cohort are concatenated into a single aggregated dataset and compared with corresponding ground-truth SUVR values. The resulting pooled correlation reflects the model’s global ability to reconstruct tau  distributions across heterogeneous anatomy and disease stages.

The proposed INR accurately reconstructed voxel-level tau distributions, achieving a pooled Pearson correlation of R = 0.959 (p < 0.01; Fig. 2a). To quantify individual-level performance, voxel-wise predicted and ground-truth SUVR values were correlated separately for each participant and averaged across the cohort, yielding a mean per-subject correlation of R = 0.891±0.111. These results indicate robust reconstruction accuracy while accounting for inter-subject anatomical and pathological variability, and suggest that the INR captures nonlinear spatial relationships between regional tau burden, morphology, and voxel-level deposition.
A representative subject with Alzheimer’s disease demonstrated exceptional spatial concordance, with a voxel-wise correlation of R = 0.989 (p < 0.01; Fig. 2c). Glass-brain visualizations further confirmed strong qualitative agreement between predicted and ground-truth tau distributions, including preservation of regional gradients and focal high-uptake patterns (Fig. 2d).

Effect of atrophy:

To assess the contribution of structural degeneration, model performance was evaluated with and without MRI-derived regional atrophy features. Cortical and subcortical volumetric z-scores were removed from the input while keeping the model architecture, training procedure, and data partitions unchanged. Excluding atrophy led to a substantial performance reduction, with pooled correlation decreasing to R = 0.8309 (p < 0.01; Fig. 2b). This decline indicates that regional SUVR alone is insufficient to fully recover voxel-level tau patterns and that morphometric information provides complementary cues essential for preserving fine-grained spatial structure and biologically meaningful tau propagation.

Regional analysis:

To ensure anatomical and biological validity, performance was evaluated within individual brain regions. For each region in the Desikan–Killiany parcellation, voxel-wise predicted SUVR values were compared with ground truth to compute region-specific Pearson correlations. This analysis guards against inflated pooled performance driven by a small subset of dominant regions and verifies consistent accuracy across cortical and subcortical structures.

The model achieved strong regional fidelity, with a mean regional correlation of R = 0.936 and most regions exceeding R > 0.90. Regions critically implicated in AD pathogenesis showed particularly high accuracy, including the right hippocampus (R = 0.909), mid-temporal cortex (R = 0.958), entorhinal cortex (R = 0.952), and inferior parietal cortex (R = 0.958).

Ablation study:

An ablation analysis quantified the contribution of input features. The full model incorporating spatial coordinates, regional tau SUVR, and regional atrophy achieved the highest performance (R = 0.959, RMSE = 0.187, PSNR = 32.60). Removing atrophy substantially reduced accuracy (R = 0.830), while a coordinate-only model failed to recover meaningful tau structure (R = 0.281). These results confirm that both regional molecular and structural information are necessary for accurate voxel-level reconstruction.

Conclusion

The results demonstrate that the proposed INR framework can accurately reconstruct high-resolution voxel-level tau distributions from regional summary measurements while preserving spatial heterogeneity and inter-subject variability. The strong pooled voxel-wise correlation (R = 0.959; Fig. 2), together with high mean per-subject performance, indicates that the model captures individualized patterns of tau deposition rather than relying on population-average uptake trends. These findings support the feasibility of recovering biologically meaningful molecular imaging signals from lower-dimensional inputs, providing a scalable and clinically relevant pathway for individualized tau mapping in settings where full tau-PET acquisition is costly, limited, or impractical.

A central finding of this study is the critical contribution of MRI-derived regional atrophy to voxel-level reconstruction accuracy. When cortical and subcortical volumetric atrophy measures were excluded, predictive performance declined substantially (from R = 0.959 to R = 0.8309; Fig. 2b), demonstrating that structural degeneration conveys essential subject-specific information beyond regional tau SUVR alone. This observation is consistent with prior evidence showing that tau accumulation and cortical atrophy are tightly coupled processes in Alzheimer’s disease, often co-localizing in regions of early neurodegeneration.^[3] By integrating morphometric information into the INR framework, the model effectively leverages complementary structural and molecular signals, enabling reconstructions that reflect underlying disease biology rather than superficial intensity correlations.

Qualitative evaluation further supports the biological plausibility of the reconstructed tau maps. Glass-brain visualizations (Fig. 2d) revealed strong spatial concordance between predicted and ground-truth SUVR distributions, particularly within temporoparietal and occipital cortices--regions known to exhibit substantial tau burden in symptomatic Alzheimer’s disease. Preservation of regional gradients and focal high-uptake patterns suggests that the INR captures meaningful disease-related signal rather than overfitting to noise or global uptake patterns. Notably, these reconstructions were achieved without explicit spatial priors, indicating that regional SUVR and atrophy features implicitly encode information about disease spread and regional vulnerability. 

The model also demonstrated robust region-wise performance across brain areas critically implicated in Alzheimer’s pathology, including the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, mid-temporal cortex, and inferior parietal cortex.^[4] Importantly, voxel-level agreement was maintained across both cortical and subcortical structures, indicating that the model generalizes across diverse anatomical regions without smoothing away focal tau accumulation. This contrasts with prior CNN-based and generative PET synthesis approaches, which often regress toward population-mean patterns or fail to recover localized high-uptake regions, particularly in early or heterogeneous disease stages.

Although this study focused on a single tracer and cohort, the INR framework is inherently modality-agnostic and extensible. Future work may incorporate additional molecular and functional modalities, such as Aβ PET, functional MRI, or diffusion MRI, as well as longitudinal data to model disease progression. 

The ability to generate high-resolution tau maps in seconds offers clear clinical advantages by potentially reducing scan duration, tracer dose, and radiation exposure while improving accessibility. By enabling individualized voxel-level tau mapping without full tau-PET scans, this framework may broaden access to molecular disease characterization, support earlier diagnosis, facilitate clinical trial enrollment, and enable patient-specific monitoring of therapeutic response.
