Introduction

In this talk, I will present the hypothesis that desynchronization is the critical mechanism by which psychedelics cause both their unique subjective effect, and lasting plasticity and therapeutic effects. I will focus on our recent human imaging results supporting this hypothesis. 

A single dose of psilocybin, a psychedelic that acutely causes distortions of space–time perception and ego dissolution, produces rapid and persistent therapeutic effects in human clinical trials. In ordinary consciousness, neurons are highly synchronized with their local electrical field. In animal electrophysiology studies, 5HT2A agonists cause acute desynchronization and induce persistent increases in synaptogenesis in cortex and hippocampus. But it remains unclear how brain network changes relate to subjective and lasting effects of psychedelics in humans. 

Methods

In this randomized crossover study, we use a longitudinal precision connectomic approach to acute and persisting effects of psilocybin in the cortex, basal ganglia, hippocampus, and cerebellum. Healthy adults ages 19-45 received psilocybin (25mg, oral) or methylphenidate (40mg, oral) spaced 1-2 weeks apart. Participants (N=7) underwent numerous MRI visits (18 visits/participant avg) before, during, and for two weeks after oral psilocybin or methylphenidate (MTP, placebo). Participants (N=4) returned 6-12 months later to repeat an abbreviated imaging protocol with a second psilocybin dose. Task fMRI and physiological monitoring (respiration, pulse oximetry) were measured, in addition to resting fMRI, to control for brain state and physiological confounds. We measured spatio-temporal synchronization of fMRI sigals using normalized global spatial complexity (NGSC). The relationship between subjective psychedelic experiences and neurobiological signals was assessed using a linear mixed effects model which included fixed effects for class (baseline drug, after drug), task/rest, and head motion, and random effects for subject.

In a complementary study in mice, we used wide-field optical imaging in awake Thy1-jRGECO1a mice to determine whether psychedelic (DOI) induced changes in hemodynamics arise from neuronal, vascular or neurovascular effects and to precisely model impulse response function between neuronal and hemodynamic signals.

Results

Psilocybin massively disrupted functional connectivity (FC) in cortex and subcortex, acutely causing more than threefold greater change than methylphenidate. These FC changes were driven by brain desynchronization across spatial scales (areal, global), which dissolved network distinctions by reducing correlations within and anticorrelations between networks. Psilocybin-driven FC changes were strongest in the default mode network, which is connected to the anterior hippocampus and is thought to create our sense of space, time and self. Individual differences in FC changes were linked to the subjective psychedelic experience (mystical experience questionnaire, total score). And disruption to subjective change in sense of time and space was selectively correlated with desynchronization within cortical nodes of the default mode network.

In mouse data, psychedelic produced small but consistent changes to the impulse response function between neuronal and vascular signals and differential changes to neuronal and hemodynamic connectivity measures.

Conclusion

Psychedelic-induced ego dissolution and altered time perception correlate with desynchronization of activity in default mode network, whereas psychedelic visual imagery tracks with disruption in visual cortex — supporting the link between disrupted modular functioning and phenomenology of drug effects. Desynchronization is the critical mechanism by which psychedelics cause their unique subjective effect, and may relate to lasting plasticity and therapeutic effects.
