Introduction

Dynamic hemodynamic parameters provide key information about blood velocity and transit within the cerebral vasculature. In particular, mean transit time (MTT) and arterial–venous transit time (AVT) are important biomarkers of vascular function and are altered in cerebrovascular disorders such as stroke and vascular dementia1.

In CT, PET, and dynamic susceptibility contrast MRI (DSC-MRI), MTT is commonly estimated as the ratio of cerebral blood volume to cerebral blood flow (CBV/CBF). However, accurate MTT estimation is limited by the difficulty of measuring the arterial input function (AIF) in purely intravascular voxels at typical imaging resolutions. As an alternative, tracer wash-out dynamics can provide a robust MTT-related index by quantifying the rate at which contrast exits tissue.

AVT is often measured using wide-field fluorescence imaging or two-photon microscopy following bolus injection of fluorescent dyes, but these methods require exogenous contrast agents and repeated injections2, limiting longitudinal applicability. To address this, we previously developed a BOLD-DSC approach that uses endogenous deoxyhemoglobin (dHb) as a blood-pool contrast induced by brief N₂ inhalation3,4. While this method avoids exogenous tracers, its spatiotemporal resolution limits precise measurement of arterial–venous transit and compartment-specific wash-out dynamics.

Here, we overcome these limitations by combining optical intrinsic signal imaging (OISI) with a transient hypoxic challenge to generate a first-pass dHb response. This approach enables high-resolution mapping of perfusion timing and wash-out dynamics across arteries, tissue, and veins throughout the mouse cortex.

Methods

Optical Intrinsic Signal Imaging

Optical intrinsic signal imaging (OISI) was performed using a high-speed sCMOS camera with dual-wavelength illumination at 530 nm (green, CBV-weighted) and 625 nm (red, BOLD-weighted) (Fig. 1a). The two wavelengths were acquired in an interleaved manner with a 50-ms interval, yielding an effective frame rate of 20 Hz and a temporal resolution of 100 ms per wavelength. Hemoglobin concentration changes (HbR, HbO, and HbT) were calculated from the dual-wavelength signals using the modified Beer–Lambert law (Fig. 1b).

Surgical Preparation

For optical imaging, a thinned-skull preparation was performed under 2% isoflurane anesthesia using a handheld drill. The skull surface was reinforced with a thin layer of cyanoacrylate glue (Loctite 401, Henkel). Meloxicam (1 mg/kg) was administered postoperatively to minimize inflammation. Animals were allowed to recover fully from anesthesia before being returned to their home cages.

Hypoxic Stimulation

Two inhaled gas mixtures were used: a normoxic gas (40% O₂ / 60% N₂, supplemented with 1.5% isoflurane during anesthetized experiments) and a hypoxic stimulus (100% N₂). Gas delivery was controlled via a breathing cone connected to a solenoid two-way pinch valve, which ensured stable pressure and rapid switching between gas mixtures. Hypoxic stimulation was triggered by a pulse stimulator (Master-9). To prevent gas accumulation, exhaled and residual gases were continuously removed by maintaining a slight negative pressure in the exhaust line.

Experimental Procedure

During imaging, mice were head-fixed in a heated cradle, and physiological parameters, including heart rate and respiratory rate were continuously monitored. Hypoxic challenges of 1, 2, or 5 s duration were applied depending on the experimental condition.

Results

1. Mapping brain perfusion using first-pass dHb under different brain states

In the first experiment, we investigated brain perfusion under 1.5% isoflurane anesthesia. A brief 5-s inhalation of 100% N₂ induced a robust increase in deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) with minimal accompanying changes in baseline hemodynamics (Fig. 1b), consistent with a BOLD-DSC–like contrast mechanism.

Using this first-pass HbR signal, we computed voxelwise onset-time and time-to-peak maps (Fig. 1c). These maps clearly differentiated arteries, veins, and parenchymal tissue, with arteries showing the earliest onset and shortest time to peak, followed by veins and then tissue. By combining the vessel map (green) with the perfusion maps (onset time and time-to-peak), we were able to reliably separate arterial, venous, and tissue compartments (Fig. 1d).

We next performed an ROI-based analysis to quantify regional perfusion delays across the cortex. Sensory areas (e.g., SSp-ul, SSp-bf, and SSp-ll) exhibited faster perfusion dynamics, whereas default-mode-network (DMN) regions located beneath large draining veins showed slower responses. These regional differences emphasize the importance of accounting for perfusion timing when interpreting hemodynamic signals in applications such as resting-state connectivity or vasomotion studies.

To examine how brain state affects perfusion, we repeated the experiment in awake mice. Unlike anesthetized animals, awake mice may perceive hypoxic stimulation, potentially evoking additional hemodynamic responses that could bias perfusion estimates. To minimize this effect, we first evaluated N₂ inhalation durations of 1, 2, and 5 s. As expected, 2-s and 5-s stimuli produced large hemodynamic changes, whereas a 1-s N₂ pulse selectively increased HbR with minimal baseline disturbance (Fig. 2b). Therefore, the 1-s stimulus was used to calculate onset-time and time-to-peak maps in awake mice (Fig. 2c).

Using the same vessel and perfusion-based criteria as in the anesthetized condition, we separated tissue, arterial, and venous compartments. Comparison between brain states revealed longer perfusion delays in awake mice relative to those under isoflurane anesthesia, indicating a substantial modulation of cerebral perfusion dynamics by brain state.

2. Differential wash-out dynamics in arteries, tissue, and veins

In addition to perfusion onset and peak timing, the hypoxia-induced HbR response revealed clear differences in wash-out dynamics among arteries, tissue, and veins (Fig. 2e), suggesting an alternative metric related to mean transit time (MTT).

We quantified these differences by calculating the wash-out time and full half-width at maximum (FHWM) for each compartment. Arteries and veins exhibited shorter wash-out times compared with tissue, whereas FHWM was prolonged in both tissue and veins (Fig. 2f). These findings indicate distinct transit and dispersion characteristics across vascular compartments, further highlighting the utility of first-pass HbR measurements for characterizing cerebral perfusion dynamics.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully measured arterial–venous transit time with high sensitivity using intrinsic optical imaging of hypoxia-induced first-pass dHb. Under 1.5% isoflurane anesthesia, we observed region-specific variations in transit time, which may help explain differences in resting-state fMRI dynamics. Notably, arterial-to-venous transit was markedly slower in awake mice than under isoflurane anesthesia, consistent with the higher baseline cerebral blood flow observed during anesthesia.

Beyond onset and peak timing, analysis of wash-out dynamics further differentiated vascular compartments: arteries and veins exhibited rapid wash-out, whereas tissue showed prolonged clearance, with broader signal dispersion observed in tissue and veins. These wash-out characteristics provide complementary information related to mean transit time and vascular dispersion.

Together, these findings establish first-pass dHb imaging as a sensitive tool for characterizing cerebral perfusion timing, vascular compartmentalization, and brain-state–dependent hemodynamic regulation.
