Introduction

Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), known for its obsessive and compulsive behaviors, is an etiologically heterogeneous disorder that included varying course of symptoms associated with onset age, sex, comorbid conditions, medication status and chronicity. For example, the prevalence of OCD is greater in males during childhood and greater in females during adolescence/adulthood. Moreover, the rate of a comorbid condition in pediatric OCD is high (56%-64%) and anxiety is the most common. There is a general understanding of the cortico-striatal-thalamic-cortical network being central to OCD, and likewise, cortical-limbic networks associated with heightened response to emotionally stimuli is implicated as core to in the pathology of anxiety disorder. However, it is unclear to what extent OCD with a comorbid anxiety share similar dysfunction to emotional stimuli such as in the hippocampus, especially from the perceptive of biochemical alterations. 

The hippocampus is rich in glutamatergic neurons and NMDA mediated synaptic plasticity is central for subserving memory. Utilizing ¹H fMRS, which is the only functional imaging technique not constrained by hemodynamics that can detect task-evoked changes in glutamate1, we have previously demonstrated increased hippocampal glutamate modulation in healthy adults during memory encoding processe2. Here we propose a well-demonstrated “Old vs New recognition” episodic memory task combined with ¹H fMRS to investigate whether OCD with comorbid anxiety (past/present) show exaggerated hippocampal glutamate modulation during epochs of episodic memory encoding/recognition of negatively/positively valanced images compared to healthy control (HC). We hypothesize that during memory encoding, negatively valences images will evoke a greater hippocampal glutamate response in OCD youth compared to HC suggesting a potential shared psychopathology related to cortical-limbic network dysfunctions in OCD with comorbid anxiety.

Methods

Eleven adolescents with OCD (DSM-V diagnosed, 10F/1M; 16.6±2.7yrs; 12.6-19.9 yrs) and 13 HC (12F/1M; 16.8 ±2.5yrs; 13.4-20.2yrs) participated. Of the 11 OCD youth, 9 had a comorbid anxiety disorder diagnose and the remaining two had a past diagnose with an anxiety disorder.

The “Old vs New recognition” episodic memory task3 included paired epochs of Encoding (remembering presented images) and Recognition (indicating whether the presented image is ‘old’), both 27s in duration and a 13.5s rest epoch (memory retention, fixation crosshair) placed in between the two (Figure 1a). 54 age-appropriate images of Positive and Negative valance were selected from the normed EmoMadrid database, which have been rated for Valence (from -2 for very negative to +2 for very positive and 0 for neutral) and Arousal (from -2 for very calming to +2 for arousing, and 0 for neutral). Images were categorized into the two valence types (i.e., Positive and Negative paired epochs) with Arousal ratings ranked (Negative ~ Positive). During Encoding, six images per valence category were presented for 4s each (500-1500ms randomly jittered inter-stimulus interval). During Recognition, four images were “old” and two were “new” (all from the same valence category). A total of 3 Encoding/Recognition paired epochs per valance category (Positive/Negative) were conducted (randomized; TA: 8:06min). Response time, hit rate, and false alarm) were recorded using a ﬁber-optic response touchpad (Current Design Systems, Inc).

The neuroimaging data was acquired on a 3T Siemens Cima.X system with a 32-channel volume head coil.  The protocol started with T1w images (MPRAGE, TR= 2.5s, TE= 2.98ms, TI= 1.07s, FA= 80, FOV= 256x256mm2, 176 sagittal slices, pixel resolution= 1x1x1mm3 and TA= 5:58min), which guided the placement of a single voxel over the right hippocampus (18x30x13mm3 or 7.02cm3; Figure 1b) for the ¹H fMRS acquisition. To ensure consistent and reliable placement of the voxel across the participants an in-house developed AVP procedure was utilized4. The ¹H fMRS protocol included the acquisition of 36 measurements (of 4 averages each or temporal resolution of 13.5s) during the episodic memory task. The acquisition parameters included the PRESS sequence with OVS, VAPOR for water suppression and FASTESTMAP for shimming, TR= 3375ms, TE= 19.2ms, bandwidth= 2 kHz, and 2048 data points. A second acquisition of 9 measurements (4 averages each) was conducted during visualizing a fixated crosshair to reflect basal metabolite levels (TA: 2:01min). Lastly, a (fully relaxed) water unsuppressed ¹H MRS spectrum with a TR of 10sec, 2 averages and 1 measurement was acquired after each water-suppressed acquisition as part of the absolute quantification approach. Regarding the processing/quantification, all 45 ¹H MRS measurements were first denoised using the Marchenko-Pastur (MP) PCA approach followed correcting each measurement for phase (0th and 1st order) and spectral shift using the CORRAW option of LCModel. The six ¹H MRS measurements per epoch condition (i.e., two per epoch and three paired epoch) were then averaged and fitted with LCModel, which provided a single glutamate measure per epoch condition (Encoding/Recognition and Positive/Negative) (Figure 1b). The 10 interleaved rest epoch measurements were also averaged and fitted, which provided the mean rest glutamate level as the covariate in the analyses. was used for spectral quantification (Figure 1). The averaging of the 9 fixated crosshair ¹H fMRS provided a “basal” measurement of glutamate.

A repeated measure GEE approach was used to test significant glutamate level differences between Valance type (Positive vs Negative) for each epoch mode, Encoding and Recognition, with the following terms in the model: Group (OCD, HC), Valance type (Positive, Negative), Group-by-Valance interaction, and the mean glutamate level from the rest epochs as the covariate. Post-hoc analyses were conducted with Least Squares Means statistics.

Results

Overall, the data quality for this small sample was reasonable with only one dataset being rejected due to poor quality (OCD participant not included in the count). Additionally, only two glutamate measurements were excluded due to movement (FWHM exceeding 20Hz). Based on GLM statistics, the spectral linewidth of the NAA signal was not significantly different between groups (OCD: 9.2±0.8Hz vs HC:8.3±.4Hz; p=.27); however, the S/N of NAA was significantly higher in HC compared to OCD (12.5±.6 vs 9.5±.5; p=.0002). The overall mean Cramer-Rao lowest bound (CRLB) for glutamate was 5.3±.3 (ranged between 3-9). More importantly, the basal glutamate level, as well as the other key metabolites (NAA, PCr+Cr, GPC+PC and myo-inositol) were not significantly different between the two groups (e.g., mean glutamate for OCD: 9.67±.34 vs HC: 9.84±.30; p=.69; Figure 2a).

During the Encoding phase of the memory task, the Group-by-Valance interaction was significant (χ2= 4.12, p=.043) where negatively valanced images evoked a significantly greater effect with OCD youth evidenced by a higher hippocampal mean Glu level compared to HC (post hoc: p= .026; Figure 2b). During the Recognition epochs, the Group-by-Valance interaction was not significant (χ2= 1.78, p=.18) suggesting that Recognition evoked similar Glu modulation between groups for both Valance type.

Conclusion

In this preliminary study utilizing an innovative functional imaging technique, we show for the first time heightened engagement of the right hippocampus that is specific during episodic memory encoding of negatively valanced images in OCD youth compared to HC youth. Both, OCD and HC individuals demonstrated modest hippocampal glutamate modulation during memory recognition that did not differ between groups for both valance type (not shown); again, emphasizing the encoding of negatively valenced images being most salient. Moreover, basal hippocampal levels of glutamate, which closely mimics conventional ¹H MRS (without task), were not significantly different between the groups emphasizing the importance of ¹H fMRS for discerning biochemical differences under specific task condition. 

It is important to note that 9 of the 11 OCD participants also had a comorbid anxiety disorder and the remain 2 had a past diagnose with an anxiety disorder. Though based on a small sample, results are encouraging in providing support of a potential shared psychopathology related to heightened hippocampal response in OCD with comorbid anxiety. However, further studies are warranted to increase the power with a larger sample, as well as to confirm that these heightened hippocampal responses to the encoding of negatively valanced images are specific to OCD with comorbid anxiety and not OCD without any comorbid conditions. 

Lastly, this has the potential of tracking associations between changes in atypical hippocampal glutamate function during affective episodic memory and changes related to comorbid conditions, medication status and/or symptoms (improving, worsening or not changing). In all, these results also fuel the need of longitudinal studies to better characterize the course of OCD from childhood to adolescence
