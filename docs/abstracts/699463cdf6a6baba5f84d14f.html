Introduction

A major open question at the intersection between cognitive and systems neuroscience is how distributed coordinated activity across the brain can support key processes underlying adaptive cognitive behaviour, such as compositional recombination. There are two key sub-processes underlying compositional cognition: the capacity to categorise, store and recall specific Components (i.e., existing knowledge or behaviours), and the Recombination process that flexibly combines components to be applied across different contexts.1 During compositional cognition, the two processes work in tandem: Components ensure functional specialisation within specific contexts, whereas Recombination is a flexible process that generalises across contexts. Differentiating the brain networks responsible for these distinct aspects of compositional cognition is crucial for advancing our understanding of the process.

Previous work in human functional MRI (fMRI) has typically focused on compositionality as a cerebral cortical process,2,3 with a growing body of animal work highlighting the role of the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus in compositional processes.4 However, an understanding of how these cortical mechanisms interact with the subcortex and cerebellum to implement compositional cognition remains incomplete. We hypothesised that: 1) Recombination would be consistent and generalisable across varied rule contexts, while Components would be specifically and selectively recruited for each rule; 2) Components would involve specialist regions, whereas Recombination would recruit a distributed network across cortical and cerebellar regions; and 3) networks underlying Components and Recombination should work together dynamically in order for effective compositional cognition to occur.

Methods

Ninety-five participants completed the Concrete Permuted Rules of Operation (C-PRO) paradigm while undergoing functional MRI scanning as part of an openly available dataset (OpenNeuro, accession number ds003701).2 The C-PRO paradigm operationalises compositional cognition by introducing distinct domains of rules as Components that are recombined during the task (Figure 1a). By varying the specific combination of rules, the C-PRO creates different task contexts (Figure 1b). Participants were presented with three rules, with one per task domain (Motor, Logic and Sensory). For example, if the instructions were “BOTH” (logic rule), “VERTICAL” (sensory rule), and “LEFT INDEX” (motor rule), participants would press a button with their LEFT INDEX finger if BOTH bars were VERTICAL (Figure 1c).

To detect time-resolved task-related brain activity associated with each of the Component constructs within the C-PRO task, we subjected pre-processed BOLD data from 482 regions that covered the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, basal ganglia and thalamus to a finite impulse response (FIR) model. We applied a mean-centred, task-based partial least squares (PLS) analysis to determine latent variables that capture maximal covariance between brain activity and behaviour, separating out brain patterns for each task domain (Figure 1d).

To identify regions associated with the Recombination process we reasoned that: i) they should be engaged across most trials (i.e., high mean FIR response); ii) they should not be selectively associated with specific Components (i.e., PLS loading near the origin); and iii) their activity should be correlated with Component brain regions on those trials corresponding to that Component: for instance, they should co-activate with “left hand” brain regions during “left hand” trials.

We defined characteristics of the Recombination network using three measures: the dimensionality of the region FIR response, the extent of network integration, and similarity of response across different task contexts. Dimensionality was calculated using the participation ratio – specifies the number of dimensions required to capture the variance of the data, network integration was measured using the participation coefficient, and response similarity was measured by calculating the Pearson’s correlation between FIR responses across different task contexts. 

To test whether the characteristics of the Recombination network generalised beyond the fMRI data, we analysed 40 recurrent neural networks that were trained to perform multiple cognitive tasks.5 The networks underwent a similar pipeline: identifying Recombination units, then calculating dimensionality, integration and response similarity.

Results

From the PLS analysis, we identified a low-dimensional orthogonal basis set associated with the task (the top three latent variables explained 88.7% of the total variance). The first latent variable was dominated by Motor rules (left hand vs. right hand) and separated the left and right motor regions of the cerebral and cerebellar cortex (Figure 2a). The second latent variable was mainly driven by Logic rules (positive vs. negative) and separated higher cognitive areas from unimodal and memory-associated areas (Figure 2b). The third latent variable was driven by Sensory rules (visual vs. auditory) and separated attentional processing and higher order visual processing from primary auditory and primary visual regions (Figure 2c).

We associated each brain region with a latent variable (creating a 3-dimensional space), where the absolute value of a region’s loading on each latent variable is interpretable as the extent to which a particular region was associated with a specific task dimension. We identified a set of regions that were selectively recruited for each task rule (Component network), and a set of regions that selectively interacted with the Component network across rules (Recombination network; Figure 2d-f). We identified 54 regions that were associated with specific task dimensions (Component network) consisting of regions from the somatomotor and prefrontal cortical regions, and anterior cerebellar regions (lobules I-IV; Figure 2g, silver). For the Recombination network, we found 51 regions from the frontoparietal cerebral cortex and posterior cerebellum (lobules VI, VIIb, VIIIa; Figure 2h).

In the fMRI data, when comparing characteristics of the Recombination and Component networks we found that the Recombination network had significantly lower dimensionality (t86 = -10.61, p < 1e-16, 95% CI [-1.73 -1.18]), was more integrated (t103 = 2.93, p = 0.004, 95% CI [0.003 0.016]) and demonstrated more similar responses across task contexts (t9,913 = 42.58, p < 1e-16, 95% CI [0.11 0.12]) compared to the Component network. These results were reproduced using neural networks, where Recurrent units had significantly lower dimensionality (t39 = -3.31, p = 0.002, 95% CI [-0.66 -0.16]), were more integrated (t78 = 5.51, p = 4.54e-07, 95% CI [0.03 0.07]), and were more correlated across tasks compared to Component units (t1,255 = 8.46, p < 1e-16, 95% CI [0.12 0.19]). Overall, the results from the neural networks aligned with our findings from the fMRI data, demonstrating that these characteristics are underlying features that separate Recombination and Component processes during compositional cognition, irrespective of the specific implementation-level details of each system.

Conclusion

Compositional cognition can be characterised by two related, yet separable, processes: the specialised Components that are recruited to achieve the task demands, and the more generalised process of Recombination that facilitates these components to be applied in novel situations. Here, we used dimensionality reduction, time-varying modelling and functional connectivity to interrogate fMRI measurements collected during a compositional task (C-PRO) to dissociate these processes. We found clear evidence that the two processes differed. First, Components were embedded in specific contexts and rules, predominately identifiable in specialist cortical regions, whereas Recombination was a feature that generalised across all contexts, involving a distributed cortico-cerebellar network. We demonstrated that compositional cognition required specific, dynamic interactions and differentiation between these two processes, and that the Recombination network was low-dimensional, integrated, and generalised across task contexts. Finally, we observed that the network signatures of Recombination observed in human neuroimaging data were also present in recurrent neural networks trained on multiple cognitive tasks. Overall, these findings demonstrate that compositional cognition is facilitated by interactions between distributed networks serving Recombination and Component processes.
