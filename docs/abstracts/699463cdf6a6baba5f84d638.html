Introduction

Executive Functions (EF) are higher-order, top-down cognitive processes essential for effective decision-making, problem-solving, and responding to novel situations. Age-related decline in EF can significantly impact daily functioning and overall quality of life; however, this decline is not uniform across all EF domains (working memory, response inhibition and cognitive flexibility)1,2. While traditional cognitive interventions often target frontal theta oscillations to enhance EF in general3 , the aging brain undergoes significant network reorganization differentially across domains, suggesting that distinct oscillatory patterns may emerge as compensatory mechanisms to restore age-related deficits4. This highlights an urgent need for cognitive rehabilitation therapies tailored to individuals specific to their neurophysiological profiles. Developing such tailored approaches first requires identifying reliable neurophysiological biomarkers that accurately capture the subtle, domain-specific decline in EF. Our study aimed to identify such novel, EEG-based oscillatory markers associated with age-related changes across the three core domains of EF. 

Methods

We collected 64-channel EEG data from 26 young (14M, mean age = 25.8 ± 2.8) and 22 elderly (13M, mean age = 57.3 ± 6.6) healthy participants while they performed three distinct cognitive tasks, each testing a core domain of EF: 3-back task for working memory, stop-signal task for response inhibition5, and the number-letter switching task for cognitive flexibility. Behavioural performance was quantified using task-specific metrics to capture age-related differences. For the 3-back task, we calculated accuracy, mean reaction time (RT), and signal detection measures (d’ sensitivity and C-criterion). For the stop-signal task, the key metric was the Stop-Signal Reaction Time (SSRT), calculated via the integration method, replacement of go omissions. Participants with p(respond|signal) < 0.3 or > 0.7 were excluded from further analysis, both for behavioral and EEG data analyses. The number-letter switching task was analysed using both conventional approach (based on task rule changes) and a finer-grained classification that categorized trials based on both task rule and response hand (e.g., no switch (NS), motor switch (MS), cognitive switch, cognitive-motor switch (CMS)), yielding measures of RT, accuracy, and switch cost normalized to accuracy. 

All EEG data analysis was performed in the Brainstorm toolbox (MATLAB-based), utilizing robust, component-specific methods to characterize age-related neural dynamics. For the 3-back and number-letter switching task, Event-Related Potentials (ERPs) were computed using the Residue Iteration Decomposition (RIDE) toolbox, a MATLAB-based method that decomposes ERP signals by modeling and realigning components with variable latencies across trials. RIDE takes single-trial EEG data along with stimulus onset and response timing information to iteratively estimate and realign latency-jittered components, thereby providing a clearer representation of underlying neural dynamics. Due to the nature of the stop-signal task (lacking measurable reaction times for successful stops), ERPs were computed without RIDE for this task. Time–frequency analysis was performed on the CSD-transformed EEG data for all three tasks. Morlet wavelet transform in brainstorm was performed with default parameters -a central frequency of 1 Hz and a full-width half maxima of 3sec, providing a balanced trade-off between temporal and frequency resolution. During the decomposition, the evoked component of the oscillations was removed to isolate induced spectral changes related to task performance. All statistical analyses were performed using either MATLAB, R studio or Brainstorm. Behavioral data were first assessed for normality using the Anderson–Darling test and for sphericity using Mauchly’s test. Parametric comparisons were conducted using independent t-tests, while Mann–Whitney U tests were applied when assumptions were violated. Repeated-measures comparisons were analyzed using non-parametric ART- ANOVA, and post hoc comparisons were done using Tukey test. Task-related EEG effects were assessed using cluster-based permutation t-tests implemented with a FieldTrip-based function in Brainstorm, using 5000 randomizations and a minimum of 2 neighboring channels per cluster. Additionally, Spearman correlation analyses were performed to examine relationships between EEG markers and behavioral metrics.

Results

Across all tasks, the elderly group exhibited poorer performance as expected. In the 3-back task, although reaction times did not differ significantly between groups, elderly participants showed significantly lower overall accuracy compared to the young group (p = 0.02). In the stop-signal task, despite comparable stop-signal delays between groups, Welch's unpaired t-test revealed significantly longer SSRTs in the elderly group compared to the young group (p = 0.019). No significant group differences were found for other behavioral measures. In case of number-letter switching task, across both analytical approaches, significant main effects of group and condition were observed for reaction time and accuracy, with no significant group × condition interaction. Post-hoc comparisons in the categorized analysis indicated that the CS condition was associated with significantly lower accuracy and longer reaction times compared to the NS conditions. EEG analyses across three distinct cognitive tasks revealed significant age-related differences in both ERPs and oscillatory power.

 3-back task: Young adults exhibited a stronger frontal negativity between 250–600 ms post-stimulus, whereas elderly participants showed a reduced parietal positivity consistent with a diminished P300 response for hit trials. Power spectral analyses indicated no reliable group differences during hit trials after correction for multiple comparisons. However, correctly rejected non-target trials demonstrated a significant reduction in frontal broadband power between 200–600 ms in the elderly group as compared to the young group. Correlation analyses further revealed that, in older adults, P300 peak amplitude was positively associated with task accuracy, whereas the frontal negativity area under the curve showed a negative correlation with accuracy, indicating differential contributions of evaluative and control-related processes to performance.

 Stop-signal task:  During successful stop trials, elderly individuals exhibited significantly reduced beta and low-gamma power relative to younger participants, primarily over right centro-parietal regions. No group differences were observed during failed stop trials. Within-group comparisons revealed marked trial-type differentiation in the young group, with successful stopping characterized by decreased delta and theta power alongside increased alpha, beta, and gamma power. In contrast, the elderly showed differentiation only in low-frequency bands, with no modulation of beta power between successful and failed stops. Baseline analyses revealed elevated relative beta power in older adults across regions, suggesting possible saturation or altered spectral distribution that may limit task-related modulation and obscure trial-specific effects. We did not see any significant correlations between SSRT and EEG outcomes. 

Number-letter switching task: Elderly adults showed a reduced N150 over central sites in the early time window (0–200 ms) for CS condition. In the later time window (200–400 ms), they also exhibited reduced P300 amplitudes over parieto-temporal regions across CS, CMS, and MS conditions. Power spectral analyses across conditions did not yield significant clusters; however, mixed-model ANOVAs revealed two critical findings. Firstly, there was a significant main effect of group on frontal theta power, with the elderly group exhibiting reduced overall power compared to the young group, alongside a significant group × condition interaction. Post-hoc analyses of this interaction indicated that frontal theta power showed a significant increase in peak amplitude in the young group during the CS condition as compared to the NS condition. Conversely, the elderly group showed no significant modulation of frontal theta power across conditions. Secondly, a significant difference was also observed for parietal alpha power-the elderly adults exhibited reduced alpha desynchronization compared to the young group. 

Conclusion

Our study aimed to characterize how healthy aging alters EF by linking behavioural measures to EEG-based neurophysiological features. By examining working memory, response inhibition, and cognitive flexibility within the same cohort, we observed that age-related executive dysfunction cannot be explained by generalized slowing or uniform performance decline. Instead, it reflects a dissociation between preserved surface-level performance and compromised underlying neural control.

 In the working memory domain, elderly adults maintained response speed despite reduced accuracy, indicating a shift toward prioritizing speed over accuracy. This behavioral pattern was accompanied by weakened ERP markers of stimulus evaluation and updating, including reduced frontal negativity and attenuated P300 responses. An anterior-to-posterior shift in neural engagement further suggested compensatory recruitment of parietal regions to offset diminished early frontal control. However, this compensation was delayed and inefficient, supporting partial maintenance of performance at the cost of accuracy and neural precision.

 Response inhibition deficits in older adults were most clearly indexed by prolonged stop-signal reaction times, reflecting a reduced capacity to cancel ongoing actions. Neurally, this impairment was associated with a failure to up-regulate oscillatory beta power in frontal regions during successful stopping. Elevated baseline activity further indicated increased neural noise, resulting in less distinct neural states and a reduced ability to generate the transient, high-fidelity beta events critical for efficient inhibitory control. Together, these findings point to a degradation of the neural “braking system” rather than a simple reduction in inhibitory control.

 In the cognitive flexibility task, age-related changes reflected impairments in both early attentional processing and later task-set updating. Reduced P300 amplitudes indicated deficits in rule updating, while lower fronto-central theta power suggested diminished working memory capacity and reduced recruitment of control mechanisms. Although older adults showed reduced switch costs, this apparent benefit came at the expense of longer reaction times and reduced alpha desynchronization, consistent with slower processing and diminished executive attention rather than preserved flexibility.

 Across all three executive domains, a common mechanistic pattern emerged. Elderly adults exhibited weaker ERP components, reduced modulation of task-related oscillations, and higher levels of neural noise. Critically, there was a consistent lack of task-appropriate neural modulation, suggesting a constrained dynamic range that limits the ability to flexibly scale neural resources in response to changing demands. Although compensatory mechanisms like posterior recruitment emerge, they are slower and less effective. Taken together, these findings reinforce that a one-size-fits-all approach to cognitive rehabilitation is unlikely to be effective. Cognitive performance can mask substantial neural dysfunction, emphasizing that rehabilitation strategies must be tailored to individual neural profiles rather than behavioral outcomes alone.
