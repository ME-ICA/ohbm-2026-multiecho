Introduction

Over the past millennia, humans have developed graphic symbolic systems that enable the durable visual preservation and transmission of cognitive constructs. Among these systems, distinct notations were created for language and for music, each relying on a limited set of elementary visual symbols and on combinatorial rules governing their organization.

The acquisition of literacy relies on brain plasticity—the capacity of the brain to modify its functional properties under the influence of learning—and on cortical specialization, whereby specific cognitive functions become supported by dedicated brain regions. The neural impact of word literacy has been extensively studied. One well-established consequence is the emergence of a reproducible region in the left ventral occipitotemporal cortex (VOTC), the Visual Word Form Area (VWFA), which becomes selectively tuned to the visual identification of letters and words 1.

In contrast, the neural bases of music literacy remain less well understood. Previous studies have suggested the existence of a parallel, though distinct, specialization for music reading within the visual cortex 2,3, but the regions involved in musical and verbal reading appear to be largely overlapping, leaving the nature and origin of any domain-specific specialization unresolved.

Here, we aimed to characterize the specialization of the visual cortex for musical and verbal symbols, and to investigate its underlying principles. We dissociated different categories of musical notation (notes vs. other musical symbols such as clefs and accidentals) and manipulated melodic and rhythmic complexity orthogonally. Using ultra-high-field 7-Tesla fMRI (1.5 mm isotropic resolution) in professional musicians and matched non-musician controls, we were able to probe functional specialization at the voxel level in individual participants, thereby overcoming the limitations associated with group averaging in previous studies.

Methods

Seventeen professional musicians with high music-reading proficiency and seventeen matched non-musician controls participated in the study. All participants were right-handed, English speakers, and had acquired reading and spoken English in childhood. Musicians had also learned to read musical notation from childhood. Music-reading proficiency was assessed using a self-report questionnaire and confirmed by years of formal musical training.

Data were acquired on a 7-Tesla MRI scanner (Siemens Magnetom). Visual stimuli comprised: (1) words, (2) short musical sequences varying orthogonally in melodic (high vs. low) and rhythmic (high vs. low) complexity, (3) musical symbols from key signatures including clefs and accidentals, and (4) low-level control stimuli consisting of scrambled versions of each stimulus category.

The experiment followed a block design with ten blocks of 60s each. Within each block, stimuli were presented in nine category mini-blocks (12 stimuli per mini-block) interleaved with one baseline mini-block. Each stimulus was presented for 200ms with an inter-stimulus interval of 300ms, resulting in mini-block durations of 6s. Baseline mini-blocks consisted of a fixation cross and were presented at the beginning and end of the experiment, as well as between blocks. The order of category mini-blocks was pseudo-randomized within each block.

Participants were instructed to maintain fixation throughout the experiment and to perform an orthogonal oddball detection task, responding with a right index finger button press whenever a grid target replaced a stimulus within a category mini-block.

Functional MRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep. Whole-brain group-level general linear models (GLMs) were first computed to assess category sensitivity by contrasting each symbolic condition against its scrambled control, as well as to examine the effects of melodic and rhythmic complexity.

Individual-level analyses were then performed on first-level GLM z-maps. These maps were masked within six regions of interest (ROIs): bilateral ventral occipitotemporal cortex (VOTC), lateral occipital cortex (LOC), and intraparietal sulcus (IPS). Within each ROI, voxels selective for a single category and voxels responsive to multiple categories were identified and quantified. To assess category specialization, the observed number of voxels responsive to multiple categories was compared to the expected overlap assuming independent voxel-wise sensitivities to each category. A significantly lower observed overlap than expected was taken as evidence for functional specialization.

Results

At the group level, contrasts of symbolic stimuli versus scrambled controls identified regions sensitive to high-level symbolic features. Word stimuli elicited robust activation along the left fusiform gyrus, encompassing the Visual Word Form Area (VWFA), together with the classical left-lateralized reading network. Musical notes primarily activated the lateral occipital cortex (LOC) and the intraparietal sulcus (IPS), with a leftward predominance. Musical signatures engaged components of both systems, activating bilateral fusiform regions overlapping with the left VWFA, as well as left LOC and bilateral IPS (Fig. 1a).

Group-level manipulations of musical structure revealed no robust activations for melodic structure when contrasting notes with versus without melodic organization at corrected statistical thresholds. Weak effects emerged only at exploratory thresholds in posterior occipitoparietal regions overlapping with areas engaged by musical notation, suggesting limited sensitivity to higher-level melodic structure at the group level. In contrast, rhythmic structure elicited reliable activations within ventral occipital regions, consistent with sensitivity to low-level visual properties of rhythmic notation.

Individual-level analyses revealed that the apparent overlaps observed at the group level masked substantial voxel-wise specialization. Across all regions of interest, more than 90% of voxels were sensitive to a single symbol category, and voxels responsive to multiple categories were significantly less frequent than expected under a voxel-wise independence model, indicating pervasive functional specialization at the voxel level.

Despite this strong selectivity, asymmetric patterns of functional sharing were observed. Word-sensitive voxels responded reliably to musical signatures but not to musical notes. Conversely, note-sensitive voxels responded to musical signatures but not to words. Signature-sensitive voxels occupied an intermediate position, responding to both words and notes in a region-dependent manner. Notably, no voxel-level sharing was observed between words and notes.

Applying a strict definition of selectivity (greater activation for one category than for the other two, after subtraction of scrambled control conditions), word-selective voxels were confined to the left ventral occipitotemporal cortex, whereas note-selective voxels were predominantly located in bilateral IPS and right LOC. The left LOC was the only region containing both word- and note-selective voxels, organized along an anterior–posterior gradient, with word-selective voxels located anterior to note-selective voxels. Strictly signature-selective voxels were nearly absent, reflecting their shared sensitivity with the other symbol systems (Fig. 1b).

Finally, voxel-wise analyses of musical structure revealed that sensitivity to rhythmic structure was largely driven by low-level visual features, whereas sensitivity to melodic structure characterized a subset of note-sensitive voxels within IPS and LOC. These findings indicate that higher-level musical structure is encoded within parieto-occipital regions specialized for musical notation.

Conclusion

Previous neuroimaging studies of music and word reading, primarily conducted at 3 Tesla, have reported largely overlapping activations for musical and verbal symbols within the visual cortex, leading to the conclusion that these symbolic systems rely on shared neural substrates 2,3. By combining ultra-high-field 7-Tesla fMRI with individual-level, voxel-wise analyses, the present study demonstrates that this apparent overlap largely reflects limitations of spatial resolution and group averaging. At finer spatial scales, musical and verbal symbols engage largely distinct populations of voxels, revealing a degree of functional specialization that is not detectable at conventional field strengths.

Beyond dissociating musical and verbal reading, our approach further reveals differential processing within the musical domain itself. Distinct patterns of activation were observed for musical notes and other musical symbols (e.g., clefs, accidental), indicating that the visual system differentiates between functionally distinct components of musical notation. This finding suggests that specialization is shaped not only by broad symbolic domains, but also by the internal structure and computational demands of the symbol system.

The cortical distribution of note-sensitive voxels, primarily involving the intraparietal sulcus and lateral occipital cortex, is consistent with previous work implicating parieto-occipital regions in music reading and visuospatial transformations 4,5. Together with these studies, our results support the view that music notation recruits neural circuits involved in spatial mapping, and sensorimotor integration, distinct from those supporting word recognition.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although all musicians were experienced music readers, their level of expertise was not fully homogeneous, which may have introduced variability in the degree of specialization observed. Second, musical symbols were presented in a controlled and visually simplified context, which only partially captures the ecological complexity of real-world music reading, a highly multimodal and dynamic activity.

At the time of submission, analyses focused on the musician group. Ongoing analyses in matched non-musician controls will be completed prior to the conference and will allow us identify which aspects of visual cortical specialization reflect general properties of symbolic systems and which emerge specifically through long-term musical training.

Despite these limitations, the present findings offer important perspectives. They contribute to a broader understanding of brain plasticity associated with literacy and the emergence of cortical specialization for culturally acquired symbol systems. Moreover, they provide novel insights into music reading, a cognitive skill that remains comparatively understudied in cognitive neuroscience. By isolating early perceptual stages of musical symbol processing, this work represents a first step toward characterizing the neural foundations of a complex multimodal behavior that integrates visual perception, abstract structure, and action planning.
