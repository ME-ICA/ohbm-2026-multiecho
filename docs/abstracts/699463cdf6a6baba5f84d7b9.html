Introduction

Resting-state EEG is widely used to evaluate neural dynamics especially in laboratory conditions, but little is known about how naturalistic physical activity—such as walking—acutely alters canonical eyes-open (EO) and eyes-closed (EC) resting-state EEG rhythms. Understanding such modulations in EEG is essential for interpreting the possible differences in laboratory-based recordings vs. real-life-like ambulatory measurements1 and may reveal rapid activity-dependent biomarkers relevant, e.g., in clinical applications such as addressing early neurodegeneration2.

Here we investigated whether 5 minutes of normal walking followed by 5 minutes of fast walking alters resting-state EEG oscillatory activity in healthy adults. We specifically examined ROI-based effects in the frontal, frontomotor, and posterior networks, given their strong role in sensory and cognitive control systems in aging2,3,4.

This analysis represents the first stage of a broader study in which ambulatory EEG will be addressed in individuals suffering from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) during a real-life-like experiment5. Our aim is to determine whether physical-activity-induced modulations of resting EEG may serve as rapid functional probes of neural integrity in MCI.

We report here the initial findings from seven healthy adults, establishing feasibility of recording and identifying oscillatory features that are reactive during short walking bouts. 

Methods

Seven healthy adults (mean age 25 yrs) completed two resting-state EEG measurements (eyes open, EO; eyes closed, EC), followed by 5 minutes of normal walking and 5 minutes of fast walking on a treadmill during which EEG was recorded. After the walking tasks another set of resting-state measurements were conducted (EO and EC). Data were recorded with a 64-channel mobile EEG system.

Data preprocessing included band-pass filtering (1–40 Hz), artifact rejection (e.g.,ICA-based ocular/muscle artifact removal), and channel interpolation. Power spectral densities (PSD) were estimated using the Welch’s method. Regions-of-interest (ROIs) were defined anatomically to cover posterior, frontal, and frontomotor channels.

Outcome measures included power at delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), and alpha (8–12 Hz) frequency bands per ROI, as well as alpha peak frequency derived from PSD maxima within 8–12 Hz.

Paired comparisons between pre- and post-resting-state EEGs were performed using non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon signed-rank). EO and EC conditions were analyzed separately.

Results

Significant changes emerged only in the EO condition (Figure 1), with no detectable pre–post-EEG differences in the EC condition, suggesting that the brain’s visually-engaged brain areas are more sensitive to short bouts of walking.

 Significant power decrease (p< 0.05) after walking was observed in the posterior channels in the delta band and fronto-motor channels in the theta band, while a significant increase in power (p< 0.05) was observed in the posterior channels in the alpha band. Peak alpha frequency showed a significant shift (p< 0.05) to lower frequencies in the frontal and posterior channels. 

No significant power or frequency changes were observed in the EC condition.

Conclusion

Short bouts of normal and fast walking produce rapid, state-dependent alterations in resting-state EEG in healthy adults. EO—but not EC—resting dynamics exhibited changes in delta power, alpha power, and alpha peak frequency, with effects localizing to posterior, frontal, and frontomotor networks.

These findings highlight the sensitivity of resting-state EEG to recent physical activity and the potential of walking-induced neural modulation to serve as a rapid functional probe of network integrity. The relevance of these features as candidate biomarkers for early cognitive decline will be addressed next.
