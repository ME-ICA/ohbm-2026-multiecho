Introduction

Electrical field potentials recorded with human intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) reflect the summed activity of large neuronal populations. Field potentials are often decomposed into multiple components. Two of the most widely studied components are the stimulus-evoked potential, a stereotyped voltage waveform time-locked to stimulus onset, and high-frequency broadband power changes. Both signals have been extensively used to characterize visual processing in human cortex with iEEG.

An important question is whether these two signals capture independent aspects of neural processing. Prior work has reached differing conclusions. One study reported distinct spatial summation properties for broadband and evoked responses, with broadband responses—but not evoked responses—showing compressive spatial summation, closely aligned with fMRI BOLD signals, suggesting different underlying integration mechanisms (Winawer et al., 2013). In contrast, a large recent study reported that evoked and broadband iEEG signals had highly similar response profiles to face stimuli in ventral occipito-temporal cortex (Jacques et al., 2022).

In this study, we examined the relationship between broadband and evoked responses using a wide variety of visual stimuli. By comparing broadband to evoked responses across stimulus contrast, pattern, and size, we aimed to characterize conditions under which the two signals show similar response patterns and conditions under which they diverge.

Methods

We re-analyzed human iEEG recordings from early visual cortex (V1, V2, and V3) from two published datasets (Hermes et al., 2019; Groen et al., 2022). The first dataset included recordings from 15 electrodes across 3 subjects, and responses to 86 distinct stimulus classes, with each stimulus repeated 9–15 times per subject. The second dataset included recordings from 64 electrodes across 11 subjects, and responses to 27 distinct stimulus classes, with each stimulus repeated 6–36 times per subject.

We quantified the stimulus-induced broadband response and the stimulus-evoked potential in each electrode for each stimulus class. Broadband activity was quantified by first computing the time-varying envelope of spectral power from 60 to 200 Hz, averaging this time series across repeated trials with the same stimulus class, and then summing the averaged time series from 0 ms to 1,000 ms following stimulus onset. The evoked responses were derived by low-pass filtering the voltage response (0 to 40 Hz) and averaging the voltage across repeated trials. Response magnitude was then quantified as the temporal summation of squared voltage, corresponding to summation of signal power, over the same stimulus-locked window.

We compared these measures across a range of visual stimulus manipulations, including contrast, texture density, orientation (grating-like vs curved patterns), and size. A subset of stimuli varying in position and size were used to estimate population receptive field (pRF) models within a compressive spatial summation (CSS) framework. This allowed for comparison of receptive field centers and spatial compression between broadband and evoked measures.

Results

1. Contrast and texture. We first considered the pattern of responses to stimulus contrast and texture pattern. Broadband and evoked responses were similar across two stimulus manipulations. For curved stimuli, the broadband and evoked responses scaled systematically with contrast and with texture density. However, the two signals differed in their preference for straight vs curved stimuli: when the magnitude of the two signals were normalized to match for curved stimuli, the evoked responses were larger for grating-like (i.e., straight) stimuli (Fig. 1A). Comparison to a previous fMRI study with the same stimuli (Fang et al, 2023) indicates that the fMRI response is more similar to iEEG broadband than the iEEG evoked response, in terms of sensitivity to curved vs straight stimuli. 

2. Spatial summation. We next examined spatial summation properties using population receptive field modeling. Estimated population receptive field center locations showed close correspondence between broadband and evoked responses, indicating that both signals captured responses from comparable spatial locations (Fig. 1B). However, the broadband responses exhibited stronger compressive spatial summation, indicated by an exponent of 0.3 in the CSS pRF model fits. In contrast, evoked responses showed weaker compression: The best-fitting exponent was approximately 0.5, and a linear summation model (exponent of 1) explained as much variance in the data as a CSS model. Thus, broadband and evoked signals differed in how visual inputs were integrated across space (Fig. 1C), consistent with the prior study (Winawer et al., 2013). As with spatial pattern, the broadband response agreed better with results from fMRI measurements.

Conclusion

Together, these results suggest that broadband and evoked responses capture both shared and distinct aspects of visual processing. The two signals behaved similarly in their responses to variation in contrast and texture density, while diverging in their preferences for straight vs curved stimuli, and in their spatial summation properties. Where the signal differed, the broadband responses were more similar to the fMRI response, and likely better reflect the aggregate metabolic demand of the neural activity. The evoked response may be more dependent on the degree of neural synchrony in the response. Taken together, our findings demonstrate that the relationship between broadband and evoked responses depends on which stimulus properties and response measures are emphasized in analysis.
