Introduction

Structural T1-weighted (T1w) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides high-resolution anatomical references for brain injury assessment and neurotechnology applications but cannot be acquired consistently for patients in the intensive care unit (ICU). MRI can be contraindicated due to  clinical instability, metallic objects in the body, or intracranial pressure monitors that are not MRI compatible. In contrast, computed tomography (CT) is fast and bedside-capable, requiring only a fraction of the time of MRI and serving as the routine first-line modality in neurocritical care.1, 2 However,  CT’s limited soft-tissue contrast makes it less suitable for detailed cortical images. 

This contrast gap is a major obstacle for advanced neurotechnologies, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), which are built around MR-guided navigation.2 These techniques rely on precise anatomical targeting, which depends on clear, high-resolution, subject-specific T1w images to optimize coil placement, electric-field modeling, and reproducibility.3 Deep learning synthesis methods, such as super-resolution and contrast conversion tools (e.g., SynthSR), can generate realistic-looking images from a variety of low-resolution inputs.4 However, these methods can also obscure subject-specific anatomical landmarks and features, such as lesions and atrophy, which are often smoothed away or normalized. This can complicate the precision of neuronavigational techniques, particularly when accurate mapping of the cortex and lesion borders is required.1,3 Here, we aimed to overcome current methodological barriers in ICU neuronavigation by developing a segmentation-based approach to MRI synthesis that leverages readily accessible CT scans and avoids the pitfalls of deep learning methods.

Methods

Data

We leveraged data from two participants enrolled in an ongoing study testing the ability of TMS-electroencephalography (EEG) to detect and predict recovery of consciousness in the intensive care unit (clinicaltrials.gov, NCT06568536). Patients were enrolled with surrogate informed consent based on a protocol approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board.

Both patients had paired non-contrast head CT scans acquired as part of routine clinical care and 3D T1-weighted MRI acquired as part of the research protocol. CT imaging was performed on two different systems: Siemens Biograph 64 and Siemens SOMTATOM Force. MRI was performed on a Siemens MAGNATOM Vida Fit 3T scanner. All data were reconstructed using dcm2niix version v1.0.20250505 conversion software.

Preprocessing

We developed a tissue-segmentation-based pipeline to synthesize T1w images from head CT while preserving the individual anatomical differences. For each subject, we used the T1w image as a reference and rigidly registered it to MNI152 standard space. The registered CT was then resampled to the T1 grid and brain extraction was performed, followed by N4 bias correction within the brain mask. CT intensities were windowed (percentile-based truncation) and normalized, and the T1 brain was similarly normalized. We then applied SynthSR in CT mode to obtain a subject-specific proxy T1 volume. All images were conform-resampled to the SynthSR grid for downstream analyses. 

Segmentation and Target Quantiles

Tissue segmentation was then performed using SynthSeg5 to generate probabilistic posteriors. Posteriors were aggregated into five tissue classes (white matter, grey matter, cerebrospinal fluid, cerebellum, and brainstem). For each class, we computed target intensity quantiles (e.g., 5, 25, 50, 75, 95th percentiles) from the proxy T1 within the brain mask, yielding a per-class quantile dictionary specific to each subject. 

Class Conditional CT to T1 Mapping

Using the normalized CT image, class posteriors, and target quantiles, each tissue class was used to estimate source CT quantiles within a robust subset of voxels (posterior-weighted in-brain region). A piecewise-linear monotone lookup table was then constructed to map CT quantiles to proxy T1 quantiles for that class. The resultant synthetic T1 image was computed as a posterior-weighted mixture of these mapped intensities across classes. To obtain a volume with the skull and extracranial tissue in-tact, we fused the synthetic T1 brain with the original full-head CT, preserving the T1-like contrast while maintaining the true CT signal elsewhere. Zero-valued padding voxels were reassigned to air intensity to avoid artifacts from being rendered around the head. 

MRI Range Intensity Standardization

The resultant synthetic T1 image contained a combination of T1 and CT voxel intensity units. To avoid image rendering errors, we mapped all voxels into an MRI-like intensity range. Using the subject’s normalized T1 and the fused full-head image, we first compute robust brain percentiles within the brain mask in each image. We then estimate a global linear transform that maps the fused brain percentiles onto the corresponding T1 percentiles. This affine mapping was applied to the entire fused volume, and the result is clipped to conventional MRI range [0-1] and scaled to 0-4095 for 16-bit export, yielding a full head synthetic T1 that is compatible with standard MRI-guided visualization and neuronavigation. 

Evaluation

To quantify how closely the synthetic images matched the ground truth, we compared each synthetic image to its paired T1 within the brain mask after intensity normalization to [0,1]. We computed voxelwise mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) to assess the overall agreement between the images. Additionally, we estimated slice-wise structural similarity index (SSIM ; averaged across axial slices) to evaluate how well the structures and tissue contrasts were preserved. 

Results

Across both subjects, the synthetic volumes achieved an average MAE of 0.17 and RMSE of 0.21, indicating moderate voxelwise differences but generally consistent tissue contrast reproduction. The mean slice-wise SSIM of 0.83 suggests that while the synthetic images successfully preserved the main structural and contrast patterns of the underlying T1 images, some deviation from the ground truth remained.  

Visual inspection of absolute error volumes showed the largest deviations clustered along tissue boundaries, particularly at periventricular CSF-WM interfaces and along the edge of the cortical ribbon. These observations are consistent with small residual misregistration and partial-voluming effects. In contrast, deep white matter and subcortical structures maintained minimal error, indicating agreement for a bulk of tissue contrasts where boundaries were less ambiguous. 

 

As proof of principle, we uploaded the original CT, the ground truth T1-MRI image, and the synthesized T1 image on a NBT 2.2 Nexstim neuronavigation system (Nexstim, Finland). Here we show the 3D rendering generated by the neuronavigation system (Figure 2). Original CT images uploaded to the Nextstim system resulted in 3D rendering of the head resembling a cube, with no visible brain structures available in the slice view. The expected visualization of the T1 image would include a full model of the head with special attention to ears and nose rendered for calibration purposes. Therefore, it was critical for the synthesized T1 image to mimic these features. 

Conclusion

We present a CT-to-T1 contrast synthesis method that leverages existing neuroimaging tools combined with a lightweight, class-conditional lookup-table mapping to generate T1 volumes from routine clinical CT scans. A key innovation of this approach is the use of subject-specific proxy T1 from SynthSR to define target tissue intensity distributions. Rather than relying on a fixed atlas or require supervised training on large, paired datasets, this strategy is well adapted to individual subjects, making it particularly well-suited for low-data settings where only a handful of paired samples may be available.
