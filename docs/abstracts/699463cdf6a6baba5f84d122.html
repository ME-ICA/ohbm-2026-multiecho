Introduction

The substantia nigra (SN) is a major dopaminergic nucleus that has been implicated in the pathophysiology of several psychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders.1 Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of magnetization transfer (MT)-based imaging approaches in visualizing neuromelanin-rich areas within the SN, offering a potential window to dopaminergic neuron integrity.1 However, the biophysical mechanisms underlying this contrast remain under debate, limiting its clinical application. Notably, while neuromelanin-rich areas exhibit several MRI-sensitive biophysical properties (e.g., unique relaxivity and susceptibility due to the presence of neuromelanin-bound iron and large dopaminergic neurons), a spatial mismatch has been consistently observed between regions showing high MT-weighted contrast and those with elevated R2*, susceptibility, or R2 values.2 These inconsistencies suggest that multiple microstructural properties with distinct spatial distributions across the SN may contribute to the observed signals.

To advance our understanding of the SN microstructure and its spatially varying impact on MRI contrasts, we acquired quantitative multimodal imaging data to directly compare MT-based neuromelanin contrast-to-noise ratio (MT-CNR) with quantitative metrics of macromolecular proton fraction (MPF) and transverse relaxation rates (R2 and R2*). Combined with a novel profiling approach, we aimed to characterize the spatial gradients of specific biophysical properties across the SN and examine their association with neuromelanin contrast in MT-weighted images.

Methods

Twenty-seven healthy participants (mean age 26.6 [SD 4.8], 17M/10F) were scanned on a Siemens 3T Prisma scanner with a 32-channel head coil. T1w MPRAGE images were obtained for anatomical reference. A quantitative MT (qMT) protocol3 was acquired, including one MT-weighted (TR=29ms, TE=2.43ms, FA-10°, off-resonance saturation pulse duration=12.3ms, FA=560°, offset-frequency=4kHz) and two non-MT-weighted gradient-echo sequences (FA=4°/25°, TR=21ms, TE=2.43ms). All qMT images were acquired with 1.25x1.25x1.3mm3 resolution. A B1+ field map was acquired using turboFLASH B1+ mapping sequence.

To estimate transverse relaxation parameters, we acquired a multi-echo gradient-echo sequence (mGRE; TR=92ms, 14 echoes at TE=1.90-32.98ms, 1.25x1.25x1.3mm3 resolution) and a multi-echo turbo-spin-echo sequence (TSE; TR=11s, 3 echoes at TE=25-108ms, turbo-factor=5, 1.2x1.2x2.5mm3 resolution). 

MPF was fitted from qMT images using in-house developed scripts based on the two-pool model of the MT effect with B1+ and B0 field correction. R2* and R2 were fitted voxel-wise to a mono-exponential function using mGRE and TSE magnitude images. MT-based neuromelanin contrast-to-noise-ratio (MT-CNR) maps were calculated using the qMT protocol MT-weighted image, with the reference cerebral crus region adapted from the JHU atlas. In two subjects, we also acquired high-resolution neuromelanin imaging (0.6x0.6x2mm3 resolution, TR=458ms, TE=2.87ms, FA=40°) to compare the resulting CNR values.

All images used in the analysis were transformed to 1mm3 resolution in individual space and rotated to a true sagittal view with the axial plane perpendicular to the floor of the fourth ventricle (automated using FreeSurfer segmentations, MATLAB, and FSL flirt; Figure 1). For SN segmentation, we used a deep learning tool (DBSegment: https://github.com/LuxImagingAI/DBSegment) for initial localization, followed by fine-tuning of the mask based on the intensity of the MT-CNR image. To characterize spatial variations of MRI metrics within the SN, we performed a profiling analysis along three principal anatomical axes (anterior-posterior, AP; left-right, LR; superior-inferior, SI): for each subject, mean metric values of the left and right SN were extracted along the principal axes, corresponding to the x, y, and z dimensions of the image matrix. To allow averaging across participants, individual profiles were interpolated to 100 equally spaced points.

We used linear models to examine the association between MT-CNR and quantitative MRI metrics. In analyses involving profile values, participant ID was included as a random-effect term to account for the non-independence of profile points from a single participant. The original, non-interpolated profile data were also used to avoid potential artifacts introduced by resampling. All analyses were performed in MATLAB.

Results

As shown in Figure 1, spatial variations of quantitative metric values were seen within the SN mask delineated based on the MT-CNR, resulting in distinct contrast patterns. Specifically, R2* showed hyperintensities in the more anterior, superior, and lateral end of the SN, while MPF values were relatively uniform across the structure, which were consistent with the profile in the lower right panel of Figure 1. 

Linear mixed-effect modeling for metric profiles further revealed an overall negative association between MT-CNR and MPF across all principal axes (indicated by the main effect in Figure 2). In comparison, R2 had significant positive correlations with MT-CNR along the LR and AP directions only, whereas R2* showed no significant association across axes. Interestingly, spatial location along the axes seemed to moderate these relationships, as evidenced by the separation of colors in the scatter plots. To test this effect, a spatial grouping variable was included in the model, separating the profile points based on their proximity to the two ends. Significant spatial interaction effects were found in all but one model, particularly along the SI axis and between R2* and MT-CNR. 

Pearson’s correlation between regional-average MRI metrics across subjects showed that MT-CNR was positively associated with R2 (Pearson’s r=0.30, p=0.03), negatively associated with MPF at a trend level (r=-0.26, p=0.06), and not significantly associated with R2* (r=-0.16, p=0.25).

Conclusion

Spatial variations of MT-CNR within the SN were strongly associated with MPF, supporting previous reports that a lower macromolecular/higher free-water pool fraction is a major contributor to the neuromelanin-MT contrast.4 R2 showed a generally positive association with MT-CNR; however, its spatial gradient more closely resembled that of R2*, which exhibited large variations along anatomical axes. Given the strong dependence of R2* on iron, this pattern may reflect spatial heterogeneity in iron concentration or type of iron-binding compounds (e.g., ferritin vs. neuromelanin) within the SN. 

Our findings also revealed an interesting interaction effect where the association between quantitative MRI metrics and MT-CNR is dependent on spatial location within the SN. This effect is particularly strong with R2*, indicating the presence of distinct local microstructural patterns (e.g., iron concentration, water pool size) driving different relationships between magnetization transfer and susceptibility-based measures. Overall, this observation suggests that the hyperintensity regions in MT-CNR likely cover biologically heterogeneous SN subregions rather than neuromelanin-rich areas alone. This may include both pars compacta and pars reticulata – subnuclei that are known to differ in iron storage mechanisms.5 These results therefore also highlight the potential of multimodal qMRI in delineating microstructurally and functionally distinct SN subregions, which may exhibit differential vulnerability to specific disease processes.
