Introduction

Paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), representing chronically active inflammation, are frequently observed in multiple sclerosis (MS)1. Thalamus atrophy is also known as a sensitive indicator of the progression of neurodegeneration1. Furthermore, recent studies have suggested that impaired brain clearance function in MS may contribute to the persistence of chronic inflammation and subsequent neurodegeneration. 

Diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) has been developed as an imaging marker partially reflecting the brain clearance function of the brain interstitium2. Decreased ALPS index has been reported in MS, suggesting impaired perivascular drainage around medullary veins3. Additionally, recent reports have consistently shown a significant choroid plexus (CP) enlargement from the earliest phases of MS1, associated with higher inflammatory activity, more severe brain atrophy, clinical disability, and cognitive impairment4. The CP volume (CPV) increase in patients with MS likely reflects dysregulated immune cell migration and activation, and the release of proinflammatory factors within the central nervous system. Given that the CP produces approximately 65%–80% of the total cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)5, CP dysfunction may alter CSF secretion, affecting brain fluid dynamics and brain clearance function. 

Despite these converging observations, few studies have comprehensively evaluated the relationship between impaired brain clearance, chronic inflammation, and neurodegeneration in MS. Herein, this study investigated the associations between brain clearance function (the ALPS index and CPV), chronic inflammatory activity (PRLs), and brain atrophy (thalamus volume), in patients with MS.

Methods

The study subjects were 69 MS patients (mean age = 41.6 ± 9.2 years, male/female = 18/51) and 30 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs, mean age = 39.1 ± 10.3 years, male/female = 7/23). All subjects underwent 3T MRI (Vantage Centurian, Canon Medical Systems) to obtain 3D T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), 3D fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion-weighted imaging, and quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM). 

Acquired sequences included (a) sagittal 3D T1-weighted turbo field echo, field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm, matrix = 256 × 256, repetition time (TR) = 5.5 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.5 ms, flip angle = 4°, acquisition time = 8.53 min; (b) sagittal 3D FLAIR, FOV = 256 × 256 mm, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm, 192 slices, matrix = 256 × 256, TR = 4,800 ms, TE = 270 ms, inversion time (TI) = 1,650 msec, echo train length (ETL) = 167, acquisition time = 6.15 min; (c) axial pulsed-gradient spin echo single-shot diffusion-weighted echo planar imaging multi shells at b-value = 700 sec/mm² with 23 motion probing gradient and b = 0 volumes were acquired, FOV = 128 × 128 mm, voxel size = 1.8× 1.8× 1.8 mm, TR = 5,000 ms, TE = 80 ms, flip angle = 90°, and b = 0 volumes with reversed polarity of gradients for distortion correction, acquisition time = 10 min; (d) QSM, FOV = 230× 230, pixel size = 0.60 × 0.60 mm, 135 slices, thickness = 2 mm, matrix = 384 × 382, TR = 39 ms, TEs = 5.5:6:35.5 ms, flip angle = 17°, acquisition time = 6 min; both magnitude and phase images for each echo were saved. 

For the ALPS index calculation, diffusion data were denoised and artifacts removed. A semi-automated pipeline was used to draw 5-mm-diameter regions of interest (ROIs) bilaterally over the projection and association fibers at the corona radiata level (Fig.1a)2. The mean ALPS index was calculated from the left and right ALPS indices. Similarly, mean fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) were calculated as the averaged values across the left and right ROIs and used as covariates. 

T1WI were segmented using FreeSurfer version 6.0 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) to obtain CPV in the lateral ventricles (manually corrected) (Fig.1b), thalamus volume, gray matter volume (GMV), white matter volume (WMV), and intracranial volume (ICV). All volumetric measures were normalized by ICV. The number of PRLs was determined from QSM and 3D FLAIR by consensus between two board-certified neuroradiologists (Fig.1c). 

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0. A general linear model (GLM) was used to compare the ALPS index, CPV, thalamus volume, and GMV between the MS group and the HCs. For the ALPS index, age, sex, ICV, mean FA, and mean MD were used as covariates, while age and sex were used for other indices. Within the MS group, partial correlation analysis was performed between PRLs count, thalamus volume, GMV, WMV, and brain clearance function indices (the ALPS index and CPV) using the same covariates. The false discovery rate method was used to correct for multiple comparisons, and p<0.05 was considered significant. Furthermore, mediation analyses were performed using PROCESS model 4 with MS exposure as the explanatory variable, thalamus volume as the dependent variable, and brain clearance function indices as mediating variables, with the same covariates. Notably, because CPV in the MS group was associated with both thalamus volume and PRLs count, we additionally conducted mediation analyses setting thalamus volume as the dependent variable, with (1) PRLs count as the explanatory variable and CPV as the mediating variable, and (2) CPV as the explanatory variable and PRLs count as the mediating variable. 

Results

The MS group showed significantly lower ALPS index (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.54), thalamus volume (p = 0.02, Cohen's d = 0.56), GMV (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 19.11), and WMV (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 3.33), and significantly higher CPV (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = -0.82) compared with the HCs.

Within the MS group, thalamus volume correlated significantly with the ALPS index (r = 0.36, p = 0.004) and CPV (r = -0.43, p = 0.004). PRLs count correlated significantly with thalamus volume (r = -0.43, p = 0.002), GMV (r = -0.42, p = 0.002), and CPV (r = 0.40, p = 0.002) in the MS group. However, PRLs count didn’t correlate significantly with the ALPS index (r = -0.12, p = 0.37) and WMV (r = -0.20, p = 0.11) in the MS group. Furthermore, the brain clearance indicators (the ALPS index and CPV) partially mediated the thalamus atrophy associated with MS exposure (Fig.2ab). In MS group, CPV and PRLs count mutually mediated each other’s relationship with thalamus volume, suggesting bidirectional interactions between impaired brain clearance and chronic inflammation (Fig.2cd).

Conclusion

In this study, the patients with MS showed impaired brain clearance function (reduced ALPS index, enlarged CPV) alongside widespread brain atrophy, consistent with previous studies1,3,4. Secondly, in patients with MS, thalamus volume was significantly correlated with the ALPS index and CPV. These results may suggest that dysfunction of interstitial and CSF-mediated clearance pathways may contribute to accumulation of waste products, such as inflammatory substances, in the brain, damaging nerve cells and causing thalamic atrophy to progress. Thirdly, PRLs count was significantly correlated with the thalamus volume, CPV, and GMV in patients with MS. The PRLs are defined as chronically active MS lesions with peripheral iron-laden microglia, and release of toxins from these microglia to surrounding oligodendrocytes may limit remyelination in the lesion and further promote the demyelination process. These pathological processes may particularly affect gray matter structures, including the thalamus, which plays a central role in various neural circuits. The CP expansion has been shown to be strongly associated with increased astrocyte and microglial activity4. Activated microglia are a key feature of PRLs, reflecting persistent, chronically active inflammation. Therefore, our findings, consistent with previous studies4, support a shared pathological mechanism linking CP expansion with inflammatory activity in chronic MS lesions. Fourth, both the ALPS index and CPV mediated the association between MS exposure and the thalamus volume in all participants. These findings indicate that thalamus atrophy due to MS may be partly driven by dysfunction in brain clearance pathways. Additionally, in the patients with MS, CPV and PRLs count mutually mediated the association with thalamus volume. These results could suggest that impaired brain clearance system and chronic neuroinflammation exert mutually reinforcing effects on thalamic neurodegeneration.
