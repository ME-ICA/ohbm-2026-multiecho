Introduction

Our work with simultaneous wide-field fluorescent Ca2+ (WF-Ca2+) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has uncovered a range of cross-modal convergent and divergent patterns in spontaneous brain activity measurements1–3. Altogether, our prior investigations have demonstrated that linking WF-Ca2+ and BOLD (blood-oxygen-level dependent) fMRI signals – specifically bridging the gap between fast neuronal calcium dynamics and the slower BOLD signal – remains challenging at the network-level when examining spontaneous (as opposed to evoked) activity patterns. 

To better resolve these neurovascular signals, there are clear gains to be made by the addition of established intrinsic optical oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin measures within our existing multimodal imaging framework4. These measures provide direct access to vascular dynamics that bridge calcium activity and BOLD contrast, offering complementary information about neurovascular coupling beyond either modality alone.

To this end, we have significantly extended the capabilities of our original fMRI-compatible WF-Ca2+ imaging system to include measures of oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin reflectance imaging. We engineered a fully customizable laser diode-based system designed to interleave specific wavelengths for hemodynamic contrast. This novel hardware configuration integrates 530nm and 630nm excitation for intrinsic oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin measurements, respectively, alongside standard 470nm excitation for WF-Ca2+ imaging. Here, we present the hardware developments and proof-of-principle data demonstrating the successful integration of simultaneous multi-contrast optical data acquisition of cortex-wide calcium fluorescence and intrinsic hemodynamic measures, using our modified BOLD fMRI compatible system.

Methods

One C57BL/6J (B6) mouse (N=1) expressing GCaMP6s in excitatory neurons was prepared via neonatal transverse sinus injection of a viral vector at birth (P0-1)5. A second mouse without GCaMP expression was also imaged. At 3 months-of-age, skull-thinning and head-plate implantation for head immobilization and permanent optical access to the cortex was performed as we have described previously (Fig. 1A-C)3. After recovery from the surgical procedures, imaging was performed under light isoflurane anesthesia (~0.5%). We have shown that this preparation is compatible with longitudinal simultaneous WF-Ca2+ and BOLD-fMRI in awake as well as anesthetized mice3.

Custom-built optical fiber components (FOV ≈ 1.4 cm2) enable in-scanner wide-field intrinsic oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin imaging and WF-Ca2+ imaging (in the GCaMP expressing mouse) simultaneously by interleaving the four required wavelengths. The four different colors were delivered using a novel inexpensive laser-diode based system (built in-house) that was controlled by an Arduino-based acquisition controller synchronized with two CMOS cameras. This design is a notably versatile approach that circumvents the need for expensive (and underpowered) light sources while allowing flexible wavelength selection.

The 400nm background and 480nm excitation wavelengths, used for GCaMP fluorescence imaging, were interleaved with the 530nm and 630nm illumination reflectance wavelengths used for detected intrinsic spectral conversion hemodynamic signals. For proof-of-principle data, experiments were performed on the benchtop within our mock-scanner set-up (used for training mice to undergo simultaneous WF-Ca2+ and fMRI without anesthesia). 

Data were acquired using a PCO Camera and Software version 4.19.0. WF-Ca²⁺ fluorescence and oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin reflectance signals were acquired using the following sequence: violet (395nm, GCaMP-insensitive), cyan (470nm, GCaMP-sensitive), green (550nm, isosbestic, blood-volume-sensitive), and red (640nm, deoxy-hemoglobin-sensitive) at 20Hz (Fig. 1D/E)4.

All imaging data were spatially aligned to a standardized cortical template and atlas containing 60 cortical regions-of-interest derived from the Allen Atlas3. WF-Ca2+ imaging data were preprocessed using BioImage Suite (https://bioimagesuiteweb.gith ub.io) and our open-source in-house tools developed for this purpose, as we have described previously2,3. Intrinsic oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin imaging data were preprocessed using the Modified Beer-Lambert Law (MBLL) equation as described by Ma et al. (Fig. 2)4.

Briefly, MBLL converts reflectance changes into hemoglobin concentration changes. Specifically, we utilized the 530nm (isosbestic, sensitive to blood volume) and 630nm (sensitive to deoxy-hemoglobin) wavelengths to solve a system of linear equations: Δμa(λ) = εHbO(λ)Δ[HbO] + εHbR(λ)Δ[HbR]. Here, Δμa(λ) represents the change in absorption coefficient calculated from the log-ratio of reflectance (DR/R), corrected by wavelength-dependent differential pathlength factors (DPF) to account for tissue scattering. Total hemoglobin (Δ[HbT]) was derived as the sum of Δ[HbO] and Δ[HbR].

To characterize neurovascular coupling dynamics, we extracted mean timecourses (T=450 frames) from the cortex (averaging over all 60 ROIs), as well as region-specific timecourses from each ROI. Inter-contrast temporal lag in spontaneous activations as well as spatial patterns in connectivity were examined. BIS was used to compute correlation matrices using both the WF-Ca2+ and oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin imaging data. Matrices were Fisher-Z transformed (Fig. 2I/J).

Results

WF-Ca2+ and intrinsic oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin imaging data were visually inspected and showed the anticipated spatiotemporal patterns associated with spontaneous cortical activity under low-dose isoflurane anesthesia (Fig. 2A). Regional, bilaterally symmetric activity was observed in the ΔF/F (F, fluorescence) WF-Ca2+ and oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin imaging movies (Fig. 2B-E).

Global (whole cortex) and region-specific timecourses showed the anticipated inter-contrast lag between neuronal and vascular signals. Timecourses (t = 48.9s) of total hemoglobin concentration change (Δc[HbT]) and the change in fluorescence (ΔF/F, neural signal) from the whole cortex as well as each ROI were visualized. Representative examples are shown in Fig. 2B-E. Specifically, the Δc[HbT] and ΔF/F at frames 150-250 show an example of the anticipated pattern. Peaks in Δc[HbT] occur approximately 25 frames (or ~2.5 seconds) after peaks in WF-Ca2+ fluorescence (Fig. 2F-G), consistent with established patterns of neurovascular coupling dynamics (Fig. 2A). Notably, hemoglobin signals exhibited broader positive spatial spread than the initiating calcium signals, reflecting both optical scattering effects and the integrative nature of cortical vascular architecture. As a result, temporal separation between Δc[HbT] and ΔF/F was most pronounced in global signals, whereas individual ROIs showed reduced separability, highlighting a limitation in spatial specificity imposed by vascular diffusion.

Functional connectivity matrices revealed the anticipated bilaterally symmetric pattern alongside high correlations between nodes belonging to the same canonical cortical resting state networks (e.g., the default mode network) (Fig. 2H). Similarities, and differences, between the WF-Ca2+ and oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin correlation matrices were observed and warrant further study. For example, WF-Ca2+ networks emphasized anatomically coherent modules, whereas HbT networks seemed to capture spatially diffuse co-fluctuation patterns (Fig. 2I/J). However, despite some differences, there were also notable similarities (as expected) adding to the existing evidence that WF-Ca2+ and WF-HbT signals share common characteristics in spatiotemporal patterning during spontaneous activity. However, it is also important to note that the divergence of these signals is of value and can only be studied through the simultaneous application of multimodal methods.

Altogether, these observations demonstrate that WF-Ca2+ and WF-HbT capture overlapping yet non-identical components of spontaneous brain activity, providing complementary information relevant to interpreting BOLD-fMRI connectivity.

Conclusion

This work sets the stage for the first implementation of cortex-wide simultaneous WF-Ca2+, intrinsic oxy/deoxy-hemoglobin, and BOLD-fMRI. Thus, enabling an in-depth characterization of spontaneous brain activity using multiple complementary sources of contrast. By quantifying both shared and modality-specific connectivity patterns, this framework supports a quantitative evaluation of the relative contributions of neural and vascular factors to resting-state connectivity as measured by the clinically accessible BOLD-fMRI. 

Our prior work has established simultaneous wide-field Ca2+ and fMRI and provided two complementary measures of spontaneous brain activity from across the mouse cortex. Yet, bridging these measures has proven challenging. Here, our findings highlight that calcium and hemodynamic signals from wide-field imaging share common spontaneous components while exhibiting systematic differences in spatial organization and interhemispheric coupling. These distinctions are not limitations, but rather the scientific motivation for multimodal imaging, as they reflect fundamentally different physiological processes underlying functional connectivity.

This framework has important translational implications for interpreting fMRI connectivity in both animal models and humans, particularly in conditions such as aging and neurodegeneration (e.g. Alzheimer’s disease), where vascular dysfunction may confound neural inferences. By anchoring BOLD-fMRI signals to invasive optical measures, this work advances our collective understanding of neurovascular coupling and the imaging measures we use for evaluating health and disease.
