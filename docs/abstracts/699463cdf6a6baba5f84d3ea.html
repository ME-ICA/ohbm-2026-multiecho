Introduction

The link between structural and functional brain connectivity changes over the human lifespan. In previous work, we presented the Krakencoder, a joint connectome mapping tool that bidirectionally translates between structural (SC) and functional connectivity (FC), as well as across atlases and processing choices, via a common low-dimensional latent representation1. We showed that this model, trained on young adults from HCP-YA, can capture much of the variability present in other age groups, but it may not capture key changes that occur during development or aging. 

Here, we train a new model on data from healthy subjects aged 8-100, combining multiple HCP-related studies using different MRI acquisition approaches. Combining data from multiple sites and studies creates unique challenges, particularly when there is no or minimal demographic overlap between subjects in different studies or sites. Naively removing site and study information from the latent representation through adversarial learning may inadvertently remove information about subject age that we wish to preserve. We consider both adversarial learning and age-conditioned adversarial learning to determine which best harmonizes the dataset.

Methods

We compiled a multi-study cohort that combines healthy subjects from HCP-YA (22-37 n=958), HCP-Lifespan (8-21 n=532, 36-100+ n=659), and healthy young adults (21-36 n=95) from three studies from the Connectomes Related to Human Disease (CRHD: HCP-EP, PDC, DCAM). The HCP-YA and HCP-Lifespan have substantially different acquisition techniques, and non-overlapping age ranges. The CRHD healthy controls use the same fMRI and dMRI acquisition parameters as HCP-Lifespan, and match the age range of HCP-YA. This yields a combined cohort of 2244 healthy subjects spanning a broad age range, with sufficient overlap in age and site to address site and study differences. (See Figure 1a).

To harmonize the Krakencoder's latent representation across the multiple sites and studies in the combined dataset, we added two additional decoders to our model. Decodersite and Decoderstudy are trained to predict site and study from the 128-dimensional latent space, minimizing cross-entropy loss for these categorical predictions. Adding a gradient reversal layer to Decodersite and Decoderstudy makes this an adversarial learning problem, where each decoder is trained to predict site or study, but the gradient signs are inverted when back-propagating to the connectivity encoders. In this way, the encoders are trained to produce a latent representation from which site and study are difficult to predict2.

Because age and study covary in this dataset, naively reducing study predictability risks removing features related to age. To examine this possibility, we trained three expanded Krakencoder architectures: 1) DomainAdv adding only site and study adversarial decoders 2) DomainAdv+demo adding site and study adversarial decoders, as well as age and sex decoders, and 3) DomainAdvcond+demo, a conditional adversarial model adding site and study decoders, as well as age and sex decoders, but site and study decoders are trained to predict output labels from a combined input concatenating the 128-dimensional latent space and the subject’s true age3. Figure 1b shows a schematic of the DomainAdv+demo and DomainAdvcond+demo architecture. We trained 4 models for each architecture, with loss term weights on the demographic predictions ranging from 1-1000. Each model was trained for 2000 epochs using a subset of 1634 subjects, validated on 160 subjects during training, and evaluated on a held-out set of 450 subjects.

Results

After training each model, we computed latent vectors for our validation subjects and fit cross-validated linear models to predict site (ridge classifier), study (ridge classifier), and age (kernel ridge regression), to assess how predictable each variable is from the latent space. We found that the DomainAdvcond+demo model trained with equal loss weights of 100 for site, study, age, and sex decoders, achieved the best overall balance of removing site and study information while preserving age (See Figure 2a). For the final model, we compute the latent vectors for the held-out evaluation subjects and fit new demographic predictions.

Comparing the connectome prediction performance for the “conn only” model with “DomainAdvcond+demo”, we see that performance is still good, though there is some reduction when suppressing site and study information in the latent representation. In Figure 2b, we see that connectome identifiability for within-modality predictions (fusionFC→FC and fusionSC→SC) is not affected, but that inter-modality identifiability (fusionFC→SC and fusionSC→FC) is reduced by an average of 1.3%. In Figure 2c, reconstruction accuracy (correlation between predicted and measured connectomes, after subtracting population mean) is reduced by a greater amount, with an average reduction of 14%. 

Conclusion

We demonstrate that a conditional adversarial extension of the Krakencoder can effectively harmonize a newly assembled multi-site, multi-study lifespan connectome dataset while preserving age-related variability. Suppressing site and study information leads to a reduction in connectome prediction correlations, as expected given the substantial domain shift across acquisition protocols and cohorts, reflecting the removal of study-specific variability during harmonization. The near-negligible impact on prediction identifiability indicates that subject-specific, biologically meaningful variance is largely retained. Together, these results show that conditional adversarial harmonization provides a principled balance between reducing unwanted site effects and maintaining the core information necessary for cross-modal and lifespan connectome modeling. This approach yields a unified model trained on a large, diverse age range that is well suited for studying brain connectivity across development and aging.
