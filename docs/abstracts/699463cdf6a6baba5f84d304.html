Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common neurodegenerative disorder, characterized by extracellular amyloid beta accumulation and intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau-related neurofibrillary tangles, ultimately leading to synaptic dysfunction, neuronal loss, and cognitive decline. Synaptic dysfunction leads to changes in the brain’s functional architecture that can be measured by resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI). Traditional measures like functional connectivity (FC) show reduced network efficiency and disrupted connectivity in AD patients, particularly in the default mode network (DMN). However, FC ignores short-time-scale functional dynamics, which could be captured by more sensitive and accurate measures such as the co-activation patterns (CAPs) that enable the investigation of brain dynamics at single time-frame resolution. CAPs have shown promise to accurately classify rodent models of AD1 and other neurodegenerative disorders2 from healthy controls and, more importantly, identify early alterations. The European prevention of Alzheimer’s disease (EPAD) study is a large multicentre cohort study that includes neuroimaging data, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers, and neuropsychological assessment (clinical dementia rating (CDR)) from 1500 non-demented participants3. Individuals with preclinical or prodromal AD demonstrated CSF Aβ42 levels < 1000 pg/mL and were termed A+. A- individuals with a CDR of 0 were termed as healthy individuals4. In our study, we aimed to use RS-CAPs to characterize brain functional changes in A+ (irrespective of T status) individuals from the EPAD database.

Methods

We used rs-fMRI data acquired from 74 healthy (mean age: 63.8, 31% male) and 67 A+ individuals (mean age: 67.7, 49 %male) using Siemens 3T scanner at a TR of 1.84s for this study. Raw images were preprocessed using geometric distortion correction, motion correction, spatial smoothing (FWHM = 4mm) and high-pass temporal filtering at 0.001Hz5. Subject-level rs-fMRI scans were registered to the 3D T1-weighted images and resampled to MNI space. Pre-processed data were parcellated into 132 regions of interest (ROIs) covering the whole brain. Next, we concatenated these ROI-based data from healthy controls and clustered the timeframes with K-means++ clustering using correlation distance for a range of clusters between 2 and 30. The number of clusters for which the explained variance saturated was taken to be optimal. Group-level CAPs were obtained by ROI-wise averaging of the z-scored BOLD signals across all timeframes belonging to the same cluster. These group-level CAPs from healthy controls were then projected onto every timeframe of each A+ individual so that each timeframe was labelled with the label of the group-level CAP of healthy controls with which it showed the highest correlation (Figure 1). Group-level A+ CAPs were obtained by ROI-wise averaging across all timeframes with the same label. Subsequently, we obtained the duration and occurrence percentage of each CAP in each subject as well as average ROI-level activation across all occurrences of a CAP for both healthy controls and A+ individuals. These properties were statistically compared between the two groups using two-tailed, two-sample T-tests corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control for false discovery rate (FDR). 

Results

We found six CAPs optimally explained approximately 63% of the variance across all healthy participants. CAPs 1 (Figure 1A, top panels) and 2 showed activation and deactivation, respectively, of the DMN. CAPs 3 and 4, respectively, showed the activation and deactivation of the visual networks, while CAPs 5 (Figure 1B, top panels) and 6 corresponded with the activation and deactivation of the limbic system, consisting of the amygdala and the hippocampus. Inter-group comparisons of CAP properties showed significantly lower (p < 0.05, FDR corrected) activation in posterior cingulate and medial prefrontal cortex, as well as precuneus, in the DMN CAP in the A+ individuals compared to healthy controls (Figure 1A, bottom panel). On the other hand, both CAP 3 and 5, associated with visual and limbic systems, respectively, showed significantly higher (p < 0.05, FDR corrected) activations in the A+ individuals (Figure 1B, bottom panel for CAP 5). The occurrence percentages of the DMN-associated CAP and visual-cortex-associated CAP were significantly lower and higher, respectively, in the A+ individuals compared to healthy controls.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate early alterations in specific transient, short-timescale brain states in amyloid-positive individuals. While hypoactivation was observed in the DMN-associated CAP, visual and limbic system-associated CAPs demonstrated hyperactivation. Both hypo- and hyper-functional connectivity have been reported as early changes across the spectrum of preclinical to symptomatic AD. Our findings indicate that this dichotomy is reflected in the differential directionality of alterations in specific dynamic brain states that make up the static functional connectivity. They underline the potential of CAPs to detect and offer novel insights into early preclinical AD-related changes in the brain’s functional architecture.
