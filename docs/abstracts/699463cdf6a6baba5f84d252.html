Introduction

Multimodal brain imaging measurements have shown widespread genetic and phenotypic correlations with neurodegenerative diseases and other brain-related traits.1 However, the overall contribution of multimodal brain imaging measurements to the genetic architecture of these diseases/traits remains unclear, as existing studies have focused on pairwise imaging-disease correlations. Quantifying the overall contribution of genetically shared brain imaging components to disease heritability is essential for understanding disease etiology, identifying relevant neural systems, and prioritizing imaging-derived targets for therapeutic development.

Methods

We applied PHBC (pleiotropic heritability with bias correction)2 to estimate the pleiotropic shared heritability (h2pleio) of a target disease with brain imaging measurements from three modalities. h2pleio of a target disease quantifies the joint contribution of a set of brain imaging measurements to the heritability of the disease, while accounting for correlations among the imaging phenotypes and correcting for biases arising from finite sample size. PHBC estimates h2pleio from GWAS summary statistics by estimating the proportion of variance explained from the genetic correlation matrix between the target disease and auxiliary traits, employing a Monte-Carlo bias correction procedure to obtain unbiased estimates. We analyzed target diseases and brain-related traits and auxiliary brain imaging traits with Z-score > 6 for nonzero SNP-heritability (h2). To avoid unstable estimates caused by collinearity among auxiliary brain imaging traits, we restricted to the auxiliary trait with the higher heritability Z-score whenever two auxiliary traits exhibited high genetic correlation (rg2>0.5).

Results

We analyzed GWAS summary statistics for 29 target neurodegenerative diseases and brain-related traits (average N=361K, average h2 = 0.14) and 3,935 brain imaging measurements from the UK Biobank (including sMRI, dMRI, fMRI; average N=33K). After h2  and rg2 restrictions, a total of 136 sMRI T1 traits, 72 dMRI traits, and 88 fMRI traits were retained as auxiliary traits (average h2 = 0.20). 

We determined that brain imaging measurements explained a significant proportion of disease heritability. When analyzing each brain imaging modality in turn, fMRI explained the largest h2pleio/h2 among the three modalities (avg = 6.4%, jackknife s.e.(avg) = 0.7% for the same 10 diseases), while dMRI and sMRI explained slightly lower h2pleio/h2 (4.7%, jackknife s.e.(avg) = 0.6% and 4.4%, jackknife s.e.(avg) = 0.4%), respectively). 

h2pleio/h2 w.r.t brain imaging phenotypes varied substantially across target diseases/traits. Neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer's disease, stroke, Parkinson’s disease and multiple sclerosis) consistently exhibited high h2pleio/h2 in all three modalities (11% (s.e. 3.5%) for fMRI, 11% (s.e. 2.7%) for dMRI and 7.1% (s.e. 1.2%) for sMRI). Notably, several estimates in this category exceeded 10% in more than two modalities, e.g. stroke reached 22% (s.e. 5.7%) and 14% (s.e. 3.9%) explained by dMRI and sMRI; Alzheimer’s disease reached 27% (s.e. 17%) and 15% (s.e. 7.1%) explained by fMRI and dMRI. The specific auxiliary brain-imaging traits explaining pleiotropic shared heritability of target diseases provide insight into the disease-relevant neurobiological mechanisms. From the results of all three modalities, Alzheimer’s disease is predominantly explained by limbic network and default-mode network (DMN), involving hippocampus and medial temporal cortical regions such as the entorhinal and parahippocampal cortices, accompanied by widespread memory-related white-matter pathways. In contrast, stroke shows a broader involvement of subcortical structures (thalamus, amygdala, putamen), projection white-matter tracts and thalamocortical white-matter tracts, and functional connectivity within sensorimotor and visual networks, consistent with vascular-territory–driven damage. These patterns were consistent with prior neuroimaging studies of Alzheimer’s disease and stroke.3,4 

Varying proportions of target disease heritability were captured by different brain imaging modalities. For example, cognitive traits (intelligence, reaction time and years of education) had the highest h2pleio/h2 of 9.7% (s.e. 2.0%) explained by fMRI, compared to dMRI (4.2%, s.e. = 0.6%) and sMRI (3.8%, s.e. = 0.7%). Psychiatric diseases exhibited moderately high h2pleio/h2 w.r.t. fMRI (7.0%, s.e. = 1.0%), compared to dMRI (4.8%, s.e. = 0.8%) and sMRI (3.0%, s.e. = 0.4%). This modality-specific contribution of fMRI traits in capturing the shared heritability of psychiatric disorders are consistent with previous studies.5 Several psychiatric diseases also exhibited fairly high h2pleio/h2 above 15%, e.g. anorexia nervosa had h2pleio/h2 of 21% (s.e. 8.6%) explained by fMRI; autism had h2pleio/h2 of 17% (s.e. 7.5%) and 15% (s.e. 4.9%) explained by dMRI and fMRI. When interpreted the fMRI results by the resting-state functional network parcellation, h2pleio/h2 of autism is predominantly captured by sensory network, DMN, and frontoparietal control network, which are involved in self-referential processing, social cognition, sensory integration, and top-down cognitive control. In contrast, h2pleio/h2 of anorexia nervosa is more selectively captured by DMN and salience network, associated with interoceptive awareness and cognitive control over reward-related processing, which are consistent with known biology.6

Conclusion

We have determined that a substantial proportion of the heritability of neurodegenerative diseases and brain-related traits is explained by multimodal brain-imaging measurements spanning three modalities. All three modalities explained a significant proportion of neurodegenerative disease heritability, while we observed modality-specific contributions for other disease categories.
