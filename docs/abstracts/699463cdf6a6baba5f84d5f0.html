Introduction

The organization of the human cerebral cortex can be characterized along a unimodal-to-transmodal hierarchy, describing the pathway of information processing from primary to association regions. Diverse structural and functional properties of the cortex are patterned along this hierarchy, such as myelination, cortical folding, neurodevelopment, and fMRI connectivity1. However, the dominant axes of cortical geometry deviate from this hierarchy. When estimated from an eigendecomposition of a cortical surface mesh, cortical geometry is instead organized along a set of dominant rostrocaudal, mediolateral, and dorsoventral axes, called eigenmodes. This divergence is surprising, as neural field theory, a well-validated framework of modelling macroscale brain dynamics, predicts features like hierarchical organization are fundamentally constrained by underlying cortical geometry2. Subsequent empirical work has further supported these predictions, modelling diverse task-evoked brain function from geometric eigenmodes3. However, if geometric eigenmodes do not reflect the unimodal-to-transmodal hierarchy, how can they accurately predict brain function? One possible explanation may lie with unimodal sensory function; the rostrocaudal, mediolateral, and dorsoventral eigenmodes are each anchored by primary visual, somatosensory, and auditory areas. Recent work found the union of seed-based functional connectivity from these primary areas can reconstruct the unimodal-to-transmodal hierarchy4, suggesting hierarchical organization is driven by converging sensory integration. Thus, while geometric eigenmodes do not reflect unimodal-to-transmodal axes, they may capture specific domains of unimodal sensory function, which in turn facilitate macroscale hierarchical organization. Here, we hypothesized that hierarchical unimodal-to-transmodal organization can be reconstructed from geometric eigenmodes, when used as a proxy measure of unimodal function via sensory integration mapping.

Methods

Using MRI data from the Human Connectome Project, we tested whether geometric eigenmodes encode sensory function by modelling sensory integration directly from cortical geometry. Originally applied in fMRI4, the sensory integration model first extracts spatially averaged timeseries from three unimodal seed regions: the primary visual cortex (V1), the primary somatosensory cortex (S1), and the primary auditory cortex (A1), defined according to the Glasser MMP parcellation. The sensory integration model then predicts whole-brain fMRI timeseries through a general linear model using sensory timeseries as predictors. The beta coefficients of the linear model thus reflect the seed-based sensory functional connectivity from primary visual, somatosensory, and auditory areas to all other brain regions. Sensory specialization and integration can thus be estimated by determining which sensory domain a given brain region is most strongly connected to. This is achieved within a circular polar space where each primary region is mapped to three equidistant angles (e.g., 0°, 120°, 240°), forming the initial coordinate framework. Based on the ratio of sensory functional connectivity, regions with a preferential affinity towards a single specific modality possess an angle closer to that particular sensory pole (e.g., 0°, 120°, 240° for visual, somatosensory, and auditory seeds). In contrast, regions involved in multisensory integration possess an intermediary angle, positioned somewhere between these pre-defined poles (e.g., 60°, 180°, 300°). 

Although sensory angle differentiates between visual, somatosensory, and auditory preferences, it cannot quantify the extent to which any specific modality is expressed. Accordingly, the sensory integration model introduces a secondary dimension, sensory magnitude. While sensory angle is determined from the ratio of sensory functional connectivity maps individually, sensory magnitude is calculated from the sum of all three sensory functional connectivity maps. These sums are ranked and rescaled from 0-1, such that areas well explained by primary sensory function possess higher sensory magnitude, regardless of modality. In contrast, regions with lower sensory functional connectivity, such as transmodal association areas, possess lower magnitude.

Thus, sensory integration maps create a two-dimensional framework, using sensory angle to characterize multisensory function across three primary domains and sensory magnitude to contextualize this function through a unimodal-to-transmodal hierarchical lens (Fig. 1). 

The three sensory functional connectivity maps characterize how well explained a given cortical area is by a particular primary modality. However, each of the first three non-constant geometric eigenmodes exhibit similar peaks within one of V1, S1, and A1 respectively, causing a qualitative similarity to sensory functional connectivity. Specific features of unimodal function are likely captured by these dominant geometric modes. To test this assumption, we created a geometric sensory integration mapping by substituting sensory functional connectivity with the rostrocaudal, mediolateral, and dorsoventral geometric eigenmodes. All eigenmodes were calculated by constructing the Laplace-Beltrami operator from a downsampled, left–right symmetric version of the FreeSurfer’s fsaverage population-averaged template surface mesh and solving the corresponding eigenvalue problem. 

To measure the accuracy of our geometric model, we compared it against a reference model using sensory functional connectivity from 167 subjects from the Human Connectome Project (mean age = 29.4 years, 101 females, 66 males). Model accuracy was quantified as the mean difference in angle and magnitude between geometric and functional models. To ensure robustness, we applied nonparametric permutation testing via Monte-Carlo simulations, using 10,000 permutations of randomly spun maps from the geometric model to form a null distribution.

Results

Sensory functional connectivity was highly correlated with the first three geometric eigenmodes, which reflected rostrocaudal, mediolateral, and dorsoventral patterns respectively. Each eigenmode corresponded to a distinct sensory domain (Fig. 2A; rostrocaudal-visual: r = 0.52; mediolateral-somatosensory: r = 0.57; dorsoventral-auditory: r = 0.35). When these features were combined through the geometric sensory integration model, the sensory angle of the resulting model accurately delineated between primary areas of the cortex, with the visual, somatosensory, and auditory cortex labelled as separate zones (Fig. 2B; in red, green, and blue respectively). This geometric organization closely aligned with the functional model, where these areas were similarly separated. The mean angular difference between the two models was small (δ = 69.74), with post-hoc permutation spin testing using 10,000 rotations indicating this result was statistically significant (d = 132.63; p < .001). Notably, there was a pronounced negative relationship between angular difference and sensory magnitude (Fig. 2C). The greatest angular differences were found in the association cortex, as transmodal areas diverged the most between models.

As differences between geometric and functional models were largest within the transmodal association cortex, additional eigenmodes were required to better characterize transmodal regions and reproduce the unimodal-to-transmodal hierarchy (Fig. 2D). Incorporating additional eigenmodes provided marked improvements in model performance, with an increased r2 = 0.64 by 16 modes, reflecting the first three eigengroups, alongside the zeroth non-constant mode. Estimates of magnitude based upon the first 16 modes also demonstrated multiple hierarchical features absent in fewer eigenmodes, such as a pronounced DMN and the inclusion of the angular gyrus at the transmodal apex.

Conclusion

By substituting sensory functional connectivity with compact measures of cortical geometry, we created an accurate model of multisensory integration across visual, somatosensory, and auditory domains. Individually, each sensory domain was represented by a specific eigenmode of cortical geometry, where each primary region occupied an extreme position along a corresponding eigenmode. Specifically, the rostrocaudal mode characterized visual function, the mediolateral mode characterized somatosensory function, and the dorsoventral mode characterized auditory function. Together, the union of these three eigenmodes created an angular space that accurately mapped unimodal function and whole-brain multisensory integration. However, the differentiation of transmodal from unimodal features required additional input from successive eigenmodes beyond the first three modes alone. Together, these results suggest that macroscale integration of sensory stimuli may be constrained by cortical geometry, as the dominant modes of cortical geometry best characterize unimodal function.
