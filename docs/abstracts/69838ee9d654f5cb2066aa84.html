Introduction

Cochlear implants (CIs) restore access to sound in individuals with severe-to-profound sensorineural hearing loss, yet many CI users continue to experience substantial difficulty understanding speech in background noise despite adequate aided audibility. This persistent “speech‑in‑noise” deficit likely reflects not only limitations of the peripheral device, but also reorganization of large‑scale cortical networks supporting auditory, attentional, and cognitive control processes. Characterizing how these networks are altered in CI users, and how they adapt under different signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) conditions, is therefore essential for explaining variability in outcomes and for guiding targeted rehabilitation strategies.

Network neuroscience addresses this question by modeling the brain as a graph, with nodes representing cortical regions and edges representing functional connectivity. Healthy brain networks typically show small world organization and rich mesoscale structure, whereas clinical populations often exhibit disrupted integration and segregation. However, constructing such graphs from EEG or MEG is challenging, connectivity estimates are dense and noisy, and graph properties are highly sensitive to arbitrary threshold choices. Poorly chosen thresholds can either obscure genuine differences or introduce spurious network effects, particularly in between‑condition comparisons.

Topological data analysis (TDA) provides a complementary perspective by quantifying higher order features that conventional graph metrics do not capture. Embedding graph theoretic and topological constraints into the thresholding step enables data driven, biologically plausible selection of edge density that favors networks with canonical brain like organization. Here, we propose an integrated framework that combines topology constrained dynamic network construction with topology informed deep learning in CI users performing a speech in noise task. 

Methods

Participants

Fifty three CI users (male/female: 28/25; 64 years (range: 27–77 years)) were enrolled in this study, and a within-subjects design was employed across two experimental conditions (high-SNR/low-SNR). CI configurations (bilateral (12), bimodal (31), unilateral (8)) were documented. Pre-implant deafness and CI experience averaged 30.7 and 6.7 years, respectively.

ITCP

Speech perception in noise was assessed with the Iowa Test of Consonant Perception (ITCP) 1, a closed-set four-alternative forced-choice task on word-initial consonants in 120 consonant–vowel–consonant (CVC) words. Each trial began with multi-talker babble; the target word was presented 1 s after noise onset. Two SNRs were tested: +15 dB (high-SNR) and +7.5 dB (low-SNR), implemented by varying noise level while holding target level constant.

EEG  Pre-processing 

EEG was recorded with a 64-channel ActiveTwo BioSemi system using an extended 10–20 montage. Preprocessing in MATLAB R2021b included downsampling to 512 Hz, 1–40 Hz band-pass filtering, 60 Hz line-noise removal with the CleanLine EEGLAB plugin 2, artifact attenuation using independent component analysis 3, and re-referencing with the reference electrode standardization technique (REST) 4. Continuous data were segmented from 100 ms before noise onset to 1500 ms after target onset, and epochs with amplitudes exceeding ±75 μV were rejected.

Source reconstruction

Source activity was estimated with a depth-weighted L2-norm minimum-norm solution implemented in Brainstorm 5. Cortical current density was reconstructed on a 15,002-vertex cortical grid and averaged within 31 predefined functional regions of interest (ROIs) 6. Each epoch was represented as X ∈ RN✖T✖E, where N is the number of ROIs, T the number of samples per 2.5s epoch (fs =512Hz), and E the number of artifact-free trials.

Graph construction and data‑driven density selection

For each subject, frequency band, and condition, functional connectivity was computed as the debiased weighted phase lag index (wPLI) between all ROI pairs in 0.5 s windows within each 3.5 s epoch, yielding a sequence of connectivity matrices W(t). To make graphs comparable and suppress weak edges, we applied proportional thresholding at a subject- and band-specific edge density. Candidate densities were d ∈ {0.05, 0.10,…, 0.30}. For each d, we retained the strongest fraction d of edges in every window and set all remaining weights to zero, obtaining a family of sparse weighted adjacency matrices {Ad.(t)}. For each density we computed (i) small-worldness of each graphs σ(d), defined relative to degree-preserving random graphs 7, and (ii) H1 total persistence TP1(d),a 1D persistent homology metric 8, following evidence that brain networks are both small-world and topologically rich 9. Both metrics were z-scored across densities and averaged to yield S(d). The density d* was then used as a data‑driven hyperparameter for that subject and band. Importantly, d* does not depend on the condition label, so the same d* is applied to all windows and both conditions for that subject, avoiding circularity or information leakage in subsequent deep learning analyses.

Topology‑Informed Augmentation, Training, and Evaluation

We implemented a spatio-temporal graph attention network (ST-GAT) consisting of a temporal encoder that summarizes within-channel dynamics and a spatial encoder that models inter-channel dependencies via attention-based message passing. To assess whether topology-informed augmentation improves generalization, we implemented a training-only augmentation pipeline based on Common Orthogonal Basis Extraction. Training and evaluation used a stratified 5-fold cross-validation scheme with a nested split to maintain a train(60%), validation(20%), test(20%) ratio per fold. Performance was summarized using accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Then, we used gradient-based saliency to quantify how each frequency band and ROI contributed to the predictions.

Results

Classification performance without augmentation

We first evaluated the baseline ST-GAT model trained on the original (non-augmented) dataset. Across the five stratified cross-validation folds, the model achieved a mean test accuracy of 0.745 ± 0.094, indicating that dynamic source-level graphs contained discriminative information about listening condition (high vs low SNR). Mean sensitivity was 0.727 ± 0.251, and mean specificity was 0.764 ± 0.261, reflecting moderate but relatively unstable detection of both high-SNR and low-SNR trials across folds. The large standard deviations, particularly for sensitivity and specificity, suggest substantial dependence on the specific train–test split, consistent with limited sample size and the high capacity of the ST-GAT model.

Effect of topology-informed augmentation

We then introduced topology-informed augmentation based on COBE, applied only to the training data. Augmentation strength was parameterized by the number of augmented replicas per original trial (0–4), corresponding to effective training set sizes of 1×, 2×, 3×, 4×, and 5×.

With 2× augmentation, mean test accuracy increased modestly to 0.764 ± 0.093. Specificity improved to 0.800 ± 0.089, whereas sensitivity remained stable a 0.727 ± 0.223, indicating that augmentation primarily reduced false positives (i.e., mislabeling low-SNR trials as high-SNR) without compromising sensitivity.

With 3× augmentation, accuracy remained at 0.764 ± 0.097. Sensitivity decreased slightly to 0.709 ± 0.210, whereas specificity increased further to 0.818 ± 0.191. This pattern suggests a bias towards more conservative classification of the positive class, with better rejection of negatives but mildly reduced detection of positives.

The best overall performance was obtained with 4× augmentation. At this setting, mean test accuracy reached 0.800 ± 0.094, representing the highest accuracy across all augmentation levels. Sensitivity improved to 0.764 ± 0.187, and specificity reached 0.836 ± 0.036. Notably, the standard deviation of specificity decreased substantially compared with lower augmentation levels, indicating more stable true-negative performance across cross-validation folds when moderate augmentation was applied.

Further increasing augmentation to 5× did not yield additional gains. Mean accuracy dropped back to 0.764 ± 0.105, and sensitivity decreased to 0.691 ± 0.123, while specificity remained high at 0.836 ± 0.106. The increase in variability at this highest augmentation level suggests that excessive replication may introduce undesirable shifts in the training distribution or amplify idiosyncratic structure.

Gradient-based saliency–derived importance indicated that, among the six frequency bands, low alpha contributed 25.05% of the overall importance, and among the 31 ROIs, the right angular gyrus and left posterior middle frontal gyrus showed the highest contributions at 4.08% and 3.94%, respectively.

Summary of augmentation effects

Overall, the results reveal a non-monotonic relationship between augmentation strength and generalization. Moderate topology-informed augmentation (particularly 4×) improved both accuracy and stability relative to the non-augmented baseline, whereas excessive augmentation (5×) did not provide additional benefits and even degraded sensitivity. These findings support the view that COBE-based augmentation can regularize ST-GAT training when applied in an appropriate regime, leveraging shared topological structure while avoiding over-distortion of the original data manifold.

Conclusion

This study introduces a unified framework that links topology‑constrained graph construction with topology‑informed deep learning to model dynamic cortical networks in CI users listening to speech in noise. We combined (i) source‑level EEG functional connectivity, (ii) data‑driven edge‑density selection guided jointly by small‑worldness and persistent homology, and (iii) a spatio‑temporal graph attention network trained with topology‑informed augmentation based on COBE. This pipeline enables reliable within‑subject classification of high‑ versus low‑SNR trials and that moderate topology‑informed augmentation yields non‑trivial gains in accuracy and stability.

A first key contribution is the density selection strategy. Rather than imposing an ad hoc proportional threshold, we treated graph density as a subject‑ and band‑specific hyperparameter and chose the value that maximized a composite score combining classical small‑worldness and H₁ total persistence. The resulting graphs occupy a regime where clustering, path length, and loop structure are jointly enhanced, in line with canonical properties of large‑scale brain networks. This procedure avoids circularity and minimizes information leakage into downstream classification and potential future GNN applications.

A second contribution is the explicit use of topological structure for data augmentation. COBE exploits shared subspaces across trials while preserving condition‑specific variability, which is particularly appropriate for EEG‑derived graphs in which both global topology and local fluctuations are behaviorally meaningful. We observed a non‑monotonic relationship between augmentation strength and generalization. This pattern is consistent with an effective regularization regime in which the model is exposed to a richer but still realistic ensemble of spatio‑temporal graphs, without over‑distorting the underlying data manifold.

Gradient‑based saliency analyses further suggest that the learned decision function is neurobiologically interpretable. Low‑alpha activity contributed the largest share of spectral importance, consistent with the role of alpha‑band dynamics in attentional gating and task engagement. At the regional level, the right angular gyrus and left posterior middle frontal gyrus emerged as the most influential ROIs. The angular gyrus is a key hub of the default‑mode network that flexibly toggles between internally oriented and externally driven processing, and its prominence here is compatible with increased demands on integrating contextual and mnemonic information under noisy listening 10. The left posterior middle frontal gyrus is a core node of the fronto‑parietal control network, supporting top‑down attentional control and working memory 11,12 its elevated importance aligns with the need for sustained cognitive control during challenging speech‑in‑noise perception in CI users 13.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although our sample is relatively large for an EEG study in CI users, it remains modest for training deep GNN architectures and warrants replication in independent cohorts. We focused on a single connectivity estimator (wPLI) and a specific ST‑GAT design; alternative FC measures, priors on density, or temporal encoders may reveal complementary aspects of network dynamics.

Future work should extend this framework in several directions. First, relating topological and graph‑theoretic descriptors to individual differences in speech‑in‑noise performance and clinical variables could help explain heterogeneity in CI outcomes. Second, topology‑informed augmentation could be evaluated in other modalities and patient populations to test its generality as a regularization strategy for neuroimaging‑based GNNs. Together, these directions may ultimately link topology‑aware graph learning more directly to mechanistic models of cortical adaptation and to clinically actionable markers in cochlear implant rehabilitation.
