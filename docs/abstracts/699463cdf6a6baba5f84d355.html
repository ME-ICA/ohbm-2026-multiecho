Introduction

Childhood maltreatment (CM) is a pervasive environmental risk factor that substantially increases vulnerability to a broad spectrum of psychiatric disorders, including major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia spectrum disorders.1 Structural and functional neuroimaging studies have consistently linked CM to brain network alterations,2 yet it remains unclear whether these changes primarily reflect disorder-specific mechanisms or a shared, transdiagnostic neurobiological consequence of early adversity. Connectomics offers a powerful framework to characterize whole-brain network organization, allowing examination of how CM relates to global and regional patterns of structural connectivity across diagnostic categories. This study tested the hypothesis that CM is associated with distinct alterations in structural brain networks across affective and psychotic disorders as well as healthy individuals, thereby constituting a transdiagnostic connectomic signature of early-life stress. Clarifying such shared network-level correlates of CM may inform mechanistic models of how adversity becomes embedded in brain architecture across traditional diagnostic boundaries.

Methods

Data were drawn from the Marburg-Münster Affective Disorders Cohort Study,3 comprising N=2,011 adults aged 18–65 years including patients with major depressive disorder (MDD, n=827), bipolar disorder (BD, n=134), schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SZ, n=118), and healthy controls (HC, n=932) with harmonized clinical and imaging protocols. All participants underwent high-resolution T1-weighted structural MRI and diffusion-weighted imaging on 3T scanners with standardized acquisition parameters. Structural brain networks were reconstructed from structural and diffusion-weighted MRI using deterministic tractography based on the Cammoun subdivision of FreeSurfer’s Desikan Killiany atlas as implemented in the CATO toolbox,4 yielding weighted connectivity matrices. Graph theoretical metrics were computed from these connectivity matrices, including number of connections, normalized and non-normalized network efficiency, and normalized clustering coefficient as indices of network integration and segregation. CM was assessed with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) using established clinical cut-offs to classify participants into CM versus no-CM groups. Group-level analyses applied analyses of covariance to test main effects of CM, diagnostic group, and CM-by-diagnosis interactions on global network metrics, controlling for age, sex, and scanner site; false discovery rate (FDR) correction was used for multiple comparisons. To localize connectomic alterations, network-based statistic (NBS5) analyses identified subnetworks differing between CM and no-CM groups while controlling for diagnosis and confounds, with permutation-based family-wise error control.

Results

Across the full transdiagnostic sample, individuals with a history of CM showed significantly increased global structural connectivity compared with those without CM exposure (see Figure 1). Specifically, CM was positively associated with higher number of connections (F(1, 2002)=9.780, pFDR=0.01; estimated marginal means (EMMs): CM: 95%-CI=[533.9; 540.6], no-CM: 95%-CI=[529.1; 535.0]), elevated normalized network efficiency (F(1, 2002)=7.556, pFDR=0.02; CM: 95%-CI=[0.860; 0.863], no-CM: 95%-CI=[0.857; 0.860]), and increased non-normalized network efficiency (F(1, 2002)=11.051, pFDR=0.01; CM: 95%-CI=[0.405; 0.407], no-CM: 95%-CI=[0.403; 0.405]), indicating enhanced global integration of the structural connectome. In contrast, normalized network clustering was negatively associated with CM (F(1,2002)=5.597, pFDR=0.045; CM: 95%-CI=[4.976; 5.065], no-CM: 95%-CI=[5.034; 5.111]), reflecting reduced local network segregation in CM-exposed individuals. Critically, there were no significant CM-by-diagnosis interaction effects on any global network measure, indicating that the influence of CM on structural connectome organization was similar across MDD, BD, SZ, and HC. NBS analyses revealed a large-scale network of edges showing hyperconnectivity in CM-exposed individuals compared to those without CM history (NBS t-threshold=[0.9, 1.5]; maximum significant NBS t-threshold=1.5, pFWE<0.05, 364 edges, 113 nodes). This CM-related hyperconnected network remained significant when controlling for psychiatric diagnosis. Further details can be found in the respective article, published in European Neuropsychopharmacology (DOI: 10.1016/j.euroneuro.2025.09.009).

Conclusion

This large-scale transdiagnostic analysis identifies a robust structural connectomic signature of childhood maltreatment, characterized by globally increased connection density, enhanced network efficiency, and reduced local clustering, alongside a widespread hyperconnected subnetwork. The absence of significant CM-by-diagnosis interactions implies that early adversity shapes brain network architecture through processes that are largely independent of those conferring risk for, or maintaining, specific psychiatric syndromes, consistent with models of CM as a transdiagnostic liability factor. The observed pattern of increased efficiency and reduced clustering may reflect a shift toward more integrated, less segregated network organization, potentially representing compensatory reorganization or maladaptive strengthening of long-range connections at the expense of local processing efficiency. This reconfiguration could contribute to altered threat processing, emotion regulation, and self-referential thought, which over time may increase vulnerability to a range of mental health problems. By demonstrating that CM is associated with a convergent, reconfiguration of structural brain networks across psychiatric diagnoses, these findings advance mechanistic understanding of how environmental adversity may become embedded in the human connectome.
