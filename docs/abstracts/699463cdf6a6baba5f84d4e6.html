Introduction

Learning to read is a complex developmental process that requires the integration of phonological, orthographic, and semantic information. Neuroimaging studies in adults have identified a predominantly left-lateralized network supporting reading, including the inferior and middle frontal gyri, posterior temporal regions, and inferior parietal cortex.1 However, how these neural systems emerge and are organized during early childhood remains less well understood.  

Beyond core language processes, verbal working memory has been proposed as a key ability supporting reading, as it enables the temporary maintenance and manipulation of phonological information during decoding and word recognition. Behavioral evidence suggests a close link between working memory capacity and reading ability. For example, performance in working memory measures such as digit span, sentence span, and counting span has been shown to predict word reading performance in children aged 8 to 16 years.2 Whether similar relationships are present at earlier stages of reading development, and how verbal working memory relates to the neural correlates of reading in young children, remain largely unexplored.  

To address these gaps, the present study used functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), a child-friendly neuroimaging method with better tolerance to motion, to investigate the cortical networks supporting word reading and verbal working memory in children aged 5 to 7 years. By combining task-based fNIRS with standardized behavioral measures, this study addressed two research questions (RQs): (1) how task conditions modulated cortical hemodynamic responses during a word reading and verbal working memory (VWM) tasks, and (2) whether individual differences in reading and working memory abilities predicted task-related hemodynamic responses during these tasks.  

Methods

Forty-six children aged 5-7 were initially invited to participate in the fNIRS study, with behavioral testing of reading and working memory conducted separately. Two participants were excluded from the study due to incidental findings. For the word reading task, participants were excluded for poor task performance (defined as scoring ≤ 75% in the control condition or ≤ 15% in the regular word condition; n=11), and for poor fNIRS data quality (n=2), resulting in a final sample of 31 participants (age: M=6.62 years, SD=0.50; female=14; Kindergarten 2 (K2)=15, Primary grade 1 (P1)=16) included in the fNIRS analyses. Eight additional participants were excluded from the behavioral analyses due to missing data. For the VWM task, participants were excluded for task incompletion (n=2), poor task performance (defined as scoring ≤ 75% in the medium-load control condition or ≤ 16.6% in the medium-load task condition; n=8), and poor fNIRS data quality (n=3), yielding a final sample of 31 participants (age: M=6.66 years, SD = 0.57; female=13; K2=14, P1=17). Seven more participants were excluded from the behavioral analyses due to missing data.

During fNIRS recording, participants completed two event-related tasks. The word reading task (Fig. 1a) required reading aloud four word types: regular and irregular words (consistent and inconsistent letter-sound correspondence, respectively), pseudo-words (made-up pronounceable words following phonological rules but without meaning), and control stimuli (strings of repeated consonants requiring a “pass” response). The delayed match-to-sample VWM task (Fig. 1b) presented a sequence of letters followed by a delay, after which children identified the missing letter in the probe sequence. Task conditions used unique letters, whereas control conditions repeated the same letter throughout the sequence. Memory load was manipulated at two levels: high load with five letters, and medium load with three letters interspersed with two hashtags. Outside of fNIRS, participants completed standardized behavioral assessments, including the Phonemic Decoding (PD) Efficiency subtest from TOWRE-2, the Word Reading (WR) subtest from the Singapore Ability Scales, and the Number Memory Forward (NM_F) and Number Memory Reversed (NM_R) tests from TAPS-4.

Brain activity was recorded using a Dual Brite MKIII fNIRS device (Artinis Medical Systems) at wavelengths of 762 nm and 844 nm with a sampling rate of 50 Hz. The optode design (Fig. 1c) consisted of 46 channels, including 24 channels on the left hemisphere, and 22 channels on the right hemisphere with 2 additional short-separation channels, targeting the bilateral inferior parietal lobules (IPL), angular gyri (AG), inferior frontal gyri (IFG), middle frontal gyri (MFG), and posterior temporal cortices (pTG).

fNIRS data were preprocessed separately for each task using Homer3 (BUNPC, Boston, MA). Preprocessing steps included quality checks and channel pruning (runs were excluded if more than 25% channels were rejected in both hemispheres), motion artifact correction using spline interpolation and wavelet filtering, and conversion to oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) concentrations using the modified Beer-Lambert Law. The resulting HbO and HbR time series and the excluded channel information were imported into MNE-NIRS for General Linear Model analyses for each task, producing ROI-level beta coefficients per participant. 

Linear mixed-effects (LME) models were then implemented in R (lme4 package) to address the research questions. For RQ1, models examined how task conditions (reading: word type; VWM: task × load) modulated HbO responses. For RQ2, participants’ behavioral scores were included to assess whether individual differences in reading and working memory abilities predicted task-evoked brain activity. Follow-up post hoc comparisons were conducted using emmeans package, and pairwise correlations with Pearson’s correlation (R stats package).

Results

RQ1 (Word Reading). We first examined whether word type modulated cortical HbO responses during word reading. LME models on ROI-level HbO estimates showed that no condition or ROI exhibited significant effects after multiple comparison correction. That said, uncorrected analyses suggested condition-related trends in temporo-parietal regions. Compared with the control condition, irregular words were associated with reduced HbO in the left AG and the right pTC (p < .05). Pseudo-words in the left AG (p = .069) and the right pTC (p = .078) were marginally significant. Post hoc pairwise comparisons after Tukey's correction showed that the left AG had lower HbO activation for irregular and pseudo words versus control (p < .01) and higher HbO responses for regular versus irregular/pseudo words (p < .01), with no difference between irregular and pseudo words (Fig. 2a). In the right pTC, irregular words showed lower HbO activation than control (p < .01; Fig. 2b). These results indicate that word type selectively modulates HbO responses in temporo-parietal regions during word reading.

RQ1 (VWM). Similarly, the LME models were set up for the VWM task, which revealed significant effects in the left AG, including a main effect relative to the reference ROI (right IFG; p = .017, FDR-corrected), a Load effect (High − Medium; p = .012, FDR-corrected), and a Task × Load interaction (p = .012, FDR-corrected), indicating task-dependent modulation of load-related HbO responses in this region. Post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed a significant negative load effect during control conditions (High < Medium; p = .002, FDR-corrected) and a positive load effect during task conditions (High > Medium; p = .041, FDR-corrected; Fig. 2c).

RQ2 (Word Reading). To examine whether individual differences in reading and working memory abilities predicted HbO responses of different word types, LME analyses across ROIs revealed several interactions (p < .05, uncorrected), primarily involving NM_F and NM_R. During pseudo-word reading, HbO responses in the left AG correlated with NM_F scores, and in the right AG, left MFG, and right pTC with NM_R scores. During irregular word reading, HbO in the right IPL and right MFG correlated with NM_R. For regular words, HbO responses in the right MFG correlated with NM_R scores. Across all word types, HbO responses in the left IFG were associated with NM_R scores. After FDR correction within each ROI, only the irregular word × NM_R interaction in the right MFG remained significant (p = .04). A follow-up pairwise correlation revealed a positive relationship between the HbO response in the right MFG during the irregular word condition and NM_R scores (r = 0.36, p = 0.09; Fig. 2d), indicating that higher reversed number memory was associated with greater activation during irregular word reading. 

RQ2 (VWM). The LME analyses for the VWM task revealed several interactions between individual differences in PD, NM_F, NM_R, and the VWM-evoked HbO responses (p < .05, uncorrected). Positive main effects of PD were shown in the bilateral IFG, with an additional load-dependent modulation in the left MFG. Working memory measures exhibited task- and load-dependent interactions primarily in the parietal regions at uncorrected level, including task-specific and task-by-load effects of NM_R in the right IPL and a task-by-load interaction of NM_F in the left IPL. The strongest effect was observed in left IPL (p = 0.009, uncorrected), involving a Task × Load × NM_F interaction. An exploratory Pearson correlation was conducted between NM_F scores and HbO responses in the left IPL during the high-load VWM condition. The result showed a positive but insignificant correlation (r = .38, p = .069; Fig. 2e). These findings suggest that individual differences in working memory may contribute to task- and load-dependent modulation of frontoparietal HbO responses. 

Conclusion

The current study used fNIRS to investigate how task demands influenced cortical HbO responses during word reading and VWM tasks in early childhood, and how individual differences in reading and working memory abilities were associated with task-evoked cortical activation. By characterizing task-based neural activations and individual-difference modulation within the same cohort, this work provides a cohesive account of early fronto-parietal engagement across language and working memory domains. 

For RQ1, across word reading and VWM tasks, task demands were associated with modulation of activity within a fronto–temporo–parietal network, though with differing levels of statistical robustness. During word reading, word-type modulation of temporo-parietal regions was observed at uncorrected levels, suggesting possible sensitivity of the left AG and right pTC to differences in reading-related processing demands. In contrast, during the VWM task, the left AG showed robust task- and load-dependent modulation that survived correction for multiple comparisons. Taken together, these findings suggest that temporo-parietal regions, particularly the left AG, may contribute to both reading-related integration and verbal working memory demands in early childhood, with clearer evidence for load-related engagement during VWM. 

For RQ2, individual differences in reading and working memory abilities were associated with task-related HbO responses in a region- and condition-specific manner, although these effects were generally modest. During word reading, several ability-by-condition interactions were observed at uncorrected levels across frontal and temporo-parietal regions, with only the interaction between irregular word reading and reversed number memory scores in the right MFG surviving within-ROI correction. During the VWM task, phonemic decoding and working memory abilities showed associations with frontal and parietal activation that varied with task context and load, but none of these effects remained significant after correction. These findings indicate that individual differences modulate neural engagement during both reading and VWM in a conditional manner, while task demands play a more dominant role in shaping cortical responses.  

Previous literature has demonstrated a close relationship between working memory and reading, as reading comprehension relies on the temporary maintenance and manipulation of phonological information and the integration of visuospatial representations. Meta‑analytic evidence has shown a reliable moderate correlation between working memory and reading ability, with the domain‑general central executive component particularly implicated in early reading acquisition, and verbal working memory strongly linked to reading performance in later grades.3 Consistent with this literature, the present finding of the overlapping left AG engagement across word reading and VWM tasks suggests shared neural resources that support both reading and verbal working memory in early childhood. This neural overlap highlights the potential for temporo–parietal regions to play a role in foundational cognitive processes that underpin both reading and working memory development. In addition, the association between NM_R and the right MFG activation during irregular word reading suggests that children with stronger working memory may engage frontal regions more effectively when processing complex or less predictable words, highlighting how domain-general cognitive capacities may support the development of specialized reading networks. 

Our results are constrained by a modest sample size, which may have limited statistical power to detect individual-difference effects after correction. Future longitudinal studies with larger samples are needed to further clarify how developmental trajectories of reading and working memory abilities relate to the neural mechanisms supporting word reading and verbal working memory.
