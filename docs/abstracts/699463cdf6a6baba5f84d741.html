Introduction

Functional connectivity (FC) derived from functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) provides crucial insights into brain network dynamics underlying consciousness and sleep.^1 When combined with electroencephalography (EEG), which allows standard sleep-stage scoring, fMRI has revealed stage-specific patterns of brain activity, such as decreased connectivity within the default mode network (DMN) and increased thalamocortical decoupling during deep non rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep.^2,3 However, the potential of FC to reliably decode different sleep stages remains insufficiently explored. Furthermore, REM sleep has rarely been recorded with MRI before,^4 due to its late onset (typically after 90 minutes of sleep) and frequent awakenings in the scanner environment. Consequently, there is limited consensus on how large-scale brain networks reorganize during REM sleep, with conflicting reports on connectivity changes in frontoparietal and limbic networks.^5 To address this issue, narcoleptic subjects appear to be a good target population, thanks to their propensity to easily fall asleep and rapidly reach REM phase. In the present study, we leverage a unique simultaneous EEG-fMRI dataset covering both NREM and REM sleep in narcoleptic individuals to (i) identify key brain networks distinguishing different sleep stages, including REM, and (ii) assess the capacity of FC measures to classify these stages, providing a step toward objective, network-based sleep biomarkers. We then also repeat the analysis on a dataset of healthy controls (HC) covering NREM sleep, to assess the reproducibility of the findings and generalizability of the classification.

Methods

Eighteen narcoleptic patients and eighteen healthy controls were included in the analysis. Sleep was recorded using simultaneous EEG-fMRI during multiple naps inside the 3T MRI scanner. REM sleep was captured only in narcoleptic participants, who more readily reached this stage. Sleep stages were scored in 30-second EEG epochs according to standard sleep scoring guidelines (American Academy of Sleep Medicine), and each window was assigned to either wake, N1, N2, N3, REM or unknown class. FC was computed as pairwise Pearson correlations between regional fMRI time series in 30-second sliding windows matching with the EEG epochs. Preprocessing was performed with Matlab 2025b and SPM12, and included motion correction, 5 mm smoothing, regression of motion parameters, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid signals, and [0.03–0.15 Hz] bandpass filtering to reduce windowing-related artifacts. Brain parcellation was performed using the Glasser 360-region cortical atlas and the 19-region Human Connectome Project (HCP) subcortical atlas.

To identify FC patterns characteristic of each sleep stage, the mean value of each connection within a given stage was tested against zero using a linear mixed-effects model with subject as a random intercept. Pairwise differences between sleep stages were then assessed using linear mixed-effects models with sleep stage as a fixed effect and again subject as a random intercept to account for repeated measures.

To evaluate the ability of FC to classify sleep stages, a support vector machine (SVM) was trained on windowed FC values using a 5-fold cross-validation framework. Class imbalance was addressed by under-sampling the majority class and applying the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to the minority class. Analyses were conducted separately in both cohorts to assess reproducibility. Additionally, to evaluate classifier generalizability, we performed cross-population testing by training the classifier on one group and testing it on unseen subjects from the other group (narcoleptic on healthy controls, and vice versa).

Results

The linear mixed-effects model results for NREM sleep were consistent across the two analyzed datasets and in line with previous findings^1, providing initial support for the generalizability of results derived from the narcoleptic population. This analysis revealed characteristic FC patterns associated with wakefulness and different sleep stages, as well as significant differences between them (Fig. 1; p < 0.05, FDR-corrected). During wakefulness, we observed the emergence of well-known interactions within and between resting-state networks, particularly the somatomotor (SMN) and ventral attention (VA) networks (Fig. 1a, wake). Similar patterns were also present during sleep, especially in N1–N2 and REM, confirming the perseverance of resting-state networks in these phases, as shown as well by previous reports for NREM sleep.^1

Notably, a general reduction in FC was observed during deeper sleep (N3), where networks appeared more segregated, and connectivity was largely confined to within-subnetwork and intra-hemispheric interactions (Fig. 1a, N3). This trend, in line with previous findings,^3 was further supported by pairwise FC comparisons between stages, which revealed widespread significant differences between deep sleep (N3) and wakefulness, as well as between N2 and N3. In particular, FC increased from wake to N2 for selected connections involving SMN and VA networks, before markedly decreasing during N3.

Only very few significant differences were detected between wakefulness and N1 (Fig. 1b, wake-N1), which is consistent with the transitional nature of this sleep stage. Further, these differences suggested increased cortico-cortical FC in N1, as well as decreased thalamo-cortical connections, confirming previous reports of FC changes during the transition from wakefulness to light sleep.^1,3 Interestingly, REM sleep also showed strong similarities with wakefulness, with only a limited number of significant differences, mainly reflecting increased FC within SMN–VA circuits, and decreased FC along the visual and dorsal attention (DA) networks (Fig. 1b, wake-REM). With respect to deep sleep, REM differed instead for a general FC increase characterizing mainly primary sensory and attention networks (negative areas in Fig. 1b, N2-REM and N3-REM), but also a decrease in subcortico-cortical connections involving thalamus and cerebellum. 

In both datasets, sleep-stage classification showed good performance for wakefulness (F1 > 0.61) and deep sleep stages N2/N3 (F1 > 0.62), but poor performance for N1 (Fig. 1c-d). Classification of REM sleep was also very limited (F1 = 0.08), although performance improved when restricting the analysis to subjects with available REM recordings (F1 = 0.36). Misclassification of N1 as either wake or N2 was expected, as N1 represents a transitional stage between these states and lacks a distinctive FC signature, consistent with the results reported above.

Beyond the limited sample size for REM, its low classification accuracy—driven primarily by confusion with wakefulness—suggests substantial inter-individual variability in REM-related brain dynamics. This variability is not surprising and may reflect the well-known cognitive and emotional heterogeneity associated with dream experiences during this stage.

Importantly, satisfactory classification performance for wakefulness and N2/N3 was preserved in cross-dataset analyses, where individuals from one dataset were classified using a model trained on the other. Specifically, macro F1-scores of 0.63 and 0.54 were obtained when testing narcoleptic subjects using a classifier trained on healthy controls and vice versa. These results demonstrate good generalizability of the classifiers and robustness of sleep-stage–specific FC patterns across populations and acquisition protocols.

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings indicate that large-scale FC patterns capture robust and reproducible signatures of vigilance state, particularly for wakefulness and deeper stages of sleep. The gradual modulation of FC across stages, with increasing segregation during N3 and partial reconfiguration in REM, supports the view that sleep is characterized by systematic, stage-dependent changes in whole-brain organization rather than abrupt state transitions. The limited discriminability of N1 and REM further underscores the transitional and heterogeneous nature of these stages, at both the group and individual levels.

Notably, this work is among the first to systematically characterize fMRI-derived FC patterns emerging during REM sleep. Importantly, the preservation of classification performance across independent datasets demonstrates that sleep-stage–specific FC patterns generalize across populations and acquisition protocols, highlighting their potential relevance for cross-cohort studies and translational applications. Overall, these results suggest that FC provides a meaningful and robust framework for characterizing brain states across the sleep–wake cycle.
