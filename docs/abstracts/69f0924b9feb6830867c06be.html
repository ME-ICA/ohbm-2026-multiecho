Introduction

Gyrification refers to a fundamental patterning process of the human cerebral cortex as a consequence of brain growth. The degree of folding, as quantified by the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) derived local gyrification index (LGI) implemented in FreeSurfer, is related to brain function [1]. Such an association is most likely based not only on folding differences in anatomically defined regions attributed to specific functions like cognition and somatosensation (regional variability), but also on differences in regional folding between individuals (inter-individual variability) in populations. Analysis in about 3,000 related individuals (pedigrees) demonstrated heritability estimates (h2) for the LGI between 44-55% [2]. Pathologically induced changes of the LGI were observed in neuropsychiatric diseases. For example, patients with 22q11 deletion syndrome - who carry a markedly increased risk of developing schizophrenia - show regionally decreased LGI in parietal and temporal cortices compared to controls [1]. 

Here we aimed to investigate the analytical potential of the LGI as a trait for genome-wide association studies (GWAS) at the population level. To address this, cortices of 3,144 adults from the German population were parcellated using the latest cytoarchitectonically derived areal maps from the Julich-Brain Atlas [3], and the variance of area-specific LGI explainable by single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with a minor allele frequency >1% were determined as heritability estimate (h2).

Methods

MRI data for this pilot study were acquired on 1.5T scanners from 3,316 participants of two cohorts (SHIP-START-2, SHIP-TREND-0) of the Study of Health in Pomerania (SHIP), a population-based project in Western Pomerania, the north-east area of Germany. FreeSurfer (version 7.4.1) was used to reconstruct the cortical surfaces and extract the LGI measures from T1-weighted images. The GLOBAL LGI was defined as the arithmetic mean between the LGI of the left and the right brain hemisphere. The FreeSurfer implemented Julich-Brain atlas (version 3.1) comprised 296 maps and was used for microanatomical brain parcellation to define the regions of interest, i.e. LOBES and PRIMARY CORTICES. The four LOBES LGI were computed based on 32 temporal, 94 frontal, 44 parietal, and 38 occipital maps. The four PRIMARY CORTICES LGI for somatosensory, visual, motor, and auditory consisted of eight, two, four and six cytoarchitectonic areas, respectively. Heritability analyses were conducted using the GCTA-GREML (version 1.95.0) software based on a genetic relationship matrix generated from 2.1 M SNPs that were imputed applying a newly developed method to remove batch effects within the SHIP samples genotyped on three different microarray types [4]. In total, 3,144 individuals with genetic and MRI data were available and included in the analyses.

Results

For comparison, we performed analyses in three test scenarios. The first was GLOBAL: LGI=3.0, variance (V)=0.02, and h2=51.4%. The second was LOBES: Temporal LGI=3.72, V=0.04, and h2=39%; Frontal LGI=3.3, V=0.03, and h2=42.9%; Parietal LGI=3.1, V=0.02, and h2=57.1%; Occipital LGI=2.8, V=0.01, and h2=49.4%. To assess relationships between the four brain lobes, we performed correlation analyses, and found that Frontal and Temporal LGI showed the strongest correlation (r=0.92), while Frontal and Occipital LGI showed the weakest (r=0.66), these and other pairwise correlations were significant (p<0.0001, Fig. 1A). The third was PRIMARY CORTICES: Somatosensory LGI=3.3, V=0.03, and h2=53.7%; Visual LGI=2.9, V=0.03, and h2=52.6%; Motor LGI=2.9, V=0.02, and h2=48.8%; Auditory LGI=4.8, V=0.11, and h2=34.5%.

Conclusion

Here, we tested whether a cytoarchitecture-informed LGI phenotype enables a systematic interrogation of common genetic variation underlying human gyrification, particularly in GWAS from the ENIGMA Consortium that require standardized and easily adoptable protocols. As an initial step, we compared a global cortical phenotype with phenotypes reflecting a subdivision into the four major cortical lobes. Parietal and occipital lobes exhibited the smallest LGI values and variance but the highest heritability, whereas frontal and temporal lobes showed the largest LGI values and variance but the lowest heritability. Interestingly, these heritability estimates are in line with the estimates obtained from family studies suggesting that the genetic variation of the LGI is not driven by rare variants. We then focused on the four primary cortices as a biological model, given their relatively low inter-individual variability in functional specialization and their clear anatomical landmarks. Somatosensory (S1; parietal) and visual (V1; occipital) cortices showed the highest heritability, while motor (M1; frontal) and auditory (A1; temporal) cortices showed the lowest, mirroring the lobe-wise pattern [5]. These findings suggest that Julich-Brain-derived maps enable a biologically grounded analysis of both regional and inter-individual variability in the MRI-based phenotype LGI, thereby supporting their suitability for future well-powered consortial GWAS.
