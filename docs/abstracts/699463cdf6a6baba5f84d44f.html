Introduction

Vulnerability to fraud and financial exploitation among older adults is a growing public health concern. With the rapid proliferation and widespread penetration of digital technologies, email phishing has emerged as a particularly common and costly form of deception, with often dramatic consequences for both individuals and society at large. Growing evidence supports that cognitive decline and socioemotional changes with age contribute to greater exploitation risk among older adults,1 but the neural mechanisms of heightened susceptibility to deception in aging are not yet well understood. Of note, white matter tracts are particularly vulnerable to the aging process; and social white matter tracts, such as the anterior thalamic radiation,2 are crucial in communications across distributed regions of the “social brain”, likely implicated in deception detection. This study, for the first time, examined the hypothesis that integrity of the anterior thalamic radiation, a white matter bundle connecting dorsomedial nucleus with the prefrontal cortex, would mediate age-related differences in susceptibility to email phishing, a potent, fast-evolving Internet deception threat including among older adults.

Methods

Fifty-one younger (mean age 21.82 ± 3.47 years) and 46 cognitively healthy older (mean age 68.87 ± 6.56 years) adults completed our novel ecologically-valid PHishing Internet Task (PHIT; Figure 1A), which provides a behavioral measure of real-life email phishing susceptibility.3 In this task, participants, unbeknownst to them, receive simulated phishing emails (Figure 1B for sample email) directly to their personal email inbox over a 30-day period (two emails per day). Participant responses were rated as: whether the participant opened at least one email (0 = no, 1 = yes); clicked on at least one email link (0 = no, 1 = yes); and submitted information on at least one landing page (0 = no, 1 = yes). Phishing susceptibility was quantified as the total number of these actions taken by a participant, resulting in a score ranging from 0 to 3. Higher scores indicated greater susceptibility to phishing emails, reflecting lower ability to detect deceptive content. Participants were fluent in English, had at least an eighth-grade education (younger adults: M = 15.38, SD = 2.28; older adults: M = 16.66, SD = 2.72), and access to a personal email account they used regularly. Older adults underwent the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status4 and were eligible when meeting the cut-off value of > 30. All participants were scanned on the 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma scanner located in the McKnight Brain Institute at the University of Florida and were administered T1-sequences for anatomical mapping and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) for assessing white matter microstructural integrity. T1-weighted Magnetization-Prepared Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echo (MPRAGE) anatomical scans were obtained using the following parameters: repetition time/echo time/inversion time = 2300/2.96/900 ms; flip angle = 9°; 1mm isotropic resolution. Multi-shell DWI data was acquired with a 2mm isotropic spin-echo EPI sequence (repetition time/echo time = 3000/66 ms; posterior-anterior [PA] phase encoding) with 109 measurements (isotropically spaced around a sphere) across 3 shells with gradient directions (9 b = 0, 7 b = 300, 29 b = 1000, and 64 b = 2000 s/mm2). Five additional b = 0 images were collected with reversed phase-encoding (AP) for distortion correction. DSI Studio5 (version 2024 March) was used to execute deterministic tractography for construction of the anterior thalamic radiation. White matter integrity metrics (i.e., mean diffusivity and fractional anisotropy) were extracted from the anterior thalamic radiation. To reduce the number of comparisons and maximize statistical power, diffusion metrics were averaged across hemispheres to obtain a single value for the anterior thalamic radiation. Mean global white matter metrics (averaged across all white matter voxels in the brain) were computed and entered as a covariate in the mediation models to account for the general impact of white matter. To test our hypothesis (separately for mean diffusivity and fractional anisotropy), regression-based mediation analyses employing the bootstrap method (5,000 bootstraps) were conducted with the Hayes’s PROCESS macro (model 4, version 5.0). A significant indirect effect (i.e., mediation effect) was reflected in the 95% confidence interval (CI) not including zero. Age was entered as a binary variable (dummy-coded as 0 = younger and 1 = older) in all statistical analyses. All analyses also controlled for sex and education.

Results

The total effect of age group on phishing susceptibility was significant (B = 0.869, t(92) = 4.562, p < 0.001). Entering anterior thalamic radiation mean diffusivity as mediator into the model (Figure 2), the direct effect of age group on phishing susceptibility remained significant, but with reduced magnitude (B = 0.669, t(91) = 3.163, p = 0.002). Further, the indirect effect of age group on phishing susceptibility via anterior thalamic radiation mean diffusivity was also significant (B = 0.200, 95% CI [0.013, 0.432]). These findings support a partial mediation of anterior thalamic radiation mean diffusivity in the effect of age group on phishing susceptibility. Anterior thalamic radiation fractional anisotropy, in contrast, did not show a significant mediation effect (indirect effect: B = 0.059, 95% CI [−0.070, 0.241]).

Conclusion

Findings from this study suggest that age-related degradation of the anterior thalamic radiation is associated with greater susceptibility to deception in older adults, here measured in real life in the form of risk of falling for phishing emails. These results importantly advance a social white matter-based account of age-related exploitation risk, shedding light on the neurobiology underlying social decision-making deficits in aging. Future investigation is warranted to determine the joint effects of brain structure and function on deception detection in aging given that efficient decision making and social cognition are supported not only by cortical brain areas but also by white matter tracts connecting those regions.
