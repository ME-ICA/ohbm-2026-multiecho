Introduction

Human emotion recognition is fundamental to social interaction, yet the developmental onset of this ability remains unclear. Previousstudies have reported inconsistent findings. Most evidence suggests that infants can discriminate basic emotions from facial expressions from around five months of age1,2, whereas fewer studies report sensitivity in younger infants or even newborns. One possible explanation is the limited sensitivity of commonly used methods, such as EEG and eye tracking, which may fail to detect early neural signatures of emotional processing. Emotional processing may therefore be present earlier in development than the current consensus suggests. Research into early emotion recognition has been constrained by the limitations of existing neuroimaging techniques. EEG has limited spatial precision, and fNIRS lacks the temporal resolution required to capture rapid emotion-related neural dynamics. As a result, current methods are not well suited to investigating the timing and neural sources of early emotional processing. Optically pumped magnetometers (OPM) offer a high-fidelity neuroimaging approach that overcomes many of these limitations. OPM-based magnetoencephalography (MEG) enables flexible sensor placement close to the scalp3,4, providing high spatial and temporal resolution while remaining well tolerated by infant participants5. OPM-MEG therefore represents a step-change in infant neuroimaging and is particularly suited to developmental research. During infancy, sensory systems mature at different rates. Visual development is relatively protracted, whereas auditory processing develops earlier. Auditory emotional cues may therefore provide an earlier window into emotion recognition. In this study, we investigated neural responses to emotionally distinct vocal stimuli in 2- to 3-month-old infants, using OPM-MEG to characterise early emotion-related brain activity.

Methods

Seventeen infants aged two to three months participated in the study. Infants were recruited if they were born full term, had no health conditions diagnosed by a healthcare professional, and were raised in English speaking households in the United Kingdom. Caregivers were eligible to participate only if they had no metal implants and no history of claustrophobia. Prior to the experiment, caregivers received a full explanation of the study procedures, and written informed consent was obtained. Infant brain activity was recorded using a custom built 50 channel OPM-MEG system developed at the University of Birmingham for infant research. Data were acquired inside a magnetically shielded room, with the infant accompanied by a caregiver and an experimenter. To minimise electromagnetic interference, infants were placed in a purpose-built cot with all metal components removed. A three dimensionally scanned infant specific OPM helmet was mounted on the cot, and a custom designed sliding headrest was used to ensure accurate and stable positioning of the infant head within the helmet. Auditory stimuli consisted of semantically neutral sentences, for example “The book is green”, spoken with either happy or angry emotional prosody. Stimulus duration ranged from one-to-one point five seconds. Each emotional condition included up to 120 trials, with the interstimulus interval adjusted according to infant state and tolerance. The experiment was paused or terminated if the infant became unsettled. The study was approved by the University of Birmingham STEM Ethics Committee. OPM-MEG data were down sampled to 1000 Hz and band pass filtered between 1 and 100 Hz. Notch filters at 50 and 100 Hz were applied to remove power line noise. Signal space projection was used to remove cardiac related artefacts. Time windows containing muscle or movement related noise were rejected. The pre-processed data were epoched from minus 0.5 to 2 seconds relative to stimulus onset. Event related fields for happy and angry voice conditions were calculated by averaging within participants and baseline normalised using the minus 100 to minus 1 ms interval, before grand averaging across participants for each condition. 

Results

Clear auditory evoked responses to vocal stimuli were observed in both emotional conditions, with prominent components emerging at approximately 100, 200, and 370 ms following stimulus onset. Across components, a tendency towards larger response amplitudes was observed for happy voices compared with angry voices. The early component at around 100 ms showed amplitude differences between emotional conditions, with a peak-to-peak amplitude of approximately 80 fT for angry voices and 150 fT for happy voices. Field topography for the angry condition exhibited a more left lateralised distribution compared with the happy condition. A subsequentcomponent at around 200 ms showed larger responses overall, with peak-to-peak amplitudes of approximately 300 fT for angry voices and 450 fT for happy voices. The corresponding field distributions appeared slightly right lateralised and bilaterally distributed across conditions. The later component at around 370 ms showed comparable amplitudes for angry and happy voices, with peak-to-peak values of approximately 320 fT and 340 fT, respectively, and a similarly left lateralised field distribution. 

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that the infant brain is sensitive to vocal emotional cues within the first months of life. Using high spatiotemporal resolution OPM-MEG, we show that infants aged two to three months exhibit differential neural responses to spoken sentences conveying happy and angry emotional prosody. Emotion dependent auditory evoked responses were observed at multiple latencies following stimulus onset, indicating rapid and dynamic processing of vocal emotional information at a very early developmental stage. Distinct response components at approximately 100, 200, and 370 ms suggest that early vocal emotion processing may involve multiple stages of neural computation. Differences in response amplitude between emotional conditions, with a general tendency towards larger responses to happy voices, were evident at early and intermediate latencies. These effects were observed in infants far younger than the age at which reliable behavioural discrimination of emotional expressions is typically reported. The findings highlight the importance of auditory emotional cues in early development, given the early maturation of the auditory system and the central role of vocal signals in caregiver infant interaction. Methodologically, this work demonstrates the value of OPM-MEG for studying early functional brain development, enabling well tolerated recordings in awake infants with sufficient temporal and spatial resolution to capture rapid emotion related neural dynamics. Together, these results indicate that the foundations of emotional communication are established remarkably early in human development and can be directly investigated using advanced infant compatible neuroimaging techniques.
