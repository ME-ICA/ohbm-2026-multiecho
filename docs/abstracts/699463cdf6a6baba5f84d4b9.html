Introduction

In functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) signals in white matter (WM) were long regarded as non-neuronal noise, based on the assumption that meaningful neurovascular coupling is largely confined to gray matter (GM). Recently, accumulating evidence demonstrated that WM exhibits reliable, anatomically organized low-frequency BOLD fluctuations that are synchronous at rest and modulated by task demands,^1 and that WM functional connectivity forms reproducible network architectures and dynamically couples with cortical systems.^2 Moreover, WM functional connectivity has been shown to correlate consistently with intracranial stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) connectivity across frequency bands and to be constrained by underlying WM anatomical pathways.^3 Collectively, these findings indicate that WM BOLD signals reflect intrinsic functional activity rather than physiological artifact, and that WM functional connectivity captures genuine neural synchronization. Yet, despite growing recognition of WM functional MRI signals, the functional significance and biological mechanisms underlying the development and individual variability of WM functional activity remain unclear. In the present study, we conducted a population-level, systematic phenome- and genome-wide investigation to characterize the phenotypic landscape and genetic architecture of intrinsic WM functional activity.

Methods

Fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF) was used to index intrinsic WM functional activity from resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) data in approximately 37,000 UK Biobank participants (age range: 44–81 years) who passed imaging and genetic quality control (Application Number 25641). fALFF quantifies the relative contribution of spontaneous low-frequency BOLD oscillations to the total signal, thereby capturing the strength of intrinsic neural activity while reducing sensitivity to physiological noise.^4 rsfMRI and T1-weighted structural MRI data were acquired across UK Biobank imaging centers on Siemens Skyra 3T scanners using standardized acquisition protocols. Imaging data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep, including motion correction, susceptibility distortion correction, spatial normalization to MNI152 space, and tissue-type segmentation. The JHU ICBM-DTI-81 WM atlas was used to define 48 major WM tracts. Mean rsfMRI time series were extracted within each tract, and fALFF was computed as the ratio of spectral power within the 0.01–0.15 Hz frequency band to the total power across the full detectable frequency spectrum.^5 Phenome-wide association scans (PheWAS) were conducted using the Phenome Scan Analysis Tool (PHESANT) to systematically test associations between WM fALFF measures and approximately 6,500 phenotypes spanning sociodemographics, lifestyle, cognition, environment, physical measures, medical/hospital records, and brain/body imaging traits. PHESANT automatically selected appropriate regression models according to phenotype type (linear, logistic, ordered logistic, or multinomial), and phenome-wide significance was determined using a global Bonferroni correction accounting for all WM tract–phenotype tests. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) were performed separately for each tract-specific WM fALFF measure using PLINK2, based on UK Biobank genotyping data. Independent lead variants were identified using linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping. Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based heritability of each tract-specific WM fALFF measure was estimated using LD score regression based on the GWAS results. Gene-based association and gene-set enrichment analyses were conducted using MAGMA to identify genes and biological pathways associated with intrinsic WM functional activity. All PheWAS and GWAS analyses were adjusted for age, sex, ancestry principal components, and imaging covariates.

Results

Scanning across the UK Biobank phenome, we identified 12,885 significant associations between tract-specific WM fALFF measures and 1,245 phenotypes after global multiple-testing correction (P < 0.05/6,445 phenotypes/48 tracts) (Fig. 1-A). Phenotypically, WM fALFF showed strong associations with brain MRI–derived measures, including cortical and subcortical morphologies as well as WM microstructural indices, indicating tight coupling between intrinsic WM functional activity and large-scale brain organization. Beyond imaging, significant associations extended to cardiometabolic traits (e.g., blood pressure, heart rate, cardiovascular conditions), physical measures, lifestyle factors (e.g., tobacco smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep), mental health status (e.g., depression, anxiety), and cognitive performance (e.g., reaction time, fluid intelligence, numerical memory). Together, these results highlight WM fALFF as a systems-level neural trait linking brain physiology to broader health and behavioral variation. SNP-based heritability analyses revealed partial but biologically meaningful genetic contributions to intrinsic WM functional activity, with heritability estimates reaching h² ≤ 0.21 across tract-wise WM fALFF measures. Notably, higher heritability was observed in several projection and limbic pathways, including the anterior limb of the internal capsule (ALIC, h² = 0.16), posterior limb of the internal capsule (PLIC, h² = 0.19), and the fornix (FX, h² = 0.21), indicating tract-specific genetic regulation alongside substantial non-genetic modulation. Our GWAS analyses identified 173 genetic associations at the genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸), corresponding to 43 independent lead variants (Fig. 1-A). These variants mapped to genes implicated in neural development, axonal organization, and intracellular signaling, including PCNT, DIP2A, AKT3, MAGI2, PPP5C, PLEC, DCC, and SERPINA10. MAGMA gene-based analyses identified 13 significant genes (P < 0.05/18,307 genes), supporting a polygenic architecture of intrinsic WM functional activity. Gene-set enrichment analyses further revealed 11 significantly enriched pathways (P < 0.05/7,546 gene sets) related to axonal and myelin biology, transmembrane and receptor-mediated signaling, neurodevelopmental patterning, and synaptic and transmembrane regulation. Collectively, these multi-layer genetic findings provide convergent molecular evidence that WM fALFF reflects biologically meaningful, genetically regulated aspects of long-range neural communication.

Conclusion

In line with the growing recognition that WM BOLD activity reflects genuine neural processes rather than noise, the current study provides large-scale phenome-wide and genome-wide evidence that intrinsic WM functional activity represents a biologically grounded brain phenotype. Based on the current findings, we propose a biological model (Fig. 1-B) in which intrinsic WM functional activity reflects the baseline coordination and efficiency of axonal signal transmission, shaped by genetically driven developmental programs governing axonal architecture and myelin biology, and dynamically modulated by cortical network activity, physiological state, and environmental influences. In this framework, intrinsic WM functional activity represents an intermediate systems-level phenotype linking genetic variation to large-scale brain communication and downstream health and behavioral outcomes. Together, this work establishes a conceptual and empirical foundation for incorporating WM functional activity into imaging-genetics and precision neuroscience frameworks.
