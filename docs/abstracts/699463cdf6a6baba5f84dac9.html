Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases display specific patterns of gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) atrophy that lead to cognitive impairment and dementia. While regional patterns of GM and WM loss have been examined in neurodegenerative disorders, direct voxel-wise comparisons of GM and WM atrophy across multiple neurodegenerative diseases within a single harmonized dataset remain limited1–3. The Canadian Consortium on Neurodegeneration in Aging (CCNA) is a large and harmonized dataset that facilitates disease-specific structural mapping and comparisons across diagnostic groups. The CCNA includes cognitively intact elderly adults (CIE), patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), Lewy body dementia (LBD), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), subjective cognitive impairment (SCI), and Parkinson's disease (PD).

Here, we compared voxel-wise patterns of GM and WM morphology across CCNA diagnostic groups relative to cognitively intact elderly controls (CIE).  Beyond identifying disease-specific structural differences, we investigated whether the relationship between regional brain atrophy and global cognitive performance differs across diagnostic groups. This approach allows us to distinguish shared versus disease-specific structure–cognition relationships within a harmonized multi-diagnostic cohort.

Methods

T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and clinical data from 1001 participants were extracted from the COMPASS-ND cohort of the CCNA, which provides harmonized clinical and MRI data across multiple neurodegenerative populations. All image processing steps (i.e., pre-processing, linear, nonlinear registrations, and segmentation) were visually quality controlled, blind to the clinical diagnosis, and the cases that did not pass this quality control step or included motion related artifacts (N = 159) were excluded from the subsequent analyses. Six diagnostic cohorts, SCI (n = 116), MCI (n = 331), AD (n = 125), FTD (n = 25), LBD (n = 20), and PD (n = 106), as well as CIE (n=98) as the reference group were included. Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, education, and global cognitive status (MoCA). All structural MRI scans were acquired using the Canadian Dementia Imaging Protocol, a harmonized protocol designed to reduce inter-site variability4. T1-weighted scans underwent standard voxel-based morphometry (VBM) preprocessing steps using PELICAN, our in-house open-source image processing pipeline based on the MINC-Toolkit v2 and Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) toolkits. Tissue segmentation was performed using BISON. Voxel-wise morphometry gray and white matter tissue probability maps modulated by jacobean determinants of the nonlinear deformations5. Finally, the mean VBM values in 108 cortical and subcortical GM regions were extracted using the CerebrA and MNI-PD25 atlases. 

Voxel-level linear regression analyses were conducted for GM and WM VBM maps within the MNI ICBM152 brain mask. Voxel-wise VBM values were used as the dependent variables while the diagnostic group was used as the categorical independent variable of interest, contrasting each disease cohort against controls (Model 1: VBM ~ Diagnostic Group + Covariates). The model covariates included age, sex, education, and Total Intracranial Volume (TIV) as covariates. All results were reported after False discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons with significance threshold of 0.05. 
Two linear regression models were also fitted across all diagnostic groups (SCI, CIE, MCI, AD, PD, FTD, and LBD) to examine the associations between regional GM VBM values and global cognition. The first model assessed the association between regional VBM and MoCA across all diagnostic groups, while accounting for group differences as an additive covariate: 
Model 2) MoCA ~ VBM + Diagnostic Group + Covariates 
In this model, VBM was the variable of interest, reflecting the associations between regional GM atrophy and MoCA scores. The second model included an interaction term between Diagnostic Group and VBM to examine whether the relationship between regional VBM and MoCA differed across diagnostic groups for any regions: 

Model 3) MoCA ~ VBM + Diagnostic Group + Diagnostic Group:VBM + Covariates

In this model, Diagnostic Group:VBM was the variable of interest, reflecting the potential differences in the slopes of the associations between VBM and MoCA in specific diagnostic groups. Age, sex, education, and TIV were included in both models as covariates, and statistical significance was assessed after FDR correction. 

Results

Voxel-Wise Atrophy Patterns

Across diagnostic groups, widespread and diagnosis-specific GM and WM patterns of atrophy (statistically significant after FDR correction) were observed relative to cognitively intact elderly controls (Figure 1). These effects generally followed known disease-specific neurodegeneration patterns and varied in spatial extent across models. AD patients showed widespread GM atrophy, with prominent involvement of bilateral Lateral Orbitofrontal, Medial Orbitofrontal, Fusiform, Hippocampus, and multiple regions in cortical and subcortical areas across the brain. GM effects in the FTD group were present in bilateral Medial Orbitofrontal, Amygdala, right Lateral Orbitofrontal, and left Superior Temporal, Middle Temporal. LBD patients exhibited an atrophy pattern in the bilateral Amygdala, right Fusiform, and left Medial Orbitofrontal. In MCI, GM effects were detectable but more limited than in AD, with the involvement of bilateral Amygdala, Hippocampus, Ventral Diencephalon. PD patients also showed a voxel-wise GM atrophy pattern in the left amygdala relative to CIE. No clear voxel-wise GM atrophy patterns were evident for SCI relative to CIE. 

Significant WM atrophy was also present in the AD group, and was extensive in periventricular and deep WM regions. WM atrophy was more subtle in the MCI group and restricted to periventricular WM. WM atrophy in FTD was more widespread in the anterior WM regions, as expected. Interestingly, LBD patients exhibited asymmetric WM atrophy patterns which were more severe in the left hemisphere.  No clear voxel-wise GM atrophy patterns were observed for PD or SCI relative to CIE. 

Regional VBM–MoCA Associations

Based on Model 2, a significant association was observed between regional GM VBM values and MoCA across numerous regions after FDR correction, indicating that lower regional GM volume was associated with poorer cognitive performance when a common relationship between structure and cognition was assumed across all diagnostic groups with t-statistics values ranging between 2.1 and 7.83 (FDR-corrected p < 0.05, Figure 1.C). After introducing a Diagnostic Group:VBM interaction term (Model 3), a significant interaction effect was observed between regional GM VBM and Diagnostic Group in the FTD and PD groups, suggesting that atrophy in these regions contributed to additional cognitive deficits in these groups. In FTD, these effects were observed in the medial orbitofrontal cortex, insula, parahippocampal, pallidum, accumbens area, fusiform gyrus, ventral diencephalon, superior frontal gyrus, posterior cingulate cortex, thalamus, and caudate nucleus (t-statistics values ranging from 0.98 to 3.67, FDR-corrected p < 0.05).  In PD, these effects were present in the rostral and caudal middle frontal, lateral, and medial orbitofrontal cortices, insula, fusiform gyrus, middle and superior temporal gyri, entorhinal cortex, amygdala, thalamus, precuneus, supramarginal gyrus, superior parietal gyrus, and inferior frontal regions (t-statistics values ranging from 2.63 to 4.04, FDR-corrected p < 0.05). No diagnosis-by-VBM interaction effects remained significant after FDR correction in other diagnostic groups.

Conclusion

Consistent with prior comparative studies2, our voxel-wise VBM analysis revealed distinct and diagnosis-specific structural signatures across neurodegenerative conditions, with differences in the extent and spatial distribution of gray and white matter involvement. AD showed the most widespread cortical and subcortical GM atrophy pattern accompanied by prominent deep and periventricular WM alterations, and MCI demonstrated atrophy patterns consistent with AD, but with a lower severity. FTD exhibited frontal and temporal involvement consistent with the literature. PD displayed minimal voxel-wise abnormalities relative to CIE, limited to the amygdala and insular cortex.

Furthermore, our regional analyses highlighted differences in how brain atrophy related to cognitive performance across diagnoses. When a common structure-cognition relationship was assumed across all groups, lower regional GM volume was broadly associated with poorer MoCA performance (Figure1.C). However, allowing this relationship to vary by diagnosis, we found a diagnosis-specific modulation of the relationship between regional GM VBM and cognition in PD and FTD, indicating that in these conditions, cognitive performance is linked to regional patterns of atrophy different from those observed across diagnoses.

In summary, these findings suggest that as neurodegenerative diseases are characterized by different patterns of structural brain atrophy, the extent to which regional atrophy translates into cognitive impairment might be different across diagnoses. Particularly in PD and FTD, there was evidence of diagnosis-specific structural atrophy-cognition associations. These results underscore the importance of harmonized datasets spanning multiple diagnoses to study the transdiagnostic impacts of brain atrophy on cognition. Along the same line, this enables us to consider diagnosis specific patterns when linking the brain structure to cognitive outcomes for disentangling shared and disease-specific effects in heterogeneous neurodegenerative populations. Certain limitations need to be taken into account. First, sample sizes were not consistent across diagnostic groups, with relatively smaller cohorts of FTD and LBD, which may have limited statistical power to detect more extensive atrophy patterns in these groups. In addition, heterogeneity in clinical subtypes, particularly in the FTD group, may have further reduced sensitivity to variant- specific patterns and spatially localized patterns of atrophy.
