Introduction

Resting-state fMRI research increasingly interprets spontaneous signal fluctuations as manifestations of autonomic arousal, mediated by brainstem nuclei and ascending neuromodulatory systems [1]. Brief bursts of sympathetic activity leading to transient arousal events have been shown to be associated with widespread BOLD modulations across both cortical and subcortical regions.^1

However, the detection of spontaneous arousal events based on peripheral physiology signals typically recorded during fMRI remains challenging due to multiple confounding effects. Spontaneous eye blinks are known to be modulated by vigilance level and task demands ^2, and a recent fMRI study showed that they are accompanied by activations of the ascending arousal network (AAN) and its efferent targets, supporting them as accessible markers of transient arousal events in rest.^3

A body of EEG literature renders further support by showing blink-related oscillations (BROs) across multiple frequency bands that are modulated by factors such as cognitive load, sensory context, and ageing.^4,5,6 However, the relationship between blink-related EEG oscillations and blink-evoked fMRI responses remains unknown.

Here, we investigate spontaneous eye blinks as markers of transient autonomic arousal based on simultaneous EEG–fMRI recordings from healthy participants during rest with eyes open. Blink-related neural dynamics are first characterised in the EEG using event-related spectral analyses and in the fMRI using a lagged voxel-wise general linear model. Features derived from these analyses are then integrated in an ROI-wise EEG–fMRI coupling framework to assess how EEG BROs relate to fMRI brain activations and deactivations.

Methods

Simultaneous EEG–fMRI was acquired from 28 healthy adults (18 females, 27.8 ± 6.2 years), comprising two independent cohorts undergoing eyes-open resting state scans of 7 min (n = 13) or 10 min (n = 15) duration. All recordings used identical acquisition parameters. EEG was recorded with a 32-channel MR-compatible system (Brain Products, 31 EEG + 1 ECG channel) at 5 kHz, synchronised to the MR clock. MRI scans were acquired on a 3T Siemens Vida system with a 64-channel head coil (T2*-weighted GRE-EPI, TR/TE=1260/30 ms, 2.2 mm isotropic voxels, GRAPPA=2, SMS=3).

EEG preprocessing followed a two-stage pipeline in MATLAB/EEGLAB. MR gradient artefacts were removed using average artefact subtraction with a 30-volume template plus optimal basis set correction ^7; R-peaks were identified using long short-term memory-based R-peak detection with manual verification, and the pulse artefact was removed via the PROJIC method.^8 Eye blinks were then detected using BLINKER^9 applied to frontal channels, followed by manual correction, merging events occurring within 1 TR (1.26 s). From these events, we derived a binary eye blink time series. For spectral analyses, the MR-corrected signal was band-pass filtered (1–40 Hz), cleaned with independent component analysis, bad channels removed, data re-referenced to the average, and additional artefactual components rejected using probabilistic classification.

Fully preprocessed EEG was epoched from −3 to +3 s around each blink, and event-related spectral perturbations (ERSPs) were computed in EEGLAB using Morlet wavelets (1–30 Hz). Power was converted to dB relative to a −1.5 to −0.5 s pre-blink baseline. Group-level ERSPs from O1, O2, Oz, Pz and POz channels were assessed with one-sample t-tests across subjects at each time–frequency bin and thresholded at p<0.01 (uncorrected) to identify significant event-related synchronisation/desynchronisation (ERS/ERD) clusters, which were then classified according to their canonical frequency band (Figure 1a).

fMRI preprocessing was implemented in FSL and included fieldmap-based distortion correction, motion realignment, high-pass temporal filtering (0.01 Hz cutoff), and spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel (3.3 mm FWHM).

For each subject, lagged voxel-wise GLMs were fitted with a single regressor of interest given by the blink time series convolved with the canonical double-gamma HRF and its temporal derivative, considering 21 lags (-10 to +10 TR, or -12.6 s to +12.6 s). The resulting subject-level and group-level Z-statistic maps were then averaged within 126 ROIs: 100 cortical parcels from the Schaefer-100 atlas ^10, 10 cerebellar regions and 6 subcortical nuclei from AAL116^11, and 10 brainstem nuclei from the Harvard Ascending Arousal Network atlas^12, therefore obtaining both subject- and group-level Z-statistics for each ROI and lag (Figure 1b).

To assess the relationship between the EEG BROs and fMRI (de)activations, a linear multivariate modelling analysis was performed. From the subject-level ERSPs, the mean dB change within the identified clusters was computed as a cluster-size–weighted average across electrodes, thus getting four BRO features per subject: delta ERS, alpha ERD, low-beta (13–20 Hz) ERS and high-beta (21–30 Hz) ERS.

For each ROI, the group-level lag-dependent Z-statistic curve was used to identify the pre-blink maximum and post-blink minimum. Subject-level Z-values at these ROI-specific lags were then extracted, producing two fMRI features per ROI (Zmax, Zmin). For each ROI, two multivariate linear models were fitted, one for the fMRI Zmax and one for the fMRI Zmin as dependent variables, each with the four EEG features as predictors. For each model, t-statistics for each predictor were mapped back to the corresponding ROIs in MNI space, generating whole-brain maps of the association between blink-related oscillations and pre-blink activations or post-blink deactivations (Figure 1c).

Results

Blink-related EEG oscillatory dynamics are shown in Figure 2a. Group-averaged ERSPs revealed consistent spectral modulations across the analysed occipital and parietal electrodes. A strong and sustained delta-band ERS was observed around blink onset across all chosen electrodes. In addition, a transient low- and high-beta ERS was detected, present across all selected channels but weaker in magnitude compared to the delta response. A more prolonged alpha-band ERD followed blink onset, which was most prominent at midline parietal electrodes (Pz and POz) and absent from the occipital sites (O1, O2 and Oz).

Blink-related fMRI responses are summarised in Figure 2b. Across ROIs, the Z-statistic curves exhibited a consistent biphasic pattern characterised by pre-blink activation followed by post-blink deactivation. Pre-blink activations were strongest within brainstem nuclei belonging to the AAN, most prominently in the Laterodorsal Tegmental nucleus, but also the Locus Coeruleus. Subcortical involvement was also evident, most notably in the thalamus. Cerebellar regions showed marked pre-blink activations, particularly within posterior cerebellar lobules and the vermis. Within the cortex, visual areas as well as the precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex were activated. Post-blink strong deactivations were observed across widespread cortical areas, including visual, parietal, somatomotor, temporal, and prefrontal regions. In contrast, the subcortex, cerebellum, and brainstem nuclei presented comparatively weaker post-blink deactivation effects.

Associations between blink-related EEG oscillations and fMRI responses are shown in Figure 2c. Results are presented for low-beta (13–20 Hz) and high-beta (21–30 Hz) ERS features, which exhibited the largest and spatially extensive associations with fMRI measures. For pre-blink activations (Z-statistic maxima), high-beta ERS showed positive associations across multiple cortical regions, including prefrontal, parietal, temporal, visual, and somatomotor areas. Low-beta ERS exhibited fewer and weaker associations with pre-blink activations. The strongest EEG–fMRI associations were observed for post-blink deactivations (Z-statistic minima). Namely, low-beta ERS showed a widespread negative relationship with Z-statistic minima across large portions of the cortex, indicating that stronger low-beta ERS was associated with more pronounced post-blink deactivations. These effects were widespread and spanned prefrontal, parietal, visual, somatomotor, temporal, and medial cortical regions, with additional involvement of subcortical structures such as the thalamus and the Pontis Oralis of the brainstem. High-beta ERS also presented widespread associations, which were lower in magnitude and generally positive, meaning that, in contrast to low-beta ERS, it was related to weaker post-blink brain deactivations.

Conclusion

This study investigated spontaneous eye blinks as markers of transient arousal events by integrating blink-related EEG oscillations with fMRI signal change in a simultaneous EEG–fMRI framework.

Firstly, we were able to detect eye blinks from the EEG recorded during fMRI acquisitions after MR-induced artefact correction and before further denoising. After full denoising, we found BROs consistent with prior work, both in frequency and timing.^4,5,6 At the haemodynamic level, blink-related fMRI responses showed a consistent biphasic pattern, characterised by pre-blink activations followed by widespread post-blink deactivations. The activation found in brainstem nuclei such as the Locus Coeruleus, known to be associated with wakefulness, vigilance and increased sympathetic tone^13, and in the thalamus, followed by widespread cortical suppression, matches earlier fMRI findings on autonomic arousal^1,14, further linking spontaneous blinks to arousal surges. The observed prominent activation of cortical visual areas is possibly due to the interruption of visual input caused by eyelid closure. Additionally, the activation of the precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex is also described in the literature, as these regions have been identified as cortical sources of BROs and key hubs for environmental monitoring.^15

The EEG–fMRI coupling analysis revealed a relationship between blink-related EEG beta-band oscillations and blink-evoked fMRI dynamics. Notably, a stronger low-beta ERS was associated with more pronounced post-blink cortical deactivations across widespread regions, suggesting a positive association with vigilance level and arousal events. In prior studies, blink-related low-beta ERS has been shown to be a measure of the level of consciousness and awareness and thus appears to index the brain’s capacity to react to external stimuli.^5 Together, this suggests that blink-related beta activity may reflect the intensity of transient autonomic arousal events, with stronger beta responses accompanying larger arousal-related shifts toward a more externally oriented and vigilant state.

In summary, this work highlights spontaneous eye blinks as brief autonomic arousal-linked events that engage brainstem neuromodulatory systems, elicit characteristic EEG oscillatory signatures, and drive structured whole-brain fMRI responses. By directly coupling blink-related EEG oscillations with blink-evoked fMRI dynamics, this study provides a multimodal link between signatures of spontaneous blinking that had previously been studied only separately. Together, these findings further support the view that spontaneous eye blinks reflect transient autonomic arousal surges rather than purely oculomotor processes, positioning them as a powerful, non-invasive window into arousal, vigilance and autonomic fluctuations during resting state. Further work should investigate how spontaneous blinking relates to complementary autonomic markers, such as cardiac or respiratory measures, to better characterise this relationship.
