Introduction

Gender-affirming estradiol therapy (GAET) is commonly used by transfeminine and nonbinary individuals assigned male at birth to align physical characteristics with their gender identity. Short-term GAET has been shown to significantly improve mental health and is positively related to cognitive performance in young adults,1 but little is known about the effects of long-term GAET on brain health in aging transfeminine adults. As part of typical aging, white matter (WM) shows signs of declining microstructural organization, directionality, and demyelination. Ultimately, WM degeneration may be an early biomarker of dementia even before cognitive impairment is detected. In animal and cell models, WM properties like myelin and axon density are positively related to estradiol, and in postmenopausal cisgender women, estradiol therapy is related to higher fractional anisotropy (FA) and WM organization compared with placebo. However, in transfeminine adults, a nascent literature suggests that as little as four months of GAET is associated with a decline in WM microstructural properties. Two studies reported a correlation with GAET and lower WM organization and density in transfeminine adults under 45 years of age. A pre-post comparison of WM microstructure after four months of GAET demonstrated a reduction in FA and an increase in mean diffusivity (MD) in transfeminine adults,2 while a cross-sectional comparison demonstrated lower FA in transfeminine adults taking GAET compared with those who had never taken GAET.3 To date, no study has assessed long-term effects of GAET on WM in transfeminine adults older than 50 years. Given the opposing associations between WM microstructure and short-term estradiol in cisgender and transfeminine adults, and age-related WM degeneration, it is critical to understand the impact of GAET on the aging transgender brain.

Methods

To quantify WM microstructure diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) was acquired as it is sensitive to tissue composition and fibre organization. DTI was acquired for transfeminine adults with at least 5 years of GAET (n=6; mean age: 62.9 ±8.2 years), cisgender women taking estradiol therapy (ET; n=7; mean age: 62.3 ±7.4 years), and cisgender men (n=12; mean age: 62.1 ±6.2 years). Participants were age- and education-matched (mean education: 16.8 years). Participants were scanned in a 3T MAGNTOM Prisma scanner with a 32-channel headcoil. DTI (30 diffusion directions + 7 b=0) was acquired using a 2D spin echo pulse imaging sequence with 2.2 mm isotropic resolution. DTI data were processed using FSL (FMRIB Software Library) following the FSL DTI Toolbox pipeline. Images were corrected for distortions from eddy currents using FSL’s eddy current correction tool. B0 inhomogeneity distortions were corrected using linear registration to a T1 structural scan as no reverse phase-encoding directions were acquired for use with Top-Up. Scalar DTI maps were derived from FSL’s DTIFIT and used to quantify four WM microstructure indices: FA, a measure of tissue organization and directionality; and MD, axial (AD) and radial diffusivity (RD), proxies for WM density, tortuosity, and myelination respectively. A mean FA WM skeleton of the sample was used for tract based spatial statistics (TBSS) analyses for each WM index.4 Voxelwise statistics were performed using FSL’s randomise tool and threshold-free cluster enhancement with family-wise error correction of 0.05 using 500 permutations. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were run for each WM index and revealed nonsignificant f-tests. Due to the small and uneven sample sizes between groups, post-hoc two-group unpaired t-tests comparing cisgender women to transfeminine adults, cisgender men to transfeminine adults, and cisgender women to cisgender men were also run for each WM index. 

Results

ANOVAs for each WM index revealed nonsignificant f-tests, although some of the pairwise comparisons were significant or approaching significance. Therefore, exploratory two-group t-tests were run post-hoc. The t-tests revealed significant differences in WM microstructure indices between cisgender women and transfeminine adults. Specifically, cisgender women had higher FA (p = 0.002) and lower MD (p = 0.03) and RD (p = 0.01) compared with transfeminine adults. Differences in AD did not reach significance. Significant clusters (k > 100 voxels) showed a pattern of higher FA and lower MD and RD in cisgender women compared to transfeminine adults bilaterally in the corpus callosum, corticospinal tract, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, and superior longitudinal fasciculus. Additionally, cisgender men had higher FA than transfeminine adults (p = 0.03) bilaterally in the corticospinal tract and superior corona radiata. There were no other significant differences observed between cisgender men and transfeminine adults or cisgender women.

Conclusion

The findings are interpreted to suggest that WM microstructure in transfeminine adults had less directionality compared with cisgender men, indicated by lower FA. Transfeminine adults also had less WM directionality, density, and myelination compared with cisgender women taking ET, as indicated by FA, MD, and RD, respectively. Since significant clusters denoting variance in FA, MD, and RD were identified in WM tracts important for visual processing, and visuospatial and verbal working memory these differences may reflect different processes of age-related cognitive decline.5 Importantly, the WM patterns identified in this study primarily differentiate the two groups with feminine gender identity: transfeminine adults and cisgender women, despite similarities between these two groups in gender identity, age, and use of estradiol-based therapies. Yet, these groups do have difference social and psychological experiences such as those related to gender socialization, gender minority stress, stigma, and isolation. Together, with the variation in directionality between transfeminine adults and cisgender men, and the lack of WM variation observed between cisgender women and cisgender men, these findings may reflect a sex- and gender-related difference in estrogenic mechanisms. It may be that gender identity, and its related social factors, interact with WM microstructure, and also that the effects of estradiol on WM vary by chromosomal background (e.g., XX, XY). Thus, there may be an interplay of sex, gender, and estradiol in the brain that we are currently exploring.

Future analyses on these data will include adding a fourth group of transfeminine adults not taking GAET. Additionally, cognitive performance, gender minority stress, biomarkers of stress, and inflammatory markers will be considered alongside WM microstructure to better understand the interaction between microstructural changes and behavioural outcome in this understudied population. These preliminary data mark an important advance in transfeminine brain health across the lifespan and, for the first time, begin to describe how long-term GAET interacts with the brain in an aging population.
