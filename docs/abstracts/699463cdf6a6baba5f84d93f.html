Introduction

Understanding how stroke-related injury translates into variability in impairment and recovery remains a major challenge. Stroke produces highly diverse structural lesions as well as diverse motor and cognitive deficits, complicating efforts to link symptoms and recovery profiles to damage in individual brain regions.

Biomarkers such as corticospinal tract injury explain part of the variance in motor recovery (~20%), yet a substantial proportion remains unexplained, particularly for complex behaviors that require both motor and cognitive control. Prior work has shown that recovery of complex motor behavior after stroke depends on injury to secondary motor regions,1 as well as white-matter disconnections.2 These findings suggest that more complex motor behavior does not rely solely on descending motor pathways, but on the integrity of broader, distributed networks. Furthermore, growing evidence exists that measures of both structural and functional connectivity (FC) account for variability in post-stroke behavior and recovery, though their relative contributions differ by behavioral domain.3

Structural disconnection (SD) quantifies how lesions interrupt white-matter pathways, while FC reflects functional communication within and between large-scale brain networks. Global efficiency (GE) is a key metric in graph-theoretical FC analysis, quantifying how efficiently information is transmitted across networks. Using EEG, this metric can be examined across distinct frequency bands, each reflecting specific modes of brain-network communication and underlying neurophysiological processes. Specifically, Delta-band (δ) connectivity is strongly associated with post-stroke structural brain damage and network disruption. 

Here, we test whether δ-GE mediates the effect of SD on motor recovery. We hypothesize that greater ipsilesional SD is associated with poorer motor recovery, and that δ-GE within relevant networks partially mediates this relationship.

Methods

Subacute patients with a first-time unilateral stroke were enrolled during inpatient rehabilitation. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and all participants provided written informed consent.

2.1. Structural Disconnection (SD)

Stroke lesions were manually outlined on each participant’s acute clinical neuroimaging. Ischemic lesions were delineated on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), aided by fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and T1-weighted images when available. Intracerebral hemorrhages were outlined on computed tomography (CT). All lesion masks were reviewed by a vascular neurologist experienced in neuroimaging research and subsequently normalized to 1 mm standard MNI brain space.

SD was estimated using the NeMo Network Modification Tool, which overlays each lesion onto a large HCP-derived white-matter tractography reference dataset (420 individuals; 50% female; ages 25-35 years).4 The tool computes a change-in-connectivity (ChaCo) score, representing the proportion of streamlines passing through each voxel that are disrupted by the lesion (0 = no disconnection; 1 = complete disconnection). For this study, ChaCo values were averaged to yield indices of ipsilesional SD and interhemispheric SD.  

2.2. Global Efficiency (GB)

Resting-state EEG was recorded for 3 minutes eyes-open rest by using a 256-channel HydroCel Geodesic Sensor Net and high-impedance amplifier (Net Amp 300, EGI). The EEG recording occurred during the inpatient rehabilitation.

EEG data were preprocessed and analyzed using MATLAB (R2024b). Signals were band-pass filtered (0.25–50 Hz), segmented into 1-s epochs, and cleaned using automated and visual artifact rejection, including independent component analysis (ICA) to remove ocular and muscular artifacts. Noisy channels were identified, rejected, and subsequently interpolated. FC was calculated from source-reconstructed EEG data by computing coherence between cortical regions in the delta frequency band, using Hilbert-transformed signals. GE was computed using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox and was normalized using a null model preserving individual network characteristics . δ-GE asymmetry was then computed to quantify how differently the ipsilesional and contralesional to information integration throughout the brain. Positive values indicate higher ipsilesional than contralesional global efficiency, negative values indicate the opposite, and values near zero reflect hemispheric symmetry. 

2.3. Motor behavior

The Box and Blocks Test (BBT) measures manual dexterity by quantifying how many 1×1-inch blocks a person can transfer from one compartment of a box to the other within 60 seconds and thereby captures integrated sensorimotor-cognitive demands (e.g., repetitive movements, attention, and psychomotor speed). Post-stroke BBT performance is highly variable but typically substantially reduced, with many individuals scoring 30-50 blocks/min when compared to 71 and 76 blocks/min for age-matched healthy women and men. A change of 5-6 blocks is considered clinically meaningful in stroke populations. BBT performance was assessed following admission to the inpatient rehabilitation facility and before discharge. The change in BBT score between these two visits was calculated to quantify BBT recovery.

2.4. Statistical Analysis

The hypothesis that FC mediates the effect of SD on changes in BBT performance was tested in two consecutive parts. First, the assumptions required for mediation analysis were tested with partial Pearson correlations performed between: (A)  SD and δ-GE asymmetry, and (B)  δ-GE and BBT recovery (Figure 1). In the second part, mediation analyses were performed using SD measures as predictor and δ-GE asymmetry as mediator to explain the outcome of BBT recovery. All statistical tests included age, sex, and time post-stroke at EEG recording as covariates. Statistical thresholds were set at α=0.05.

Results

A total of 18 out of 65 enrolled participants met all inclusion criteria for the present analyses, meaning they had complete and successfully processed imaging data (lesion masks and structural disconnection estimates), EEG data, and two BBT tests. 

The included participants had a mean age of 65.31 ± 18.90 years, 44% were female. Upper limb impairments ranged from moderate to severe, reflected in a mean baseline BBT performance of 17.72 ± 14.38 blocks/min. All included subjects had an ischemic stroke. 

Importantly, the included participants did not differ significantly from the 47 excluded subjects across key demographic or clinical characteristics: Age (70.88 ± 13.34 years; t (df = 23.78) = 1.15, p = 0.26), sex: (39% female; χ² (df = 1) = 0.0002, p = 0.99), days post-stroke at study inclusion (9.98 ± 5.49 days; t (df = 36.00) = –1.44, p = 0.16), baseline BBT score at T1 (23.63 ± 16.98 blocks/min; t (df = 36.50) = 1.40, p = 0.17).

The BBT performance of the included participants was assessed at two time points: T1 (16.06 ± 6.77 days post-stroke) and T2 (26.39 ± 11.34 days post-stroke). Across the included cohort, the BBT recovery between T1 and T2 was 6.44 ± 6.52 blocks/min. 

3.1. Associations between SD, δ-GE asymmetry, and BBT recovery

As illustrated in Figure 1, all assumptions required for mediation analysis were met. Greater structural disconnection (SD) in the ipsilesional hemisphere was significantly associated with greater global efficiency asymmetry in resting-state EEG functional connectivity (r(13) = 0.44, p = 0.02; Fig. 1.A). Higher SD thus corresponded to relatively increased global efficiency in the ipsilesional hemisphere compared to the contralesional side. Similarly, global efficiency asymmetry was positively associated with improvements in manual dexterity, as measured by BBT recovery (r(13) = 0.54, p = 0.04; Fig. 1.B). Individuals in whom ipsilesional efficiency exceeded contralesional efficiency tended to show greater gains in manual performance during inpatient rehabilitation.

3.2. Mediation effects of functional connectivity

The mediation model (Fig.2) revealed a significant indirect effect of ipsilesional SD on BBT improvements via global efficiency asymmetry (ACME = 6.7, p = 0.048).

This indirect pathway accounted for 32% of the variability in BBT change scores. The direct effect of SD on BBT change was not significant (ADE = 14.4, p = 0.18), although the total effect combining direct and indirect components (c = 21.1, p = 0.18) was in the expected positive direction.

Taken together, these results indicate that the relationship between structural injury and functional recovery of hand dexterity is partly transmitted through early post-stroke reorganization of functional network efficiency, particularly the relative efficiency balance between ipsilesional and contralesional hemispheres.

Conclusion

The aim of this study was to improve understanding of how stroke-related anatomical injury contributes to variability in recovery of hand and arm function by jointly considering structural and functional properties of large-scale brain networks. Consistent with our hypothesis, we found that delta global efficiency (δ-GE) asymmetry mediated the effect of ipsilesional white-matter structural disconnections (SD) on recovery measured by change in BBT performance.

These findings are supported by prior work demonstrating that white-matter disconnections, particularly involving the superior longitudinal fasciculus, which connects frontal and parietal cortices with sensorimotor regions, play a critical role in explaining variability in recovery of complex motor functions after stroke,2 herein as BBT recovery. Moreover, our results extend existing evidence on the relationship between structural and functional connectivity3,5 by providing evidence consistent with a causal pathway linking SD, FC, and recovery of complex motor behavior.

Mechanistically, these results suggest that structural damage compromises the efficiency of large-scale network communication, thereby impairing the integration of motor and cognitive processes required for complex dexterous behavior. Functional network asymmetry, specifically reduced global efficiency in the ipsilesional hemisphere relative to the contralesional hemisphere, may reflect maladaptive network reorganization or interhemispheric imbalance that constrains recovery. Taken together, these findings support the conceptualization of stroke not merely as focal tissue damage but as a disorder of large-scale network organization affecting both structural and functional connectivity.

A key strength of this study is the integration of acute clinical stroke data with detailed neurophysiological measures, enabling a network-based framework linking lesion-induced structural disconnections, functional network dynamics, and behavioral outcomes. Clinically, these findings highlight the potential value of combining structural and functional network metrics to predict recovery trajectories, identify individuals at risk for poor hand function recovery, and inform targeted rehabilitation strategies. For example, interventions aimed at enhancing ipsilesional network efficiency or restoring interhemispheric balance, such as non-invasive brain stimulation, task-specific motor training, or neuromodulation, could be guided by individualized network profiles derived from SD and FC measures.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample size was relatively small; however, the cohort was representative of the larger study population with respect to key clinical characteristics, including age, sex, and stroke type. In the near future, we expect to expand the sample to approximately 50 participants, which will enable subnetwork-specific analyses (e.g., frontoparietal, dorsal attention, ventral attention, and sensorimotor networks), region- and voxel-level investigations,5 and the evaluation of multiple graph-theoretical metrics across additional relevant frequency bands.
