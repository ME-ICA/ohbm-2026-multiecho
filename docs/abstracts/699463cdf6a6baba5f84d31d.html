Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a debilitating eating disorder (ED) with a high mortality rate. Aberrant neuroplasticity is posited to be a pathophysiology that underlies the condition,1 indicated by reduced brain-derived neurotropic factor (BDNF) and cortical thinning. There are no pharmacological treatments approved for the treatment of AN, and talking therapies offer limited benefit in maintaining remission; therefore, novel treatment avenues are desperately needed. Psilocybin-therapy has demonstrated therapeutic benefit in alleviating psychiatric symptoms, including promising findings of safety and potential efficacy in a pilot open-label study in 10 females with AN.2 The "psychoplastogen" theory posits that a single dose of these drugs can induce rapid changes in structural and functional neuroplasticity, including an acute “hyperplastic” state, leading to enduring behavioural changes. A pro-plastic effect of classic psychedelics is supported by an increasing body of preclinical evidence, whereas the plasticity-enhancing effects in humans are more scarce due to methodological limitations when studying the living brain. However, long-term potentiation (LTP) is a form of Hebbian neuroplasticity that can be non-invasively measured in humans using an electroencephalography (EEG) visual paradigm.3 This paradigm has successfully identified neuroplasticity deficits in other psychiatric conditions, including major depressive disorder (MDD), and has shown modulation of LTP following ketamine4 and lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) microdoses5 in depressed cohorts. To date, the visual LTP (vLTP) paradigm has not been used to investigate the effects of high-dose classic psychedelics or to index neuroplasticity in AN. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether this cohort of females with AN exhibited deficits in baseline vLTP that correlated with disorder severity, and whether high-dose psilocybin would induce enhancements in vLTP. 

Methods

Twenty-one adult females with a primary DSM-5 diagnosis of AN in the PANOREXIA study (NCT04505189) underwent 3 psilocybin (COMP360: a proprietary, pharmaceutical-grade, synthetic psilocybin formulation, optimised for stability and purity, developed by COMPASS Pathfinder Ltd, United Kingdom) dosing sessions in a fixed-order (dose 1: 1 mg, dose 2: 25 mg, dose 3: 25 mg), alongside therapy. Approximately 27 hours after each dose, participants completed the vLTP paradigm. To limit carry-over effects, dosing sessions and recordings were separated by 2 weeks.3 The post-dose 1 recording was used as a "proxy baseline" for comparison with the high doses, as no effect of 1 mg was anticipated based on previous literature.5 

Data were collected using a 64-channel Anticap with Ag/AgCl active shielded electrodes and two wireless 32-channel BrainVision LiveAmp amplifiers. Participants sat centrally 100 cm from the screen in a dark room and were asked to limit excessive blinking and eye movement.

The vLTP paradigm uses a high-frequency photic tetanus (~9 Hz)  to induce visually-stimulated LTP-like plasticity within the visual cortex, a paradigm translated from pre-clinical work inducing LTP by electrical stimulation in rodents. The tetanus is preceded (pre-tetanus) and followed (post-tetanus) by non-tetanising 1 Hz stimuli to assess modulation of the visual evoked potential (VEP) after the tetanus. Two of these 1 Hz stimulus blocks are repeated 2 minutes (early post-tetanus) and 30 minutes (late post-tetanus) after the photic tetanus, to index early and enduring processes of LTP, respectively. Stimulus blocks consist of intermittent vertical or horizontal sine grating stimuli with a central red fixation dot, with stimulus orientation counterbalanced for the photic tetanus. Amplitude enhancements in the negative N1b  (~132 - 200 ms) and the positive P2 (~178 - 240 ms) components are recognised as robust markers of the vLTP paradigm.3

Early and late difference waves were computed by subtracting the pre-tetanus data from the post-tetanus data (early and late). Analyses were performed in SPM12 using MATLAB. Results were presented as clusters of significance across time and space, with Family-Wise Error correction (FWEc) implemented using Random Field Theory. Both FWE-corrected and uncorrected results are reported, utilising p<0.05 corrected and p<0.001 uncorrected thresholds. The analyses were restricted to the pre-defined time window (1–300 ms) and occipital-parietal-central Region of Interest (ROI). A p-value of <0.05 (FWEc) was considered significant.

Two ANOVAs (early vs. pre and late vs. pre) were used to confirm the induction of vLTP by the paradigm during post-dose 1 (“proxy baseline”). Pearson correlations were conducted between vLTP capacity (minimum peak N1b and maximum peak P2 values) and baseline Eating Disorder Examination (EDE) interview scores, controlling for baseline Body Mass Index (BMI), with Benjamini-Hochberg correction applied. The EDE is a clinician-administered interview assessing the severity of ED symptoms. 

In parameter-finding analyses, early and late difference waves were submitted to 2 x 2 ANOVAs (psilocybin dose: Dose 1 or Dose 2/Dose 3 x tetanus orientation: tetanised or non-tetanised) to confirm the induction of vLTP by the paradigm, irrespective of pharmacological intervention, and to identify significant clusters of N1(b) or P2 potentiation for further dose-dependent analyses. Two-tailed paired t-tests (Dose 1 vs. Dose 2; Dose 1 vs. Dose 3) examined the effect of dose within these clusters. Exploratory ANOVA F-contrasts were performed across 1 – 300 ms to further examine dose-dependent effects. Drug effect data were correlated with clinical outcomes, including raw and percentage change in global EDE and Readiness and Motivation Questionnaire (RMQ) precontemplation scores (measure of ED-related motivation to change).

Results

LTP induction by the visual paradigm at the proxy baseline (Dose 1) was successfully confirmed by one early N1b and two late P2 significant clusters. There were no significant correlations between baseline EDE scores and N1b potentiation. There was a statistically significant negative correlation of moderate magnitude between the potentiation of the P2 in one of the identified clusters (184 ms – 218 ms) and the baseline EDE interview scores (r = -0.64, p = 0.008). This relationship, indicating that higher baseline AN psychopathology severity was associated with a reduced capacity for visually-induced LTP (vLTP) induction, survived the control for multiple comparisons and baseline BMI (r = -0.64, p = 0.01, p.fdr = 0.04).

No significant drug effects were identified during the early post-tetanus condition.

In dose 1 vs. dose 2 late condition main effect of drug analysis (1 – 300 ms), a negative cluster (160 ms – 168 ms, peaking at 162 ms), which did not survive FWE correction for multiple comparisons (F(68) = 12.7, p.uncorrected = 6.89 x 10-4, p = 0.31 FWE-c), was reported. However, this cluster sat outside the time window of P2 potentiation, which had previously been identified in the parameter finding analyses (182 - 224 ms).

Dose 1 vs. dose 3 analysis of the main effect of drug across the entire VEP time window (1 - 300 ms) revealed two negative FWE-uncorrected clusters (p < 0.001). The first of these peaked at 188 ms (174 ms – 200 ms, F(60) = 16.7, p.uncorrected = 1.31 x 10-4, p = 0.09 FWE-c) and the second at 158 ms (152 ms – 170 ms, F(60) = 15.6, p.uncorrected = 2.13  x 10-4, p = 0.14 FWE-c). Only the cluster peaking at 188 ms sat within a previously identified time window of P2 potentiation (186 ms – 220 ms). Directionality assessment indicated a positive direction of this drug effect, indicating that this finding could be due to P2 enhancement by dose 3 (T(60) =   3.94, p.uncorrected = 6.53 x 10-5, p = 0.048 FWE-c).

Results across all correlation analyses revealed no significant relationships between changes in clinical outcomes and drug-induced P2 potentiation, suggesting that this drug effect was not associated with greater clinical improvement.  

Conclusion

This study is the first to use the vLTP paradigm to index Hebbian neuroplasticity in AN and the first to report the effect of a high dose of a classic psychedelic on vLTP. 

The primary finding indicates a significant, moderate negative correlation whereby higher baseline AN symptom severity correlated with an attenuated capacity for vLTP induction, as indexed by reduced P2 potentiation. The survival of this neuroplastic deficit after controlling for BMI suggests that reduced LTP capacity may not merely be a manifestation of “neuroprogression” - whereby compensatory neurobiological and neuroanatomical changes occur to conserve energy as an adaptive result of malnutrition - but rather may relate to the core cognitive symptoms of AN that potentially predate the condition, positioning this deficit as a potential premorbid predisposing factor in disorder development. This result substantially contributes to the limited existing evidence of neuroplastic deficits in AN and supports further work to investigate the vLTP paradigm in this patient group. These findings have implications for identifying a neuroplastic biomarker for disorder severity, diagnosis, and treatment response, as well as for identifying individuals at a greater risk of disorder development.

Regarding the pharmacological intervention, greater late post-tetanus P2 enhancement was indicated after the second 25 mg dose (Dose 3) compared to the proxy baseline dose (Dose 1), at an uncorrected p<0.001 significance threshold. This finding tentatively and partially supports the hypothesis that a high dose of psilocybin would enhance LTP. Despite not reaching FWE-corrected significance, likely due to the small sample size, this result represents the first indication of high-dose classic psychedelic-induced neuroplasticity enhancement in human subjects. The enhancement of the P2 component at approximately 27 hours post-dosing suggests that psilocybin-induced pro-plasticity effects potentially endure following the acute experience. This tentative evidence partially replicates the P2 potentiation observed following ketamine treatment in MDD patients.4 

Importantly, no significant relationship was found between the drug-induced P2 potentiation and changes in clinical outcomes. This result is in line with a previous ketamine study, in which depressive symptoms were alleviated during the time window of P2 potentiation, without a significant relationship identified.4 It is hypothesised that while neuroplasticity may remain a crucial underlying mechanism, its primary role may be to augment receptivity to the adjunct therapeutic interventions, rather than directly inducing clinical symptom improvement. Due to the limited sample size and low power of the analyses, the findings reported here were underpowered to make conclusive and reliable interpretations and are tentatively reported. As such, findings should be interpreted with caution, and efforts should be made to replicate and substantiate these findings in a higher-powered study.
